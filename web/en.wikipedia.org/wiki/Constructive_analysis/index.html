Constructive analysis

In mathematics, constructive analysis is mathematical analysis done according to the principles of constructive mathematics — that is, accepting a proof that an object exists only if the object can, in principle, be constructed or computed. The modern approach is associated above all with Errett Bishop, whose 1967 book Foundations of Constructive Analysis showed that large parts of classical analysis could be developed constructively without abandoning its numerical meaning.

Constructive real numbers

A constructive real number is typically given by a sequence of rational numbers together with a modulus of convergence — an explicit rate at which the sequence settles down. This definition avoids relying on the unrestricted law of excluded middle. Because constructive logic cannot decide arbitrary equalities on such sequences, the usual trichotomy of the classical real line fails: one cannot prove that every real number is positive, negative or zero. Instead constructive analysis uses the apartness relation: two reals are apart when a positive rational distance can be exhibited between them.

Differences from classical analysis

Some cornerstones of classical analysis fail constructively or require strengthened hypotheses. The intermediate value theorem cannot be proved in full generality; instead one obtains approximate versions (any continuous function on an interval with values of opposite sign at the endpoints takes values arbitrarily close to zero), or an exact theorem under additional conditions such as local non-constancy. The least-upper-bound principle likewise holds only in approximate form. Bishop's programme answers such gaps by reformulating theorems — for example, defining compactness directly through total boundedness and completeness — so that the resulting theory retains computational content and applies to computable analysis and to the internal logic of a topos.

Schools

Constructive analysis comes in several varieties, often associated with Bishop's neutral framework (BISH), Brouwer's intuitionistic analysis, which adopts continuity principles and bar induction, and the recursive constructive mathematics of the Russian school, which assumes a form of Church's thesis. Results proved in Bishop's framework are valid in all of these systems, as well as classically.
