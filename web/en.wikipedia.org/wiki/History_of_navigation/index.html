History of navigation

DisciplineNavigation
Key toolsCompass, astrolabe, chronometer, GPS
MilestonesPolynesian voyaging; longitude problem solved (18th c.)

The history of navigation recounts how sailors learned to find their way across oceans — by stars, compass, charts and clocks — before the satellite era.

Early navigation

Polynesian navigators settled the Pacific using stars, swells, winds and birds. Mediterranean sailors coasted by landmarks; the magnetic compass, known in China by the 11th century, reached Europe by the 12th. Latitude could be found from the Sun and Pole Star with quadrant and astrolabe.

The longitude problem

Dead reckoning could not fix longitude. After disasters such as the Scilly wreck of 1707, Britain offered a great prize; John Harrison's marine chronometers (H4, 1759) and the lunar-distance method (Maskelyne's Nautical Almanac, 1767) made accurate longitude practical.

Electronic navigation

Radio direction finding, radar, LORAN and inertial systems served the 20th century, until satellite navigation — above all GPS (fully operational 1995) — made precise positioning universal and largely displaced classical celestial navigation, which navies still teach as a backup.
