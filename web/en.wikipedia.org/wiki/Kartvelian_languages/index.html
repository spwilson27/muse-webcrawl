Kartvelian languages

The Kartvelian languages (Georgian: ქართველური ენები), also known as the South Caucasian or Iberian languages, are a language family indigenous to the South Caucasus, spoken chiefly in Georgia and by small communities in northeastern Turkey. The family comprises four languages: Georgian, Svan, Mingrelian, and Laz.

Classification and history

Georgian is by far the largest Kartvelian language, with around four million speakers, and is the official language of Georgia; it has a literary tradition reaching back to the 5th century AD and its own alphabet. Svan, spoken by a few thousand people in the highland region of Svaneti, is generally considered the first branch to split from the common proto-language. Mingrelian and Laz form the Zan subgroup: Mingrelian is spoken in western Georgia (Samegrelo), and Laz mainly along the southeastern Black Sea coast of Turkey. None of the three smaller languages has an official written standard, and all are considered endangered to varying degrees.

The Kartvelian family is not demonstrably related to any other language family. Attempts to link it to the North Caucasian families, Basque, or a proposed "Nostratic" macrofamily remain unproven, and Kartvelian is usually treated as an isolate family native to the Caucasus region since prehistoric times.

Features

Kartvelian languages are agglutinative and notable for consonant clusters, a polypersonal verb system marking subject and objects, split ergativity in certain tense series (the so-called screeves), and ejective consonants. Georgian uses a case system of seven cases and an alignment that shifts between nominative-accusative and ergative patterns depending on the tense series.
