Smart card

A smart card (chip card, or integrated circuit card, ICC) is a physical electronic authentication device, used to control access to a resource. It is typically a plastic credit card-sized card with an embedded integrated circuit (IC) chip. Many smart cards include a pattern of metal contacts to electrically connect to the internal chip. Others are contactless, and some are both. Smart cards can provide personal identification, authentication, data storage, and application processing. Applications include identification, financial, mobile phones (SIM cards), public transit, computer security, schools, and healthcare.

History

The basis for the smart card is the invention of the silicon integrated circuit chip in 1959. German engineer Helmut Gröttrup proposed integrated circuits on a payment card in 1968, and French inventor Roland Moreno filed the original patent for the memory chip card in 1974. In 1977 Michel Ugon of Honeywell Bull invented the first microprocessor smart card. Bull, Schlumberger and others commercialised the technology in France in the early 1980s, where it was adopted for payphones (télécarte) and, from 1992, for all French debit cards (Carte Bancaire). The EMV standard, developed by Europay, Mastercard and Visa in the 1990s, spread chip payment cards worldwide.

Design

A smart card may have the following generic characteristics:

  • Dimensions similar to those of a credit card; the most common format, ID-1, measures 85.60 × 53.98 mm.
  • Contains a tamper-resistant security system (for example a secure microcontroller) and provides security services (e.g. protects in-memory information).
  • Managed by an administration system which securely interchanges information and configuration settings with the card, including card issuance, data and application management.

Contact cards communicate through electrical contacts meeting ISO/IEC 7816. Contactless cards use radio-frequency induction (NFC, ISO/IEC 14443) and power the chip from the reader's field. Hybrid and dual-interface cards combine both. Communication with the chip uses APDU commands defined in ISO/IEC 7816-4; on-card software may be a fixed application or a multi-application platform such as Java Card or MULTOS.

Applications

  • Financial: credit/debit cards (EMV), stored-value and loyalty cards.
  • Telecommunications: the SIM/USIM card in every GSM and later mobile phone.
  • Identification: national ID cards, passports' chips, driving licences, and corporate access badges.
  • Transport: contactless transit fare cards such as London's Oyster and Hong Kong's Octopus.
  • Computer security: logon credentials and the US Department of Defense Common Access Card (CAC).
  • Pay television: conditional-access cards for satellite and cable receivers.

Security

Smart cards are designed to be tamper-resistant: the chip can hold cryptographic keys that never leave the card, performing signing and decryption internally, typically after the holder enters a PIN. Attacks include side-channel analysis (power and timing analysis), fault injection, and probing; countermeasures include sensors, shielding and constant-time cryptography. Common Criteria evaluations are widely used to certify card security.
