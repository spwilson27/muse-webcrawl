Lexical set

A lexical set is a group of words that share a particular phonological feature, used by linguists to describe and compare vowel systems across dialects of a language. The concept was developed by the phonetician John C. Wells in his work Accents of English (1982).

Wells's standard lexical sets

Wells assigned keywords to the vowel phonemes of Received Pronunciation and General American — for example KIT, DRESS, TRAP, LOT, STRUT, FOOT, BATH, CLOTH, NURSE, FLEECE, FACE, PALM, THOUGHT, GOAT, GOOSE, PRICE, CHOICE, MOUTH, near, square, start, north, force, cure, happY, lettEr, and commA. Because the keywords are stable spellings, researchers can describe an accent by stating which words share a vowel in that accent, rather than by transcribing every word.

Uses

Lexical sets are widely used in dialectology and sociolinguistics — for instance, to describe the TRAP–BATH split in southern British English or the LOT–THOUGHT (cot–caught) merger in North America — and sets have since been devised for other languages and for consonants.
