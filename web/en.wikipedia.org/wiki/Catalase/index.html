Catalase

EC number1.11.1.6
SubstrateHydrogen peroxide
ProductsWater and oxygen
LocationPeroxisomes (chiefly)

Catalase is a common enzyme found in nearly all living organisms exposed to oxygen, including bacteria, plants, and animals. It catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide — a harmful by-product of metabolic processes — into water and oxygen:

2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2

By destroying hydrogen peroxide, catalase protects cells from oxidative damage by reactive oxygen species. It is one of the most efficient enzymes known, with one of the highest turnover numbers of any enzyme: a single catalase molecule can convert millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules into water and oxygen each second.

Structure

Typical catalases are tetramers of four polypeptide chains, each over 500 amino acids long and containing an iron-containing haem group at its active site, which carries out the reaction. In animal cells the enzyme is concentrated in peroxisomes; in the human body it is found in high concentrations in the liver.

Applications and tests

The catalase test, in which a drop of hydrogen peroxide is added to a bacterial sample and observed for bubbling, is a standard microbiology technique used to distinguish staphylococci (catalase-positive) from streptococci (catalase-negative). Catalase is also used in the food industry to remove hydrogen peroxide from milk in cheese production, in textile processing, and in contact lens cleaning systems. A deficiency of catalase in humans causes the rare genetic disorder acatalasia, which is associated with oral ulceration and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes.
