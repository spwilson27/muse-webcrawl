Barry James Marshall (born 30 September 1951) is an Australian physician, Nobel Prize laureate, and professor of clinical microbiology at the University of Western Australia. Marshall and his colleague Robin Warren showed that the bacterium Helicobacter pylori causes most peptic ulcers, overturning decades of medical doctrine that ulcers were caused primarily by stress and stomach acid.

The Helicobacter discovery

Working at Royal Perth Hospital in the early 1980s, Marshall collaborated with pathologist Robin Warren, who had observed curved bacteria colonising the stomachs of patients with gastritis. In 1982 they cultured the organism — initially classified as a Campylobacter and later named Helicobacter pylori — and in 1983 proposed that it caused gastritis and peptic ulceration.

Self-experimentation

After initial scepticism from the medical establishment and failure to infect animal models, Marshall famously drank a broth culture of H. pylori in 1984. Within days he developed symptoms of gastritis, which biopsy confirmed, and the condition resolved after antibiotic treatment. The episode provided dramatic early evidence for the hypothesis.

Recognition

Antibiotic eradication therapy transformed peptic ulcer disease from a chronic, recurring condition managed with surgery or lifelong acid suppression into an illness curable with a short course of treatment. Marshall and Warren received the Albert Lasker Award in 1995 and shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005. Marshall continues research at the University of Western Australia, including work on the Ondek biotech company and H. pylori as a basis for oral vaccine delivery.
