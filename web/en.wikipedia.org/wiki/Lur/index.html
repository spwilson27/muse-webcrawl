A lur, also lure or lurr, is a long natural blowing horn without finger holes, played by embouchure like a trumpet. Two main types exist: the Bronze Age bronze lur of Scandinavia, a large, gracefully curved cast-bronze instrument typically found in pairs in bogs in Denmark and southern Sweden; and the later wooden lur of medieval and folk Norway, a long trumpet of wood (often wrapped in birch bark) used by herders for signalling and calling cattle. The name is also applied to similar Scandinavian herding horns.

Bronze lurs

Bronze lurs date from the Nordic Bronze Age, roughly the 13th to 6th centuries BC. About 56 examples have been found in Denmark, southern Sweden, northern Germany and Latvia, most famously the six nearly intact lurs from the bog at Brudevælte in Denmark, discovered by a peat-cutter in 1797. They consist of an S-shaped tube of cast bronze sections up to about 2.4 metres long, ending in a decorated disc-shaped bell, and were almost always deposited in pairs tuned to the same key — suggesting ritual or ceremonial use, consistent with their depiction in Bronze Age rock carvings and their deposition in sacred bogs. Surviving instruments remain playable and have been recorded.

Wooden lurs

The medieval and folk lur of Norway (Norwegian: lur, neverlur when birch-bark wrapped) is a straight or slightly conical wooden tube, commonly 1.5–2 metres long. Traditionally made by splitting a billet of pine or spruce, hollowing it, gluing the halves and binding them with willow or birch bark, it was used by mountain dairy herders (in the seter tradition) to call livestock, signal across valleys and, in folklore, to frighten away predators and trolls. Shorter herding horns of birch bark (bukkehorn is a related but different instrument made from a goat horn) belong to the same folk tradition.

Modern use

The word survives in the Danish butter brand Lurpak, whose logo shows two crossed bronze lurs. Reproductions of bronze lurs are played in early-music and archaeological contexts.
