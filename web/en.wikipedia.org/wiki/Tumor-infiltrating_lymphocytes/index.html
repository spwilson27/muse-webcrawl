Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are white blood cells — principally T lymphocytes — that have left the bloodstream and migrated into a tumor. Their presence within the tumor microenvironment is part of the immune system's attempt to recognise and attack cancer cells. TILs are found in many solid tumors and their abundance and composition carry prognostic and predictive significance.

Composition and significance

TIL populations typically include cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, helper CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells, and smaller numbers of B cells and natural killer cells. In cancers such as melanoma, ovarian cancer and colorectal cancer, a dense infiltration of CD8+ T cells is generally associated with better patient outcomes, reflecting an active anti-tumor immune response. Conversely, tumors dominated by regulatory T cells or exclusion of T cells from the tumor core tend to have worse prognoses. Pathologists can score TILs on stained tissue sections — for example the stromal TIL score in breast cancer, which helps predict response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

TIL therapy

TILs also form the basis of an adoptive cell therapy pioneered by Steven Rosenberg and colleagues at the U.S. National Cancer Institute. In TIL therapy, lymphocytes are harvested from a patient's resected tumor, the tumor-reactive cells are expanded in the laboratory — typically with interleukin-2 — to billions of cells, and then reinfused into the patient after lymphodepleting chemotherapy, followed by IL-2 support. The approach has produced durable complete responses in some patients with metastatic melanoma, and in February 2024 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted accelerated approval to lifileucel (Amtagvi), the first TIL therapy, for advanced melanoma previously treated with other therapies. Research continues into TIL therapy for cervical, lung and other cancers.
