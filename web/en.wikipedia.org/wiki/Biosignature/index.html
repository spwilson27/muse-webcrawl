Biosignature

Hero image: atmospheric spectroscopy of a distant planet, searching for chemical signs of life.

A biosignature is any substance, element, isotopic pattern, or phenomenon that provides scientific evidence of past or present life. Measurable attributes of life include its complex physical and chemical structures, its use of free energy, and the production of biomass and waste. Biosignatures are central to astrobiology's search for life on Mars, the icy moons of the outer Solar System, and exoplanets.

Types

Gaseous biosignatures are the most discussed for exoplanets: oxygen and its photochemical product ozone, produced on Earth overwhelmingly by photosynthesis; methane in thermodynamic disequilibrium with oxygen; and gases such as nitrous oxide and methyl chloride. Surface biosignatures include the vegetation "red edge" — the sharp rise in plant reflectance in the near-infrared. On planetary surfaces and in rocks, biosignatures include stromatolites and microbial textures, fractionated carbon and sulfur isotopes, biomarker molecules such as hopanes and steranes, and chiral excesses in organic compounds, all of which are used to interpret the earliest evidence of life on Earth.

False positives and ambiguity

The field stresses that no single measurement suffices: abiotic processes can mimic many biosignatures. Oxygen can accumulate on planets losing water to space; methane is produced geologically by serpentinization; and the phosphine reported in Venus's clouds in 2020 sparked debate about both the detection itself and possible abiotic chemistry. Controversies over the Martian meteorite ALH84001 (1996) and Viking lander experiments (1976) shaped today's rigorous, context-based frameworks for assessing candidate biosignatures.

Search strategies

Current efforts include rover missions analyzing Martian sediments (with sample return planned), telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope characterizing exoplanet atmospheres, and proposed missions to Europa and Enceladus, whose subsurface oceans may bear chemical traces of life. The guiding principle is the accumulation of independent, corroborating lines of evidence in a well-understood planetary context.
