Boilerplate contract

A boilerplate contract is a standard-form contract between two parties that does not allow for negotiation or alterations. Such contracts are sometimes called contracts of adhesion. The term boilerplate refers to sections of standard text, terms and conditions that are used repeatedly and are often printed in fine print at the end of documents.

Boilerplate language is stored and reused in many different documents — for example, standard clauses covering force majeure, severability, entire agreement, governing law, assignment and notices. By reusing carefully drafted wording, businesses reduce drafting costs and legal risk, though critics argue that standard terms may be unfair when imposed by a party with greater bargaining power on one that has no realistic ability to negotiate.

Origin of the term

The term comes from the steel plates used as templates in steam boiler manufacture, and in the 19th century was applied to the syndicated, ready-to-print material supplied to newspapers on metal plates. It came to denote any stock, unchanging copy, including standard legal provisions.

Criticism and regulation

Because boilerplate terms are rarely read, many jurisdictions regulate them. Consumer law in the European Union, the United Kingdom and elsewhere allows courts to strike down unfair terms in consumer contracts; doctrines such as contra proferentem construe ambiguities against the drafter, and surprising or onerous clauses may require explicit notice to be enforceable.
