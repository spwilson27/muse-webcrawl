Scottish fiddle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Scottish fiddle tradition encompasses the distinctive regional styles of fiddle playing in Scotland, developed since the instrument became established in Scottish dance music in the late seventeenth century. Though the instrument is the standard violin, Scottish fiddling is defined by its dance-derived repertoire — strathspeys, reels, jigs, marches, and slow airs — and by characteristic bowing and ornamentation, above all the driving rhythms and "Scotch snap" (a short note followed by a longer one) that give the music its lift.

Regional styles

Four broad regional styles are usually distinguished. The Northeast style, centred on Aberdeenshire, is elegant and technically polished, shaped by classical influences and the printed collections of the Gow family. The West Coast and Highlands style is closer to the pipe tradition, with tunes adapted from bagpipe repertoire and ornaments echoing pipe grace notes. The Shetland style, influenced by Norwegian music and long isolation, is lively and rhythmical, using ringing open strings. The Borders style is influenced by Northumbrian and English playing across the border.

History

The fiddle displaced older instruments as Scotland's principal dance instrument in the 1700s. The first printed collection of Scottish fiddle music appeared in the 1740s. Niel Gow (1727–1807), a fiddler from Dunkeld who played for the Duke of Atholl, became the tradition's defining figure, and his sons published many of his compositions; William Marshall and later James Scott Skinner (1843–1927), "the Strathspey King", a virtuoso who recorded some of the earliest fiddle discs, shaped the concert style of the Northeast. Captain Simon Fraser's collection of Highland airs (1816) preserved Gaelic repertoire.

Modern tradition

Scottish fiddle music remains at the heart of ceilidh dancing. Fiddle societies and orchestral fiddle groups flourish, competitions are held at festivals such as the Glenfiddich Fiddle Championship, and contemporary players such as Aly Bain (from Shetland), Alasdair Fraser, and younger bands like Blazin' Fiddles have carried the tradition internationally, influencing Celtic music in Canada — particularly the Cape Breton style, which descends directly from Scottish immigrants.
