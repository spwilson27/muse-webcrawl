The Afghan mujahideen were the Islamist guerrilla fighters who waged war against the Soviet Union and the Soviet-backed Democratic Republic of Afghanistan during the Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989), and then fought among themselves in the Afghan civil war that followed. "Mujahideen" is the plural of mujahid, one who engages in jihad.

Factions

The resistance was never unified. The principal Sunni parties based in Peshawar — the "Peshawar Seven" — included Gulbuddin Hekmatyar's Hezb-e Islami, Burhanuddin Rabbani's Jamiat-e Islami (whose military commander Ahmad Shah Massoud became the most celebrated resistance leader), and the parties of Yunus Khalis, Abdul Rasul Sayyaf and others, divided by ethnicity, ideology and personal rivalry. Shia groups based in Iran formed the "Tehran Eight".

Foreign support

The mujahideen received extensive covert assistance — funnelled mainly through Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence — from the United States (Operation Cyclone, one of the largest covert programmes of the Cold War), Saudi Arabia, China and others. The supply of US-made Stinger anti-aircraft missiles from 1986 significantly blunted Soviet air superiority. The USSR withdrew in February 1989; the mujahideen captured Kabul in April 1992 after the collapse of the Najibullah government, but their interim government immediately fractured into civil war, creating the chaos from which the Taliban emerged in 1994–96.
