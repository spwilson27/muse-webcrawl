The Mexican drug war (Spanish: Guerra contra el narcotráfico en México) is an ongoing asymmetric armed conflict between the Mexican government and various drug trafficking syndicates. When the Mexican military intervened in December 2006 under President Felipe Calderón, violence escalated sharply as rival cartels fought for control of trafficking routes into the United States.

Background

Mexican trafficking organisations grew in power as Colombian cartels were weakened in the 1990s, shifting the cocaine trade's logistics to Mexico. The end of one-party PRI rule in 2000 disrupted prior informal arrangements between traffickers and officials. Calderón's deployment of federal forces to Michoacán in December 2006 marked the start of the militarised campaign.

Course of the conflict

The government's "kingpin strategy" of capturing or killing cartel leaders fragmented organisations — notably Los Zetas breaking from the Gulf Cartel, and the Sinaloa Cartel splitting — spawning more violent splinter groups. Homicides peaked around 2011, declined briefly, then rose to record levels in 2018–2019 under President Andrés Manuel López Obrador, whose "hugs, not bullets" (abrazos, no balazos) policy de-emphasised confrontation while a new National Guard was created. The Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG) emerged as Sinaloa's principal rival.

Human cost

Since 2006, Mexico has recorded hundreds of thousands of homicides, with organized-crime-related killings estimated in the hundreds of thousands; more than 100,000 people are officially registered as disappeared. Journalists and local politicians are frequent targets, making Mexico one of the deadliest countries for the press.

US involvement

The United States supports Mexico through the Mérida Initiative (from 2008), providing equipment, training and intelligence, while the US remains the main demand market and a principal source of cartel firearms. High-profile US operations, such as ATF's Operation Fast and Furious, became scandals in both countries.
