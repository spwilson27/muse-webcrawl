Mao Zedong Thought

Mao Zedong Thought (Chinese: 毛泽东思想; pinyin: Máo Zédōng sīxiǎng), also known outside China as Maoism, is a variety of Marxism–Leninism developed by Mao Zedong (1893–1976), leader of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). It is the official guiding ideology of the CCP alongside Marxism–Leninism, and it remains enshrined in the party's constitution and that of the People's Republic of China. The term was formally generalised at the Seventh National Congress of the CCP in 1945, where Liu Shaoqi presented it as the synthesis of Marxist theory with the practice of the Chinese revolution.

Core components

Mao Zedong Thought is held by the CCP to comprise the application of Marxism–Leninism to the specific conditions of China. Its principal components include:

  • New Democracy — the theory that in semi-colonial, semi-feudal countries the revolution proceeds in two stages: first a new-democratic revolution uniting several classes against imperialism and feudalism, then a socialist revolution.
  • The mass line (qúnzhòng lùxiàn) — the leadership method summarised as "from the masses, to the masses": gathering the scattered ideas of the masses, concentrating them into policy, and returning them to the masses for implementation.
  • Protracted people's war — a military strategy based on mobilising the peasantry, establishing rural base areas, and gradually encircling the cities from the countryside.
  • Theory of contradictions — developed in Mao's essays On Contradiction (1937) and On Practice (1937), holding that contradiction is universal and that in any process one principal contradiction predominates.
  • Continuing the revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat — the theory, developed later in Mao's life, that class struggle continues within socialist society and that bourgeois elements can arise within the party itself, which formed the ideological basis of the Cultural Revolution.

Development

Mao's theoretical writings date from the 1920s and 1930s, including the Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan (1927), On Guerrilla Warfare (1937), and On Protracted War (1938). During the Yan'an Rectification Movement of 1942–1944 his ideas were systematised and promoted throughout the party. After the founding of the People's Republic in 1949, Mao Zedong Thought guided policies such as land reform, the Great Leap Forward, and the Cultural Revolution.

Maoism outside China

Abroad, "Maoism" often denotes movements that adopted Mao's theories of people's war and continuous revolution, including the Shining Path in Peru, the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist), and the Naxalite movement in India. In 1993 the Communist Party of Peru and allied organisations codified "Marxism–Leninism–Maoism" as a claimed third and higher stage of Marxism, a formulation not used by the CCP itself.

Assessment

After Mao's death in 1976, Deng Xiaoping and the CCP leadership retained Mao Zedong Thought in the party constitution while judging that Mao's later policies contained serious errors; the 1981 party resolution assessed Mao as a great revolutionary whose merits were primary and whose mistakes were secondary.
