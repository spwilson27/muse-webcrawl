Apollodorus of Damascus

Apollodorus of Damascus (c. 50 – c. 130 AD) was a Greek engineer and architect from Damascus, Roman Syria, who served as the favourite architect of the emperor Trajan. He is credited with many of the monumental building works of Trajan's reign, including Trajan's Bridge over the Danube, Trajan's Forum and Trajan's Column in Rome, and he wrote a treatise on siege engines, the Poliorcetica. He is regarded as one of the greatest architects of the Roman world.

Career under Trajan

Apollodorus accompanied Trajan on the Dacian Wars as a military engineer. His most famous engineering feat for the campaigns was Trajan's Bridge (103–105 AD) across the Danube near Drobeta — at more than 1,100 metres, the longest arch bridge in the world for over a thousand years, built with wooden arches on twenty masonry piers to supply the invasion of Dacia.

In Rome he directed the construction of Trajan's Forum (dedicated 112 AD), the largest of the imperial forums, including the Basilica Ulpia, the libraries, the equestrian statue of Trajan, and Trajan's Column (113 AD), whose helical relief frieze narrates the Dacian Wars. He is also associated with Trajan's Markets, the great baths of Trajan on the Oppian Hill, the rebuilding of the Pantheon (though this is debated and often assigned to Hadrian's reign), and the harbour works at Centumcellae (Civitavecchia) and Ancona, where the Arch of Trajan still stands.

Fall from favour and death

According to the historian Cassius Dio, after Hadrian succeeded Trajan in 117 AD, Apollodorus mocked the new emperor's amateur architectural designs — Hadrian had once been rebuked by Trajan for interrupting a consultation between the emperor and his architect. Dio reports that Hadrian first banished Apollodorus and later had him put to death on a fabricated charge; some later sources instead attribute to Apollodorus several Hadrianic projects, such as the Temple of Venus and Roma, so the details of his end remain uncertain.

Writings

His Poliorcetica ("On Siegecraft"), addressed to an unnamed emperor (probably Trajan), survives in Byzantine manuscripts with illustrations of siege engines, and describes rams, tortoises, towers, and other machines in the Hellenistic engineering tradition.
