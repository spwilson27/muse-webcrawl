Treaty of Paris (1763)

Treaty of Paris
Signed10 February 1763
LocationParis, France
PartiesGreat Britain; France; Spain (with Portugal)
EffectEnded the Seven Years' War

The Treaty of Paris of 1763 was signed on 10 February 1763 by Great Britain, France and Spain, with Portugal adhering, and ended the Seven Years' War (known in North America as the French and Indian War). Together with the Treaty of Hubertusburg between Prussia and Austria, it transformed the colonial balance of power, establishing Britain as the world's dominant overseas empire and drastically reducing France's presence in North America.

Terms

France ceded to Britain all of Canada and its claims east of the Mississippi River, retaining only the tiny islands of Saint Pierre and Miquelon and fishing rights off Newfoundland. In the Caribbean, France recovered Guadeloupe and Martinique — sugar islands it valued more than Canada, prompting Voltaire's famous dismissal of Canada as "a few acres of snow". Spain ceded Florida to Britain and received Louisiana west of the Mississippi, plus New Orleans, from France by the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau (1762). In India, France recovered its trading posts but recognised British ascendancy; Britain also gained Senegal, Minorca, and several Caribbean islands including Grenada and the "neutral islands".

Consequences

Britain emerged with an empire spanning North America, the Caribbean and India, but also with a doubled national debt — the attempt to make the American colonies contribute to its cost, through measures such as the Stamp Act, set the colonies on the road to the American Revolution. France, humiliated, rebuilt its navy and later intervened decisively on the American side in that conflict.
