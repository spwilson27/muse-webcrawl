Surface runoff

Surface runoff (also known as overland flow) is the flow of water occurring on the ground surface when excess rainwater, stormwater, meltwater or water from other sources can no longer sufficiently rapidly infiltrate the soil. It is a major component of the water cycle and the primary agent of soil erosion by water.

Overland flow carrying sediment from agricultural land

Generation

Runoff is generated when rainfall intensity exceeds the soil's infiltration capacity (infiltration-excess, or Hortonian, overland flow), or when the soil becomes saturated from below (saturation-excess overland flow). Impervious surfaces such as roads, roofs and pavements greatly increase runoff volume and speed in urban areas.

Effects

Surface runoff erodes soil, transports nutrients, pesticides and other pollutants into streams and lakes (contributing to eutrophication), and is a principal cause of flash flooding when large volumes arrive quickly. In agriculture, runoff removes topsoil and fertiliser; in cities it overwhelms drainage and sewer systems.

Mitigation

Runoff is managed with practices such as contour ploughing, terracing, buffer strips, cover crops, permeable paving, green roofs, rain gardens and stormwater detention basins, many grouped under low-impact development and sustainable drainage systems (SuDS).
