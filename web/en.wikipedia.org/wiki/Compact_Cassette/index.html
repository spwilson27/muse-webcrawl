The Compact Cassette, commonly known simply as the cassette tape, is a magnetic tape sound recording format introduced by Philips in Europe in 1963 and in the United States in 1964. Two miniature reels inside a plastic shell carry 3.81 mm tape past the heads at 4.76 cm/s (1⅞ ips), storing two stereo pairs of tracks — one per direction — so the cassette is flipped to play the second side.

History

Developed under Lou Ottens at Philips in Hasselt, Belgium, the cassette was initially aimed at dictation, but fidelity improvements in tape formulations (Types I–IV: ferric, chrome, ferrichrome, and metal) and noise reduction (notably Dolby B) made it a serious music format. Philips licensed the format free of charge, ensuring a single worldwide standard. The Sony Walkman (1979) turned the cassette into the dominant portable medium of the 1980s; pre-recorded cassettes briefly outsold vinyl LPs. Boomboxes, car stereos, mixtape culture, and home taping — which provoked the industry's "Home Taping Is Killing Music" campaign — defined its cultural era.

Decline and revival

The compact disc and later MP3 players displaced the cassette in the 1990s–2000s, and major-label releases largely ceased. The format also served as cheap data storage for early home computers such as the Commodore PET and VIC-20. Since the 2010s a niche revival — driven by independent labels, nostalgia, and films such as Guardians of the Galaxy — has sustained modest sales growth.
