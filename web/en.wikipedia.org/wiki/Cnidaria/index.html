Cnidaria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Cnidaria
Species: approx. 11,000 described
Classes: Anthozoa, Scyphozoa, Cubozoa, Hydrozoa, Staurozoa, Myxozoa, Polypodiozoa

Cnidaria is a phylum of aquatic animals found mostly in marine environments, comprising more than 11,000 species of jellyfish, corals, sea anemones, and hydroids. Their distinguishing feature is the cnidocyte, a specialized stinging cell containing a coiled, harpoon-like organelle called a nematocyst, used mainly to capture prey and for defence.

Anatomy

Cnidarians are radially or biradially symmetrical and diploblastic: their bodies are built from two embryonic cell layers, the ectoderm and endoderm, separated by a jelly-like layer, the mesoglea. They have a single body opening that serves as both mouth and anus, leading to a gastrovascular cavity; there is no anus, no blood, and no respiratory or excretory system. Their nervous system is a diffuse nerve net without a central brain.

Two basic body forms occur: the sessile polyp (as in sea anemones and corals) and the free-swimming medusa (as in jellyfish). Many species alternate between these forms during their life cycle.

Classification

  • Anthozoa — corals and sea anemones; polyp form only, includes reef-building corals.
  • Scyphozoa — the "true jellyfish".
  • Cubozoa — box jellyfish, some with extremely potent venom.
  • Hydrozoa — hydroids, the Portuguese man o' war, and freshwater Hydra.
  • Staurozoa — stalked jellyfish.
  • Myxozoa — microscopic obligate parasites, once classified as protozoans.

Ecology and importance

Reef-building (scleractinian) corals, which host photosynthetic dinoflagellates called zooxanthellae, create the most biodiverse marine habitats on Earth. Cnidarian stings range from trivial to lethal to humans; the Australian box jellyfish Chironex fleckeri is among the most venomous animals known. Fossil cnidarians appear in rocks of Ediacaran age, more than 550 million years old, making the phylum one of the oldest animal lineages.
