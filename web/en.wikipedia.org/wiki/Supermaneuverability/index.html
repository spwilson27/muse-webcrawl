Supermaneuverability

Supermaneuverability is the capability of fighter aircraft to execute tactical manoeuvres that are not possible with purely aerodynamic techniques. Such manoeuvres require the use of thrust vectoring in combination with a high thrust-to-weight ratio and advanced flight control systems, enabling an aircraft to remain controllable at very high angles of attack and very low — even effectively zero — airspeeds, in the so-called post-stall regime.

Characteristics

A supermaneuverable aircraft can point its nose in a direction very different from its flight path, decelerate rapidly in flight, and perform manoeuvres such as:

  • Pugachev's Cobra — the aircraft pitches up to 90–120 degrees angle of attack, briefly flying "on its tail", before recovering; popularised by the Sukhoi Su-27 family.
  • The Herbst manoeuvre (J-turn) — a rapid post-stall reversal of direction combining roll and yaw.
  • The Kulbit — a tight post-stall loop demonstrated by advanced Russian fighters.

Technology

Key enablers include thrust-vectoring nozzles that deflect engine thrust, relaxed static stability with fly-by-wire controls that keep the aircraft controllable beyond the stall, and aerodynamic features such as leading-edge extensions and canards that manage airflow at high angles of attack. Aircraft noted for supermaneuverability include the Sukhoi Su-35 and Su-57, the Rockwell-MBB X-31 experimental aircraft, and the F-22 Raptor with its two-dimensional thrust vectoring.

Tactical value debated

Advocates argue that post-stall manoeuvring can point weapons at an opponent in a close-range dogfight. Sceptics note that such manoeuvres bleed enormous energy, leaving the aircraft slow and vulnerable, and that modern helmet-mounted sights and high off-boresight missiles reduce the need to point the aircraft's nose at a target. The concept therefore remains influential in airshow performance and design philosophy, but its decisive combat value is contested.
