The history of Florida spans more than 12,000 years, from the arrival of the first Paleo-Indians through Spanish and British colonial rule to statehood in 1845 and its modern growth as a tourist and retirement destination.

Pre-Columbian era

Paleo-Indians entered Florida around the end of the last Ice Age, when sea levels were lower and the peninsula was wider and drier. Major later cultures included the Timucua in the northeast, the Calusa in the southwest, the Apalachee in the panhandle, and the Tequesta around Biscayne Bay.

Spanish Florida

Juan Ponce de León landed near present-day St. Augustine in 1513 and named the land La Florida. After failed colonies, Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded St. Augustine in 1565, the oldest continuously inhabited European settlement in the continental United States. A network of Franciscan missions stretched across the north.

British and second Spanish periods

Britain gained Florida in 1763 after the Seven Years' War, dividing it into East and West Florida. Spain regained it in 1783. Border disputes with the United States led to the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819, transferring Florida to the U.S. in 1821.

Statehood and war

Florida became the 27th state on 3 March 1845. The Seminole Wars (1816–1858) forcibly removed most Seminole people, though a remnant survived in the Everglades. Florida seceded in 1861 and rejoined the Union in 1868.

Modern era

Railroads built by Henry Flagler opened the east coast to tourism in the 1880s–1900s, followed by the 1920s land boom, the Great Depression, and massive post-1945 growth driven by air conditioning, the space program at Cape Canaveral, and Walt Disney World (1971).
