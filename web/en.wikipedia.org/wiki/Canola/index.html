Canola

Canola
KingdomPlantae
OrderBrassicales
FamilyBrassicaceae
GenusBrassica
SpeciesB. napus, B. rapa, B. juncea (cultivars)
Origin of name"Canadian oil, low acid"

Canola is a variety of rapeseed bred in Canada in the 1970s to have very low levels of erucic acid in its oil and low levels of glucosinolates in its meal. The name is a contraction of "Canadian oil, low acid," coined in 1978. Canola oil is one of the most widely consumed vegetable oils in the world, and canola meal is a major livestock feed. The crop is recognisable by the brilliant yellow flowers that cover fields in early summer.

To qualify as canola, seed must produce oil with less than 2 percent erucic acid and meal with fewer than 30 micromoles of glucosinolates per gram. Traditional rapeseed oil, by contrast, could contain 30–50 percent erucic acid, which animal studies had linked to heart lesions, and its bitter, pungent meal was poorly suited to feed.

History

Rapeseed had been cultivated for thousands of years in Asia and Europe, its oil used for lamps and, during the Second World War, as a lubricant for steam and marine engines because it adhered to wet metal. Postwar demand collapsed, and Canadian researchers sought food-grade uses for the crop. Through conventional breeding — selecting naturally low-erucic plants — scientists Keith Downey and Baldur Stefansson at the University of Manitoba and Agriculture Canada developed the first "double-low" (low erucic acid, low glucosinolate) varieties in the early 1970s: Tower (1974) from Brassica napus and Candle (1977) from B. rapa. The new name "canola" was registered in 1978 to distinguish the improved seed from traditional rapeseed.

Production

Canola is grown principally in Canada (the largest exporter, centred on the prairie provinces of Saskatchewan, Alberta and Manitoba), the European Union, Australia, China and India. Canadian production typically runs to roughly 18–20 million tonnes per year. The plants are annuals grown from small black seeds; the oil content of the seed is around 43–44 percent.

Most Canadian and American canola is genetically modified for herbicide tolerance (to glyphosate or glufosinate), which facilitates weed control. Canola is also grown as a winter or spring crop depending on climate, and it is an important rotation crop and a major nectar source for bees.

Uses

  • Canola oil: A light, neutral cooking oil with a high smoke point; low in saturated fat (about 7 percent, the lowest of common cooking oils) and relatively rich in monounsaturated fat and alpha-linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid). Used in home cooking, food manufacturing, margarine and salad dressings.
  • Canola meal: The protein-rich residue after oil extraction, widely used as livestock feed.
  • Biodiesel: Rapeseed/canola oil is a major biodiesel feedstock, especially in Europe.
  • Industrial uses: Lubricants, bioplastics, inks and cosmetics.

Health

Because of its fatty-acid profile, canola oil is generally regarded by nutrition authorities as a heart-healthy oil, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration permits a qualified health claim that canola oil may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease when it replaces saturated fat. Historical concerns about rapeseed oil centred on erucic acid; the low-erucic canola standard was created specifically to address them.

Trade disputes

Canola has figured in international trade disputes, notably China's restrictions on Canadian canola seed imports beginning in 2019, imposed amid the diplomatic row following the arrest of Huawei executive Meng Wanzhou in Vancouver; the restrictions, officially attributed to pest concerns, cost Canadian exporters billions of dollars before being lifted in 2022.
