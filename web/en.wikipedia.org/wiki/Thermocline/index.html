Thermocline

Thermocline
Definition: a layer in a body of water where temperature changes rapidly with depth
Ocean zones: mixed layer (above), thermocline, deep ocean (below)
Typical permanent thermocline depth (tropics): roughly 200–1,000 m
Also called: metalimnion (in lakes)

A thermocline is a distinct layer in a large body of water — an ocean, sea, or lake — in which temperature changes more rapidly with depth than in the layers above or below it. Because temperature strongly affects the density of water, the thermocline coincides largely with the pycnocline, the zone of rapid density change that sharply limits vertical mixing.

In the oceans

The surface ocean is warmed by the sun and stirred by wind and waves into a mixed layer of nearly uniform temperature, often tens of metres thick. Below it, temperature falls steeply through the thermocline until it approaches the near-freezing values of the deep ocean. In low and middle latitudes this "permanent" or main thermocline lies roughly between 200 and 1,000 metres and persists year-round; in the tropics it is strong and sharp, while toward the poles surface waters are already cold, the stratification is weak, and a permanent thermocline may be absent.

Superimposed on the permanent structure are seasonal thermoclines, which form in the upper ocean in spring and summer as surface heating outpaces mixing, and diurnal thermoclines that develop on calm sunny afternoons near the very surface.

In lakes

Stratified lakes in summer show the same three-part structure: a warm surface epilimnion, a cold deep hypolimnion, and between them the metalimnion, whose steepest part is the thermocline. Seasonal cooling in autumn erodes the stratification until the lake "turns over", mixing oxygen and nutrients through the water column.

Significance

The thermocline acts as a barrier: it restricts the exchange of nutrients, oxygen, and heat between the surface and deep water, shaping ocean productivity and the distribution of marine life. It strongly refracts sound, creating acoustic shadow zones and the deep sound (SOFAR) channel — effects of great importance to sonar and submarine operations. In the tropical Pacific, changes in the depth and tilt of the thermocline are central to the El Niño–Southern Oscillation: during El Niño the eastern Pacific thermocline deepens, suppressing the upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water.
