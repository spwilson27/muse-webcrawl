Acetate

An acetate is a salt formed by the combination of acetic acid with a base (e.g. alkaline, earthy, metallic, nonmetallic or radical base). "Acetate" also describes the conjugate base or ion (specifically, the negatively charged ion called an anion) typically found in aqueous solution and written with the chemical formula C2H3O2−.

Structure

The acetate anion, [CH3–COO]−, consists of a methyl group bonded to a carboxylate group. The negative charge is delocalised over the two oxygen atoms by resonance, making the two carbon–oxygen bonds equivalent. Acetate is the conjugate base of acetic acid (pKa ≈ 4.76).

Uses

Acetate salts and esters are produced on a vast scale. Sodium acetate is used in the textile industry and as a food additive; calcium acetate is used to treat hyperphosphatemia; and cellulose acetate is a major material for photographic film, textiles and cigarette filters. The term "acetate" is also used in the recording industry for lacquer-coated aluminium discs used in disc recording.

Biochemistry

In metabolism, acetate is activated to acetyl-CoA, a central intermediate in the synthesis and oxidation of carbohydrates, fats and amino acids. Acetate is also the product of the fermentation carried out by acetogenic bacteria and an important short-chain fatty acid in the gut.
