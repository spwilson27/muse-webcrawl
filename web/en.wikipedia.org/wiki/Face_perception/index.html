Face perception

Face perception is an individual's understanding and interpretation of the face. It is a rich cognitive and neural process by which humans rapidly extract identity, emotion, gaze direction, age, sex, and attractiveness from faces, and it plays a central role in social communication.

Neural basis

A distributed neural system processes faces, with core regions in the occipital face area (OFA), the fusiform face area (FFA) in the fusiform gyrus — identified prominently by Nancy Kanwisher — and the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), which handles changeable aspects such as gaze and expression. The amygdala contributes to emotional salience, especially fear.

Development

Newborns preferentially track face-like configurations within hours of birth, suggesting an innate bias. Perceptual narrowing tunes infants to the face categories they see most, underpinning the other-race effect in recognition memory.

Effects and disorders

Faces are processed holistically: inversion disrupts recognition (the face inversion effect), and composite faces fuse into a single percept. Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, can follow brain injury or occur developmentally, selectively impairing identity recognition. The Thatcher effect and pareidolia further reveal the system's specialisation.
