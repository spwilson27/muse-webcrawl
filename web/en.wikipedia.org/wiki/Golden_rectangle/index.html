A golden rectangle is a rectangle whose side lengths are in the golden ratio, 1 : (1 + √5)/2, approximately 1 : 1.618, denoted by the Greek letter φ (phi). The rectangle's defining property is self-similarity: if a square is cut from one end of a golden rectangle, the rectangle that remains is itself a golden rectangle, smaller by a factor of φ, and the process can be repeated indefinitely.

Construction

A golden rectangle can be constructed with compass and straightedge by drawing a square, finding the midpoint of one side, and swinging an arc from that midpoint through an opposite corner to extend the base to the required length. Repeatedly removing squares generates a sequence of "whirling squares", and joining the corners of these squares approximates a logarithmic spiral known as the golden spiral.

Applications

Since the nineteenth century, writers on aesthetics have claimed that the golden rectangle is the most pleasing rectangle to the eye, and have discerned its proportions in the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid, Renaissance painting and the work of Le Corbusier, who built his Modulor system on it. Modern psychological studies, beginning with Gustav Fechner's experiments, have found at most a weak and inconsistent preference for the ratio, and many historical claims are now considered unfounded. The proportions nonetheless remain popular in art, architecture and design, and the ratio appears naturally in the geometry of the pentagon and in Fibonacci-related patterns.
