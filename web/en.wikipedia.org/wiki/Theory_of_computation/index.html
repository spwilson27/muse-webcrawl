Theory of computation

Theory of computation
FieldComputer science, mathematics
SubfieldsAutomata theory; computability theory; computational complexity theory
Key modelsTuring machine, finite automaton, pushdown automaton, lambda calculus
Key figuresAlan Turing, Alonzo Church, Kurt Gödel, Stephen Cook

The theory of computation is the branch of computer science and mathematics that deals with how problems can be solved on a model of computation, using an algorithm. It asks fundamental questions: What can be computed at all? How efficiently can it be computed? The field is traditionally divided into three major branches: automata theory, computability theory, and computational complexity theory.

Automata theory

Automata theory studies abstract machines and the classes of languages they can recognize. The Chomsky hierarchy organizes formal grammars and machines: finite automata recognize regular languages; pushdown automata recognize context-free languages; linear-bounded automata recognize context-sensitive languages; and Turing machines recognize recursively enumerable languages. These models underlie compilers, text processing and the design of digital circuits.

Computability theory

Computability theory asks which problems admit an algorithmic solution at all. In the 1930s, work by Kurt Gödel, Alonzo Church and Alan Turing showed that some well-defined problems are undecidable — no algorithm can solve them in general. Turing's halting problem is the canonical example: there is no program that can determine, for every program and input, whether the program will halt. The Church–Turing thesis identifies the informal notion of effective calculability with the functions computable by a Turing machine.

Computational complexity theory

Complexity theory classifies decidable problems by the resources — chiefly time and space — required to solve them. Central complexity classes include P (problems solvable in polynomial time), NP (problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time) and NP-complete problems, the hardest problems in NP. Whether P equals NP is one of the great open problems of mathematics and computer science, with a one-million-dollar Millennium Prize attached to its solution.

History

The theory originated in mathematical logic with David Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem (decision problem), posed in 1928. Negative solutions by Church and Turing in 1936 founded the field; the subsequent development of electronic computers gave the abstract theory direct practical significance, and complexity theory took shape in the 1960s and 1970s with the work of Hartmanis, Stearns, Cook and Karp.
