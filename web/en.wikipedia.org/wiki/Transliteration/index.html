Transliteration

Transliteration
TypeText conversion between scripts
Contrasts withTranscription (sound-based), translation (meaning-based)
Examples of systemsISO 9 (Cyrillic), IAST (Sanskrit), Pinyin (Chinese, largely transcription)

Transliteration is a type of conversion of a text from one script to another that involves swapping letters (thus trans- + liter-) in predictable ways, such as Greek ⟨α⟩ → ⟨a⟩, Cyrillic ⟨д⟩ → ⟨d⟩, Armenian ⟨ն⟩ → ⟨n⟩, or Latin ⟨æ⟩ → ⟨ae⟩. Ideally a transliteration is reversible, so that the original spelling can be reconstructed from the converted text.

Transliteration versus transcription

Transliteration is not concerned with representing the sounds of the original, only its written characters; transcription, by contrast, maps sounds into the target script. For example, the Russian surname Горбачёв is transliterated Gorbačëv under ISO 9 but transcribed (in English practice) as Gorbachev. Chinese Pinyin is primarily a transcription of Mandarin pronunciation in Latin letters, not a character-by-character transliteration.

Systems and uses

Standardised systems exist for library cataloguing (the ALA-LC romanisation tables), for scholarship (IAST for Indic scripts, with diacritics such as ṇ and ā), and for passports and place names, where simplified schemes without diacritics are common. Transliteration underpins multilingual computing, search and machine translation pipelines, and informal practices such as "Greeklish" or Arabizi render languages in Latin characters for convenience.
