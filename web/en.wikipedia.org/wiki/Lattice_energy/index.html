Lattice energy

Lattice energy is a measure of the strength of the bonds in an ionic compound. It is usually defined as the energy released when one mole of an ionic crystal forms from its gaseous ions (making the value negative, exothermic), or equivalently the energy required to separate one mole of the solid into gaseous ions (positive by that convention).

Factors

Lattice energy increases with the charges of the ions and decreases with their radii, as modelled by the Born–Landé equation and the Kapustinskii equation. For example, magnesium oxide, with doubly charged small ions, has a far larger lattice energy (about −3795 kJ/mol) than sodium chloride (about −786 kJ/mol).

Born–Haber cycle

Because lattice energies cannot be measured directly, they are calculated from experimental data using the Born–Haber cycle, an application of Hess's law combining enthalpies of formation, atomisation, ionisation, electron affinity and dissociation. Agreement between cycle values and theoretical estimates indicates the degree of ionic character; discrepancies signal covalent contributions.

Significance

Large lattice energies explain the high melting points and low solubility of many ionic solids, and drive reactions such as the formation of stable oxides and fluorides.
