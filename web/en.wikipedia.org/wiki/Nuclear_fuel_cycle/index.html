The nuclear fuel cycle, also called the nuclear fuel chain, is the progression of nuclear fuel through a series of differing stages. It consists of steps in the front end (preparing the fuel), steps in the service period (using the fuel in a reactor), and steps in the back end (managing the spent fuel, with or without reprocessing, through to disposal). If spent fuel is not reprocessed, the cycle is called an open or once-through fuel cycle; if it is reprocessed and partly recycled, it is a closed fuel cycle.

Front end

  1. Mining and milling: uranium ore is extracted, mostly from open-pit or underground mines or by in-situ leaching, and milled into uranium oxide concentrate (U3O8), known as yellowcake.
  2. Conversion: the concentrate is purified and converted to uranium hexafluoride (UF6) for enrichment.
  3. Enrichment: the proportion of the fissile isotope uranium-235 is raised from its natural 0.7% to about 3–5% for light-water reactors, today almost entirely by gas centrifuge. The depleted remainder (tails) is stored as depleted uranium.
  4. Fuel fabrication: enriched UF6 is converted to uranium dioxide powder, pressed into pellets, sintered, and loaded into fuel rods assembled into fuel elements. Mixed oxide (MOX) fuel combines uranium with plutonium recovered by reprocessing.

Service period

In the reactor, uranium-235 fissions to release heat for electricity generation, while uranium-238 partly converts into plutonium-239, which also contributes energy. Fuel typically stays in the core for three to six years, reaching burnups around 50 GWd/t, before fission-product poisons and material degradation require its replacement; about a quarter to a third of the core is replaced at each refuelling outage.

Back end

Freshly discharged spent fuel is hot and highly radioactive, so it is cooled for years in spent fuel pools and then commonly in dry casks. In a closed cycle, reprocessing (such as the PUREX process) separates uranium and plutonium for reuse, leaving a smaller volume of high-level waste that is vitrified in glass. The end point of either route is a deep geological repository for the remaining long-lived waste; Finland's Onkalo facility is the first such repository to enter operation.
