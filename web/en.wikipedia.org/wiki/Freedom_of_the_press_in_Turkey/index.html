Freedom of the press in Turkey

The concept of freedom of the press in Turkey is enshrined in the Constitution of Turkey, which states that the press is free and shall not be censored. In practice, however, Turkey has repeatedly been criticised by international organisations for restrictions on media freedom, the prosecution and imprisonment of journalists, and the concentration of media ownership in conglomerates with close ties to the government. Reporters Without Borders has ranked Turkey near the bottom of its World Press Freedom Index in recent years.

History

Press freedom in the Ottoman Empire and the early Republic was limited by censorship laws and periodic closures of newspapers. The 1961 constitution strengthened formal guarantees, but military interventions in 1971 and 1980 brought renewed restrictions. In the 1990s, journalists covering the conflict with the PKK faced violence and prosecution. European Union accession reforms in the early 2000s brought some liberalisation, including the abolition of certain press offences.

Developments since 2013

The Gezi Park protests of 2013 highlighted self-censorship in mainstream media. Following the failed coup attempt of July 2016, a state of emergency decree closed more than 150 media outlets and led to the arrest of many journalists; Turkey became, for a time, the world's largest jailer of journalists according to the Committee to Protect Journalists. In 2018, most of the Doğan Media Group, the country's largest media conglomerate, was sold to the pro-government Demirören Group, bringing an estimated large majority of media reach under owners sympathetic to the government.

Legal framework

Journalists may be prosecuted under the Anti-Terror Law, the Turkish Penal Code provisions on insulting the President and state institutions, and laws governing the internet and broadcasting. The Radio and Television Supreme Council (RTÜK) regulates broadcasters and has fined critical channels. A 2022 law criminalising the dissemination of "false information" drew criticism from press freedom advocates. The Constitutional Court and the European Court of Human Rights have issued rulings finding violations of freedom of expression in a number of Turkish cases.
