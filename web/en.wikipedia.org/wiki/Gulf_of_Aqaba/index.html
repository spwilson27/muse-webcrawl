The Gulf of Aqaba (Arabic: خليج العقبة), also known as the Gulf of Eilat in Israel (Hebrew: מפרץ אילת), is a large gulf at the northern tip of the Red Sea, east of the Sinai Peninsula and west of the Arabian Peninsula. Its coastline is divided among four countries: Egypt, Israel, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia. The gulf is named after the Jordanian city of Aqaba, its largest settlement.

Geography

The gulf is one of two gulfs created by the Sinai Peninsula's split of the northern Red Sea, the other being the Gulf of Suez to the west. It reaches a maximum depth of 1,850 metres in its central area, making it far deeper than the broader Red Sea. The gulf is 160 kilometres long and up to 24 kilometres wide. It is connected to the Red Sea through the Straits of Tiran, which contain several islands, including Tiran and Sanafir.

Geology

The gulf forms part of the Dead Sea Transform fault system, the boundary between the Arabian Plate and the Sinai subplate. It contains three deep pull-apart basins: the Elat Deep, the Aragonese Deep, and the Dakar Deep. Earthquakes in the region, such as the 1995 Gulf of Aqaba earthquake, result from movement along this fault.

Tourism and ecology

The gulf's clear waters and coral reefs support rich marine life, making the resort cities of Aqaba (Jordan), Eilat (Israel), Taba (Egypt), and Haql (Saudi Arabia) popular destinations for diving and snorkeling. The gulf hosts more than 500 species of bivalves, numerous fish, and extensive fringing coral reefs.
