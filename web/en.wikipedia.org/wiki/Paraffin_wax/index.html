Paraffin wax (or petroleum wax) is a soft, colourless solid derived from petroleum, coal or oil shale, consisting of a mixture of hydrocarbon molecules containing between twenty and forty carbon atoms. It is solid at room temperature and begins to melt above approximately 37 °C (99 °F). Common applications include lubrication, electrical insulation and candles. The name should not be confused with paraffin in British usage, meaning the liquid fuel kerosene.

History

Paraffin wax was first created commercially in 1867, a decade after the first oil wells, following the identification of the substance in petroleum deposits by Carl Reichenbach around 1830. It quickly displaced tallow and stearin in candlemaking because it burned cleanly, was cheap and had no odour, and by the 20th century petroleum-derived paraffin had become the standard material for candles, wax paper and coatings.

Production

Paraffin wax is obtained from the dewaxing of lubricating-oil fractions during petroleum refining: the waxy distillate is chilled so the wax crystallises, then filtered and purified by sweating, solvent extraction or hydrotreating to give grades from crude "scale wax" to fully refined, food-grade material. Synthetic paraffin is also produced by Fischer–Tropsch conversion of coal, natural gas or biomass.

Properties and uses

Paraffin is chemically inert, an excellent electrical insulator, and a good thermal store because of its relatively high latent heat of fusion. Its uses include candle manufacture; coatings for waxed paper, cheese and food packaging; cosmetics and pharmaceutical ointments; hot-wax therapy and histology embedding in laboratories; satin finishes and polishes; crayons; and investigational use as a phase-change material for heat storage. Microcrystalline waxes, with finer crystals and higher melting ranges, are related petroleum waxes used where flexibility and adhesion matter.
