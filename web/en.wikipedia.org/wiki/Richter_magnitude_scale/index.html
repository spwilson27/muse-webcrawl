Richter magnitude scale

The Richter magnitude scale — also Richter's magnitude scale or Richter's scale — is a measure of the strength of earthquakes, developed by Charles F. Richter in collaboration with Beno Gutenberg, and presented in Richter's landmark 1935 paper. It assigns a magnitude number based on the logarithm of the amplitude of waves recorded by seismographs, adjusted for the distance between the seismograph and the earthquake's epicentre.

Definition

Richter defined the local magnitude ML as the base-10 logarithm of the maximum trace amplitude (in micrometres) recorded on a standard Wood–Anderson torsion seismometer 100 km from the epicentre. Because the scale is logarithmic, each whole-number step corresponds to a tenfold increase in measured amplitude and roughly 31.6 times more energy released.

Use and limitations

The original scale was designed for shallow earthquakes in Southern California and "saturates" for very large earthquakes, underestimating their size. Seismologists therefore now generally use the moment magnitude scale (Mw) for major events, although media reports often still describe magnitudes as "on the Richter scale."

Typical effects

Earthquakes below magnitude 2.0 are microearthquakes, generally not felt; those of 4–5 cause moderate damage near the epicentre; magnitude 7 events are major and can devastate cities; and the largest recorded earthquake, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, measured about 9.5.
