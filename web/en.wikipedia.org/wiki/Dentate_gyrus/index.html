The dentate gyrus (DG) is part of the hippocampal formation in the temporal lobe of the brain. It is the first region where all kinds of sensory information merge to form a unique representation — the entry point of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit. The dentate gyrus receives its main input from the entorhinal cortex via the perforant path and sends output through the mossy fibres of its granule cells to the CA3 region of the hippocampus proper.

Structure

The dentate gyrus consists of three layers: the molecular layer, the granule cell layer, and the polymorphic layer (hilus). Its densely packed granule cells form a distinctive V- or U-shaped band. Interneurons, including basket cells and hilar mossy cells, modulate granule cell activity.

Function

The dentate gyrus is thought to contribute to pattern separation — the transformation of similar inputs into distinct, non-overlapping outputs — which supports the encoding of new episodic memories. It is also one of only a few brain regions where neurogenesis continues into adulthood in many mammals: new granule cells are born in the subgranular zone throughout life, a process implicated in learning, memory and mood regulation.

Clinical significance

Changes in dentate gyrus function and adult neurogenesis have been associated with epilepsy (including mossy fibre sprouting in temporal lobe epilepsy), depression, and age-related memory decline.
