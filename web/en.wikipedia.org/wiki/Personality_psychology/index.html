Personality psychology

Personality psychology is the branch of psychology that studies personality — the characteristic patterns of thought, feeling and behaviour that make a person unique — and individual differences between people. It seeks to describe personality structure, explain how personality develops, and predict behaviour and life outcomes.

Major approaches

Trait theory treats personality as a set of enduring dispositions. From Allport's and Cattell's work, factor-analytic research converged on the Big Five (Five-Factor Model): openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism, now the dominant descriptive framework, with the six-factor HEXACO model as a leading alternative. Psychoanalytic approaches (Freud, Jung) emphasised unconscious conflict and development; humanistic theories (Rogers, Maslow) stressed self-concept and growth; social-cognitive theories (Bandura, Mischel) emphasised reciprocal interaction between person and situation, sparking the person–situation debate over how consistently traits predict behaviour.

Assessment and findings

Personality is measured chiefly by self-report inventories (such as the NEO-PI-R and the BFI), informant reports and behavioural observation; type-based instruments like the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator are commercially popular but psychometrically criticised. Twin studies estimate that roughly 40–50% of trait variance is heritable. Personality shows moderate rank-order stability that increases with age, mean-level maturation towards greater conscientiousness and emotional stability in adulthood, and meaningful prediction of outcomes including job performance, relationship stability, health and longevity.

Disorders

Extreme or inflexible personality patterns are classified as personality disorders in the DSM-5 and ICD-11, with current classifications increasingly dimensional, aligning disordered personality with the same trait space studied by mainstream personality science.
