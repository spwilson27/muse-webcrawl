Deuterium fusion

Deuterium fusion is a nuclear fusion reaction in which two deuterium nuclei (each containing one proton and one neutron) combine. It occurs naturally in the cores of brown dwarfs and is a fundamental reaction studied in laboratory fusion research. Objects above roughly 13 Jupiter masses can sustain deuterium fusion, a threshold often used to distinguish brown dwarfs from giant planets.

Reaction branches

The D–D reaction proceeds through two nearly equally probable branches:

  • D + D → 3He + n + 3.27 MeV
  • D + D → T + p + 4.03 MeV

The tritium and helium-3 produced can undergo further, more energetic reactions, notably D–T fusion, the easiest fusion reaction to ignite and the focus of most experimental reactors such as ITER.

In stars and brown dwarfs

Deuterium burning begins at core temperatures near one million kelvin. In young stars it temporarily slows gravitational contraction; in brown dwarfs it provides a significant energy source for tens to hundreds of millions of years before the object cools.

Terrestrial research

Deuterium–deuterium reactions were among the first fusion reactions produced in the laboratory. "Aneutronic" schemes seek to burn the helium-3 product in turn, reducing neutron damage to reactor structures.
