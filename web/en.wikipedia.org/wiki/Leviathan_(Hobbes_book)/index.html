Leviathan (Hobbes book)

AuthorThomas Hobbes
Published1651
Full titleLeviathan, or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil
SubjectPolitical philosophy

Leviathan, or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil, commonly called Leviathan, is a book written by the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes and published in 1651. It is one of the most influential works of political philosophy in the Western tradition, arguing for a social contract and rule by an absolute sovereign.

Content

The book is divided into four parts: "Of Man", "Of Common-Wealth", "Of a Christian Common-Wealth", and "Of the Kingdome of Darknesse". Hobbes describes the natural condition of mankind — the "state of nature" — as a war of all against all in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". To escape it, individuals covenant to surrender their rights to a sovereign power, the Leviathan, whose authority is undivided and absolute, though justified by the protection it provides.

The frontispiece

The celebrated engraved title page, attributed to Abraham Bosse, depicts a giant crowned sovereign whose body is composed of hundreds of individual subjects, holding a sword and a crozier over a landscape — a visual summary of the commonwealth as an artificial person made of its citizens.

Influence

Written during the English Civil War, Leviathan founded much of modern contract theory and influenced Locke, Rousseau, and later theorists, even those who rejected its absolutist conclusions. Hobbes's materialist psychology and his analysis of power remain central topics in political science and philosophy.
