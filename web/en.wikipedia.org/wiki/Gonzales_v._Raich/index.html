Gonzales v. Raich

Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1 (2005), was a decision by the United States Supreme Court ruling that under the Commerce Clause of the U.S. Constitution, Congress may criminalise the production and use of homegrown cannabis even where states approve its use for medicinal purposes.

Facts

California voters had approved Proposition 215 (the Compassionate Use Act of 1996), permitting medical marijuana. Respondents Angel Raich and Diane Monson used locally grown cannabis on physicians' advice; Monson's plants were seized by federal agents enforcing the federal Controlled Substances Act. They sued, arguing Congress lacked power under the Commerce Clause to regulate purely intrastate, noncommercial cultivation.

Holding

By 6–3, the Court upheld federal authority. Justice Stevens's majority opinion reasoned, following Wickard v. Filburn, that Congress could regulate a class of activities — here, marijuana production and consumption — that in the aggregate substantially affects interstate commerce, and that exempting intrastate medical use would undercut the federal regulatory scheme. Justice Scalia concurred on Necessary and Proper Clause grounds. Justice O'Connor dissented, warning against eroding state experimentation and federalism.

Significance

The decision is a landmark of modern Commerce Clause doctrine, demonstrating the breadth of federal regulatory power even after the limiting decisions in United States v. Lopez and United States v. Morrison. It did not invalidate state medical-marijuana laws, and numerous states have since legalised medical and recreational cannabis despite continued federal prohibition.
