Popular Front

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A popular front is a "broad coalition" of different political groupings, usually leftists and centrists, mobilized to oppose fascism or another common adversary. The term originates in the strategy adopted by the Communist International (Comintern) at its Seventh World Congress in 1935, under Georgi Dimitrov, which reversed the earlier "class against class" line and directed communist parties to ally with socialists, liberals, and other anti-fascist forces. The most famous popular fronts governed France and Spain in 1936.

France

The French Popular Front (Front populaire), uniting Léon Blum's SFIO socialists, the Radical Party, and the Communist Party (which supported but did not join the cabinet), won the elections of May 1936 after the shock of the far-right riots of 6 February 1934. Blum's government, swept in amid a wave of sit-down strikes, enacted the Matignon Agreements (June 1936): the 40-hour week, paid holidays (two weeks), collective bargaining rights, and wage increases, as well as nationalizing the armaments industry and reforming the Bank of France. Undermined by capital flight, Senate opposition, the devaluation of the franc, and tensions over non-intervention in Spain, Blum resigned in June 1937; the coalition lingered under Chautemps and Daladier until 1938–40.

Spain

The Spanish Popular Front (Frente Popular), an electoral alliance of republicans, socialists, communists, and regional parties, narrowly won the elections of February 1936, restoring the reformist programme of the Republic and releasing political prisoners. Polarization deepened, and in July 1936 a military uprising plunged Spain into civil war. The Popular Front governments (including those of Largo Caballero and Juan Negrín) led the Republican war effort until defeat in 1939.

Elsewhere and later uses

Popular front strategies and governments appeared in Chile (1938–1941, under Pedro Aguirre Cerda), and influenced labour and cultural politics in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere; in the US the "Popular Front" period deeply shaped unions, Hollywood, and the broader left during the late 1930s and the Second World War. The strategy effectively ended with the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939, though anti-fascist "national front" coalitions revived during the war and in post-1945 Eastern Europe, where communist-led "popular front" governments served as transitional regimes before full one-party rule. The generic phrase has since been reused for many broad alliances, from Chile's Popular Unity (1970) onward.
