Hibernation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hibernation is a state of minimal activity and metabolic depression that some animals enter to survive winter conditions of cold and scarce food. True hibernators — such as ground squirrels, marmots, hedgehogs and many bats — let their body temperature fall close to ambient and their heart rate drop to a few beats per minute, living on stored fat for months.

Physiology

Before winter, hibernators fatten intensely; in torpor, metabolism may fall to a few percent of normal. Periodic arousals — brief, costly returns to normal temperature — punctuate the season for reasons still studied (immune function and sleep needs are candidates). Bears hibernate more shallowly, with modest temperature drops, giving birth and nursing cubs during the denning period.

Related states and study

Estivation is the analogous dormancy for heat and drought; daily torpor in hummingbirds and small mammals is a short version. Studying hibernation informs medicine (organ preservation, induced hypothermia, muscle and bone loss in immobility) and even plans for long space voyages. Climate change is altering hibernation timing, with risks when animals emerge to find food not yet available.
