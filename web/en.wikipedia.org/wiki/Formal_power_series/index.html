Formal power series

In mathematics, a formal power series is an infinite sum considered purely as an algebraic object, without any requirement that the series converge. It is a generalisation of a polynomial, in which the number of terms is allowed to be infinite, and is an essential tool in algebra, combinatorics and number theory.

Definition

A formal power series in one variable X over a ring R is an expression of the form

a0 + a1X + a2X2 + ⋯

where each coefficient an belongs to R. The set of all such series forms a ring, denoted R[[X]], under term-by-term addition and a multiplication defined by the Cauchy product: the coefficient of Xn in a product is the finite sum ∑k=0..n akbn-k. Because every coefficient of a product involves only finitely many operations, no question of convergence ever arises.

Properties

A formal power series is invertible in R[[X]] if and only if its constant term is a unit of R. Familiar identities such as the expansion of 1/(1 − X) = 1 + X + X2 + ⋯ hold formally. Composition of series is possible when the inner series has zero constant term. Formal differentiation and integration are defined term by term, giving rise to the theory of generating functions, which encode sequences through their coefficients.

Applications

Formal power series are used to solve recurrence relations, to study group rings and complete local rings in commutative algebra, and in the theory of integer partitions, where Euler's generating function ∏(1 − xk)−1 counts partitions.
