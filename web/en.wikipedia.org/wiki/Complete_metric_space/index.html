Complete metric space

In mathematical analysis, a metric space M is called complete if every Cauchy sequence of points in M has a limit that is also in M. Intuitively, a complete space has no "missing" points: there are no gaps that a convergent process can approach without reaching.

Examples

The real numbers ℝ with the usual metric are complete — this is essentially the completeness axiom of the real number system. The rational numbers ℚ are not complete: the sequence 3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, … is Cauchy but converges to π, which is not rational. Every finite-dimensional real or complex normed vector space is complete; such complete normed spaces are called Banach spaces. Every compact metric space is complete.

Completion

Every metric space has a (essentially unique) completion: a complete metric space containing it as a dense subspace. The construction of ℝ from ℚ by Cauchy sequences is the classic example. Completion of normed spaces and the extension of uniformly continuous functions rely on this construction.

Related results

The Baire category theorem states that a complete metric space is a Baire space: it cannot be written as a countable union of nowhere dense sets. The Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees that every contraction mapping on a non-empty complete metric space has a unique fixed point, with applications to differential equations and fractals.
