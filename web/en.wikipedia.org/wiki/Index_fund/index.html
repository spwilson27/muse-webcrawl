Index fund

An index fund (also index tracker) is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) designed to follow certain preset rules so that it can replicate the performance ("track") of a specified basket of underlying investments such as a stock market index. The main advantage of index funds for investors is that they require very little management: rather than researching individual securities, the fund manager simply maintains the portfolio in proportion to the index.

History

The first index fund available to individual investors was created in 1976 by John C. Bogle, founder of The Vanguard Group. Originally called the First Index Investment Trust, it tracked the S&P 500 and was initially derided on Wall Street as "Bogle's folly". It grew into the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, one of the largest mutual funds in the world, and index funds have since grown to hold a substantial share of all equity fund assets.

Advantages

Index funds typically charge far lower fees than actively managed funds, because they trade infrequently and require no analyst team; expense ratios for broad-market index funds can be as low as a few hundredths of a percent per year. They are also tax-efficient in many jurisdictions because of low portfolio turnover, and they offer instant diversification across hundreds or thousands of securities.

Criticism

Critics note that index funds necessarily earn the market return minus fees and can never outperform; that cap-weighted indices concentrate holdings in the largest companies; and that the growth of passive investing concentrates corporate voting power in a few large asset managers. Defenders respond that the arithmetic of costs means the average actively managed fund must underperform the index after fees over long periods.
