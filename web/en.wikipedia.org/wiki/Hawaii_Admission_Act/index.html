Hawaii Admission Act

The Hawaii Admission Act, formally An Act to Provide for the Admission of the State of Hawaii into the Union (Public Law 86-3, 73 Stat. 4), was enacted by the United States Congress and signed into law by President Dwight D. Eisenhower on March 18, 1959. The act admitted the Territory of Hawaii as the fiftieth U.S. state.

Background

Following the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893 and annexation by the United States in 1898 under the Newlands Resolution, Hawaii was organized as a U.S. territory in 1900. Statehood was debated for decades; support grew after World War II, though concerns about the islands' ethnic composition, distance from the mainland, and Cold War politics slowed progress.

Provisions and referendum

The act provided for a referendum in which Hawaiian voters could accept or reject statehood. In the plebiscite of June 27, 1959, voters approved admission by a margin of roughly 17 to 1. Hawaii officially became a state on August 21, 1959.

Legacy

The Admission Act remains controversial among Native Hawaiian sovereignty advocates, who note that the referendum offered no option of independence or free association, and that the 1959 vote has been cited in debates over the legal status of Hawaii's ceded lands.
