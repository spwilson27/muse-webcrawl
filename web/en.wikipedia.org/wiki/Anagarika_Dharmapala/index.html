Anagarika Dharmapala (17 September 1864 – 29 April 1933) was a Sri Lankan Buddhist revivalist and writer. Born Don David Hewavitharana into a wealthy Colombo family of furniture merchants, he became the leading figure in the revival of Buddhism in Ceylon (now Sri Lanka) and one of the first to carry the message of Theravada Buddhism to the Western world. He adopted the name Anagarika Dharmapala — "homeless protector of the Dharma" — and lived a celibate, ascetic life in yellow robes without taking full monastic ordination for most of his career.

Early life and influences

Hewavitharana was educated at Christian missionary schools, an experience that shaped his lifelong critique of colonial and missionary influence in Ceylon. In 1880 he met Colonel Henry Steel Olcott and Madame Helena Blavatsky of the Theosophical Society, who had arrived to support the Buddhist cause; the young Hewavitharana worked with Olcott on Buddhist education and the Buddhist flag. A turning point came in 1891, when he visited Bodh Gaya in India and was distressed to find the Mahabodhi Temple — the site of the Buddha's enlightenment — under the control of a Shaivite mahant, with Buddhist worship neglected.

Maha Bodhi Society and the Bodh Gaya campaign

In 1891 Dharmapala founded the Maha Bodhi Society in Colombo (later headquartered in Calcutta) to campaign for the restoration of Bodh Gaya to Buddhist control and to revive Buddhism in the land of its birth. The campaign, pursued through journals, lectures, and litigation for decades, became the central cause of his life and helped draw international attention to Buddhist holy places in India, including Sarnath and Kusinara.

Work in the West

Dharmapala represented Theravada Buddhism at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893, where his speeches were warmly received. He lectured widely in the United States, Britain, and elsewhere, presented Buddhism as a rational, science-compatible philosophy, and worked with benefactors such as Mary Foster of Honolulu. He also founded schools, hospitals, and industrial training in Ceylon, and championed Sinhala Buddhist nationalism and temperance.

Death and legacy

In 1933, shortly before his death at Sarnath, Dharmapala took full ordination as a bhikkhu under the name Devamitta Dharmapala. He died on 29 April 1933. He is remembered in Sri Lanka as a national hero of the Buddhist revival and a forerunner of the island's independence movement, while scholars note the ambivalent legacy of his communal rhetoric alongside his genuine achievements in education and heritage restoration.
