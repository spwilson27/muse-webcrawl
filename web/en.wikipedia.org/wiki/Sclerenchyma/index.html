Sclerenchyma

In plants, sclerenchyma is one of the three types of ground, or fundamental, tissue, the others being parenchyma and collenchyma. It is a supporting tissue made of cells with thick, heavily lignified secondary walls; at maturity the cells are usually dead, having lost their protoplasts. Its function is mechanical strength and support in parts of the plant that have stopped elongating.

Types

Two main forms are recognised. Fibres are long, slender cells, often in bundles, valuable commercially as bast fibres such as flax, hemp and jute. Sclereids, or stone cells, are shorter, roughly isodiametric cells with extremely thick, pitted walls; they occur in seed coats and nut shells and give pears their gritty texture.

Properties

The secondary walls are impregnated with lignin, making the tissue hard and inextensible. Sclerenchyma occurs in stems, leaf veins, and the coverings of seeds and fruits, and forms much of the material of wood together with xylem elements.
