Bertrand paradox (probability)

The Bertrand paradox is a problem in the classical interpretation of probability theory, posed by Joseph Bertrand in his 1889 work Calcul des probabilités. It shows that the principle of indifference can produce contradictory results when it is not clear how the random mechanism generating the outcome is to be specified.

The problem

Consider an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle. A chord of the circle is chosen at random. What is the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle? Bertrand gave three plausible methods of choosing the chord, yielding the answers 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively: choosing two random endpoints on the circumference; choosing a random radius and a point on it; or choosing a random midpoint within the circle.

Significance

The paradox demonstrates that a probability is not well defined until the experimental procedure for producing the random variable is precisely specified. It became a standard motivation for more rigorous foundations of probability, and later authors, notably E. T. Jaynes, argued for a unique resolution based on symmetry and maximum entropy principles.
