Cataract surgery, also called lens replacement surgery, is the removal of the natural lens of the eye (also called the crystalline lens) that has developed a cataract, an opacification of the lens, and its replacement with an artificial intraocular lens (IOL). It is among the most commonly performed and most successful surgical procedures in the world.

Techniques

In phacoemulsification, the modern standard technique introduced by Charles Kelman in 1967, an ultrasonic probe inserted through a corneal incision of about 2–3 mm emulsifies the clouded lens, which is aspirated; a foldable IOL is then inserted into the remaining capsular bag. In extracapsular cataract extraction, the lens nucleus is removed in one piece through a larger incision, and in the intracapsular method, now rare, the entire lens and capsule are removed. Manual small-incision cataract surgery is widely used in low-resource settings because it achieves comparable results at lower cost. Anaesthesia is usually local — topical or sub-Tenon — and the operation typically takes under 30 minutes as day surgery.

Intraocular lenses

IOLs may be monofocal (fixed for one distance), toric (correcting astigmatism), or multifocal and accommodating designs intended to reduce dependence on spectacles. The first IOL was implanted by Sir Harold Ridley in London in 1949, inspired by the observation that acrylic fragments from wartime aircraft canopies were tolerated inside pilots' eyes.

Outcomes and complications

More than 90% of operations result in improved vision. Complications are uncommon but include posterior capsule opacification (treated with YAG laser capsulotomy), infection (endophthalmitis), retinal detachment, and cystoid macular oedema. Untreated cataracts remain the leading cause of blindness worldwide, and expanding access to surgery is a major goal of global eye-health programmes.
