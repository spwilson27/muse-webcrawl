James Hutton (3 June 1726 – 26 March 1797) was a Scottish geologist, agriculturalist, chemical manufacturer, naturalist, and physician. Often referred to as the "Father of Modern Geology," he played a key role in establishing geology as a modern science through his theory of uniformitarianism and his recognition of the immense age of the Earth.

Life

Hutton was born in Edinburgh, studied at the University of Edinburgh and took a medical degree at Leiden in 1749. After working in business and farming in Berwickshire — where he developed an interest in soils, drainage, and the Earth's surface — he returned to Edinburgh in 1768 and became a central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment, moving in circles that included Adam Smith, Joseph Black, and James Watt.

Theory of the Earth

In 1785 Hutton presented his Theory of the Earth to the Royal Society of Edinburgh (published in 1788, and expanded into a two-volume book in 1795). Observing features such as the angular unconformity at Siccar Point — where near-vertical Silurian greywacke is overlain by gently dipping Devonian sandstone — he argued that rock strata were deposited, uplifted, and eroded in cycles driven by internal heat, over timescales vastly exceeding biblical chronology. His famous conclusion was that geological evidence showed "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end."

Hutton also recognised that granite is intrusive and younger than the schists it penetrates (as at Glen Tilt), supporting the "plutonist" view that many rocks formed from molten material. His ideas were popularised by John Playfair's Illustrations of the Huttonian Theory (1802) and profoundly influenced Charles Lyell, whose work in turn shaped Darwin's thinking on evolution.

Other work

Hutton wrote on agriculture, meteorology (including a theory of rain), and philosophy. His Investigation of the Principles of Knowledge (1794) was a three-volume philosophical treatise. He never married and died in Edinburgh in 1797.
