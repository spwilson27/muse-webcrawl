Twelve-bar blues

The twelve-bar blues (or blues changes) is one of the most prominent chord progressions in popular music. The blues progression has a distinctive form in lyrics, phrase, chord structure, and duration. In its basic form, it is predominantly based on the tonic (I), subdominant (IV), and dominant (V) chords of a key.

Structure

The standard twelve-bar progression, in the key of C, runs:

| C  | C  | C  | C  | F  | F  | C  | C  | G  | F  | C  | G  |

That is: four bars of the tonic, two of the subdominant, two of the tonic, then one bar each of dominant and subdominant, and finally tonic and dominant (the "turnaround"). Countless variations exist: the "quick change" to IV in bar 2, the use of dominant seventh chords throughout, jazz substitutions, and minor blues forms, in which the tonic and subdominant are minor chords.

Lyrically, a blues verse typically consists of three lines: a line, its repetition (often slightly varied), and a concluding rhyming line — the AAB pattern — each sung over four bars of the progression.

History and influence

The form crystallised in African American communities in the Deep South in the early decades of the 20th century and was popularised in print by W. C. Handy's compositions such as "Memphis Blues" (1912) and "Saint Louis Blues" (1914). As the structural backbone of blues, it was carried into jazz, rhythm and blues, rock and roll, and rock — songs as varied as "Hound Dog", "Johnny B. Goode", and "Rock Around the Clock" are built on the twelve-bar form — making it arguably the most influential harmonic pattern in modern popular music.
