Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry, a subdiscipline of chemistry, involves the study of the relative spatial arrangement of atoms that form the structure of molecules and their manipulation. The study of stereochemistry focuses on stereoisomers, which by definition have the same chemical formula and sequence of bonded atoms (constitution), but differ in the three-dimensional orientations of their atoms in space.

History

The foundations of stereochemistry were laid in the 19th century. Louis Pasteur's 1848 manual separation of sodium ammonium tartrate crystals into mirror-image forms was the first demonstration of molecular chirality. In 1874, Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff and Joseph Le Bel independently proposed that the four bonds of a saturated carbon atom point toward the corners of a tetrahedron, explaining the existence of optical isomers.

Types of stereoisomerism

Enantiomers

Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images of one another. They have identical physical properties except for the direction in which they rotate plane-polarised light and their interactions with other chiral substances. A molecule with an asymmetric carbon atom (a stereocentre) is typically chiral.

Diastereomers

Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other; they have different physical properties. Cis–trans (E/Z) isomerism about double bonds and compounds with multiple stereocentres give rise to diastereomers.

Conformational isomerism

Conformers arise from rotation about single bonds, as in the staggered and eclipsed forms of ethane or the chair conformations of cyclohexane.

Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules

The Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) rules assign priorities to substituents so that stereocentres can be designated R (rectus) or S (sinister), and double bonds designated E or Z.

Importance

Stereochemistry is crucial in biochemistry and pharmacology: most biomolecules are chiral, and enantiomers of a drug can have very different biological activities, a lesson driven home by the thalidomide tragedy of the 1950s and 1960s.
