Trent Affair

Date8 November 1861
PartiesUnited States, United Kingdom
OutcomeConfederate envoys released; war averted

The Trent Affair was a diplomatic incident in 1861 during the American Civil War that threatened a war between the United States and Great Britain. The U.S. Navy captain Charles Wilkes captured two Confederate envoys aboard the British mail ship RMS Trent; the British government protested vigorously and demanded an apology and the release of the prisoners.

The seizure

On 8 November 1861, the USS San Jacinto, commanded by Captain Charles Wilkes, stopped the British mail packet Trent in the Old Bahama Channel and removed Confederate commissioners James Mason and John Slidell, who were travelling to Britain and France to seek diplomatic recognition and support for the Confederacy. Wilkes acted without orders from Washington.

Reaction and resolution

The seizure was first celebrated in the Union, but Britain considered it a violation of neutral rights and freedom of the seas, dispatched troops to Canada, and prepared for war; the London stock market fell sharply. President Abraham Lincoln and Secretary of State William H. Seward, anxious to avoid a second war — Lincoln's maxim was "one war at a time" — disavowed the seizure on the technical ground that Wilkes should have brought the ship before a prize court, and released Mason and Slidell on 26 December 1861. They sailed for Europe on 1 January 1862, and the crisis passed.

Consequences

The affair prompted Britain to strengthen its position in Canada and influenced British caution about recognizing the Confederacy thereafter; it also contributed to the formulation of rules on neutral rights later reflected in international maritime law.
