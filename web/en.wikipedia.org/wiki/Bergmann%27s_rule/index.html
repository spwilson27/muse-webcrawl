Bergmann's rule

Bergmann's rule is an ecogeographic principle stating that within a broadly distributed taxonomic group, populations and species of larger body size are found in colder environments, while smaller-bodied forms occur in warmer regions. It was formulated by the German biologist Carl Bergmann in 1847, originally from comparisons of closely related endothermic (warm-blooded) species.

Explanation

The classic explanation invokes thermoregulation: as body size increases, the ratio of surface area to volume decreases, so larger animals lose less heat per unit of mass and are favoured in cold climates; smaller animals, with relatively greater surface area, shed heat more easily in hot climates. Other proposed mechanisms include starvation resistance (larger animals store more fat and endure lean seasons), productivity gradients, and heat-dissipation limits during activity.

Examples and evidence

Supportive examples include bears (the polar bear is the largest of the bear species, while tropical species such as the sun bear are smallest), deer, penguins, and canids — coyotes and wolves tend to be larger at higher latitudes. In humans, studies of indigenous populations have found associations between colder climates, greater body mass and stockier builds, though nutrition and socioeconomic factors confound modern data.

Criticism and scope

Whether the rule applies within single species (as opposed to across related species), and whether it extends to ectotherms, remains debated; some analyses find the pattern in birds and mammals broadly holds but with many exceptions, and others argue it is strongest at higher latitudes. Bergmann's rule is one of several ecogeographic rules, alongside Allen's rule (limb length), Gloger's rule (coloration) and Foster's rule (island size change).
