Weaire–Phelan structure

In geometry, the Weaire–Phelan structure is a complex 3-dimensional structure representing an idealised foam of equal-sized bubbles, with two different kinds of cells with equal volume. One type is an irregular pentagonal dodecahedron and the other is a tetrakaidecahedron with two hexagonal and twelve pentagonal faces. In 1993, Denis Weaire and his student Robert Phelan found that this structure was a better solution of the Kelvin problem than the previous best-known solution, the Kelvin structure.

Kelvin problem

In 1887, Lord Kelvin asked how space could be partitioned into cells of equal volume with the least area of surface between them, i.e., what is the most efficient bubble foam? This problem has since become known as the Kelvin problem.

Kelvin proposed a foam of slightly curved truncated octahedra, known as the bitruncated cubic honeycomb. His proposed structure uses only one kind of cell. But it is not a valid foam because its cells have curved edges that do not meet four at a vertex at the tetrahedral angle, as required by Plateau's laws.

The Weaire–Phelan structure differs from Kelvin's in that it uses two kinds of cells, but all of the same volume: an irregular dodecahedron with pentagonal faces and a tetrakaidecahedron. The edges are very slightly curved, and the resulting structure has about 0.3% less surface area than Kelvin's.

In architecture

The structure inspired the design of the Beijing National Aquatics Centre, the "Water Cube," built for the 2008 Summer Olympics, whose exterior cladding uses a portion of the Weaire–Phelan foam as its pattern.
