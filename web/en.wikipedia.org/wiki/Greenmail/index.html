Greenmail

Greenmail or greenmailing is a corporate finance practice in which an investor acquires a substantial block of a company's shares — often while threatening a hostile takeover — and the target company then buys back the shares at a premium to end the threat. The term, a blend of "greenbacks" and "blackmail", was coined during the takeover wave of the 1980s.

Mechanics and criticism

A typical greenmail transaction involves a corporate raider amassing a stake large enough to alarm management, followed by negotiations in which the company repurchases the block at well above market price, frequently accompanied by a standstill agreement in which the raider promises not to bid again for a period of years. Critics argue that greenmail transfers wealth from ordinary shareholders to the raider and entrenches incumbent management, which can spend corporate funds to protect its own positions; defenders have contended that it can resolve takeover contests and that raiders bear real risk.

Notable cases and responses

Famous episodes involved raiders such as T. Boone Pickens, Carl Icahn, and Sir James Goldsmith; Walt Disney Productions' 1984 buyback of Saul Steinberg's stake became a widely cited example. Shareholder backlash produced countermeasures: "anti-greenmail" charter provisions requiring shareholder approval of premium buybacks from large holders, and in the United States a federal excise tax — the "greenmail tax" enacted in 1987 imposes a 50% tax on gains from greenmail. The practice largely disappeared from the United States after these reforms, though analogous tactics appear in other markets.
