Foster's rule

Also calledIsland rule
Named afterJ. Bristol Foster
FieldEvolutionary biology, biogeography
First described1964

Foster's rule, also known as the island rule or the island effect, is an ecogeographical rule in evolutionary biology stating that members of a species get smaller or bigger depending on the resources available in the environment. The rule was first proposed by mammalogist J. Bristol Foster in 1964, based on a study of 116 island species.

The pattern

Small mainland species tend to evolve larger island forms — insular gigantism, as in the Komodo dragon and the extinct dodo's giant relatives — while large mainland species tend to evolve smaller island forms, insular dwarfism, as in dwarf elephants and the tiny hominin Homo floresiensis. The pattern is attributed to altered predation, competition, and resource availability on islands.

Evidence and debate

The rule has been supported across many mammal groups and some birds, reptiles, and plants, though later studies have debated how general it is and which mechanisms dominate. It remains a central concept in island biogeography.
