Escrow

How an escrow arrangement works (image placeholder)

Escrow is a contractual arrangement in which a third party (the escrow agent) receives and disburses money or property for the primary transacting parties, with the disbursement dependent on conditions agreed to by those parties. In an escrow agreement, one party deposits funds or assets with a neutral third party, who holds them until the terms of the contract are fulfilled, at which point the funds or assets are released to the other party.

Real estate

Escrow is most commonly used in real-estate transactions, especially in the United States. A buyer's earnest-money deposit is held in escrow until closing, and after closing, lenders often require an escrow account (or impound account) into which the borrower pays a portion of each mortgage payment to cover property taxes and homeowner's insurance, which the servicer then pays when due.

Other uses

Escrow is also used in mergers and acquisitions (holdbacks securing indemnity claims), in online commerce (escrow services that hold a buyer's payment until goods are received), in banking and finance, and in software, where source code escrow deposits a program's source code with a third party so a licensee can access it if the vendor goes out of business or fails to maintain the software. In Scots law, a related concept exists whereby documents or money are held by a third party as stakeholder.
