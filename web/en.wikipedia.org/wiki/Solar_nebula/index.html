Solar nebula

The solar nebula was the gaseous cloud from which, in the so-called nebular hypothesis of the origin of the Solar System, the Sun and planets formed by condensation. The nebular hypothesis, developed by Immanuel Kant and refined by Pierre-Simon Laplace in the 18th century, holds that the Solar System formed from the gravitational collapse of a fragment of a giant molecular cloud.

Formation and evolution

About 4.6 billion years ago, the collapse of a cloud fragment — possibly triggered by a nearby supernova shock wave — caused the material to spin faster as it contracted, flattening into a rotating protoplanetary disk. Most mass concentrated at the center, heating up to form the protosun, while in the disk, dust grains collided and stuck together in the accretion process that built planetesimals and eventually the planets.

Composition and the frost line

Temperature gradients in the nebula sorted its materials: refractory metals and silicates condensed in the hot inner regions, while beyond the frost line volatile compounds such as water, ammonia, and methane could condense as ices, providing the extra solid mass that let the giant planets' cores grow quickly enough to capture hydrogen and helium gas before the nebula dispersed within a few million years.

Evidence

Observations of disks around young stars by telescopes such as ALMA, the chemical and isotopic composition of meteorites, and the architecture of exoplanetary systems all inform modern models of the solar nebula's lifetime, mass, and dynamics, including planet migration scenarios such as the Grand Tack hypothesis.
