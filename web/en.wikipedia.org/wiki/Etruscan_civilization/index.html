The Etruscan civilization was an ancient culture centred in Etruria, in what is now Tuscany and adjoining parts of Italy, which flourished from about 900 BC until its gradual absorption into the Roman Republic by the first century BC. The Etruscans — who called themselves Rasenna and were known to the Romans as Etrusci or Tusci (hence "Tuscany") — built a league of prosperous city-states, traded across the Mediterranean, and profoundly influenced early Rome in religion, engineering, art and political symbolism.

Origins and language

Ancient authors disagreed about Etruscan origins: Herodotus claimed they migrated from Lydia, while Dionysius of Halicarnassus held them indigenous to Italy. Modern scholarship, supported by archaeology and recent ancient-DNA studies, generally sees them as a largely local population continuous with the preceding Villanovan culture. Their language, Etruscan, is written in an alphabet adapted from Greek, but the language itself is not Indo-European and is only partially understood, from short inscriptions and a few longer ritual texts such as the Liber Linteus.

Society and cities

Etruria was organised as a loose league of twelve cities (the dodecapolis), including Veii, Caere, Tarquinii, Vulci, Clusium and Perusia. Etruscan society was aristocratic, yet women enjoyed a status strikingly high by Greek and Roman standards, attending banquets and owning property. Wealth came from the metal ores of Elba and the Colline Metallifere, from agriculture, and from maritime trade; Etruscan bucchero pottery, bronzes and jewellery were exported throughout the Mediterranean world. In the seventh and sixth centuries BC Etruscan power extended from the Po valley to Campania, and Etruscan kings — the Tarquins — ruled Rome itself.

Religion and art

Etruscan religion centred on elaborate divination: haruspicy (reading the livers of sacrificial animals), augury from lightning and birds, and the body of revealed doctrine known as the disciplina Etrusca, which the Romans eagerly adopted. Much of what is known of Etruscan life comes from their richly painted tombs at Tarquinia and Cerveteri, with scenes of feasting, games and music; masterpieces such as the Sarcophagus of the Spouses and the Apollo of Veii show an art both influenced by and distinct from Greek models.

Decline

From the fifth century BC the Etruscans were squeezed between Celts in the north, Greeks in the south and the rising power of Rome. Defeats at sea off Cumae (474 BC) and Rome's destruction of Veii (396 BC) began a long decline; city by city the Etruscans fell under Roman control during the fourth and third centuries. Romanisation followed, and Etruscan ceased to be spoken in everyday life by about the first century BC, though its priestly lore was still studied in imperial times.
