Operation Market Garden / Tunisian campaign

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This page treats the Tunisian campaign as it relates to the background of Operation Market Garden (September 1944). The fighting in Tunisia in 1942–43 was the last act of the North African campaign, and it formed the combat experience of several airborne and armoured formations later committed in the Netherlands.

The Tunisian campaign in brief

The Tunisian campaign (17 November 1942 – 13 May 1943) began with the Allied Torch landings in Morocco and Algeria and the race for Tunis. Axis forces under Hans-Jürgen von Arnim and Erwin Rommel held Tunisia through the winter, defeating the Americans at the Kasserine Pass in February 1943, but were steadily compressed between the British First Army advancing from the west and Montgomery's Eighth Army from Libya. After the breakthrough at the Mareth Line and the final offensive of early May 1943, Tunis and Bizerte fell and around 250,000 Axis soldiers surrendered — a capitulation comparable in scale to Stalingrad.

Connections to Market Garden

Among the troops who learned their trade in Tunisia were men of the British 1st Airborne Division's parachute battalions, who had jumped in North Africa during Torch and fought as infantry in the hills of Tunisia; the division was destroyed at Arnhem in September 1944. The campaign also exposed fault lines between British and American commanders — over pace, supply and the handling of armour — that resurfaced in 1944 in arguments about Market Garden's single-thrust strategy versus a broad-front advance. The green US II Corps' mauling at Kasserine, by contrast, informed the rapid professionalisation of the airborne divisions that jumped into Holland eighteen months later.
