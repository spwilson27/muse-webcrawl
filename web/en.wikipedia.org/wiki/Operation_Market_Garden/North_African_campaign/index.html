Operation Market Garden / North African campaign

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This page treats the North African campaign as it relates to the background of Operation Market Garden (September 1944). Several of Market Garden's senior commanders — above all Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery — had made their reputations in North Africa, and the habits of command formed there shaped the planning and conduct of the later operation in the Netherlands.

The North African campaign in brief

The North African campaign (10 June 1940 – 13 May 1943) was fought between the Axis powers and the Western Allies across Libya, Egypt and Tunisia. After early British successes against Italy, the arrival of Erwin Rommel's Afrika Korps turned the desert war into a see-saw of advances and retreats until Montgomery's Eighth Army won the decisive Second Battle of El Alamein in October–November 1942. The Anglo-American landings of Operation Torch in November 1942 then trapped the Axis between two armies, and the campaign ended with the surrender of some 250,000 Axis troops in Tunisia in May 1943.

Connections to Market Garden

Montgomery, the victor of El Alamein, commanded the Allied ground forces for the Normandy invasion and was the architect of Market Garden, the ambitious airborne plan to seize bridges over the Rhine at Arnhem. Veterans of the desert war staffed many of its formations: the Guards Armoured and 7th Armoured ("Desert Rats") divisions had fought in North Africa, as had XXX Corps' commander Brian Horrocks. Critics have argued that the caution Montgomery had learned in the desert — insistence on set-piece preparation and overwhelming logistics — sat uneasily with the speed an airborne coup de main required, and that Market Garden's narrow single-road advance repeated, in Dutch polder country, supply problems first encountered along the desert coast road.
