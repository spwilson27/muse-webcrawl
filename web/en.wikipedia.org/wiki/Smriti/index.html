Smriti (Sanskrit: स्मृति, IAST: smṛti), literally "that which is remembered," is a body of Hindu texts usually attributed to an author and traditionally regarded as sacred but of human composition, in contrast to the Śrutis ("that which is heard"), the authorless, eternal Vedas revealed to ancient seers. Smriti texts are considered derivative and less authoritative than Śruti, and where the two conflict, Śruti prevails — though in lived Hindu practice, Smriti works such as the epics and Purāṇas are far more widely read and influential.

Contents of Smriti

  • Itihāsa ("history"): the great epics Mahābhārata (which contains the Bhagavad Gītā) and Rāmāyaṇa.
  • Purāṇas: eighteen major (Mahāpurāṇa) and eighteen minor collections of myth, cosmology, genealogy and devotion, including the Bhāgavata Purāṇa.
  • Dharmaśāstras: treatises on dharma (law, duty, conduct), most famously the Manusmṛti (Laws of Manu, c. 2nd century BCE–2nd century CE), alongside Yājñavalkya, Nārada and other smṛtis.
  • Vedāṅgas and Sutras: the six auxiliary disciplines — phonetics, ritual (Kalpa), grammar, etymology, metre and astronomy — including the Śrauta, Gṛhya and Dharma sūtras.
  • Āgamas and Tantras, sectarian scriptures of Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Śākta traditions, and the foundational sūtras of the philosophical schools (darśanas).

Role and interpretation

The Dharmaśāstras were long treated in India as legal authorities; under British rule, administrators codified "Hindu law" largely from translated Smriti texts such as the Manusmṛti, a process scholars criticise for freezing a fluid textual tradition and elevating Brahmanical norms. Modern Hindu reformers from Rammohan Roy to Gandhi selectively reinterpreted Smriti, distinguishing eternal principles from time-bound social rules. The term is also used more broadly: in Buddhist contexts, its Pali cognate sati means mindfulness or recollection.
