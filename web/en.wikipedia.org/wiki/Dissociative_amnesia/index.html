Dissociative amnesia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dissociative amnesia, formerly called psychogenic amnesia, is a dissociative disorder characterised by retrospectively reported memory gaps. These gaps involve an inability to recall important personal information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, that is too extensive to be explained by ordinary forgetfulness and is not attributable to a medical condition, brain injury, or the effects of a substance.

Features

The memory loss is typically for autobiographical (episodic) information, while general knowledge and skills are preserved. Several patterns are described:

  • Localised amnesia – failure to recall events during a circumscribed period, often the hours around a trauma.
  • Selective amnesia – recall of some, but not all, events during a period.
  • Generalised amnesia – loss of memory for one's whole life history and identity; rare, and sometimes accompanied by dissociative fugue, in which the person travels away and may assume a new identity.
  • Systematised and continuous amnesia – loss confined to certain categories of information, or loss extending from a point onward, respectively.

Causes and diagnosis

The condition is associated with overwhelming stress or trauma, such as combat, accidents, disasters, or abuse. Diagnosis requires ruling out organic causes such as head injury, epilepsy, and intoxication, as well as distinguishing the condition from malingering and from other disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder, with which it frequently co-occurs.

Treatment and prognosis

Memories often return spontaneously, sometimes suddenly. Psychotherapy is the main treatment, aimed at safely recovering and integrating the memories and addressing the underlying trauma; there is no specific medication. The prognosis is generally good for localised amnesia, though generalised cases may be more persistent.
