Hysteria

Hysteria is a historically used medical and psychological term for a condition characterised by excessive or uncontrollable emotion and by physical symptoms without an identifiable organic cause. Once attributed to a "wandering womb" — the word derives from the Greek hystera (uterus) — the diagnosis was applied overwhelmingly to women and has been abandoned by modern medicine, its former territory divided among conditions such as conversion disorder (functional neurological symptom disorder), somatic symptom disorder and dissociative disorders.

History

Ideas resembling hysteria appear in ancient Egyptian and Greek texts; Plato and Hippocratic writers linked symptoms to the uterus. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries explanations shifted toward the nerves. In the 1860s–80s Jean-Martin Charcot's demonstrations at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris made hysteria a fashionable scientific subject, influencing his students, including Sigmund Freud. Freud and Josef Breuer's Studies on Hysteria (1895), centred on the case of "Anna O.", launched psychoanalysis by treating hysterical symptoms as converted psychological conflict.

Twentieth century and abandonment

Mass "hysterical" symptoms among soldiers in the First World War were reframed as shell shock. Critics increasingly attacked the diagnosis as vague, sexist and a catch-all for women's distress. The American Psychiatric Association removed "hysteria" from the DSM in 1980 (DSM-III), replacing it with more specific categories. The term survives in everyday language meaning uncontrolled panic or excitement, and in the phrase "mass hysteria" for collective episodes of anxiety or symptoms — now usually termed mass psychogenic illness.
