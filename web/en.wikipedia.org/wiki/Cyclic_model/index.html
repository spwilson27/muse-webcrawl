Cyclic model


Illustration of cyclic cosmology (placeholder image)

A cyclic model (or oscillating model) is any of several cosmological models in which the universe follows infinite, self-sustaining cycles of expansion and contraction (or other renewal). Such ideas are ancient in philosophy and religion, and within modern physics trace to the oscillating-universe proposals of the 1930s.

Variants

Classic oscillating models alternate Big Bang and Big Crunch phases, but face the problem that entropy grows each cycle (Tolman's objection), making past cycles finite in number. Later proposals avoid this: the Baum–Frampton model, Roger Penrose's conformal cyclic cosmology, in which the remote future of one aeon becomes the Big Bang of the next, and the ekpyrotic/cyclic model of Steinhardt and Turok, in which cycles of expansion, contraction and brane collision dilute entropy so cycles can be eternal in both directions. Cyclic ideas remain speculative alternatives to eternal inflation and a single-beginning universe.
