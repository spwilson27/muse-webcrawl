Berimbau

Berimbau
ClassificationMusical bow; percussion (idiophone/chordophone)
OriginAfro-Brazilian, from African musical bows
Used inCapoeira
ComponentsVerga (bow), arame (steel wire), cabaça (gourd resonator), baqueta (stick), caxixi (rattle)

The berimbau is a single-string percussion instrument, a musical bow of African origin that is the leading instrument of capoeira, the Afro-Brazilian martial art. It consists of a wooden bow (the verga), usually made of biribá wood, strung with a steel wire (arame), with a hollowed gourd (cabaça) attached as a resonator. The player strikes the wire with a thin stick (baqueta) while holding a small woven rattle (caxixi) in the same hand, and changes the pitch by pressing a coin or stone (dobrão) against the wire.

Types and role in capoeira

In the capoeira bateria (orchestra) three berimbaus traditionally play together, distinguished by the size of their gourds and their pitch: the low gunga, which leads the ensemble and sets the rhythm (toque); the middle médio, which complements it; and the high viola, which improvises variations. The toque played by the gunga dictates the style and speed of the capoeira game in the roda (circle).

Sounds

The berimbau produces three basic sounds: an open buzzing tone with the coin away from the wire, a pressed high tone with the coin firmly against the wire, and a muted sound with the coin lightly touching. Moving the gourd toward and away from the player's abdomen produces a wah-wah-like variation in resonance and volume.

History

Musical bows are ancient and widespread in Africa; the berimbau was developed in Brazil by enslaved Africans and their descendants, and by the early 20th century had become inseparably associated with capoeira in Bahia. It later entered Brazilian popular music, most famously in Baden Powell and Vinícius de Moraes's song "Berimbau" (1963).
