Regulatory taking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A parcel whose development is restricted by regulation — the classic setting for regulatory takings claims.

In United States constitutional law, a regulatory taking occurs when a government regulation limits the use of private property to such a degree that the regulation effectively amounts to a taking of the property, even though the government does not formally condemn it. Under the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment — "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation" — the owner is then entitled to just compensation.

Regulatory takings doctrine is one of the most contested areas of property law, because nearly all regulation affects property values, and courts must draw a line between ordinary regulation (no compensation) and regulation that "goes too far."

History

For much of the 19th century, the Takings Clause was understood to require compensation only for direct physical appropriation. The modern doctrine began with Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon (1922), where Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote that "while property may be regulated to a certain extent, if regulation goes too far it will be recognized as a taking." Mahon involved a Pennsylvania statute that effectively destroyed a coal company's reserved right to mine beneath surface land.

The Penn Central test

The default framework comes from Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978), which upheld the designation of Grand Central Terminal as a landmark. The Court identified three factors for an ad hoc, factual inquiry:

  • The economic impact of the regulation on the claimant;
  • The extent to which the regulation has interfered with distinct investment-backed expectations;
  • The character of the governmental action — whether it is a physical invasion or a public program adjusting the benefits and burdens of economic life.

Categorical rules

Alongside Penn Central, the Supreme Court has recognized categorical (per se) takings: permanent physical occupations, however minor (Loretto v. Teleprompter, 1982); regulations that deprive an owner of all economically beneficial use of land (Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, 1992), unless the prohibited use was never part of the owner's title under background principles of state property and nuisance law; and, more recently, government-authorized physical appropriations of a right of access treated as per se takings (Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid, 2021). Exactions on development permits are tested under Nollan and Dolan, requiring an "essential nexus" and "rough proportionality."

Related concepts

A claim that the government has taken property without formally condemning it is pleaded as inverse condemnation. Not every burdensome regulation qualifies: zoning ordinances, environmental restrictions, rent control, and historic-preservation laws are routinely upheld when they leave the owner with reasonable economically viable use of the property as a whole, and courts generally measure impact against the "parcel as a whole" rather than a segmented interest.
