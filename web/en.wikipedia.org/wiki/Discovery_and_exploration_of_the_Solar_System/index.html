Discovery and exploration of the Solar System

The discovery and exploration of the Solar System is the product of millennia of observation, culminating in the era of telescopic astronomy and, since the mid-twentieth century, robotic spaceflight. For most of human history the Solar System was understood through the naked-eye planets — Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn — whose motions against the fixed stars drove the development of astronomy in many cultures.

From antiquity to the telescope

Babylonian, Greek, Indian, and Chinese astronomers recorded planetary positions and predicted eclipses. The heliocentric model proposed by Copernicus in 1543, Kepler's laws of planetary motion, and Newton's law of universal gravitation established the modern dynamical picture. Telescopic observation added Uranus (1781, William Herschel), Neptune (1846, predicted mathematically by Le Verrier and Adams and observed by Galle), the asteroids, and Pluto (1930, Clyde Tombaugh, later reclassified as a dwarf planet).

The space age

The launch of Luna 2 (1959) made the Moon the first object reached by a spacecraft; Luna 9 made the first soft landing in 1966, and Apollo 8 and Apollo 11 carried humans to lunar orbit and the surface in 1968–69. Mariner 2 reached Venus in 1962 and Mariner 4 flew by Mars in 1965. The 1970s saw landers on Venus and Mars, the Viking missions, and the grand tour of the outer planets by Voyager 1 and 2, launched in 1977, which visited Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune and are now in interstellar space.

Modern exploration

Subsequent exploration has included orbiters and rovers at Mars (Sojourner through Perseverance), the Galileo and Juno missions at Jupiter, Cassini at Saturn, MESSENGER and BepiColombo at Mercury, New Horizons at Pluto and Arrokoth, sample-return missions to asteroids and comets, and the discovery by ground- and space-based telescopes of thousands of Kuiper Belt objects and exoplanets, which reframed understanding of the Solar System's own origin and diversity.
