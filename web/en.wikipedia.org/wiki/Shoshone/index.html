Shoshone

The Shoshone or Shoshoni (Shoshoni: Newe, "People") are a Native American people whose traditional homeland covers much of the Great Basin and northern Rockies — present-day Nevada, Utah, Idaho, Wyoming and surrounding states. They speak Shoshoni, a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family.

Groups and history

Traditionally the Shoshone are described in three broad divisions: the Western Shoshone of Nevada and Utah, hunter-gatherers organised in small family bands; the Northern Shoshone of Idaho and northern Utah, who adopted horses and mounted bison hunting; and the Eastern Shoshone of Wyoming's Wind River country. Sacagawea, the Lemhi Shoshone woman who guided the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806), is the most famous member of the nation; her people later played key roles in the fur trade era.

Nineteenth century and reservations

Under leaders such as Chief Washakie, the Eastern Shoshone negotiated treaties that created the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming (1863/1868), shared today with the Northern Arapaho. The Northern Shoshone and Bannock hold the Fort Hall Reservation in Idaho; other Shoshone communities include the Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of Duck Valley and numerous Western Shoshone bands in Nevada. The 1863 Bear River Massacre in present-day Idaho, in which US troops killed hundreds of Northwestern Shoshone, remains one of the deadliest attacks on Native people in US history.

Culture

Traditional Great Basin subsistence centred on pine nuts, seeds, roots and jackrabbits, while mounted groups hunted bison on the plains. Today the Shoshone tribes maintain their languages, ceremonies and tribal governments, and continue to assert treaty rights to ancestral lands.
