Rolls-Royce Avon

Rolls-Royce Avon axial-flow turbojet

The Rolls-Royce Avon was the first axial flow jet engine designed and produced by Rolls-Royce. Introduced in 1950, it went on to become one of the most successful post-war engine designs, and was used in a wide range of aircraft, in both military and civil roles. Production of aircraft versions ended in 1974 after more than 11,000 engines had been built; adapted industrial and marine versions, used for power generation and as gas compressors, have remained in service far longer.

Design and development

The Avon began as the AJ.65 (Axial Jet, 6,500 lbf) designed under Alan Arnold Griffith's influence, with an axial compressor derived from wartime studies. Early versions suffered compressor surging, which was cured by redesign including variable inlet guide vanes and a bleed system. The engine grew from around 6,500 lbf of thrust in the Avon RA.3 to over 16,000 lbf with afterburning in the RA.29 that powered the English Electric Lightning.

Applications

The Avon powered many landmark aircraft: the Hawker Hunter, de Havilland Comet airliner (the first jet airliner to use it), the Sud Aviation Caravelle, the English Electric Canberra and Lightning, the de Havilland Sea Vixen, the Vickers Valiant V-bomber, and the Swedish Saab 32 Lansen and Saab 35 Draken, the latter licence-built by Svenska Flygmotor as the RM5 and RM6. It was also built under licence in Australia for the CAC Sabre.

Industrial use

Adapted as the Avon industrial gas turbine, the engine found a second career driving generators and pipeline compressors, prized for its reliability; many units remain in service decades after production ended.
