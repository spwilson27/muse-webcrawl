Theria

Theria
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Mammalia
Subclass: Theria (Parker & Haswell, 1897)
Infraclasses: Metatheria (marsupials), Eutheria (placentals)
Distinguishing feature: give birth to live young without a shelled egg

Theria (from Greek θηρίον, "wild beast") is a subclass of mammals comprising the marsupials and the placental mammals — that is, all living mammals except the egg-laying monotremes. Therians give birth to live young, nourished before birth either in a short gestation followed by development in a pouch (marsupials) or through a well-developed placenta (placentals).

Characteristics

Shared therian features include live birth (viviparity) without a shelled egg; mammary glands with nipples (absent as such in monotremes, which secrete milk onto skin patches); a dentary-squamosal jaw joint with the middle ear ossicles fully detached from the jaw; and, in the skeleton, characteristic features of the ankle and shoulder girdle, including loss of the coracoid bone as a separate element in adults. The external ear (pinna) is a therian innovation, though it has been lost secondarily in some aquatic forms.

Evolution

The lineage leading to Theria split from the monotreme line deep in the Mesozoic. The oldest generally accepted therian fossils date from the Middle-to-Late Jurassic and Early Cretaceous; creatures such as Juramaia from the Jurassic of China push the origin of the eutherian line back to around 160 million years ago, while the diversity of Early Cretaceous forms like Eomaia shows that therians were already radiating while dinosaurs dominated the land. The marsupial (metatherian) and placental (eutherian) lineages had diverged by the Early Cretaceous.

Classification

  • Metatheria — includes the crown group Marsupialia (opossums, kangaroos, koalas, wombats, dasyurids, and their kin), about 330 living species.
  • Eutheria — includes the crown group Placentalia, over 5,000 living species, from shrews and bats to whales and primates.

Together the two groups account for the overwhelming majority of modern mammal diversity and biomass, a radiation completed after the end-Cretaceous extinction removed the non-avian dinosaurs.
