Marginal value theorem

Illustration: Graphical solution of the marginal value theorem (placeholder image)

The marginal value theorem (MVT) is an optimality model in behavioural ecology that predicts how long an optimally foraging animal should remain in a patch of resources before leaving to find a new one. It was developed by Eric Charnov in 1976 and is one of the foundational results of optimal foraging theory.

The model

The model assumes that resources occur in discrete patches, that a forager's rate of energy intake within a patch declines over time as the patch is depleted, and that travelling between patches takes time during which no energy is gained. The theorem states that the optimal strategy is to leave a patch when the instantaneous (marginal) rate of energy gain within the patch falls to the average rate of gain for the whole habitat, including travel time. Graphically, the average intake rate is the slope of the line from the origin (offset by travel time) tangent to the cumulative gain curve; the point of tangency gives the optimal patch residence time.

Predictions

The theorem predicts that foragers should stay longer in patches when travel time between patches is long, should stay longer in high-quality patches, and should give up patches at the same marginal rate across all patch types in a habitat. Many experimental studies — for example with great tits, starlings carrying food to nestlings, and parasitoid wasps — have found qualitative agreement with these predictions, though animals often stay somewhat longer than the simple model predicts. The logic of the theorem has also been applied by analogy to information foraging, human attention, and even the allocation of effort by predators and pollinators.
