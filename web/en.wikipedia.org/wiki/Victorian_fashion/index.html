Victorian fashion

Victorian fashion comprises the styles of clothing, accessories and grooming worn in Britain and its empire during the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901). The period saw rapid change driven by industrialisation, which made textiles cheaper and more widely available, and by new technologies such as the sewing machine and synthetic aniline dyes.

Women's fashion

Early Victorian women's dress featured sloping shoulders, a narrow pointed waist and full skirts. In the 1850s and 1860s skirt volume was achieved with the crinoline — first layered petticoats, then the cage crinoline of steel hoops — before fullness shifted to the back with the bustle in the 1870s and 1880s. The corset remained a fixture throughout, shaping the silhouette prized in each decade. Mourning dress followed elaborate codes, and Queen Victoria's own decades of mourning black after 1861 reinforced its social importance.

Men's fashion

Men's dress grew more sombre and uniform over the century: the frock coat gave way to the morning coat and then the lounge suit, with dark wool fabrics, waistcoats, white shirts and cravats or neckties. Top hats were standard formal wear for much of the period, later joined by the bowler hat (from 1849) and the homburg.

Children and working dress

Children were largely dressed as miniature adults in formal settings, though boys wore dresses in early childhood and girls' hemlines rose with age. Working-class clothing was practical and often second-hand, while the spread of ready-to-wear manufacturing and department stores gradually democratised fashion.

Reform movements

The century also saw campaigns for dress reform, criticising tight-lacing and heavy skirts on health grounds, and the aesthetic dress movement associated with artists such as William Morris, which favoured looser, "artistic" garments. By 1901 the rigid silhouettes of the mid-century were softening into the S-bend corset shape of the Edwardian era.
