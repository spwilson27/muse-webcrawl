Phoenicia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Phoenician merchant ship of the type carved on sarcophagi and coins]

Phoenicia was an ancient maritime civilisation of city-states along the Levantine coast (modern Lebanon and parts of Syria and Israel), flourishing from about 1200 BC. The cities of Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad and Beirut were never a single state, but shared a language, religion and genius for trade. The Phoenicians were the ancient world's great sailors and merchants, producers of Tyrian purple dye and fine glass, founders of colonies across the Mediterranean — most famously Carthage — and inventors of the alphabet from which Greek, Latin and most modern alphabets descend.

Trade and colonies

Cedar-hungry Egypt had traded with Byblos for millennia, but after 1200 BC Phoenician ships ranged the whole Mediterranean, seeking silver in Iberia, tin in the Atlantic, and founding trading posts and colonies in Cyprus, Malta, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and Spain, including Gadir (Cádiz), among the oldest cities in western Europe. Herodotus credits Phoenician sailors, sent by Pharaoh Necho II around 600 BC, with the first circumnavigation of Africa.

Decline and legacy

The cities paid tribute successively to Assyria, Babylon and Persia, whose fleets they largely manned. Alexander the Great captured Tyre after a famous siege in 332 BC, and the cities slowly Hellenised, then passed to Rome. Their alphabet — 22 signs for consonants, adapted from earlier Proto-Canaanite script — was their most durable gift, transmitted by the Greeks to Europe and by Aramaic scripts to much of Asia.
