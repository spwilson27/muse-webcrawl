Being and Nothingness

Being and Nothingness: An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology (French: L'Être et le néant) is a 1943 book of philosophy by Jean-Paul Sartre, his principal philosophical work and a founding text of existentialism. Written while Sartre was a teacher and soldier, it develops a phenomenological account of consciousness, freedom and human relations, drawing on Husserl, Heidegger and Hegel.

Main ideas

Sartre distinguishes two modes of being: the being-in-itself (en-soi), the self-identical being of things, and the being-for-itself (pour-soi), consciousness, which is characterised by a "nothingness" — it is not a fixed essence but a perpetual self-transcendence toward possibilities. From this follows Sartre's famous conclusion that for human beings "existence precedes essence": we are, as he puts it, "condemned to be free," and attempts to deny this freedom amount to bad faith (mauvaise foi), exemplified by his portrait of a waiter playing at being a waiter.

The book's third part analyses being-for-others: the Look of the Other, which objectifies and shames the self, and concrete relations including love, masochism and sadism, which Sartre presents as fundamentally conflictual. A final part sketches an existential psychoanalysis and gestures toward an ethics of freedom that Sartre never completed.

Reception

Being and Nothingness made Sartre the leading figure of French existentialism after the Second World War and influenced philosophy, literary criticism and theology. It has also been criticised for its length, difficulty and bleak account of interpersonal relations; Sartre himself later tried to reconcile its individualism with Marxism in the Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960).
