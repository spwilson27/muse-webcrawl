Silt

Silt is granular sediment with particle sizes between those of sand and clay — roughly 0.002 to 0.063 mm on the Wentworth scale — composed mainly of quartz and feldspar. It is carried by running water, wind, and glaciers, and settles in floodplains, deltas, and lake beds.

Formation and transport

Silt is produced by mechanical weathering, especially glacial grinding, which generates the fine "rock flour" that colours meltwater streams milky turquoise, and by abrasion in rivers. Because its particles are small and roughly equant, silt is easily suspended in flowing water yet settles more readily than clay. Wind-blown silt accumulates as loess, the fertile deposits that blanket much of northern China and parts of Europe and North America.

Significance

Silt-laden rivers such as the Nile, the Yellow River (Huang He), and the Mississippi historically renewed the fertility of their floodplains with annual deposits, underpinning early agricultural civilisations; the Yellow River's name and course changes reflect its immense silt load. Conversely, siltation is a serious problem for reservoirs, harbours, and irrigation works, and fine sediment clouds water and harms aquatic life. In soil science, silt loams are prized agricultural soils that hold moisture and nutrients well.
