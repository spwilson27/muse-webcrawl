Kumis (also spelled koumiss; from Turkic kımız) is a fermented dairy product traditionally made from mare's milk. The drink remains important to the peoples of the Central Asian steppes, especially among ethnic Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Mongols, and Yakuts. In Kyrgyzstan it is called kymyz, and in Mongolia a distilled or fermented version is known as airag.

Production

Raw mare's milk cannot be drunk in quantity because of its high lactose content, but fermentation by lactic acid bacteria and yeasts (typically in a leather sack or wooden barrel, churned with a special paddle) converts much of the sugar to lactic acid and a small amount of ethanol and carbon dioxide. The result is a mildly alcoholic (0.7–2.5% ABV), slightly fizzy, sour drink that keeps longer than fresh milk. Fresh mare's milk has about 40% more lactose than cow's milk, giving kumis its relative strength for a fermented milk drink.

History

Fermented mare's milk is among the oldest beverages of the Eurasian steppe. Residue analysis suggests mare milking by the Botai culture of Kazakhstan around 3500 BC. Herodotus described the Scythians processing mare's milk, and in the 13th century the Franciscan traveller William of Rubruck gave a detailed account of kumis-making among the Mongols. In the 19th century, "kumis cures" at sanatoria in Russia were fashionable treatments for tuberculosis and other illnesses.

Consumption

Kumis is drunk from wooden bowls during the summer milking season; the first kumis of the year is celebrated in festivals in Kyrgyzstan and Mongolia. Industrial versions are produced in Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, and Mongolia for domestic sale and export, and a cow's-milk imitation is made where mares are unavailable. In Kyrgyzstan the drink is so emblematic that a bottle of kymyz appears on the tray of the national epic hero imagery, and Bishkek's skyline includes the Kymyz monument in folk usage.
