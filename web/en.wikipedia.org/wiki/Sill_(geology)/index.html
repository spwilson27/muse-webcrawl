Sill (geology)

In geology, a sill is a tabular sheet intrusion of igneous rock that is intruded between older layers of sedimentary rock, beds of volcanic lava or tuff, or along foliation in metamorphic rock. A sill is a concordant intrusive sheet, meaning that a sill does not cut across preexisting rock beds. In contrast, a dike is a discordant intrusive sheet, which does cut across older rocks. Sills are fed by dikes, except in unusual occurrences where they form in nearly horizontal beds attached to a dike. The rocks must be brittle and fractured to create the planes along which the magma intrudes the parent rock bodies, whether this occurs along preexisting planes between sedimentary or volcanic beds or weakened planes related to foliation in metamorphic rock. These planes or weakened areas allow the intrusion of a thin sheet-like body of magma parallel to the existing bedding planes, concordant fracture zone, or foliations.

Formation

Sills form when magma is injected along a plane of weakness, typically a bedding plane, at relatively shallow depths in the crust. Because magma is generally less dense than the surrounding rock, it rises until it reaches a level where lateral spreading becomes easier than vertical ascent, often beneath a dense or impermeable layer. The magma then spreads out sideways, prising apart the strata to form a sheet that may range from a few centimetres to hundreds of metres in thickness and extend over many square kilometres.

Well-known examples

  • The Great Whin Sill in northern England, a dolerite intrusion of late Carboniferous age that underlies much of Northumberland and Durham and forms the outcrop on which parts of Hadrian's Wall were built.
  • The Palisades Sill along the Hudson River in New York and New Jersey, an Early Jurassic diabase intrusion roughly 300 metres thick, famous for its well-developed layering produced by crystal settling.
  • The extensive sills of the Karoo basin in South Africa, associated with the Karoo large igneous province.
  • The sills of the Ferrar province in Antarctica.

Differentiated sills

Thick sills of mafic magma may undergo fractional crystallisation and gravitational settling of early-formed crystals while still molten, producing a layered internal structure with more evolved (silica-rich) compositions towards the top or centre of the sheet. The Palisades Sill is a classic example, displaying an olivine-enriched layer near its base. Such differentiated sills have been used as natural laboratories for understanding magmatic processes that also operate in larger magma chambers.

Economic significance

Sills can be economically important. Some mafic sills host deposits of nickel, copper and platinum-group elements, and the heat from sill intrusions can mature organic-rich sediments, generating petroleum, or drive hydrothermal systems. Sills also form resistant caprocks that influence landscape development, producing escarpments and waterfalls where they are exposed by erosion.
