Islamic terrorism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Islamic terrorism, also called Islamist terrorism or jihadist terrorism, refers to acts of terrorism motivated by or attributed to Islamist ideologies. Groups so described have included al-Qaeda, the Islamic State (ISIL/ISIS), Boko Haram, al-Shabaab and factions of the Taliban, among others. The term and its use are contested: many Muslims, scholars and officials object that it associates the religion of Islam as a whole with violence, and some style guides and governments prefer terms such as "jihadist" or "violent extremist".

History

Modern Islamist militancy draws in part on the thought of twentieth-century ideologues such as Sayyid Qutb, whose writings reframed jihad against regimes deemed apostate. The 1979 Iranian Revolution, the Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989), during which transnational volunteers fought with US- and Saudi-backed mujahideen, and the Arab–Israeli conflicts all shaped the movements that followed.

Major organisations and attacks

Al-Qaeda, founded in 1988 by Osama bin Laden, carried out the 1998 US embassy bombings in East Africa, the 2000 USS Cole bombing, and the September 11 attacks of 2001, which killed nearly 3,000 people and prompted the US-led "War on Terror" and invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq. The Islamic State, an al-Qaeda offshoot, seized large territories in Iraq and Syria in 2014, declared a caliphate, and directed or inspired attacks in Europe and elsewhere before losing its territory by 2019. Boko Haram's insurgency in Nigeria and al-Shabaab's in Somalia have each killed tens of thousands, the great majority of them Muslims in the affected regions.

Ideas and debates

Analysts debate the relative importance of religious doctrine, political grievances, local conflicts and organisational dynamics in explaining such violence. Classical Islamic military jurisprudence prohibits the deliberate killing of non-combatants, and major Muslim bodies have condemned attacks on civilians, while militant groups advance their own scriptural interpretations that most scholars reject.

Victims and impact

Data sets such as the Global Terrorism Database record that most victims of attacks by Islamist groups have been Muslims in countries including Iraq, Afghanistan, Nigeria, Syria and Pakistan. Counter-terrorism responses have included military campaigns, surveillance and financial controls, and have themselves been debated for their human-rights and civil-liberties effects.
