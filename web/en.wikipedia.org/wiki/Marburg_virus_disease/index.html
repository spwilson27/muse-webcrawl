Marburg virus disease (MVD), formerly known as Marburg haemorrhagic fever, is a severe and often fatal viral haemorrhagic fever caused by the Marburg virus, a member of the filovirus family closely related to the Ebola virus. Its natural reservoir is the Egyptian fruit bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus). There is no approved vaccine or specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive.

History

The disease was first recognised in 1967 when two simultaneous outbreaks occurred among laboratory workers in Marburg and Frankfurt, Germany, and in Belgrade, Yugoslavia (now Serbia), who had handled African green monkeys imported from Uganda; 31 people fell ill and seven died. Outbreaks in Africa have since been documented in Angola (2005, the largest, with 252 cases and 227 deaths), the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya, Uganda, Guinea, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, and Tanzania (2023 and 2025). In 2008 the first exported case in a tourist returning from Uganda was reported in the Netherlands.

Transmission and symptoms

Humans are infected through prolonged exposure to fruit bat roosts or through contact with the blood, secretions, or bodily fluids of infected persons or animals. Person-to-person transmission occurs through direct contact with bodily fluids of the sick, contaminated bedding, or burial practices involving contact with the deceased. After an incubation period of 2–21 days, the illness begins abruptly with high fever, severe headache, muscle aches, and diarrhoea, progressing in severe cases to bleeding from multiple sites, multi-organ failure, and shock. Viral persistence in immune-privileged sites after recovery has been documented, with sexual transmission reported from survivors.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention rests on avoiding bat caves and mines, rigorous infection control in health facilities, and safe burial practices. Treatment is supportive — rehydration, management of symptoms, and treatment of secondary infections. Experimental vaccines and monoclonal antibodies are under development, and during outbreaks candidate vaccines have been used in ring-vaccination trials.
