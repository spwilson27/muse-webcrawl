Coin for Charon

An obol, the customary fare for Charon's ferry across the Styx.

The coin for Charon is the practice, widespread in the ancient Greek and Roman world, of placing a coin in or on the mouth of a dead person before burial. In Greek mythology the coin served as payment—often called Charon's obol—to Charon, the ferryman who carried the souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx into the underworld.

Practice

The customary fare was a low-value bronze or silver obolos or, in Roman times, a small bronze coin such as an as. Coins are found in burials placed in the mouth, in the hand, or over the eyes of the deceased across the Mediterranean world from the 5th century BC into late antiquity. Literary references appear in Aristophanes' The Frogs, where the fare is jokingly put at two obols, and in Lucian and other later authors.

Interpretation

Scholars debate how literally the mythological explanation was believed: the coin may also have served as a viaticum (provision for a journey), as a token sealing the mouth against returning spirits, or as a display item among grave goods. Related coin-deposition customs are known in many other cultures, and the phrase "Charon's obol" survives archaeologically as a term for coins recovered from burials.
