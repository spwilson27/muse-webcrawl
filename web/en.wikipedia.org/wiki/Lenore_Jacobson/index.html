Lenore Jacobson

Lenore F. Jacobson (1922–c. 1990s) was an American elementary school principal and researcher best known as the co-author, with Harvard psychologist Robert Rosenthal, of Pygmalion in the Classroom (1968), the landmark study demonstrating the effect of teacher expectations on student performance — the so-called self-fulfilling prophecy or "Pygmalion effect" in education.

The Oak School experiment

Jacobson was principal of an elementary school in South San Francisco (referred to in the study as "Oak School"). In the experiment, pupils took a nonverbal IQ test presented to teachers as a predictor of intellectual "blooming." Teachers were given the names of a randomly selected group of children said to be on the verge of unusual intellectual growth. In fact, the names were chosen at random. When the children were retested at the end of the school year, the designated "bloomers" — especially in the first and second grades — showed significantly greater IQ gains than their classmates.

Impact and debate

The study suggested that teachers' expectations, communicated through subtle channels such as tone of voice, attention, and encouragement, can shape actual student achievement. Pygmalion in the Classroom became one of the most influential and debated works in educational psychology, prompting extensive replication research and methodological criticism (including questions about the test used and ceiling effects), as well as the complementary concept of the negative "Golem effect" for low expectations.
