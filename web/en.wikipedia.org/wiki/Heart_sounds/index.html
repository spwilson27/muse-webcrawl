Heart sounds

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heart sounds are the noises generated by the beating heart and the resultant flow of blood through it, heard with a stethoscope during auscultation. In healthy adults there are two normal heart sounds, S1 and S2 — often described as a "lub" and a "dub" — produced by the closure of the heart valves. Additional sounds (S3, S4), splitting of the normal sounds, and abnormal sounds such as murmurs, rubs and clicks provide important clinical clues to cardiac disease.

Primary sounds

First heart sound (S1)

S1 marks the beginning of systole and is caused mainly by closure of the atrioventricular valves (mitral and tricuspid) when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure. It is best heard at the cardiac apex and coincides with the carotid pulse.

Second heart sound (S2)

S2 marks the end of systole and the beginning of diastole, caused by closure of the semilunar valves (aortic, A2, and pulmonary, P2). During inspiration A2 and P2 normally separate ("physiological splitting"); fixed or paradoxical splitting suggests pathology such as atrial septal defect or left bundle branch block.

Additional sounds

  • S3 — a low-pitched early diastolic sound from rapid ventricular filling; normal in children and pregnancy, but in older adults a sign of heart failure ("ventricular gallop").
  • S4 — a late diastolic sound from atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle, heard in hypertrophy and hypertension ("atrial gallop").
  • Ejection clicks and the opening snap of mitral stenosis, mid-systolic clicks in mitral valve prolapse, and the pericardial friction rub of pericarditis.

Murmurs

Heart murmurs are prolonged sounds caused by turbulent blood flow, graded on the Levine scale from I (very faint) to VI (audible without the stethoscope touching the chest). Innocent flow murmurs are common in children, while others indicate valve stenosis or regurgitation, septal defects or other structural disease.
