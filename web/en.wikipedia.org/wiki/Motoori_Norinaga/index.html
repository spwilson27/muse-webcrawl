Motoori Norinaga (本居 宣長, 21 June 1730 – 5 November 1801) was a Japanese scholar of kokugaku ("national learning") active during the Edo period. He is conventionally ranked among the "four great scholars" of kokugaku alongside Kamo no Mabuchi, Kada no Azumamaro, and Hirata Atsutane. His life's work was the Kojiki-den, a monumental commentary on the Kojiki (712), Japan's oldest extant chronicle, which occupied him for some 35 years.

Life

Born in Matsusaka in Ise Province (now Mie Prefecture) into a merchant family, Norinaga studied medicine in Kyoto and practised as a physician in his home town while pursuing scholarship in the evenings. A turning point came in 1763 when he met Kamo no Mabuchi, who inspired him to study the ancient Japanese classics through philological methods and to undertake the Kojiki commentary. His school, the Suzunoya, attracted hundreds of students.

Thought

Norinaga sought to strip away what he saw as foreign accretions — Confucian and Buddhist modes of thought, the karagokoro or "Chinese heart" — to recover a pure, native Japanese sensibility embodied in ancient texts. In his literary criticism, notably on The Tale of Genji, he articulated the concept of mono no aware, a sensitive empathy toward the pathos of things, arguing that the purpose of literature was not moral instruction but the expression of feeling. His reading of the Kojiki laid foundations both for modern Shinto theology and for later Japanese nationalism, a legacy that remains debated.

Major works

  • Kojiki-den (Commentary on the Kojiki), 44 volumes, completed 1798
  • Mononoaware ron and essays on The Tale of Genji, including Shibun yōryō
  • Naobi no mitama (The Rectifying Spirit), a statement of his Shinto beliefs
  • Uiyamabumi, an introductory guide for students
