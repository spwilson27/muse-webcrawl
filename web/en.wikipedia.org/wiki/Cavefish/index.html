Cavefish

Cavefish or cave fish is a generic term for fresh and brackish water fish adapted to life in caves and other underground habitats. Related terms are subterranean fish, troglomorphic fish, troglobitic fish and stygobitic fish. More than 200 species are known, found on all continents except Antarctica, in families as diverse as characins, catfishes, loaches, cyprinids and amblyopsids.

Adaptations

Obligate cave dwellers (troglobites) show a characteristic suite of regressive and constructive traits: loss of eyes and pigmentation (many are blind and pinkish-white), alongside enhanced non-visual senses — more sensitive lateral lines, expanded taste buds, and heightened senses of smell and touch — plus slowed metabolism and life-history changes that suit food-scarce environments. The Mexican tetra (Astyanax mexicanus), which exists in both eyed surface and blind cave forms that remain interfertile, is a major model organism for studying these evolutionary changes. The northern cavefish and other members of the North American family Amblyopsidae carry their eggs in their gill chambers (branchial brooding).

Notable species

The widemouth blindcat of Texas aquifer systems, the Alabama cavefish, the golden cave fish (Sinocyclocheilus grahami) of China, the waterfall-climbing cave fish of Thailand (Cryptotora thamicola), which "walks" with tetrapod-like fin movements, and the cave-dwelling species of Madagascar and Croatia illustrate the group's diversity. China has the greatest cavefish diversity of any country.

Conservation

Subterranean habitats are fragile, and many cavefish are known from single cave systems or wells. Groundwater extraction and pollution threaten numerous species; a large proportion of described cavefish are listed as threatened on the IUCN Red List.
