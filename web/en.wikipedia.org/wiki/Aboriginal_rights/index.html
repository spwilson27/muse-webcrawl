Aboriginal rights

Aboriginal rights are the rights held by Indigenous peoples by virtue of their prior occupation of their territories, distinct from rights granted by the state. In common-law jurisdictions the concept encompasses Aboriginal title to land, rights to hunt, fish and gather, self-government claims, and the protection of Indigenous cultures and languages. The doctrine developed most fully in Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the United States.

Canada

In Canada, "Aboriginal rights" is a term of art under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, which recognises and affirms the existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada — First Nations, Inuit and Métis. Landmark judgments include Calder v British Columbia (1973), which confirmed Aboriginal title exists in Canadian law; R v Sparrow (1990), the first section 35 test case; Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997), defining the content and proof of title; and Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia (2014), the first declaration of Aboriginal title to a specific territory.

Australia and elsewhere

Australia recognised native title in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), overturning the enlarged doctrine of terra nullius, and Parliament responded with the Native Title Act 1993. The 1967 referendum empowered the Commonwealth to legislate for Aboriginal people and count them in the census. Internationally, the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) articulates standards for land, consultation and redress, and ILO Convention 169 binds its parties to similar principles.
