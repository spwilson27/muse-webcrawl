Jinn (Arabic: جنّ, jinn), also romanised as djinn or anglicised as genies, are invisible creatures in early Arabian mythology and later Islamic theology. Like humans, they are created with free will, are capable of good and evil deeds, and will face judgment. According to the Quran, jinn were created from a smokeless, scorching fire, distinct from angels, who were created from light, and humans, who were created from clay.

Pre-Islamic background

Belief in jinn pervaded pre-Islamic Arabia, where they were thought to inhabit deserts, ruins, and wild places, to inspire poets, and to be capable of possessing people. The word derives from the Semitic root j-n-n, meaning "to hide" or "to cover", reflecting their invisibility to humans.

In the Quran and Islam

Jinn are mentioned frequently in the Quran, and the seventy-second chapter, Surah Al-Jinn, is named for them. The Quran relates that a group of jinn listened to the Prophet Muhammad reciting the Quran and believed. Iblis, the Islamic devil, is described as one of the jinn who refused God's command to bow before Adam. King Solomon (Sulayman) was said to command hosts of jinn who built and dived for him. In Islamic belief, each person is accompanied by a jinn companion called a qarin.

Types of jinn

Later folklore classified jinn into types: the powerful and rebellious marid; the fiery ifrit; the carrion-eating ghoul (ghul) of graveyards and deserts; and the shayatin, devils who tempt humans to sin. In the One Thousand and One Nights, jinn appear in famous tales such as Aladdin and the magic lamp (in translation, "genie" derives through French from Latin genius but was applied to jinn).

In folk belief and culture

Belief in jinn remains widespread across the Muslim world, where they are invoked in explanations of illness, misfortune, and possession, and are the object of amulets, exorcisms (ruqya), and protective verses. Jinn have become global figures of popular culture through translations of the Arabian Nights, films, and games.
