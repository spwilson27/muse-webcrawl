Geographical determinism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geographical determinism, also known as environmental determinism, is the doctrine that the physical environment — particularly climate and terrain — determines or strongly shapes human culture, social development, and history. It stands in contrast to possibilism, the view associated with Paul Vidal de la Blache and Lucien Febvre that the environment sets limits and offers opportunities which cultures exploit in different ways.

History

Ideas of climatic influence on peoples appear in ancient writers such as Hippocrates and Ibn Khaldun, whose Muqaddimah linked civilization to climate zones. The doctrine was systematized in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by geographers including Friedrich Ratzel, Ellen Churchill Semple (author of Influences of Geographic Environment, 1911), and Ellsworth Huntington, who argued that temperate climates produced energetic, advanced civilizations while tropical climates induced indolence.

Decline and criticism

Environmental determinism fell from favor after the Second World War. Critics charged that it was simplistic, neglected human agency and technology, and had been used to rationalize colonialism and racial hierarchies. Mainstream geography replaced it first with possibilism and later with more nuanced approaches examining reciprocal human–environment relations.

Modern revivals

Modified versions have reappeared in popular and scholarly work. Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) argued that continental geography — the east–west axis of Eurasia, its domesticable species, and disease pools — explains broad patterns of global inequality; Diamond describes his position as emphasizing environmental factors rather than strict determinism, and the book has been criticized by anthropologists and historians for underplaying politics and culture. Development economists such as Jeffrey Sachs have also studied how climate, disease burden, and access to coasts affect economic growth.
