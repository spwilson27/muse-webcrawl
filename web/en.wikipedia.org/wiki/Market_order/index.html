Market order

A market order is an order to buy or sell a stock or other financial instrument immediately at the best available current price. Market orders are the most basic kind of trade order: because the trader does not specify a price, the order is executed as quickly as possible, taking whatever price the market offers, so long as the security is actively traded.

Characteristics

The main advantage of a market order is certainty of execution; the main disadvantage is uncertainty of price. In a fast-moving or thinly traded market, the execution price may differ substantially from the last quoted price — a phenomenon known as slippage. For very liquid, large-capitalisation stocks the difference is usually a fraction of a cent, but for illiquid securities the risk is greater.

Comparison with other order types

A market order contrasts with a limit order, which specifies the maximum price a buyer will pay or the minimum a seller will accept and therefore may not execute at all, and with a stop order, which becomes a market order once a trigger price is reached. Orders may also carry time-in-force instructions such as day orders or good-til-cancelled orders.

Use in practice

Market orders suit investors who value immediate execution over small price differences, and are common in highly liquid markets. Brokers and exchanges warn against using them at the market open or in volatile conditions, when spreads are wide and prices gap. Placing genuine market orders is a legitimate activity, unlike the fictitious orders used in market manipulation schemes such as spoofing. The aggregate flow of market and limit orders determines the observable market trend.
