Graveyard

A graveyard adjoining a parish church.

A graveyard is a place where the remains of dead people are buried or otherwise interred. The word graveyard is often used interchangeably with cemetery, although in common usage a graveyard is typically smaller and associated with a church — in which case it may also be called a churchyard — while a cemetery is a larger, often municipally or privately managed burial ground not necessarily tied to a place of worship.

History

Burial grounds have accompanied settled communities since prehistory. In medieval Europe the dead were usually buried in the churchyard surrounding the parish church, with the most prestigious plots nearest the altar. Overcrowding and public-health concerns in rapidly growing industrial cities led, from the early 19th century, to the creation of large garden cemeteries on urban outskirts, such as Père Lachaise in Paris (opened 1804) and the "Magnificent Seven" cemeteries of London. These landscaped grounds became places of public recreation as well as burial.

Gravestones and customs

Graves in graveyards are commonly marked by a headstone or gravestone recording the name of the deceased, dates of birth and death, and often an epitaph or religious symbol. Customs vary widely by culture and faith: Jewish graveyards traditionally use simple markers and visitors leave small stones on graves; Islamic burial grounds orient graves toward Mecca; many Asian traditions combine burial with ancestor veneration. In England, the demotic term "graveyard shift" for night work is said to derive from the churchyard watch once kept against body snatchers, though the phrase's origins are debated.

Conservation

Historic graveyards are valued as habitats for lichens, wildflowers and wildlife, and as archives of local history. Genealogists and volunteer projects record inscriptions before weathering makes them illegible.
