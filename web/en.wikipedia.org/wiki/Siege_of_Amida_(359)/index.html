Siege of Amida (359)

Siege of Amida
Date359 AD (about 73 days)
LocationAmida, Roman Mesopotamia (modern Diyarbakır, Turkey)
ResultSasanian victory; city captured and sacked
BelligerentsRoman Empire vs. Sasanian Empire

The siege of Amida was an engagement of the Roman–Persian wars in 359 AD, in which the Sasanian Persian army of King Shapur II captured the strongly fortified Roman city of Amida in northern Mesopotamia after a siege of some 73 days. The siege is famous because the historian Ammianus Marcellinus, an eyewitness serving on the staff inside the city, left a vivid first-hand account of it in his Res Gestae.

The basalt walls of Amida, overlooking the Tigris valley.

The siege

Shapur II invaded Roman Mesopotamia in 359 with a large army stiffened by allied contingents, including the Chionites under their king Grumbates, whose son was killed by a ballista bolt while approaching the walls to demand surrender — an incident Ammianus describes with great pathos. The Persians assaulted the walls with siege towers, rams and artillery, while the defenders replied with scorpions and ballistae and made repeated sorties. A plague inside the city, caused by unburied corpses, weakened the garrison. The siege ended when a Persian siege mound reached the walls and a tower collapsed, allowing the attackers to break in; the city was sacked, and many inhabitants were deported.

Ammianus himself escaped by night through a postern gate just before the fall. The loss of Amida was a severe blow to Roman prestige in the east and a prelude to the Emperor Julian's ill-fated Persian expedition of 363, after which Rome ceded Amida's region — including Nisibis and Singara — to Persia in the Treaty of 363.
