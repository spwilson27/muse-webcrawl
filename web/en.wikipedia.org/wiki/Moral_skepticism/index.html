Moral skepticism

Moral skepticism (or moral scepticism in British English) is a class of metaethical theories all of whose members deny that anyone has moral knowledge. More broadly, moral skeptics hold views ranging from the denial that moral propositions can be true, to denial that moral judgments are rationally justified, to denial that anyone is ever justified in believing moral claims. Moral skepticism is distinct from moral nihilism, though some forms are closely related, and distinct from moral relativism, which is often a positive doctrine about the truth-conditions of moral claims.

Forms

Philosophers commonly distinguish several varieties:

  • Moral error theory: moral judgments aim at truth but are systematically false, because there are no moral properties or facts (J. L. Mackie's "argument from queerness").
  • Non-cognitivism: moral utterances are not assertions at all but expressions of attitude or prescriptions (emotivism, prescriptivism, expressivism) — so they cannot constitute knowledge.
  • Epistemological moral skepticism: even if moral facts exist, no one is justified in believing any moral proposition, sometimes on the basis of evolutionary debunking arguments or persistent moral disagreement.
  • Pyrrhonian moral skepticism: suspends judgment on all moral questions, following the ancient Pyrrhonist practice of epoché in response to equipollent opposing arguments.

Arguments and responses

Key arguments include Mackie's argument from relativity (widespread moral disagreement) and from queerness (the strangeness of objectively prescriptive facts), Gilbert Harman's explanatory challenge (moral facts seem unnecessary to explain moral observations), and Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument. Defenders of moral knowledge respond with moral realism (rational intuitionism, Cornell realism), constructivism, and companions-in-guilt arguments noting that parallel skeptical arguments would undermine epistemic norms themselves.
