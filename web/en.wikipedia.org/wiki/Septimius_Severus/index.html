Septimius Severus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Arch of Septimius Severus in the Roman Forum, Rome]

Septimius Severus (AD 145–211; Lucius Septimius Severus) was Roman emperor from 193 to 211, founder of the Severan dynasty. A senator from Leptis Magna in Africa — the first emperor from the provinces' non-Italian stock, his Punic accent remarked at Rome — he seized power in the civil wars after Commodus' murder (the Year of the Five Emperors, 193), defeated his rivals Pescennius Niger (194) and Clodius Albinus (197), and refounded the monarchy on openly military lines: the legions enlarged, their pay doubled and privileges extended (marriage legalised), the Praetorian Guard reconstituted from his own Danubian troops. His wars annexed northern Mesopotamia, sacked Ctesiphon (198), campaigned in Africa and Arabia, and ended in Britain, where he rebuilt Hadrian's Wall, invaded Caledonia and died at York (Eboracum) in 211 — advising his sons, by the tradition, to 'enrich the soldiers and despise the rest'.

The military monarchy

Severus' settlement was candid where Augustus' had been veiled: the emperor was the army's paymaster and commander, and the Senate a decorative casualty (27 senators executed after Albinus' fall). His building proclaimed the dynasty — the Arch of Septimius Severus in the Forum, the Septizodium, Leptis Magna transformed into a marble home-city — while his wife Julia Domna, from a Syrian priestly family, presided over a court of lawyers (Papinian, Ulpian: Roman law's classical summit falls in this reign) and philosophers. Jurists, not senators, now ran the empire Severus had made.

Britain and succession

The Caledonian war (208–211), waged with his sons Caracalla and Geta as a school and a warning to the north, devastated the lowlands without annexing them; Severus died at York with the campaign unfinished, and the brothers' co-rule collapsed within the year into Geta's murder — in their mother's arms, by Caracalla's order. The dynasty Severus founded lasted until 235, its later emperors (Caracalla of the universal citizenship edict, Elagabalus, Severus Alexander) increasingly creatures of the army he had taught its power.
