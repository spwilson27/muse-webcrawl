Heart of Neolithic Orkney

The Heart of Neolithic Orkney is a group of Neolithic monuments on the Mainland of the Orkney Islands, Scotland. Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999, the group comprises four principal sites — the chambered tomb of Maeshowe, the stone circles of the Stones of Stenness and the Ring of Brodgar, and the remarkably preserved stone-built village of Skara Brae — together with associated standing stones such as the Barnhouse Stone and the Watch Stone.

The sites

Skara Brae is a village of stone houses, complete with stone furniture — beds, dressers, hearths and drains — preserved under sand dunes until a storm exposed it in 1850. Occupied from about 3180 to 2500 BCE, it is often called the "Scottish Pompeii" and predates the Egyptian pyramids and Stonehenge.

Maeshowe is a large chambered cairn and passage grave aligned so that the setting sun at the winter solstice shines down its entrance passage into the chamber. Inside is a famous collection of runic graffiti carved by Norse visitors in the 12th century.

The Stones of Stenness originally formed a circle of up to 12 stones, of which four survive, one nearly 6 metres tall. The Ring of Brodgar, a great stone circle 104 metres in diameter, retains 27 of its original 60 or so stones, standing on a narrow isthmus between the lochs of Stenness and Harray.

Significance

The monuments date principally to between about 3200 and 2200 BCE. Together with the nearby Barnhouse settlement and the vast ceremonial complex at the Ness of Brodgar — excavated from 2003 and including some of the largest roofed Neolithic structures in Britain — they testify to a wealthy, organised farming society at the centre of innovations, including Grooved ware pottery, that spread through Neolithic Britain. UNESCO inscribed the sites as outstanding testimony to the cultural achievements of the Neolithic peoples of northern Europe.

Management

The sites are cared for by Historic Environment Scotland. The Heart of Neolithic Orkney was one of the first Scottish World Heritage Sites and is managed under a plan addressing tourism pressure, coastal erosion (a serious threat to Skara Brae) and climate change.
