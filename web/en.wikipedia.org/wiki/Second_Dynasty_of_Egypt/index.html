The Second Dynasty of ancient Egypt (Dynasty II, c. 2890 – c. 2686 BC) is the latter of the two dynasties of the Egyptian Early Dynastic Period, when the seat of government was centred at Thinis. It is most known for its last ruler, Khasekhemwy, but is otherwise one of the most obscure periods in Egyptian history.

Though archaeological evidence of the dynasty is rather limited, contrasting data from the First and Third Dynasties indicates important institutional and economic developments during Dynasty II.

History

The founder of the dynasty was Hotepsekhemwy, whose Horus name may commemorate the reconciliation ("the two powers are at peace") of rival factions after the troubled end of the First Dynasty. His successors included Raneb (Nebre) and Nynetjer, under whom the administration of the country appears to have grown more complex.

The middle of the dynasty is poorly documented. Kings such as Weneg, Senedj and Peribsen are known from fragmentary sources. Peribsen is notable for replacing the falcon of Horus atop his serekh with the Set animal, a change sometimes interpreted as evidence of religious or political division. His successor Khasekhemwy bore both Horus and Seth above his serekh and reunified the country, apparently after civil conflict in the north; he built extensively at Hierakonpolis and Abydos, and is regarded as the last king of the dynasty.

Rulers

The pharaohs of the Second Dynasty ruled for about 204 years, from c. 2890 to c. 2686 BC. Lists preserved in later king lists — the Abydos, Saqqara and Turin lists — preserve the names, though their order and identities remain debated. Known rulers include Hotepsekhemwy, Raneb, Nynetjer, Weneg, Senedj, Peribsen, Sekhemib and Khasekhemwy.
