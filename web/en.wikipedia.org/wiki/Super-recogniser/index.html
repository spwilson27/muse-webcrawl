Super-recogniser

Super-recognisers can remember and identify faces seen only briefly, even years later.

A super-recogniser is a person with exceptionally high ability to recognise faces. The term was coined in a 2009 paper by researchers at Harvard University and University College London, who estimated that roughly 1–2% of the population may qualify as super-recognisers. They represent the opposite end of the spectrum of face recognition ability from people with prosopagnosia ("face blindness").

Super-recognisers can remember faces they have encountered only once, sometimes years earlier, and can match identities across changes in age, hairstyle, lighting, and image quality. Their ability appears to be strongly heritable and largely specific to faces rather than a general advantage in memory or perception.

Testing

Researchers typically identify super-recognisers using standardised tests such as the Cambridge Face Memory Test (CFMT), the Glasgow Face Matching Test, and more demanding tests of long-term face memory and matching across different images of the same person. Performance well above the average range on several such tests is used to confirm the classification, because high scores on a single test can arise by chance.

Applications

Police forces, most notably London's Metropolitan Police, have employed super-recognisers to identify suspects in CCTV footage. The Met's super-recognisers were credited with identifying large numbers of suspects following the 2011 England riots, matching faces in hours of video footage against custody photographs. Their use has raised debate about the reliability of human face matching in legal proceedings, the risk of misidentification, and how the ability should be validated and deployed alongside automated facial recognition systems.
