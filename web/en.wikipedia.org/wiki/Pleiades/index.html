The Pleiades (/ˈpliːədiːz, ˈplaɪ-/), also known as the Seven Sisters and Messier 45, is an open star cluster containing middle-aged, hot B-type stars in the north-west of the constellation Taurus. At a distance of about 444 light-years, it is among the nearest star clusters to Earth. It is the nearest Messier object to Earth, and is the most obvious star cluster to the naked eye in the night sky. It is also observed to house the reflection nebula IC 349, the Barnard's Merope Nebula.

The cluster contains 1,000 statistically confirmed members, excluding unresolved binary stars, and is dominated by hot, blue, luminous stars that formed within the last 100 million years. Reflection nebulae around the brightest stars were once thought to be leftover material from their formation, but are now known to be an unrelated dust cloud in the interstellar medium through which the stars are passing.

Names and mythology

The name Pleiades comes from Ancient Greek Πλειάδες. It probably derives from plein (πλεῖν, "to sail") because of the cluster's importance in delineating the sailing season in the Mediterranean Sea. In Greek mythology, the Pleiades were the seven daughters of the Titan Atlas and the Oceanid Pleione: Maia, Electra, Taygete, Alcyone, Celaeno, Sterope, and Merope. Atlas was forced to hold up the heavens, and Orion pursued the sisters, so Zeus transformed them first into doves, and then into stars.

The cluster is known by many names in other cultures, including Subaru in Japanese (reflected in the logo of the automobile manufacturer), Kṛttikā in Hindu astrology, and Sorayya in Persian.

Observational history

The Pleiades have been known since antiquity and are mentioned in works by Hesiod, Homer, and in the Bible. Galileo Galilei was the first to observe the cluster through a telescope, publishing a sketch in 1610 showing 36 stars. Charles Messier catalogued it as M45 in 1769. Modern measurements from the Hipparcos satellite and the Hubble Space Telescope, and later Gaia data, have settled the distance at roughly 136 parsecs, resolving a long-standing controversy.

Composition and age

Astronomers estimate the cluster's age at between 75 and 150 million years. The cluster core radius is about 8 light-years and its tidal radius about 43 light-years. Brown dwarfs make up a significant fraction of its objects by number, though only a small percentage of its total mass, which is estimated at roughly 800 solar masses.

Future

The Pleiades will not survive as a cluster indefinitely: gravitational interactions will disperse its stars over the next roughly 250 million years. The cluster is currently passing through a cloud of interstellar dust, producing the characteristic blue reflection nebulosity visible in photographs.
