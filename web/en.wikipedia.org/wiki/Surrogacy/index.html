Surrogacy

Surrogacy is an arrangement, often supported by a legal agreement, whereby a woman (the surrogate or gestational carrier) agrees to become pregnant, carry the pregnancy to term, and give birth to a child or children for another person or persons, who are or will become the parent(s) of the child. People pursue surrogacy when pregnancy is medically impossible or dangerous, or for male couples and single men wishing to have a child.

Surrogacy arrangements vary widely in law and practice between countries

Types

In gestational surrogacy, the embryo is created by in vitro fertilisation from the intended parents' or donors' gametes, so the surrogate has no genetic link to the child. In traditional surrogacy, the surrogate's own egg is used, making her the genetic mother. Arrangements may be commercial, where the surrogate is paid beyond expenses, or altruistic, where she is not.

Legal status

Law varies widely: some jurisdictions, including several US states, permit and regulate commercial surrogacy; others, such as the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, permit only altruistic surrogacy; and several countries, including France, Germany and Spain, prohibit surrogacy altogether. Cross-border arrangements have produced notable cases of children left stateless or parentless in law, prompting calls for international regulation through the Hague Conference.

Ethical debate

Supporters emphasise reproductive autonomy and the ability of infertile people to have genetically related children. Critics raise concerns about exploitation of economically vulnerable women, commodification of reproduction, and the welfare and rights of the child and the surrogate.
