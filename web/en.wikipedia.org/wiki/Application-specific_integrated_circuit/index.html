Application-specific integrated circuit

An application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) is an integrated circuit (IC) chip customised for a particular use, rather than intended for general-purpose use. For example, a chip designed to run in a digital voice recorder or a high-efficiency video codec is an ASIC. ASICs sit between general-purpose processors (such as CPUs and GPUs) and fully programmable devices such as FPGAs: they implement a fixed function in silicon, trading flexibility for speed, energy efficiency, and low per-unit cost at high volumes.

An ASIC die: dedicated logic blocks optimised for one task achieve high efficiency.

Design

Modern ASICs are designed with hardware description languages such as Verilog or VHDL, using libraries of standard cells, memory blocks, and licensed intellectual-property cores (for example processor cores, SerDes, and memory controllers); these are called semi-custom or standard-cell designs, in contrast to full-custom designs in which transistors are laid out by hand for maximum performance. Fabrication requires photolithographic masks for each layer, whose non-recurring engineering (NRE) costs can run to millions of dollars on advanced process nodes, making ASICs economical mainly for high-volume products.

Applications

ASICs are ubiquitous: image and signal processors in cameras and phones, modem and networking chips, automotive controllers, and cryptographic accelerators. Bitcoin mining migrated from CPUs to GPUs to FPGAs and finally to mining ASICs, which compute SHA-256 hashes orders of magnitude more efficiently than general hardware. In artificial intelligence, Google's Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), announced in 2016, are ASICs designed specifically for neural-network matrix operations, and many cloud providers and device makers now build custom AI accelerators. Apple's system-on-chip designs (such as the M2) use ASIC-like domain-specific blocks for media encoding and machine learning.

Comparison with FPGAs

FPGAs can be programmed after manufacture and have low initial cost, making them suited to prototyping, low volumes, and fields needing field updates; ASICs offer lower unit cost, lower power, and higher performance once volumes justify mask costs. Structured ASICs and gate arrays occupy intermediate positions, pre-manufacturing most layers and customising only a few metal layers.
