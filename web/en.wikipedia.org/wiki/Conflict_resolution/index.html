Conflict resolution

[Image: A mediator seated between two disputing parties, facilitating discussion]

Conflict resolution is the field concerned with the methods and processes involved in facilitating the peaceful ending of conflict and retribution. It encompasses informal efforts between individuals as well as structured processes — negotiation, mediation, arbitration and diplomacy — used by groups, organisations and states. A related term, conflict management, refers to the ongoing handling of conflicts that may not be fully resolvable.

Concepts

Scholars distinguish between settling a conflict (reaching an agreement that ends overt dispute), resolving it (addressing underlying causes so the conflict does not recur), and transforming it (changing the relationships and structures that produced it). Norwegian sociologist Johan Galtung distinguished "negative peace" — the absence of direct violence — from "positive peace", the presence of just and equitable social conditions, and identified structural and cultural forms of violence beyond direct physical harm.

Common causes of conflict analysed in the literature include incompatible goals, competition over scarce resources, differing values and identities, poor communication, and perceived injustice. Escalation dynamics — mistrust, stereotyping, and retaliation cycles — can make conflicts self-sustaining even after the original issue fades.

Methods

Negotiation is direct discussion between the parties. In mediation, a neutral third party facilitates communication and helps the parties craft their own agreement, without power to impose one. In arbitration, the third party hears the case and renders a decision, which may be binding. Courts and litigation provide a formal, rights-based route, generally more adversarial and costly. Restorative justice processes bring victims, offenders and community members together to address harm and agree on repair. At the international level, methods include diplomacy, peacekeeping, sanctions, and formal tribunals.

Approaches are often arrayed along a spectrum from informal and interest-based (negotiation, mediation) to formal and rights- or power-based (litigation, strikes, war). Choosing among them depends on the relationship between the parties, the stakes, power balances, and whether an ongoing relationship must be preserved.

Applications

Conflict resolution is applied in workplaces, schools (peer mediation programmes), families, community disputes, environmental and land-use controversies, and international relations, including post-conflict peacebuilding, truth and reconciliation commissions, and disarmament and reintegration programmes. It is an academic discipline taught in peace and conflict studies and professional programmes in law, social work and public policy, drawing on psychology, sociology, political science and game theory.

Criticism

Critics note that an emphasis on "resolution" can pressure weaker parties to accept unjust settlements, that power asymmetries are not cured by neutral facilitation, and that some conflicts involve genuinely incompatible values. Others argue that conflict itself can be productive — driving social change — and that the goal should be managing it constructively rather than eliminating it.
