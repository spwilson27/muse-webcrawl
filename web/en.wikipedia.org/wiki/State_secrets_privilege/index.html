State secrets privilege

The state secrets privilege is an evidentiary rule created by United States legal precedent. Under it, the government may prevent the disclosure of information in legal proceedings if disclosure would harm national security or foreign relations. When the privilege is successfully invoked, the protected evidence is excluded, which can result in the dismissal of the case.

Origin: United States v. Reynolds

The privilege was formally recognised by the Supreme Court in United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1 (1953). Widows of civilian engineers killed in the 1948 crash of a B-29 bomber sued for the accident report; the Air Force claimed the report contained military secrets. The Court upheld the claim without examining the document. When the report was declassified decades later, it was found to contain evidence of negligence rather than secrets, fuelling longstanding criticism that the privilege had been misused; the widows' later petitions for redress ultimately failed.

Application

The privilege must be formally claimed by the head of the responsible government department after personal consideration. Courts may dismiss a case where the plaintiff cannot make a prima facie case without the privileged evidence, or where the subject matter of the suit is itself a state secret. The doctrine was invoked prominently in post-9/11 litigation, including challenges to extraordinary rendition (such as El-Masri v. United States) and warrantless surveillance programmes.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue the privilege is too easily abused to shield government misconduct and deny plaintiffs a remedy; proposed legislation (the State Secrets Protection Act) would have required judges to review the actual evidence in camera. The doctrine has roots in earlier case law including Totten v. United States (1875), which barred suits based on secret espionage contracts.
