Lobsters are a family (Nephropidae) of large marine crustaceans. They have long bodies with muscular tails and live in burrows or crevices on the sea floor. Three pairs of their five pairs of legs have claws; in the true lobsters the first pair is greatly enlarged. Economically important species include the American lobster (Homarus americanus) of the North Atlantic and the European lobster (Homarus gammarus).

Anatomy and biology

Lobsters are invertebrates with a hard protective exoskeleton, which they must molt to grow. They can live for many decades—some estimates suggest 45–50 years in the wild—and continue growing throughout life. Lobsters are mostly nocturnal scavengers and predators, feeding on fish, mollusks, other crustaceans, and algae. Their blue-green blood is based on copper-containing haemocyanin. The familiar red color appears only after cooking, when heat denatures the proteins binding the pigment astaxanthin.

As food

Lobster is a prized seafood, eaten steamed or boiled and served in dishes such as lobster bisque, lobster roll, and lobster Thermidor. In colonial New England, lobster was so abundant it was considered poverty food, fed to servants and prisoners; today it commands premium prices. Lobsters are caught in baited traps, and fisheries are regulated with size limits and protection of egg-bearing females. People with shellfish allergy must avoid lobster, as with other crustaceans such as shrimp and crab; lobster is also a feature of coastal cuisines around the world, though it is not part of Philippine cuisine traditions in the way other shellfish are.

Fisheries

The American lobster fishery in Maine and Atlantic Canada is among the most valuable single-species fisheries in the world. Management measures include trap limits, escape vents for juveniles, and V-notching of egg-bearing females. Spiny lobsters (Palinuridae), which lack large claws, support major fisheries in the Caribbean and Australia but are only distantly related to true lobsters.

Longevity research

Lobsters express telomerase throughout life, which has prompted popular claims of biological immortality; in reality, mortality rises with size because molting becomes ever more energetically costly.
