Soviet literature

Soviet literature is the literature produced in the Soviet Union (and in the Russian SFSR in particular) between the October Revolution of 1917 and the dissolution of the USSR in 1991. It encompasses work in Russian and in the many other languages of the Soviet republics, and is characterised by its complex relationship with state ideology: after an experimental flourishing in the 1920s, literature was subordinated to the doctrine of socialist realism proclaimed in 1934, enforced through the Union of Soviet Writers and the censorship apparatus of Glavlit.

The 1920s

The first Soviet decade saw a profusion of groups and styles — the Serapion Brothers, the Formalists, the proletarian writers of RAPP — and major works including Isaac Babel's Red Cavalry, Mikhail Bulgakov's early fiction, the satire of Ilf and Petrov, and the poetry of Vladimir Mayakovsky, Anna Akhmatova, Osip Mandelstam and Boris Pasternak.

Socialist realism

The First Congress of Soviet Writers (1934), dominated by Maxim Gorky and party ideologue Andrei Zhdanov, codified socialist realism: literature should depict reality "in its revolutionary development", with positive heroes and an optimistic, party-minded outlook. Canonical examples include Nikolai Ostrovsky's How the Steel Was Tempered and Mikhail Sholokhov's And Quiet Flows the Don, which won the Nobel Prize in 1965. Many writers perished in the Great Terror or were silenced.

Thaw, dissidence and late Soviet writing

After Stalin's death in 1953, the "Thaw" allowed bolder work: Alexander Solzhenitsyn's One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich (1962) was published with Khrushchev's approval, though Solzhenitsyn was later expelled from the USSR (1974). Unpublishable work circulated in samizdat or appeared abroad (tamizdat), including Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita and Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago. Official literature of the stagnation era was balanced by village prose (Valentin Rasputin, Vasily Shukshin) and the poetry of Yevgeny Yevtushenko and Bella Akhmadulina. Under glasnost after 1986, long-suppressed works were finally published in the USSR.
