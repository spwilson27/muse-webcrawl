Japonisme

A French poster from the 1890s showing clear Japanese woodblock influence

Japonisme is the French term for the influence of Japanese art and aesthetics on Western art and design, which flourished after Japan opened to trade in the 1850s. Coined by the French art critic Philippe Burty in 1872, it describes the craze for all things Japanese that swept Europe and North America in the late nineteenth century.

Origins

Following Commodore Perry's expeditions of 1853–54 and the 1858 treaties, Japanese goods — woodblock prints (ukiyo-e), ceramics, lacquerware, fans, and textiles — flooded Western markets. Japanese exhibits at the international exhibitions in London (1862) and Paris (1867, 1878, 1889) were sensations. Dealers such as Siegfried Bing in Paris promoted Japanese art, and his journal Le Japon Artistique (1888–91) spread the aesthetic.

Influence on art

Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painters absorbed Japanese compositional devices: flat planes of color, cropped and asymmetrical compositions, high viewpoints, and bold outlines. Mary Cassatt, Edgar Degas, Claude Monet (who collected over 200 Japanese prints), and Vincent van Gogh — who copied prints by Hiroshige — all show the influence. James McNeill Whistler's nocturnes and arrangements in color reflect Japanese restraint, while the Art Nouveau movement drew heavily on Japanese line and nature motifs.

Legacy

Japonisme also shaped poster art, book illustration, interior decoration, and garden design. Though the vogue faded by the early twentieth century, its absorption into modern art's vocabulary of flatness and design made it one of the most consequential cross-cultural exchanges in art history.
