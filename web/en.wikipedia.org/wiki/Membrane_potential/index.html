Membrane potential

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The membrane potential is the difference in electric potential between the inside and outside of a cell, arising from unequal distributions of ions across the cell membrane and the membrane's selective permeability. In a resting neuron it is about −70 millivolts (inside negative), maintained by the sodium–potassium pump at the cost of ATP.

Graded changes in membrane potential integrate incoming signals at synapses; if threshold is crossed, the stereotyped spike of the action potential fires. The quantitative theory — Nernst and Goldman equations for the equilibrium and resting potentials, and the Hodgkin–Huxley model of the spike — made electrophysiology a predictive science (Nobel Prize, 1963).

All cells have membrane potentials, not only excitable ones: they drive nutrient uptake in bacteria and fungi, power the sperm's journey, and, in the proton gradients across mitochondrial membranes, drive the synthesis of ATP itself (see cellular respiration) — the deepest unity between the bioelectricity of nerves and the energetics of metabolism.
