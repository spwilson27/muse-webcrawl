Urban planning

A planned urban layout, showing the interaction of land use, transport and public space.

Urban planning (also called town planning, city planning, or urban design in some contexts) is a technical and political process focused on the development and design of land use and the built environment, including air, water, and the infrastructure passing into and out of urban areas, such as transportation, communications, and distribution networks, and their accessibility.

Traditionally, urban planning followed a top-down approach in master planning the physical layout of human settlements. The primary concern was the public welfare, which included considerations of efficiency, sanitation, protection and use of the environment, as well as effects of the master plans on the social and economic activities.

History

Planned cities existed in the ancient world: the grid-planned cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation, the Hippodamian grid of Greek colonies, and Roman military camps and towns all imposed ordered layouts. In the Islamic and Chinese worlds, capitals such as Baghdad and Chang'an were also laid out to deliberate plans.

Modern urban planning emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in response to the overcrowding, pollution and disease of industrial cities. Movements such as the Garden City movement of Ebenezer Howard, the City Beautiful movement, and later the modernist planning of Le Corbusier each proposed new models for organising urban life. After the Second World War, planning became a standard function of government in most developed countries.

Theories and practice

Contemporary planning practice deals with zoning and land-use regulation, transport planning, housing, environmental protection, heritage conservation, and economic development. Since the 1960s, critics such as Jane Jacobs have argued for more participatory, mixed-use, and incremental approaches, and collaborative planning has become influential in planning theory.
