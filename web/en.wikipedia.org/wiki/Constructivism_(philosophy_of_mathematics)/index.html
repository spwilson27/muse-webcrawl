Constructivism (philosophy of mathematics)

Constructivism is a philosophy of mathematics that holds that mathematical objects must be (in principle) constructible in order to be considered to exist. To prove that an object exists, the constructivist requires a procedure for constructing the object, rather than an indirect proof that its non-existence would be contradictory. Consequently, constructive mathematics rejects the unrestricted law of the excluded middle and proofs by contradiction of existence claims.

Principles

A statement such as "there exists an x with property P" is acceptable constructively only when accompanied by a method to find such an x. A disjunction "P or Q" asserts that we know which disjunct holds. Negation is interpreted as a construction turning any proof of the statement into an absurdity. Real numbers, functions and sets are accepted only insofar as they can be given by effective rules (for example, computable Cauchy sequences with a modulus of convergence).

Schools

  • Intuitionism, founded by L. E. J. Brouwer, grounds mathematics in mental construction and rejects completed infinities; formalized by Arend Heyting in intuitionistic logic.
  • Constructive analysis, developed by Errett Bishop in Foundations of Constructive Analysis (1967), rebuilds large parts of analysis with computational content while avoiding Brouwer's controversial axioms.
  • Russian constructivism (Markov school) identifies constructible objects with computable ones under Church's thesis.
  • Finitism and predicativism impose still stricter limits, rejecting impredicative definitions.

Consequences and significance

Within constructive mathematics, some classical theorems fail or require extra hypotheses: not every bounded monotone sequence of rationals converges to a constructible limit, and the intermediate value theorem holds only in approximate form. The gain is computational meaning: a constructive proof can, in principle, be converted by the Curry–Howard correspondence into a computer program, a connection exploited in type theory and proof assistants such as Coq and Agda.
