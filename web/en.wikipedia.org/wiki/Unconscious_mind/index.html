Unconscious mind

Unconscious mind
FieldPsychology, psychoanalysis, cognitive science
Key figuresSigmund Freud, Carl Jung, Pierre Janet
Related conceptsSubconscious, preconscious, repression, cognitive unconscious
The iceberg metaphor: only a small part of the mind is conscious.

The unconscious mind (or the unconscious) consists of processes in the mind that occur automatically and are not available to introspection. Although these processes exist beneath the surface of conscious awareness, they are thought to exert an effect on conscious thought and behaviour. Empirical evidence suggests that unconscious phenomena include repressed feelings and desires, memories too painful to recall, automatic skills, and subliminal perceptions.

The concept was popularised by the Austrian neurologist and psychoanalyst Sigmund Freud, who placed the unconscious at the centre of his theory of the mind. In modern cognitive psychology, researchers more often speak of the "cognitive unconscious" — the mental processes that influence judgement and behaviour without entering awareness.

History

Ideas resembling the unconscious appear in ancient Hindu texts, in the writings of thinkers such as Spinoza and Leibniz, and in nineteenth-century German philosophy, notably in the work of Arthur Schopenhauer and Eduard von Hartmann, whose Philosophy of the Unconscious (1869) was a bestseller. The French psychologist Pierre Janet developed the related notion of the subconscious in his work on hysteria and dissociation.

Freud's theory

In Freud's psychoanalytic theory, the unconscious mind is a repository of socially unacceptable ideas, wishes and desires, traumatic memories, and painful emotions that have been pushed out of awareness by the mechanism of psychological repression. Freud compared the mind to an iceberg: the conscious mind is the visible tip, the preconscious lies just below the surface, and the vast unconscious bulk remains hidden. He held that the contents of the unconscious could be revealed through dreams, slips of the tongue ("Freudian slips"), free association, and the interpretation offered in psychoanalytic treatment. In his later structural model, the unconscious spanned parts of the id, ego, and superego.

Jung's collective unconscious

Carl Jung distinguished the personal unconscious, formed from an individual's own repressed or forgotten experiences, from the collective unconscious: a deeper layer shared by all human beings and populated by universal inherited patterns he called archetypes.

Cognitive psychology

Contemporary cognitive science accepts that much mental processing — perception, memory retrieval, language parsing, motor control, and many forms of learning and judgement — proceeds outside awareness. Experimental phenomena such as priming, implicit memory in amnesic patients, and blindsight provide evidence for sophisticated unconscious processing, though the powerful, symbol-laden unconscious of classical psychoanalysis remains scientifically controversial.
