Scopus

Producer: Elsevier
Type: Bibliographic database
Coverage: 1966–present (with pre-1996 backfiles)
Disciplines: Science, technology, medicine, social sciences, arts and humanities
Website: scopus.com

Scopus is an abstract and citation database launched by academic publisher Elsevier in November 2004. It indexes peer-reviewed literature including scientific journals, books, and conference proceedings, and provides citation tracking tools widely used in bibliometrics and university rankings. Along with Clarivate's Web of Science, it is one of the two dominant citation indexes in academic research evaluation.

Coverage

Scopus indexes content from more than 7,000 publishers, covering over 25,000 active titles (journals, book series, and conference proceedings) and tens of millions of records. Titles are selected by an independent Content Selection and Advisory Board (CSAB), which reviews journals against criteria including peer-review policy, editorial quality, regularity of publication, and citation performance. Records include cited references, generally from 1970 onward, enabling forward and backward citation searching.

Metrics and tools

Scopus supplies several journal and author metrics. CiteScore, launched in 2016, measures the average citations received per document published in a title over a four-year window. The database also underpins the SCImago Journal Rank (SJR) and the Source Normalized Impact per Paper (SNIP) indicators. Author profiles are generated algorithmically, assigning each author a Scopus Author ID and computing an h-index from their indexed publications. Institutional profiles similarly aggregate output for universities and research organisations.

Uses

Scopus data feed major global university rankings, including the QS World University Rankings and the Times Higher Education World University Rankings (which switched from Web of Science to Scopus in 2014). Research funders and national assessment exercises use Scopus for publication and citation analysis. Its coverage is broader than Web of Science in some fields — particularly conference proceedings in engineering and computer science — though it has been criticised for uneven coverage of the humanities, non-English-language journals, and for occasionally indexing predatory or low-quality titles later discontinued.
