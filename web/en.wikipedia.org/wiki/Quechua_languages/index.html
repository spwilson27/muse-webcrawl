Quechua languages

Quechua

Native to: Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador
Speakers: c. 7–10 million
Family: Quechuan

Quechua is a family of Indigenous languages of the Andes, spoken today by some 7 to 10 million people, primarily in Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador. It was the language of administration of the Inca Empire and spread widely during Inca and colonial times; it remains the most widely spoken Indigenous language family in the Americas.

Classification

Linguists divide Quechua into two main branches: Central Quechua (Quechua I), spoken in Peru's central highlands, and Peripheral Quechua (Quechua II), which includes the prestigious Cuzco variety in Peru, Bolivian Quechua, and Ecuadorian Kichwa. Mutual intelligibility between distant varieties is limited.

Features

Quechua languages are agglutinative and suffixing, with subject–object–verb word order, a three-vowel system /a i u/, and evidential suffixes that mark the source of information. In Cuzco and Bolivian varieties, stops occur in plain, aspirated, and ejective series.

Status

Quechua is co-official in Peru and Bolivia. Despite large speaker numbers, many varieties are endangered as speakers shift to Spanish. Standardisation and orthography (three-vowel versus five-vowel spellings) remain subjects of debate.
