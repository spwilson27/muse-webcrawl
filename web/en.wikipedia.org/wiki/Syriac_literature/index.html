Syriac literature

Syriac literature is the body of literature written in the Syriac language, a dialect of Middle Aramaic that arose in Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa, Turkey) in the first centuries CE and became the literary and liturgical language of Syriac Christianity across the Middle East and beyond.

Early works

Early Syriac writings include the Odes of Solomon, the works of Bardaisan, and the Peshitta, the standard Syriac version of the Bible. The fourth-century deacon Ephrem the Syrian, author of hymns and verse homilies, is regarded as the greatest Syriac writer.

Golden age and translation movement

From the fifth to the eighth centuries, Syriac scholars such as Jacob of Edessa and Sergius of Reshaina translated Greek philosophical, medical, and scientific works — including Aristotle and Galen — into Syriac, a tradition that later fed the Arabic translation movement of Abbasid Baghdad. Syriac writers produced extensive chronicles, lives of saints, theological treatises, and liturgical poetry in both the West Syriac and East Syriac traditions.

Later literature

Syriac continued as a literary language through the medieval period; Bar Hebraeus (13th century) wrote encyclopaedic works in both Syriac and Arabic. A Neo-Aramaic literary revival emerged in the 19th century, and Syriac literature remains a living tradition among Assyrian and other Syriac Christian communities.
