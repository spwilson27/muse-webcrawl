Dún Aonghasa (pronounced [ˌd̪ˠuːn̪ˠ ˈiːŋəsˠə]; anglicised as Dun Aengus) is a prehistoric hill fort on Inis Mór, the largest of the Aran Islands, in County Galway, Ireland. Situated on the island's south-western coast, it stands on the edge of a cliff roughly 100 metres (330 ft) above the Atlantic Ocean and is widely regarded as one of the finest prehistoric stone forts in Europe. The name means "Fort of Aonghas", referring to the mythical king Aonghus of Irish legend.

Description

The fort is semi-circular in plan: unlike a complete ringfort, its fourth side is formed by the cliff itself, which erosion has steadily eaten away over the millennia. It consists of three (in places four) concentric stone ramparts enclosing an area of about 5.7 hectares. The innermost enclosure, a D-shaped space of roughly 50 metres across, is surrounded by a massive drystone wall up to 4.9 metres thick and still standing some 4 metres high in places.

Between the middle and outer walls lies a dense band of upright, closely set stones known as a chevaux de frise, designed to impede attackers approaching on foot or horseback. This defensive feature is rare in Ireland but has parallels in forts in Iberia and elsewhere in Atlantic Europe, a fact sometimes cited in discussions of prehistoric contact along the Atlantic seaways.

History and excavation

The earliest defences at Dún Aonghasa date to the Late Bronze Age, around 1100 BC, when the inner enclosure was first walled. The fort was refortified during the Iron Age and remained in use, intermittently, into the early medieval period. Excavations in the 1990s, led by Claire Cotter, uncovered evidence of occupation including bronze-working, stone and bone tools, and the remains of round houses, confirming long-lived settlement rather than purely temporary refuge use.

George Petrie surveyed and drew the monument in the 19th century, and the site was taken into state care under the Office of Public Works. Today it is one of the most visited heritage sites in the west of Ireland; the absence of any guard rail at the cliff edge is frequently remarked upon by visitors.
