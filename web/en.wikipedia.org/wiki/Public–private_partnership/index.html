A public–private partnership (PPP, often P3 in North America) is a long-term cooperative arrangement between a government and private-sector companies to finance, build and operate infrastructure or deliver public services. In a typical PPP a private consortium — usually organised as a special-purpose vehicle — designs, builds, finances and maintains an asset such as a road, hospital or school for a concession period of decades, in exchange for payments from the government or charges to users, after which the asset may revert to public hands. The model is defined by the bundling of construction with long-term operation and by the contractual transfer of specified risks to the private partner.

History and spread

Concessions have centuries-old precedents (canals, turnpikes, French water concessions), but the modern PPP wave began with the United Kingdom's Private Finance Initiative, launched in 1992 and greatly expanded under the Blair government. Australia, Canada, Spain and many developing economies adopted PPP programmes, promoted by the World Bank and regional development banks as a way to mobilise private capital and expertise for infrastructure gaps.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue PPPs deliver projects on time and on budget more reliably than traditional procurement, harness private-sector efficiency, and transfer construction and availability risks. Critics counter that private finance is more expensive than government borrowing; that long contracts are inflexible; that risk transfer is often illusory because governments bail out failing essential services; and that accounting treatment historically flattered public balance sheets. The UK's National Audit Office assessments were mixed; the collapse of contractor Carillion in 2018 intensified scrutiny, and the UK government announced in 2018 that it would no longer use the PFI/PF2 model for new projects, though existing contracts run for decades.

Models

Common variants include BOT (build–operate–transfer), DBFO (design–build–finance–operate), availability-payment concessions (paid for making the asset available to standard, common for hospitals and schools) and user-pay concessions such as toll roads, in which the operator bears demand risk.
