Robbers Cave Experiment

ResearcherMuzafer Sherif and colleagues
DateSummer 1954
Participants22 boys, aged 11–12
FieldSocial psychology

The Robbers Cave experiment was a landmark study of intergroup conflict conducted in the summer of 1954 by social psychologist Muzafer Sherif and his colleagues at Robbers Cave State Park in Oklahoma. It is a foundational demonstration of realistic conflict theory: that competition over scarce resources generates hostility between groups, and that superordinate goals — goals both groups want but neither can achieve alone — can reduce it.

Design

Twenty-two boys, all white, Protestant, middle-class 11- and 12-year-olds who did not know one another, were divided into two groups matched for ability. In the first phase the groups, kept apart, formed their own identities, naming themselves the Eagles and the Rattlers. In the second phase the experimenters staged a tournament of competitions (baseball, tug-of-war, tent pitching) with prizes for the winners only. Friction rapidly escalated: name-calling, flag-burning, cabin raids and food fights followed, and sociometric measures showed strong in-group favouritism and out-group rejection.

Reducing conflict

Mere contact between the groups in the third phase (shared meals, films) did not reduce hostility. Only when the experimenters introduced superordinate goals — restoring a broken water supply, pooling money to rent a film, pushing a stalled truck together — did cooperation rebuild and cross-group friendships form.

Criticism

The study has been criticised on ethical grounds (the boys were unaware they were in an experiment, and staff intervened to provoke and channel events) and because the researchers' manipulations were unusually extensive. A earlier 1953 version of the study, in which the boys united against the experimenters, was abandoned and long unpublished.
