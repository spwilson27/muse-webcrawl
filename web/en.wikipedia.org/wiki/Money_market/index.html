Money market

The money market is a component of the economy that provides short-term funds. It deals with short-term loans, generally for a period of one year or less. The money market trades in short-term debt instruments such as Treasury bills, commercial paper, and certificates of deposit. Compared to the capital market, in which long-term debt and equity are traded, the money market is characterised by high liquidity, low default risk, and wholesale transaction sizes, typically among banks, money market funds, large corporations, and governments.

Participants

Participants include central banks (which use the money market to implement monetary policy through open market operations and standing facilities), commercial banks managing reserve and liquidity positions, corporations issuing commercial paper or parking surplus cash, money market mutual funds, and government treasuries funding short-term borrowing needs.

Instruments

  • Treasury bills — short-term government securities sold at a discount, maturing in up to one year.
  • Commercial paper — unsecured promissory notes issued by corporations, usually maturing in under 270 days.
  • Certificates of deposit — negotiable time deposits issued by banks.
  • Repurchase agreements (repos) — collateralised loans in which securities are sold with an agreement to repurchase them, often overnight.
  • Bankers' acceptances and federal funds (in the United States), interbank lending of reserve balances.

Functions

The money market allows borrowers to meet short-term obligations and lenders to earn a return on temporarily idle funds with minimal risk. Because overnight and short-term rates in the money market anchor the whole yield curve, it is the primary channel through which central bank policy rates transmit to the wider financial system. Disruptions in money markets — as in the 2008 financial crisis and the September 2019 repo market spike — can require central bank intervention.
