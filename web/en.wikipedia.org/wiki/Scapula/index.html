Scapula

Posterior (dorsal) surface of the left scapula

The scapula (plural scapulae or scapulas), also known as the shoulder blade, is the flat, triangular bone that connects the humerus (upper arm bone) with the clavicle (collar bone). Together with the clavicle it forms the shoulder girdle, which attaches the upper limb to the trunk.

Structure

The scapula lies on the posterolateral aspect of the thorax, overlying the second to seventh ribs. Its prominent features include the spine, a ridge crossing the dorsal surface that ends in the acromion, which forms the bony tip of the shoulder; the coracoid process, a hook-like projection on the anterior side; and the glenoid cavity, a shallow socket that articulates with the head of the humerus to form the shoulder joint.

Muscles

Seventeen muscles attach to the scapula, including the rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis), the trapezius, the rhomboids, the serratus anterior, and the levator scapulae. These muscles position and stabilise the shoulder during arm movement.

Function

The scapula provides attachment for the arm and allows a wide range of motion at the shoulder. Its gliding movements over the thoracic wall — elevation, depression, protraction, retraction, and rotation — are essential to normal arm function.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the scapula are uncommon and usually result from high-energy trauma. A "winged scapula", in which the medial border protrudes backwards, can result from damage to the long thoracic nerve and paralysis of the serratus anterior muscle.
