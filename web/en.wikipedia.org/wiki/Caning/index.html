Caning

Caning is a form of corporal punishment consisting of a number of hits (known as "strokes" or "cuts") with a single cane, usually made of rattan, generally applied to the offender's bare or clothed buttocks or hands. It has been used historically as a school discipline, a judicial sentence, and a penalty in military and domestic settings, and remains a judicial punishment in several countries, notably Singapore, Malaysia, and Brunei.

Judicial caning

Judicial caning was widespread in the British Empire and persisted in several successor states. In Singapore, caning is a mandatory or discretionary sentence for more than 30 offences, including drug trafficking, vandalism, robbery, and immigration offences; only men aged 18 to 50 certified medically fit may be caned, with a maximum of 24 strokes in one proceeding. The 1994 caning of American teenager Michael Fay for vandalism drew international controversy and diplomatic intervention.

In Malaysia, caning is imposed under both secular criminal law and, in some states, Sharia enactments for offences such as illicit alcohol consumption. Brunei retains caning under its penal code and Syariah Penal Code. Strokes are administered with a rattan cane by trained officers, usually in prison and under medical supervision, and are designed to be severely painful and to leave lasting marks.

School caning

Caning was long a routine punishment in schools in Britain and its former colonies. It was abolished in British state schools in 1987 and in private schools by 2003. It remains lawful in schools in some countries and in a minority of U.S. states as corporal punishment generally, though caning specifically is uncommon there.

Historical use

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, caning and flogging were common in the British armed forces and in prisons. The practice derived in part from Southeast Asian punishments using rattan, which British colonial administrations adopted because rattan inflicted severe pain with limited risk of permanent injury compared with the cat o' nine tails.

Criticism

Human rights organizations classify judicial caning as a form of torture or cruel, inhuman, and degrading punishment prohibited by international conventions. Retentionist governments defend it as an effective deterrent deeply supported by domestic public opinion.
