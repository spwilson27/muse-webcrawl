The Hutterites (German: Hutterer) are a communal ethnoreligious branch of Anabaptists, tracing their origins to the Radical Reformation of the 16th century. Like the Amish and Mennonites, they share the beliefs of adult baptism and pacifism, but uniquely among these groups they practise full community of goods: all property is held by the colony, and members receive no wages.

History

The movement began in Tyrol and Moravia in 1528, organised under Jakob Hutter, who was executed in 1536. Hutterite communities flourished in Moravia until persecution during the Thirty Years' War drove them eastward through Slovakia, Transylvania and eventually to Russia. Between 1874 and 1879, responding to the loss of military exemption in Russia, nearly all Hutterites emigrated to Dakota Territory in the United States. Persecution during the First World War — including the deaths of two imprisoned conscientious objectors, the Hofer brothers — pushed many colonies to Canada, and today most Hutterites live in Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta and the northern Great Plains states.

Colony life

Hutterites live in agricultural colonies (Bruderhöfe) of roughly 60–160 people. When a colony grows beyond about 150 members it divides, founding a daughter colony. Colonies are highly mechanised farms; work is allocated communally, meals are eaten together, and governance is by elected male leadership under a minister. Three main branches — Schmiedeleut, Dariusleut and Lehrerleut — reflect the leaders under whom the migration-era groups were organised. Dress is modest and distinctive, and most colonies speak Hutterisch, a Carinthian German dialect, alongside English.

Demography

With historically large families, the Hutterite population has doubled roughly every 20 years — a rate famously studied by demographers and geneticists — and numbered around 50,000 in the early 21st century, spread over roughly 500 colonies.
