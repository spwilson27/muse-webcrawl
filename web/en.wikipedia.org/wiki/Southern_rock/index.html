Southern rock

Southern rock is a subgenre of rock music and a genre of Americana. It developed in the Southern United States in the late 1960s and 1970s from rock and roll, blues, country, and rhythm and blues, and is characterized by electric guitar-driven arrangements, often with two or three lead guitarists, piano, and songs drawing on Southern themes, imagery, and identity.

History

The genre was pioneered by the Allman Brothers Band, formed in Jacksonville, Florida, in 1969, whose improvisational blend of blues, jazz, and country — showcased in the live album At Fillmore East (1971) — set the template. Capricorn Records of Macon, Georgia, became the genre's home label. Lynyrd Skynyrd of Jacksonville achieved the genre's greatest commercial success with songs such as "Sweet Home Alabama" (1974) and "Free Bird" (1973), until a 1977 plane crash killed singer Ronnie Van Zant and others and halted the band at its peak.

Other major acts include ZZ Top (Texas boogie), the Marshall Tucker Band, the Charlie Daniels Band, .38 Special, the Outlaws, Molly Hatchet, Blackfoot, and Wet Willie. The genre's popularity waned in the 1980s, though it persisted through reunions, jam-band descendants such as Widespread Panic and Gov't Mule, and influences on country music and later acts like the Drive-By Truckers, Blackberry Smoke, and Chris Stapleton.

Characteristics

Southern rock typically features extended guitar solos and jams, boogie rhythms, and lyrics about Southern life, pride, and outlaw themes. Its use of Confederate imagery by some bands has been controversial since the genre's beginnings, and several acts have distanced themselves from the flag in recent decades.
