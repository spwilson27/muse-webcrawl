The left atrium is one of the four chambers of the human heart, located on the left posterior side. It receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the pulmonary veins and pumps it into the left ventricle through the mitral valve during atrial systole. It is the most posterior of the heart's chambers, sitting directly in front of the oesophagus.

Structure

The left atrium is somewhat smaller and thicker-walled than the right atrium. Its cavity consists of a smooth-walled posterior part, into which the four pulmonary veins (two from each lung) open, and a smaller muscular anterior appendage known as the left auricle, whose inner surface bears pectinate muscles. Unlike the right atrium, the left atrium has no crista terminalis and receives no systemic venous blood in the normal adult circulation.

The interatrial septum between the two atria contains the fossa ovalis on its right side; on the left side the corresponding depression is less distinct. In fetal life the foramen ovale allows blood to pass directly from the right to the left atrium, bypassing the non-functioning lungs; it normally closes shortly after birth.

Function

During ventricular diastole, blood flows passively from the left atrium into the left ventricle through the open mitral valve. Atrial contraction at the end of diastole — the "atrial kick" — contributes roughly 20–30% of ventricular filling in healthy individuals, a contribution that becomes especially important when ventricular compliance is reduced. The left atrium also functions as a reservoir during ventricular systole and as a conduit in early diastole.

Clinical significance

Enlargement of the left atrium is associated with mitral valve disease, left ventricular dysfunction, and chronic hypertension. A dilated left atrium predisposes to atrial fibrillation, and the left atrial appendage is the most common site of thrombus formation in that condition, making the chamber clinically important in stroke prevention. Left atrial size is routinely measured by echocardiography and is a strong predictor of cardiovascular outcomes.
