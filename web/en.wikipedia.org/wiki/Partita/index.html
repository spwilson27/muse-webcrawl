Partita

A partita is a musical form, originally meaning a single instrumental piece, and later, in the Baroque era, a collection of musical pieces — a suite — for a solo instrument. The word comes from the Italian partita, a part or division, and in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it was also applied to a set of variations.

History

Composers of the late Renaissance and early Baroque used "partita" for sets of variations on a ground bass or melody, as in the partite of Girolamo Frescobaldi. In the later Baroque the term became essentially synonymous with "suite": Johann Kuhnau published keyboard partitas, and Johann Sebastian Bach wrote six Partitas for keyboard (BWV 825–830, published together as the first part of the Clavier-Übung), three partitas for solo violin (within the set of Sonatas and Partitas, BWV 1001–1006) and a partita for solo flute (BWV 1013).

Bach's keyboard partitas follow the suite pattern of a substantial opening movement (prelude, sinfonia, fantasia, overture, praeambulum or toccata) followed by the standard dances — allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue — with optional galanteries such as minuets, passepieds and rondeaux between the sarabande and gigue.

Later usage

After the Baroque era the term fell largely out of use, though it has occasionally been revived by modern composers for solo instrumental works in several movements, in conscious homage to Bach.
