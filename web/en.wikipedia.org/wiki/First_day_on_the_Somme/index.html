The first day on the Somme, 1 July 1916, was the opening day of the Battle of the Somme in the First World War. Thirteen divisions of the British Army (eleven from the Fourth Army and two from the Third Army) launched an offensive in Picardy, northern France, in conjunction with eleven divisions of the French Sixth Army, against the German Second Army. The day became infamous as the worst day in the history of the British Army, which suffered 57,470 casualties, including 19,240 killed.

Preparations

The offensive was planned to relieve pressure on the French at Verdun and to break through the German lines. A preliminary artillery bombardment lasting seven days fired some 1.5 million shells, but much of the German wire remained uncut and the deep German dugouts survived. At 07:30 on 1 July, after the explosion of huge mines beneath the German lines, the infantry advanced.

The attack

In the north, around Serre, Beaumont-Hamel and Thiepval, the attack largely failed: German machine-gunners emerged from their dugouts and inflicted catastrophic losses on infantry advancing in lines. Many battalions of Kitchener's volunteer New Army — including the "Pals battalions" recruited from single towns and workplaces — were effectively destroyed. In the south, by contrast, the British 18th and 30th Divisions and the French took nearly all their objectives around Montauban and Mametz, where the artillery plan had been more effective.

Consequences

Despite the disaster in the north, the offensive continued until November 1916, becoming a battle of attrition. The first day has become an enduring symbol of the futility and sacrifice of the First World War, and 1 July is still a date of commemoration, particularly in Newfoundland — where the Newfoundland Regiment was nearly wiped out at Beaumont-Hamel — and in Northern Ireland, where the 36th (Ulster) Division suffered heavy losses.
