Autofocus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An autofocus (or AF) optical system uses a sensor, a control system and a motor to focus on an automatically or manually selected point or area. Autofocus systems are standard in cameras, and are also used in microscopes, binoculars and other optical instruments. The first mass-produced autofocus camera was the Konica C35 AF of 1977; the first autofocus SLR was the Pentax ME-F (1981), and the Minolta Maxxum 7000 (1985) established the integrated body-motor AF SLR.

Phase detection compares light from opposite sides of the lens to compute focus error.

Active and passive systems

Active autofocus systems measure distance independently of the optical system, using infrared light or ultrasonic sound to perform triangulation; they were common in point-and-shoot film cameras. Passive systems analyse the image itself. Two main passive methods exist:

  • Phase detection splits incoming light into pairs of images and compares them, computing both the amount and direction of focus error, enabling fast focusing; traditional in SLRs, it is now implemented on image sensors in mirrorless cameras.
  • Contrast detection adjusts focus to maximise image contrast; it is accurate but can be slower and may "hunt" back and forth.

Modern developments

Contemporary cameras combine methods, add subject detection (faces, eyes, animals, vehicles) driven by machine learning, and offer focus tracking for moving subjects. Video autofocus requires smooth, silent focus transitions. Autofocus is contrasted with manual focus and fixed-focus designs.
