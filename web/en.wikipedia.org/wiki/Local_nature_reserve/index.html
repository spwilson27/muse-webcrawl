Local nature reserve

A local nature reserve (LNR) is a designation for a protected area of land in Great Britain, established by local authorities under the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949. Local nature reserves are places of local — rather than national — importance for wildlife or geology, managed for conservation, public enjoyment, education and research.

Legal basis and designation

Under sections 21–23 of the 1949 Act, county councils, district councils and other local authorities in England, Scotland and Wales may designate land they own or lease as an LNR, in consultation with the statutory nature conservation body (Natural England, NatureScot or Natural Resources Wales). The declaring authority normally passes byelaws to protect the site. The first LNRs were declared in the 1950s; one of the earliest was Keston Common in Kent (1953).

Scale and character

There are well over 1,600 local nature reserves in England alone, covering tens of thousands of hectares, with several hundred more in Scotland and Wales. They vary enormously: tiny urban pocket sites, former railway lines, canals, flooded gravel pits, ancient woodlands, heathland and coastal marshes. Natural England's guidance suggests an aspiration that there should be at least one hectare of LNR per 1,000 people and that everyone should live within reasonable access of such a site.

Comparison with other designations

LNR status is weaker than that of a national nature reserve (NNR), which is declared by the national conservation agency for nationally important sites, and it does not by itself carry the statutory protection of a Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI), although many LNRs overlap with SSSIs. The emphasis of the LNR designation is as much on people — access, education and local involvement, often through "friends of" volunteer groups — as on wildlife.
