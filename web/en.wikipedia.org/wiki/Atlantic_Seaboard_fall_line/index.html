Atlantic Seaboard fall line

The Atlantic Seaboard fall line, or fall zone, is a 900-mile (1,400 km) escarpment where the Piedmont and Atlantic Coastal Plain meet in the eastern United States. Much of the fall line passes through areas where little evidence of it now remains, although it was an important feature of the region's geography and settlement history.

The fall line marks the geologic boundary of hard metamorphosed terrain—the product of the Taconic orogeny and the Appalachian orogeny—to the west, and the sandy, relatively flat outwash plain of the upper continental shelf to the east. It is the surface expression (in places) of the fall zone, a more diffuse boundary. Where rivers cross the fall line, they form rapids or waterfalls, because the harder rocks of the Piedmont erode more slowly than the softer sedimentary rocks of the coastal plain.

Significance

The fall line was significant to the early European settlers for two reasons. First, the rapids and falls marked the furthest point upstream that ships could navigate from the Atlantic Ocean, making fall line locations natural heads of navigation and trading posts. Second, the falling water provided a ready source of power for mills and, later, for hydroelectric generation and industry.

As a result, many important cities grew up along the fall line, including Trenton, New Jersey; Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; Baltimore, Maryland; Washington, D.C.; Richmond, Virginia; Raleigh and Fayetteville, North Carolina; Columbia, South Carolina; Augusta, Georgia; and Columbus, Georgia. In the colonial and early national periods, roads and later railroads often followed or connected these fall line settlements.

Geology

The boundary is characterized by a zone of rapids and small waterfalls where streams descend from the resistant crystalline rocks of the Piedmont to the unconsolidated Cretaceous and younger sediments of the coastal plain. The elevation drops roughly 100 to 200 feet across the zone in many places. The fall line roughly follows the contact between the ancient basement complex and the overlapping wedge of coastal plain sediments that thickens toward the Atlantic.
