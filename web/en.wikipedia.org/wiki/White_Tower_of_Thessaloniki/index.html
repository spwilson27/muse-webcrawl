The White Tower of Thessaloniki (Greek: Λευκός Πύργος, Lefkós Pýrgos) is a monument and museum on the waterfront of Thessaloniki, capital of the region of Macedonia in northern Greece. The present tower replaced an older Byzantine fortification and is the city's most famous landmark and symbol.

History

The tower stands at the eastern end of the city's sea walls, where a Byzantine tower probably once formed part of the harbour defences depicted in early Ottoman-era sources. The surviving structure was built by the Ottomans, most likely around 1535–1536 during the reign of Suleiman the Magnificent, possibly to a design by the architect Sinan, although an alternative tradition dates it to Venetian builders. It formed part of the fortifications guarding the harbour and was joined to the city walls until the eastern walls were demolished in 1866.

For centuries the tower served as a guardhouse for Janissaries and as a prison. After Sultan Mahmud II ordered the massacre of the Janissaries in 1826, the tower became known as the "Tower of Blood" or "Red Tower" (Kanli Kule). In 1890, according to tradition, a Jewish prisoner named Nathan Guidili whitewashed the tower in exchange for his freedom, and it has been known as the White Tower ever since. After Thessaloniki became part of Greece in 1912, the tower was formally whitewashed as a symbolic gesture, though its stone has since weathered back toward a buff colour.

Architecture

The tower is a cylinder 23.5 metres in diameter at its base and 33.9 metres high, with six floors connected by an internal spiral ramp and staircase. A small turret crowns the top. Its ground-floor rooms were used for storage, and the upper floors for the garrison; cisterns and a small mosque or prayer room were once among its fittings. In the early 20th century the surrounding area was landscaped, and the Greek flag was raised over the tower.

Museum

The interior now hosts an exhibition organised by the Museum of Byzantine Culture, presenting the history of Thessaloniki from its founding in 316/315 BC to the present, with multimedia displays on each floor. The tower appears on Greek euro coinage commemorative issues and is a standard meeting point on the city's seafront promenade (Nikis Avenue).
