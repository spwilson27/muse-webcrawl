Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (July 8, 1926 – August 24, 2004) was a Swiss-American psychiatrist, a pioneer in near-death studies, and author of the internationally best-selling book On Death and Dying (1969), in which she first discussed her theory of the five stages of grief, known as the Kübler-Ross model.

Five stages of grief

Based on interviews with terminally ill patients, Kübler-Ross described a pattern of adjustment to impending death: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. The model was later widely — and sometimes uncritically — applied to grief and loss generally, though she cautioned that the stages are not a rigid linear sequence experienced by everyone.

Career and legacy

Born a triplet in Zurich, she trained in medicine in Switzerland and moved to the United States in 1958, working at hospitals in New York, Colorado and Chicago, where her seminars with dying patients became influential in humanising the treatment of the terminally ill and helped spur the hospice and palliative care movements. In her later career she wrote extensively on near-death experiences and the afterlife, views that were controversial within mainstream medicine. She was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 2007.
