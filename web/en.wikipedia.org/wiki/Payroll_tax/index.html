Payroll tax

A payroll tax is a tax levied on the wages and salaries paid by employers and/or employees, typically calculated as a percentage of pay and usually earmarked for social insurance programmes such as pensions, health care and unemployment insurance. Employers generally withhold the employee share from wages and remit both shares to the tax authority. Because the tax base is labour income, economists usually treat the combined employer–employee burden as falling largely on workers through lower wages, whatever the statutory split.

In the United States

The main federal payroll taxes are those imposed by the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA): 6.2 per cent each on employer and employee for Social Security (the Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance programme), applied only up to an annually adjusted wage base ($168,600 in 2024), and 1.45 per cent each for Medicare hospital insurance, with no cap and an additional 0.9 per cent employee tax on high earnings. Self-employed workers pay both shares (15.3 per cent combined) under the Self-Employment Contributions Act, with an offsetting income-tax deduction. A separate federal unemployment tax (FUTA) and state unemployment taxes fund unemployment insurance, and many states and cities levy their own payroll-based taxes for transit, paid leave, or disability programmes.

Payroll taxes are the second-largest source of US federal revenue after the individual income tax, and for most wage-earning households they exceed income tax paid. Their flat structure and the Social Security wage cap make them regressive relative to earnings above the cap, a frequent theme in tax-policy debate; temporary cuts (such as the 2011–12 US payroll tax holiday) have been used as economic stimulus because they reach workers quickly.

Elsewhere

Many countries finance generous welfare states largely through payroll contributions: Germany splits pension, health, unemployment and long-term-care contributions between employer and employee; France's employer social charges are among the highest in the OECD; the United Kingdom's National Insurance contributions are a payroll tax in all but name. Australia levies a state-level payroll tax on employers above wage thresholds, alongside its Medicare levy on income. Historically, dedicated payroll taxes are credited with giving social programmes durable political support by linking benefits to visible contributions.

Economic analysis

Labour economists study payroll taxes for their effects on employment and wages. In the long run, with labour supply relatively inelastic, the tax is borne mostly by workers; in the short run, employer-side increases may reduce hiring, particularly at the minimum wage, where wages cannot adjust downward. The sharp rise of payroll-tax financing is a central reason the overall tax burden on labour — the "tax wedge" — is a headline OECD statistic.
