Fair Youth

The Fair Youth is the unnamed young man addressed in the majority of William Shakespeare's sonnets: of the 154 poems in the 1609 sequence, sonnets 1–126 are directed to him. The speaker celebrates the youth's beauty, urges him to marry and beget children who will carry that beauty forward, and over the course of the sequence moves through devotion, estrangement, betrayal, and reconciliation. The identity of the Fair Youth has been one of the most debated questions in Shakespeare scholarship.

The sequence

The first seventeen sonnets — the so-called "procreation sonnets" — urge the youth to reproduce so that his beauty will not die with him:

From fairest creatures we desire increase,
That thereby beauty's rose might never die.

Later poems shift from procreation to the promise that verse itself will immortalise the beloved, as in Sonnet 18 ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?") and Sonnet 55 ("Not marble nor the gilded monuments"). The sequence is complicated by the entrance of a rival poet competing for the youth's patronage and praise, and by the youth's affair with the so-called Dark Lady, to whom the later sonnets are addressed, which the speaker discovers and laments. The Fair Youth sequence closes with Sonnet 126, an envoi in an unusual twelve-line form of couplets, warning that Nature, who has kept the youth beautiful, will eventually claim him.

Identity

Two candidates dominate speculation about a historical identity. Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton, Shakespeare's patron in the early 1590s, to whom the narrative poems Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece were dedicated, fits the role of a beautiful aristocratic young patron who resisted marriage in his youth. William Herbert, 3rd Earl of Pembroke, fits the initials "Mr. W. H." in the 1609 quarto's dedication to "the only begetter of these ensuing sonnets" — a dedication signed by the publisher Thomas Thorpe — and was similarly pressed to marry. Other proposed identifications include various courtiers and, in the sceptical view of many modern scholars, no single person at all: the youth may be a composite, a literary device, or a figure drawn from the conventions of sonnet sequences.

Interpretation

The intensity of the speaker's attachment has prompted centuries of debate about whether the poems express romantic or sexual love, elevated friendship, or patronage rhetoric in the hyperbolic conventions of the period. Victorian editors sometimes altered pronouns or apologised for the sequence; modern scholarship generally reads the poems' emotional and erotic ambiguity as intrinsic to their design, and they are now widely regarded as among the greatest lyric sequences in English.
