Mikhail Bakunin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin (30 May 1814 – 1 July 1876) was a Russian revolutionary anarchist and philosopher, the founder of collectivist anarchism. A former artillery officer turned itinerant revolutionary, he took part in the uprisings of 1848–49 in Prague and Dresden, spent years in Prussian, Austrian and Russian prisons and Siberian exile, escaped in 1861, and thereafter devoted himself to propagating anarchism across Europe.

Bakunin argued that the state, the church and hereditary property were interlocking systems of domination, and that freedom required their abolition and replacement by a free federation of communes and associations. In Federalism, Socialism and Anti-Theologism (1867) and the posthumous God and the State, he warned that a revolutionary dictatorship — including one claimed in the name of the proletariat — would produce a new tyranny, a prediction his followers later cited against Marxist states. His conflict with Marx in the First International, ending with his expulsion at The Hague Congress of 1872, split the socialist movement into anarchist and Marxist camps.

Ideas

Bakunin's collectivist anarchism proposed collective ownership of land and the means of production with remuneration according to work, organised bottom-up by federation. He championed national liberation movements (especially Slavic peoples under empire) while opposing nationalism as a state project, and supported the Paris Commune of 1871. His writings on atheism and materialism influenced revolutionary syndicalism in Spain, Italy and Latin America, where anarchism remained the dominant revolutionary current into the 1930s.
