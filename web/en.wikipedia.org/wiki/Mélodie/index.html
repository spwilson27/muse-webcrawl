Mélodie

Mélodie is the French term for a type of art song for solo voice and piano, the counterpart of the German Lied. The mélodie arose in the mid-19th century and flourished in France from the 1870s onward, as composers sought a distinctively French form of song drawing on symbolist and Parnassian poetry.

History

The earlier French romance was a simpler, strophic salon song. With Hector Berlioz's Les nuits d'été (1841) the elevated art song began to develop, but the mélodie came of age with Gabriel Fauré, whose settings of Verlaine, including the cycle La bonne chanson, defined the genre's refinement. Henri Duparc wrote a small number of highly regarded mélodies, while Ernest Chausson, Claude Debussy and Maurice Ravel extended the form — Debussy and Ravel integrating impressionist harmony and subtle piano writing. Later composers such as Francis Poulenc kept the mélodie central to French song in the 20th century.

Characteristics

Mélodies typically set texts by poets such as Baudelaire, Verlaine and Mallarmé. They are marked by careful attention to the prosody of the French language, an expressive and independent piano part, and restrained, intimate vocal lines rather than operatic display.
