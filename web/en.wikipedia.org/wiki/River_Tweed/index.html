The River Tweed is a river 156 kilometres (97 mi) long in the Scottish Borders and northern England. It rises at Tweed's Well near Moffat and flows generally eastward through the Border country before reaching the North Sea at Berwick-upon-Tweed. For the last roughly 27 kilometres of its course it forms the boundary between Scotland and England. Near its source, the Tweed's headwaters rise close to those of the Clyde and the Annan — a fact captured in the saying "Annan, Tweed and Clyde rise oot the ae hillside".

Course

From its source the Tweed flows northeast past Peebles, then east through the textile towns of Galashiels and Melrose, past Kelso — where it is joined by the River Teviot — and Coldstream, before reaching Berwick. Its tributaries include the Ettrick Water, Gala Water, Leader Water, Teviot, Till and Whiteadder Water.

Fishing and economy

The Tweed is one of Britain's most famous Atlantic salmon rivers, with a long rod-fishing season running from February to the end of November and a valuable catch-and-release fishery. The river and its tributaries are designated a Special Area of Conservation for salmon, otters and lampreys. Historically, net fisheries at the mouth were also significant, and the Tweed valley supported a major woollen textile industry — the cloth "tweed" is associated with the region, though the name may derive from a misreading of the Scots word "tweel" (twill).

History

The lower Tweed was a contested frontier for centuries between Scotland and England; the river was crossed by armies at battles such as Flodden (1513, fought near its tributary the Till) and its bridges at Berwick and Coldstream were strategically vital. The Coldstream Guards take their name from the town where General Monck raised the regiment in 1650 before marching south to restore Charles II.
