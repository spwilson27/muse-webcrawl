Decomposition

Decomposition or biodegradation is the process by which organic substances are broken down into simpler organic or inorganic matter, carried out mainly by bacteria, fungi and detritivores. It recycles nutrients in ecosystems and, in forensic science, describes the post-mortem breakdown of bodies.

Stages

For animal remains, forensic taphonomy commonly describes fresh, bloat, active decay, advanced decay and dry/skeletonised stages. Autolysis (self-digestion by enzymes) and putrefaction (microbial action producing gases and odours such as cadaverine and putrescine) begin soon after death; scavengers and insects—blow flies and their maggots—accelerate tissue loss and help estimate post-mortem interval.

Factors

Rate depends on temperature, moisture, oxygen (aerobic vs anaerobic decay), soil pH, body composition, clothing, burial and scavenger access—summarised in Casper's law comparing air, water and burial. Preservation can occur by mummification, freezing, peat bogs or adipocere formation.

Plant decomposition and soil

Leaf litter and wood are decomposed by fungi and bacteria at rates governed by lignin content and climate, releasing carbon dioxide and forming humus; decomposition is a major flux in the global carbon cycle and the basis of composting.
