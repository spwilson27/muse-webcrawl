Sinking of the RMS Lusitania

The British ocean liner RMS Lusitania was sunk on 7 May 1915 by the German U-boat U-20 during the First World War. The Cunard liner was torpedoed off the Old Head of Kinsale, Ireland, while on a voyage from New York to Liverpool, and sank in just 18 minutes, killing 1,197 of the 1,962 people aboard, including 128 Americans. The attack on a passenger ship carrying civilians turned public opinion in many countries against Germany and contributed to the eventual entry of the United States into the war in 1917.

Background

In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone in which enemy merchant ships would be sunk without warning, in response to the British naval blockade. The German embassy in Washington placed newspaper advertisements warning travellers not to sail on Allied ships, one appearing beside Lusitania's sailing schedule. Lusitania departed New York on 1 May 1915 with 1,959 people aboard and a cargo that included millions of rifle cartridges and other munitions.

The sinking

On the afternoon of 7 May, in fine weather off the Irish coast, U-20 under Kapitänleutnant Walther Schwieger fired a single torpedo that struck Lusitania's starboard side. A second, internal explosion followed — its cause long debated, with theories including exploding munitions or coal dust. The ship developed a severe list that hampered lifeboat launching; many boats capsized or could not be lowered on the port side. Lusitania sank within 18 minutes. Rescue came from Irish ports, chiefly Queenstown (Cobh), but 1,197 people died, among them the art collector Hugh Lane and the impresario Charles Frohman.

Aftermath

The sinking caused a diplomatic crisis. US President Woodrow Wilson demanded that Germany disavow the attack and respect neutral rights, and after further sinkings Germany issued the Arabic and Sussex pledges restricting U-boat attacks. When Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare in February 1917, the United States broke relations and declared war that April. Controversy has persisted over whether the Admiralty exposed the ship to danger, whether she was a legitimate military target given her munitions cargo, and the nature of the second explosion. The wreck lies in about 93 metres of water and has been explored by divers and remotely operated vehicles.
