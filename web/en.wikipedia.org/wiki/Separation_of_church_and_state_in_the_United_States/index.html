Separation of church and state in the United States


The principle derives from the First Amendment (placeholder image)

The separation of church and state is a legal and political principle in the United States derived from the Establishment Clause and Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment to the Constitution: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof."

Origins

The phrase "separation of church and state" comes not from the Constitution but from Thomas Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptist Association, in which he described the First Amendment as building "a wall of separation between Church & State". Earlier advocates of religious liberty included Roger Williams, founder of Rhode Island, who argued for a "hedge or wall of separation" to protect the church from the state. James Madison, the principal author of the First Amendment, similarly opposed government support of religion, notably in his Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments (1785).

Jurisprudence

The Supreme Court applied the Establishment Clause against the states in Everson v. Board of Education (1947), expressly adopting Jefferson's metaphor. Later decisions addressed school prayer (Engel v. Vitale, 1962), public funding of religious schools, religious displays on public property and legislative prayer. The Court's tests — including the three-part Lemon test of 1971 and later history-and-tradition approaches, as in Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022) — have shifted over time and remain a subject of political and legal debate between "separationist" and "accommodationist" readings.
