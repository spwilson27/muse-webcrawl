Awe

Awe is an emotion comparable to wonder but less joyous, and often connoting a sense of reverence, admiration, or even fear toward something perceived as vast and transcending ordinary understanding. It is commonly experienced in response to nature's grandeur, great art or music, religious experience, or encounters with exceptional human achievement.

Psychological research

In 2003, psychologists Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt proposed that awe is characterized by two appraisals: perceived vastness and a need for accommodation — the mental adjustment required when an experience does not fit existing schemas. Subsequent research has linked awe to a diminished sense of self (the "small self"), increased feelings of connectedness, greater prosocial behaviour, altered time perception, and reduced inflammatory markers. Awe can be elicited by natural landscapes, the night sky, childbirth, music, psychedelics, and collective rituals.

Philosophical and religious perspectives

Philosophers from Edmund Burke and Immanuel Kant — who analyzed the closely related feeling of the sublime — to Rudolf Otto, who described the mysterium tremendum et fascinans of religious experience, have examined awe's mixture of attraction and dread. In many religious traditions, awe before the divine is considered a foundational attitude, expressed in phrases such as the "fear of God" in the Hebrew Bible.

Variations

Researchers distinguish positive awe from threat-based awe, evoked by natural disasters or powerful and dangerous phenomena, which carries more anxiety. Cultural variation exists in how readily awe is experienced and expressed, and individual differences in openness to experience predict awe-proneness.
