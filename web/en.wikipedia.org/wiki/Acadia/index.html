Acadia (French: Acadie) was a colony of New France in northeastern North America, encompassing parts of what are now the Maritime provinces of Canada and the U.S. state of Maine. Settled by the French from 1604, its capital was Port-Royal, and its inhabitants — the Acadians — developed a distinct French-speaking culture farming reclaimed tidal marshland behind dykes.

History

The first settlement was founded in 1604 on Saint Croix Island and moved to Port-Royal in 1605 by Pierre Dugua, Sieur de Mons, and Samuel de Champlain. Control of Acadia alternated between France and England (and Scotland, whose colonists named Nova Scotia) through much of the 17th century. By the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), mainland Acadia was ceded permanently to Britain, though France retained Île Royale (Cape Breton Island).

The Expulsion

During the French and Indian War, the British authorities, doubting Acadian neutrality, carried out the Expulsion of the Acadians (Le Grand Dérangement) from 1755 to 1764, deporting more than 11,000 Acadians and scattering them among the British colonies, England, and France. Many eventually settled in Louisiana, where their descendants are the Cajuns. The deportation was immortalised in Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847).

Acadia today

Acadians were permitted to return from 1764, settling along coasts of present-day New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, and Prince Edward Island. Today Acadia commonly refers to the French-speaking regions of Atlantic Canada, whose people maintain a distinct culture, flag, and national day (15 August), centred in New Brunswick — the only officially bilingual Canadian province.
