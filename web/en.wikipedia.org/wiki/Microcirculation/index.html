Microcirculation

The microcirculation is the circulation of blood in the smallest blood vessels, the microvessels of the microvasculature present within organ tissues. The microvessels include terminal arterioles, metarterioles, capillaries, and postcapillary venules. The microcirculation also includes lymphatic capillaries and collecting ducts. The main functions of the microcirculation are the delivery of oxygen and nutrients and the removal of carbon dioxide and metabolic waste; it also serves to regulate blood flow and tissue perfusion, thereby affecting blood pressure and responses to inflammation and edema.

Structure

Blood flows from arterioles into a network of metarterioles and capillaries. Precapillary sphincters — rings of smooth muscle at the origin of each capillary — regulate how much blood enters the capillary bed at any time. Capillary walls consist of a single layer of endothelial cells, across which exchange with the interstitial fluid occurs by diffusion, filtration, and transcytosis. Postcapillary venules collect blood from the capillaries, and arteriovenous anastomoses (shunts) can bypass the capillary bed entirely, an arrangement important in thermoregulation in the skin.

Regulation

Microvascular flow is governed by local metabolic signals (vasodilation in response to low oxygen and accumulated metabolites), the myogenic response of vascular smooth muscle to pressure changes, endothelial mediators such as nitric oxide, and sympathetic neural control. In the lymphatic microcirculation, initial lymphatics absorb excess interstitial fluid and return it to the venous system.

Clinical significance

Impaired microcirculation contributes to shock, sepsis, diabetic microangiopathy (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy), and wound-healing failure. Techniques such as nailfold capillaroscopy and sidestream dark-field imaging allow direct clinical assessment of microvascular health.
