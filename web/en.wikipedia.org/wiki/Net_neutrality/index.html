Net neutrality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graphic of data packets of different services being treated unequally at a toll-booth-like gateway]

Net neutrality is the principle that Internet service providers must treat all Internet communications equally, offering users and online content providers consistent transfer rates regardless of content, website, platform, application, type of equipment, source address, destination address, or method of communication. Its supporters say it preserves the Internet's level playing field; its opponents say it restricts legitimate network management and investment.

The concept was named and popularised by Columbia law professor Tim Wu in a 2003 paper, but the debate is older: it recapitulates the common-carrier obligations of telegraph and telephone networks. The flashpoints are practices such as blocking, throttling, and paid prioritisation ('fast lanes'), and zero-rating of favoured services. In the United States the rules have oscillated with administrations: the FCC imposed strong rules in 2015, repealed them in 2017, voted to restore them in 2024, and saw them struck down by a federal appeals court in early 2025. The European Union, by contrast, has had an Open Internet Regulation since 2015.

Beyond law, the debate persists because the architecture shifted: the largest content companies now interconnect directly and run their own CDNs, so 'equal treatment' means something more complicated than it did when the argument began.
