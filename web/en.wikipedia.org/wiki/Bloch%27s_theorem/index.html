Bloch's theorem

Bloch's theorem
Field: condensed matter physics
Named after: Felix Bloch (1928)
Applies to: electrons in a periodic potential
Concepts: Bloch functions, band structure, Brillouin zone, crystal momentum

In condensed matter physics, Bloch's theorem states that solutions to the Schrödinger equation in a periodic potential take the form of a plane wave multiplied by a function with the same periodicity as the potential. Named after physicist Felix Bloch, who derived it in 1928, the theorem is the foundation of electronic band structure theory.

Statement

For a potential V(r) that is periodic with the lattice, V(r + R) = V(r) for every lattice vector R, the eigenfunctions can be written as ψ(r) = eik·r u(r), where u(r + R) = u(r) and k is the wave vector, restricted without loss of generality to the first Brillouin zone. Such eigenfunctions are called Bloch functions. The wave vector k plays the role of a quantum number, and ħk is the crystal momentum.

Consequences

Bloch's theorem implies that electrons in a perfect crystal are not localized but extend throughout the solid, scattering only from deviations from periodicity such as phonons, impurities, and defects — which explains the long mean free paths and high conductivities of pure metals. The allowed energies as functions of k form energy bands separated by band gaps, the basis for classifying materials as conductors, semiconductors, and insulators.
