Lee "Scratch" Perry (born Rainford Hugh Perry; March 20, 1936 – August 29, 2021) was a Jamaican record producer, composer and singer noted for his innovative studio techniques and production approach in the 1960s and 1970s. He was a pioneer in the development of dub music, with his early adoption of remixing and studio tricks, and produced some of the most important records by Bob Marley and the Wailers.

Early career

Perry began at Clement "Coxsone" Dodd's Studio One, working as a talent scout, disc jockey assistant, and eventually recording artist. The nickname "Scratch" came from an early recording, "The Chicken Scratch" (1965). After falling out with Dodd he worked with Joe Gibbs, then founded his own Upsetter label in 1968, scoring an international hit with "Return of Django" (1969) by his studio band the Upsetters.

The Black Ark

In 1973 Perry built the Black Ark studio behind his family home in Washington Gardens, Kingston. There, using only a four-track TEAC recorder, he produced a dense, murky, unmistakable sound — recordings for Marley and the Wailers-era material, Junior Murvin's "Police and Thieves", Max Romeo's "War Ina Babylon", the Congos' Heart of the Congos, and his own Super Ape (1976) — that rivalled the experiments of his contemporary King Tubby and defined dub's cosmic wing. His eccentric methods (blowing ganja smoke over tapes, burying microphones, endlessly bouncing tracks) became legend. The Black Ark burned down around 1979 — Perry claimed he set the fire himself to purge negative energies.

Later years

Perry spent years in England and Switzerland, collaborating with Adrian Sherwood and others, and enjoyed a late-career renaissance as a performer; his album Jamaican E.T. won the Grammy Award for Best Reggae Album in 2003. He died in Lucea, Jamaica, in 2021.
