Deep scattering layer

The deep scattering layer (DSL) is a name given to a layer in the ocean consisting of a variety of marine animals, particularly small mesopelagic fish, which was discovered through sonar. Sonar operators, puzzled by a layer that scattered sound and appeared to be the seafloor, sometimes called it the false bottom. The layer typically lies between 300 and 500 metres in depth during the day.

Discovery

The layer was noticed by US Navy sonar operators during the Second World War. It was studied by biologist Martin Johnson of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, who in 1945 proposed that it consisted of organisms performing a daily vertical migration.

Diel vertical migration

The animals of the deep scattering layer — chiefly lanternfishes, krill, siphonophores and other zooplankton and micronekton — rise toward the surface at dusk to feed on phytoplankton-rich waters, and descend again before dawn to avoid visual predators. This diel vertical migration is thought to be the largest migration on Earth by biomass, and it transports carbon from surface waters to the deep ocean. Gas-filled swim bladders and other resonant structures of the animals scatter sonar pulses strongly, which makes the layer so conspicuous on echograms.
