Register (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schematic showing common registers from low to high pitch.

In music, a register is the "height" or range of a note, set of pitches or pitch classes, melody, part, instrument, or group of instruments. A higher register indicates higher pitch. Two tones exactly an octave apart belong to different registers because pitch class is not the same as register: a note and its octave share a pitch class but sit in different registers.

The word "register" is used in several distinct but related senses: as a portion of the range of a voice or instrument, as a characteristic timbre associated with part of a range, and, in organ building, as a set of stops. Register also matters in composition and orchestration, since placing a line in a low, middle, or high register strongly affects its perceived weight, tension, and colour.

Vocal registers

In singing, a vocal register is a range of tones produced in the human voice by a particular vibratory pattern of the vocal folds. These registers include modal voice (or normal voice), vocal fry, falsetto, and the whistle register. Registers originate in laryngeal function: they occur because the vocal folds are capable of producing several different vibratory patterns, each producing characteristic sounds within a particular range of pitches and carrying certain characteristic tones.

Speech pathologists and singing teachers sometimes disagree on how many registers exist and where their boundaries lie. A widely used scheme divides the voice into chest register (modal), head register, and falsetto in men; women are often described as having chest, middle (or mixed), head, and whistle registers.

Instrumental registers

Wind instruments often have registers defined by their overblowing behaviour. The clarinet, for example, is traditionally described as having a low chalumeau register, a middle clarion register, and a high altissimo register, separated by the "break" between throat tones and the clarion register. Because the clarinet overblows at the twelfth rather than the octave, its registers do not line up the way a flute's or oboe's do.

For instruments generally, musicians speak informally of the low, middle, and high registers of an instrument's total range, each with its own timbral character: the low register of a flute is soft and breathy, for example, while its high register is bright and penetrating.

Organ stops and registral composition

On the pipe organ, a register may refer to a stop, or a rank of pipes controlled by one stop. Combinations of stops, chosen by the organist, are called registration. In music theory, some analyses treat registral placement as an independent compositional parameter: composers may fix "registral" pitches so that a pitch class is always heard in the same octave position, a device used in some twelve-tone and post-tonal music.
