Plastic deformation

In physics and materials science, plasticity (also known as plastic deformation) is the ability of a solid material to undergo permanent deformation, a non-reversible change of shape in response to applied forces. For example, a solid piece of metal being bent or pounded into a new shape displays plasticity as permanent changes occur within the material itself.

Mechanisms

In crystalline materials such as metals, plasticity arises mainly from the motion of dislocations — line defects in the crystal lattice — under shear stress, a process called slip. Other mechanisms include twinning, grain-boundary sliding, and diffusion creep. In amorphous and polymeric materials, molecular chains slide past one another under load.

Yield and hardening

The transition from elastic to plastic behaviour occurs at the yield point, characterised by the material's yield strength. As deformation proceeds, many metals become stronger and harder — work hardening or strain hardening — as dislocation density increases. Theories of plasticity describe the relationship between stresses and plastic strains using yield criteria such as the von Mises and Tresca criteria and flow rules.

Applications

Plastic deformation is exploited in metal forming processes such as forging, rolling, extrusion, and bending, and is a central concern in structural engineering, where designs must normally keep stresses below yield to avoid permanent deformation.
