Social identity model of deindividuation effects

The SIDE model explains anonymity's effects through salient group identity
Proposed byRussell Spears, Martin Lea, Stephen Reicher and colleagues
FieldSocial psychology, computer-mediated communication
Key claimAnonymity shifts behaviour toward salient group norms rather than simply disinhibiting it
Original contextResearch on computer-mediated communication (CMC), 1990s

The social identity model of deindividuation effects (SIDE) is a theory in social psychology, developed by Russell Spears, Martin Lea and colleagues in the 1990s, that reinterprets the effects of anonymity and reduced social cues — especially in computer-mediated communication — in terms of social identity and self-categorization theory.

Background: classic deindividuation

Classic deindividuation theory (Le Bon, Festinger, and later Zimbardo and Diener) held that anonymity and immersion in a crowd reduce self-awareness and personal identity, loosening inhibitions and producing impulsive, antinormative behaviour. Meta-analytic work by Postmes and Spears (1998), however, found little evidence that anonymity causes antinormative behaviour in general; instead, anonymous participants conformed more strongly to whatever norms were salient in the situation.

The model

SIDE proposes that anonymity does not erase identity but shifts the balance between personal identity and social identity. When visual anonymity depersonalises perception of self and others, a salient group identity becomes more influential, and behaviour is guided by the norms of that group. The model distinguishes a cognitive dimension — anonymity strengthens the salience of group identity when a shared identity exists — and a strategic dimension — anonymity can shield a group's members from sanctions by a more powerful outgroup, making norm-consistent behaviour (including resistance) easier to express.

Evidence and applications

Experiments in which anonymous online groups were given pro- or anti-social norms showed conformity to those norms rather than general disinhibition. SIDE has been applied to online communities, flaming and trolling, virtual teams, political mobilisation on social media, and crowd behaviour, where the related elaborated social identity model of crowd behaviour (ESIM) makes a similar argument for physical crowds. The model is widely cited as a corrective to popular "online disinhibition" accounts.
