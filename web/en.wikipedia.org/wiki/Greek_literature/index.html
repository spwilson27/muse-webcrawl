Greek literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A papyrus fragment of Homer beside a marble bust of the poet]
A papyrus fragment of Homer beside a marble bust of the poet

Greek literature — literature written in the Greek language from antiquity to the present — begins with the epics of Homer and extends through the tragedians, historians and philosophers of classical Athens, Hellenistic and Roman-era writing, Byzantine literature, and the modern literature of Greece, whose poets have twice won the Nobel Prize (Giorgos Seferis, 1963; Odysseas Elytis, 1979). It is the longest continuously documented literary tradition in Europe.

Ancient literature

Homer's Iliad and Odyssey (8th century BC) were followed by Hesiod's didactic epics and the lyric poets — Sappho, Pindar, Alcaeus. Classical Athens produced the great dramas (Greek tragedy, Aristophanes' comedy), the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, the orators and the philosophical prose of Plato and Aristotle. Hellenistic Alexandria added scholarship, the pastoral (Theocritus) and the Greek Anthology; the Roman era gave Plutarch, Lucian and the Greek novel.

Byzantine and early modern

Byzantine literature preserved the classical inheritance through chronicles, hymns (Romanos the Melodist), theology and the epic Digenes Akritas, while the vernacular tradition developed. Under Ottoman rule, demotic song and the Cretan Renaissance (Erotokritos) kept the language alive; Adamantios Korais's purified katharevousa later split Greek letters between archaists and demoticists — a 'language question' resolved only in 1976 in demotic's favour.

Modern literature

Modern Greek literature includes the national poet Dionysios Solomos, the novelist Nikos Kazantzakis (Zorba the Greek, The Last Temptation of Christ), the Alexandrian Greek Constantine P. Cavafy — among the most influential poets of the twentieth century — and the Nobel laureates Seferis and Elytis, against the backdrop of war, civil war and dictatorship that marks the modern Greek canon.
