A tower house is a type of stone structure, built for defensive purposes as well as habitation, several storeys high and typically compact in plan. Tower houses are found across much of medieval and early modern Europe — most famously in Scotland and Ireland, where thousands were built between the 14th and 17th centuries — and analogous tall fortified houses occur elsewhere, from the tower houses of San Gimignano in Italy to the mud-brick tower houses of Yemen.

Characteristics

The classic tower house is a self-contained fortified dwelling: thick rubble or ashlar walls rise three to five storeys from a small footprint. The ground floor, often vaulted in stone against fire, served for storage and livestock, with the entrance sometimes at first-floor level reached by a removable stair. Above were the hall (the principal living room), private chambers, and a parapet walk or cap-house at the wall head. Defensive features included a single strong door, small windows, murder holes, bartizans (overhanging corner turrets) and, in Scotland, an iron gate or "yett". Many stood within a walled courtyard (a barmkin) containing ancillary buildings.

Scotland and Ireland

In Scotland, tower houses were the standard residence of lairds and lesser nobility from the later Middle Ages until the 17th century; surviving plans are classified as L-plan, Z-plan and others as wings were added for comfort. Examples include Castle Fraser, Craigievar Castle and Crathes Castle. In Ireland, tower houses were built by both Gaelic and Old English (Anglo-Norman) lords — some 3,000 are thought to have existed — the best-known being Blarney Castle and Bunratty Castle. Along the Anglo-Scottish border, the simpler peel towers served a similar role for smaller landholders.

Other regions

Feuding noble families in Italian cities built tall urban towers, of which the Two Towers of Bologna and the towers of San Gimignano survive. Defensive clan towers (kule) occur in the Balkans and Caucasus, and the tower houses of Shibam and Sana'a in Yemen — multi-storey mud-brick buildings — form some of the world's oldest "skyscraper" settlements.

Decline and legacy

As artillery made small private fortifications obsolete and political conditions stabilised, tower houses were enlarged into country houses or abandoned. Many were restored in the 19th and 20th centuries; some remain inhabited, and they are a defining element of the Scottish and Irish historic landscape.
