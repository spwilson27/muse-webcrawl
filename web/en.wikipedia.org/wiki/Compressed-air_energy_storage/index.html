Compressed-air energy storage

Compressed-air energy storage (CAES) is a way to store energy for later use using compressed air. At a utility scale, energy generated during periods of low demand can be released during peak load periods. It is a form of grid energy storage alongside pumped-storage hydroelectricity.

In a typical CAES plant, surplus electricity drives motor-driven compressors that pump air into an underground storage reservoir, commonly a salt cavern, depleted gas field, or aquifer, or into above-ground vessels. When electricity is needed, the compressed air is released, heated (usually by burning natural gas, or with stored compression heat in advanced designs), and expanded through a turbine to generate electricity.

Types

  • Diabatic CAES – the heat of compression is discarded, and the air must be reheated with fuel before expansion. The two long-running utility plants, at Huntorf, Germany (commissioned 1978, 290–321 MW) and McIntosh, Alabama, USA (1991, 110 MW), are diabatic.
  • Adiabatic CAES (A-CAES) – the heat generated during compression is captured in a thermal store and returned to the air during expansion, raising round-trip efficiency and avoiding fuel use.
  • Isothermal CAES – attempts to approach constant-temperature compression and expansion through slow, staged piston machinery and heat exchange, improving efficiency at smaller scales.

Efficiency and characteristics

Conventional diabatic plants achieve round-trip energy efficiencies of roughly 42–54% (the McIntosh plant with a recuperator is at the higher end), while adiabatic designs target around 70%. CAES plants can store very large amounts of energy — the Huntorf cavern stores air at pressures of roughly 48–66 bar — and provide long discharge durations, making CAES suited to bulk, long-duration storage, but suitable geology constrains siting. China has commissioned several large advanced CAES plants since 2022, including units that store compression heat.

History

Compressed-air storage concepts date to the 1940s; the first utility-scale plant was built at Huntorf in Germany by Nordwestdeutsche Kraftwerke, beginning operation in 1978. The McIntosh plant in Alabama followed in 1991. Numerous projects, including adiabatic and above-ground designs, have been developed since the 2000s as renewable generation has increased the need for long-duration storage.
