Sentence adverb

A sentence adverb (also called a disjunct) is a word or phrase that modifies a whole clause or sentence, rather than a single verb, adjective, or other adverb within it. Sentence adverbs typically express the speaker's attitude toward, or evaluation of, the content of the sentence: for example fortunately, frankly, surprisingly, honestly, and hopefully.

Examples

  • "Fortunately, the rain stopped." — the speaker is glad the rain stopped; fortunately does not describe how the rain stopped.
  • "Frankly, the plan will fail." — frankly comments on the manner of speaking.
  • "Sadly, no one survived." — an evaluative comment on the whole proposition.

Distinguishing features

Sentence adverbs often appear at the beginning or end of a sentence, or set off by commas as a parenthetical element, and can usually be paraphrased with a clause such as "it is fortunate that…". They contrast with ordinary adverbs of manner: in "She spoke frankly", frankly describes how she spoke, whereas in "Frankly, she was wrong" it qualifies the utterance itself.

The "hopefully" controversy

The use of hopefully as a sentence adverb meaning "it is hoped that" (as in "Hopefully, the weather will improve") was widely criticized by prescriptivists in the 20th century, who objected that hopefully should only mean "in a hopeful manner". By the early 21st century the sentence-adverb sense had been accepted by most dictionaries and style guides, and the AP Stylebook formally accepted it in 2012.

Disjuncts and adjuncts

Grammarians following Quirk et al. call sentence adverbs disjuncts when they present a comment on the form or content of the utterance (attitudinal and style disjuncts), contrasting them with adjuncts, which are integrated into the clause's structure, and conjuncts, which connect clauses.
