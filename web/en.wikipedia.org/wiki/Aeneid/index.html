Aeneid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Aeneas fleeing burning Troy carrying his father Anchises on his shoulders, his son Ascanius at his side — the founding image of Roman pietas]

The Aeneid is a Latin epic poem by Virgil, written between 29 and 19 BC, telling the legendary story of Aeneas, the Trojan hero who fled the sack of Troy, wandered the Mediterranean, and came to Italy to found the Roman people. Twelve books of dactylic hexameter, half Odyssean travel and half Iliadic war, it became Rome's national epic and the supreme model of the European epic tradition.

Aeneas, buffeted by Juno's wrath, is storm-driven to Carthage and Queen Dido — their love and his divinely ordered abandonment of her form Book 4, the poem's most famous movement. In Italy, after a descent to the underworld (Book 6) where his father's shade shows him Rome's future, he wages war against Turnus and the Latins, killing Turnus in a final burst of fury that readers have debated for two thousand years.

Composition and publication

Commissioned in effect by Augustus's cultural programme, the Aeneid glorifies the Julian line (Aeneas's son Iulus is its mythical ancestor) while registering empire's costs in Dido, in Turnus and in young lives spent. Virgil, dying at Brundisium in 19 BC, asked that the unfinished poem be burned; Augustus ordered its publication with minimal editing. Its opening word — Arma, 'arms' — announces both its Iliadic matter and, by tradition, literature's most famous first line, 'Arma virumque cano' (of arms and the man I sing, in the standard gloss).

Influence

The Aeneid was the school text of Latinity for two millennia; Dante chose Virgil as his guide through Hell and Purgatory, and every epic after — Camões, Spenser, Milton — writes in negotiation with it. John Dryden's 1697 translation fixed its English reputation; classroom Latin still begins, for millions, with Aeneas.
