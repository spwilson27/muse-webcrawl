Nizari Ismaili state

The Nizari Ismaili state (Persian: دولت اسماعیلیان نزاری), also known as the Alamut state, was a Shia Islamic state formed by the Nizari Ismailis in the late 11th century, after their split from the Fatimid Caliphate over the succession to Imam al-Mustansir. Under its founder, Hassan-i Sabbah, who seized the mountain fortress of Alamut in Persia in 1090, the Nizaris built a decentralised network of strongholds in the Alborz and Zagros mountains and in Syria, resisting the much larger Seljuk Empire and other powers for some 166 years.

History

Unable to confront the Seljuks in open battle, the Nizaris relied on fortified strongholds, disciplined organisation through the da'wa (mission), and targeted killings of enemy leaders carried out by devotees known as fidā'īs — the origin of the word "assassin" in Western languages, attached to the Nizaris ("Hashshashin") by their enemies and Crusader-era writers. In Syria, the leader Rashid al-Din Sinan — the "Old Man of the Mountain" of Crusader legend — maintained the state's independence against both Muslim rulers and the Crusader states.

In 1164 the lord of Alamut, Hasan II, proclaimed the qiyāma (resurrection), a radical spiritual reinterpretation of the law. The state survived until the Mongol invasions: in 1256 Hülegü's forces compelled the surrender of Alamut and its last lord, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, and massacred much of the community in Persia; the Syrian fortresses fell to the Mamluks by 1273.

Legacy

The Nizaris re-emerged under their imams, whose line continues in the Aga Khans; today the Nizari Ismailis form the largest branch of Ismaili Islam. Alamut was also famed for its great library and for scholars it sheltered, including Nasir al-Din al-Tusi.
