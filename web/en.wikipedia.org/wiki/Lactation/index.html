Lactation

Lactation is the secretion of milk from the mammary glands and the period of time during which a mother lactates to feed her young. It is the defining trait of mammals and is controlled by the hormones prolactin, which drives milk production, and oxytocin, which triggers the milk-ejection ("let-down") reflex. In humans, lactation supports breastfeeding, which health authorities recommend exclusively for about the first six months of life.

Physiology

Mammary glands develop during puberty and complete their maturation during pregnancy, when rising levels of estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin prepare alveolar cells to secrete milk (lactogenesis). Birth and the fall in progesterone initiate copious milk secretion. Suckling stimulates prolactin release, maintaining supply in proportion to demand, while oxytocin contracts myoepithelial cells to eject milk. The first secretion, colostrum, is rich in antibodies. Milk composition changes over a feed and over the course of lactation, adapting to the infant's needs.

Benefits and practice

Breast milk provides nutrition, immune factors, and hormones; breastfeeding is associated with reduced infections in infants and lower risks of certain cancers and metabolic disease in mothers. Common challenges include latch difficulties, mastitis, and insufficient supply; lactation can also be induced in non-pregnant individuals by hormonal and mechanical stimulation. Donor human milk banks provide milk for premature and ill infants.

In other mammals

All mammals lactate, though the males of one species, the Dayak fruit bat, can do so spontaneously. Lactation length varies from a few days in some rodents to years in great apes and elephants. In agriculture, dairy animals are managed to sustain lactation for human milk production.
