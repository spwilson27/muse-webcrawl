Special education

Special education (known as special-needs education, aided education, alternative provision, exceptional student education, special ed., or SPED) is the practice of educating students in a way that accommodates their individual differences, disabilities and special needs. It involves individually planned and systematically monitored teaching procedures, adapted equipment and materials, and accessible settings.

Approaches

Services are delivered across a continuum of placements. In inclusion models, students with disabilities spend most or all of the school day with non-disabled peers, supported by accommodations and co-teaching. Other placements include resource rooms, separate special classes, separate special schools, residential facilities and home or hospital instruction. The governing principle in many jurisdictions is education in the least restrictive environment appropriate to the student.

An individualized education program (IEP) — the central planning document in the United States and similar systems — sets out the student's present performance, measurable annual goals, services, accommodations and placement, and is developed by a team including teachers, specialists and parents.

Identification and categories

Under the U.S. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), eligibility categories include specific learning disabilities, speech or language impairments, autism, intellectual disability, emotional disturbance, hearing and visual impairments, orthopedic impairments, other health impairments, traumatic brain injury, multiple disabilities and developmental delay. Response-to-intervention and psychoeducational assessment are commonly used to identify needs.

History

Formal special education developed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with pioneers such as Pedro Ponce de León (education of the deaf), Jean-Marc Gaspard Itard, Édouard Séguin and Maria Montessori. For much of the twentieth century many children with disabilities were excluded from public schooling or segregated; parent advocacy and civil-rights litigation led to landmark legislation, including Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 and the Education for All Handicapped Children Act of 1975 (now IDEA) in the United States, and similar rights-based reforms in other countries. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) affirms a right to inclusive education internationally.

Instructional methods

Evidence-based practices include direct instruction, differentiated instruction, assistive technology, applied behaviour analysis, augmentative and alternative communication, and transition planning for post-school life. Ongoing debates concern the balance between inclusion and specialized settings, funding, teacher shortages and identification disparities among racial and linguistic groups.
