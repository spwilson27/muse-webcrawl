A gyroscope is a device used for measuring or maintaining orientation and angular velocity. It is a spinning wheel or disc in which the axis of rotation (spin axis) is free to assume any orientation by itself. When rotating, the orientation of this axis is unaffected by tilting or rotation of the mounting, because of the conservation of angular momentum.

History

The principle was exploited in early devices such as the spinning top. In 1817 Johann Gottlieb Friedrich von Bohnenberger described a device with a rotating sphere. In 1852 the French physicist Léon Foucault coined the name "gyroscope" (from Greek gyros, "circle", and skopein, "to look") for a device he used to study the Earth's rotation. Practical gyrocompasses followed in the early twentieth century, notably those of Hermann Anschütz-Kaempfe and Elmer Sperry.

Principles

The fundamental behaviour of a gyroscope follows from the equation τ = dL/dt: the applied torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum. When a torque is applied perpendicular to the spin axis, the gyroscope responds by precession — rotating about an axis perpendicular to both — rather than simply tipping over. A freely mounted gyroscope resists changes to its orientation, a property called gyroscopic rigidity or inertia.

Types

  • Mechanical gyroscopes — a spinning rotor in gimbals.
  • Optical gyroscopes — ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes exploiting the Sagnac effect.
  • MEMS gyroscopes — vibrating-structure devices used in smartphones and game controllers.
  • Quantum gyroscopes — based on atom interferometry.

Applications

Gyroscopes form the core of inertial navigation systems in aircraft, ships, spacecraft and submarines. They are used in gyrocompasses, artificial horizons, autopilots, camera and ship stabilisers, the Hubble Space Telescope's pointing system, and consumer electronics for screen rotation and motion sensing.
