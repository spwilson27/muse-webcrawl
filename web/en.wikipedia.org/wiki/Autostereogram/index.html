Autostereogram

An autostereogram is a two-dimensional image designed to create the visual illusion of a three-dimensional scene. Unlike earlier stereograms, which required a stereoscope or other viewing aid to combine two separate images, an autostereogram encodes depth within a single image, most often as a horizontally repeating pattern. When viewed with the eyes converged or diverged appropriately ("wall-eyed" or "cross-eyed" viewing), the repetitions fuse and a hidden 3D shape appears to float above or below the background.

History

In 1959 Béla Julesz invented the random-dot stereogram, demonstrating that depth perception can arise from binocular disparity alone, without monocular cues. Christopher Tyler and Maureen Clarke combined the random-dot principle with a single image in 1979, creating the first single-image random-dot stereograms. In the early 1990s Tom Baccei and Cheri Smith produced the Magic Eye series of books, which became a publishing phenomenon and fixed the autostereogram in popular culture.

How it works

The image consists of a pattern repeated horizontally; the spacing between repeats is modulated according to the desired depth map — wider spacing places features farther away (for wall-eyed viewing) and narrower spacing brings them forward. Because the pattern is near-periodic, the visual system can match features from the two eyes at an unintended separation, producing a perceived surface whose depth varies across the image.

Viewing technique

For parallel viewing, the viewer relaxes the eyes as if looking through the image at a distant object, until adjacent repetitions overlap and lock. For cross-eyed images, the eyes converge in front of the plane. People differ in which method they find easier, and some viewers never succeed; autostereograms also cannot be fused by viewers lacking binocular vision.
