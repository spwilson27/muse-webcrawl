Helix-turn-helix

Helix-turn-helix is a major structural motif in proteins that can bind DNA. It is composed of two alpha helices joined by a short strand of amino acids and is found in many proteins that regulate gene expression, particularly transcription factors in bacteria, and also in eukaryotes.

The helix-turn-helix motif of the lambda repressor

Structure

The motif consists of two α-helices separated by a sharp turn of a few amino acids, often including a glycine. The second, C-terminal helix — called the recognition helix — fits into the major groove of DNA, where its amino-acid side chains make sequence-specific contacts with the bases. The first helix helps position the recognition helix and may contact the DNA backbone. The canonical motif is about 20 amino acids long.

Discovery and examples

The helix-turn-helix was first recognised by comparison of the structures of bacterial regulatory proteins including Cro, CAP (catabolite activator protein) and the lambda repressor. Variants include the winged helix-turn-helix, with an additional beta-sheet "wing", and the helix-turn-helix motifs found in homeodomain proteins, which add a third helix. In bacterial sigma factors and repressors such as LacI-family and TetR-family proteins, the motif mediates operator binding.
