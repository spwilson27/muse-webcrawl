Calcium oxalate

Calcium oxalate (in archaic terminology, oxalate of lime) is a calcium salt of oxalic acid with the chemical formula CaC2O4. It forms hydrates CaC2O4·nH2O, where n varies from 1 to 3. Anhydrous and all hydrated forms are colourless or white solids. Calcium oxalate occurs widely in nature as the main constituent of most kidney stones and as crystals (raphides) in many plants, where it serves as a defense against herbivores.

Occurrence

The monohydrate mineral whewellite and the dihydrate mineral weddellite are natural forms of calcium oxalate. In plants, calcium oxalate crystals take forms such as needle-shaped raphides, prisms, and druses; they occur in foods including spinach, rhubarb, beetroot, and kiwifruit. Dieffenbachia and other aroids contain raphides that cause pain and swelling if eaten. Calcium oxalate also accumulates in lichens and in the scales that form inside brewing and water-heating equipment ("beerstone").

Medical significance

About three-quarters of kidney stones are composed wholly or partly of calcium oxalate. Stones form when urine becomes supersaturated with calcium and oxalate; risk factors include hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, low urine volume, and low urinary citrate. Dietary oxalate contributes to urinary oxalate, but calcium intake also matters: dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, so very low-calcium diets can paradoxically increase stone risk. In primary hyperoxaluria, a genetic overproduction of oxalate leads to systemic deposition of calcium oxalate (oxalosis) in kidneys, bones, and other organs.

Properties

Calcium oxalate is poorly soluble in water (about 6–7 mg/L at room temperature), and it is also insoluble in acetic acid but dissolves in strong acids. On heating it decomposes to calcium carbonate and then calcium oxide. Its low solubility underlies both its biological roles and its persistence in kidney stones.
