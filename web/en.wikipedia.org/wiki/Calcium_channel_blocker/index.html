Calcium channel blocker

Medical information in this article is general reference material and not medical advice.

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs), also called calcium antagonists, are a class of medications that disrupt the movement of calcium ions (Ca2+) through calcium channels, principally L-type voltage-gated calcium channels. They are widely used to treat hypertension (high blood pressure), angina pectoris, and certain cardiac arrhythmias, and are among the most commonly prescribed cardiovascular drugs worldwide.

Classes

Calcium channel blockers fall into two major groups based on chemical structure and site of action. Dihydropyridines — such as amlodipine, nifedipine, felodipine and lercanidipine — act mainly on vascular smooth muscle, causing vasodilation and lowering blood pressure; they have relatively little direct effect on the heart. Non-dihydropyridines comprise the phenylalkylamine verapamil, which acts strongly on the heart, slowing conduction through the atrioventricular node, and the benzothiazepine diltiazem, which has intermediate vascular and cardiac effects.

Mechanism of action

By binding to L-type calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle, CCBs reduce calcium entry needed for contraction, relaxing arteries and reducing peripheral resistance and blood pressure. In the heart, non-dihydropyridines reduce heart rate and contractility and slow AV nodal conduction, which is useful in rate control of atrial fibrillation and in treating angina.

Uses and side effects

Beyond hypertension and angina, CCBs are used for Raynaud's phenomenon, some arrhythmias, and (in the case of nimodipine) prevention of cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage. Common side effects of dihydropyridines include ankle swelling (peripheral oedema), headache, flushing and palpitations; verapamil frequently causes constipation, and non-dihydropyridines can worsen heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and interact with beta blockers. Overdose can cause severe hypotension and bradycardia. Grapefruit juice inhibits CYP3A4 metabolism and can raise levels of several CCBs.
