Artificial selection

Artificial selection (or selective breeding) is the process by which humans deliberately breed plants and animals for particular traits. It has produced domesticated crops and livestock — and the many breeds of dog, pigeon and horse — from wild ancestors over thousands of years.

History

Farmers have practised selection since the beginnings of agriculture, preserving seeds from the best plants and breeding the most useful animals. Charles Darwin used artificial selection as a key analogy in On the Origin of Species (1859), arguing that if humans could transform species so dramatically in a short time, natural selection acting over geological time could account for the diversity of life.

Methods

Breeders select parents with desired heritable traits, control mating, and cull or exclude undesired offspring. Modern programmes use pedigree records, quantitative genetics and genomic selection. Inbreeding can fix traits but also concentrates harmful recessive alleles, a concern in many pedigree dog breeds.

Examples

Wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea) has been selected into cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi and Brussels sprouts. Maize was transformed from teosinte, and wheat, rice and dairy cattle yields have increased manyfold through sustained selection.
