Gigabit Ethernet

In computer networking, Gigabit Ethernet (GbE or 1 GigE) is the term applied to transmitting Ethernet frames at a rate of a gigabit per second (1 billion bits per second). The most popular variant, 1000BASE-T, is defined by the IEEE 802.3ab standard. It came into use in 1999, and has largely replaced Fast Ethernet in wired local networks, as a result of being significantly faster and utilizing standard 4-pair CAT5e, CAT6 and CAT6A cabling.

History

Gigabit Ethernet was the result of research conducted at Xerox Corporation in the early 1970s developing into the Ethernet standard, and of the work of the Gigabit Ethernet Alliance formed in 1996. The first standard, 1000BASE-X for fiber optics (IEEE 802.3z), was ratified in 1998; the copper standard 1000BASE-T followed in 1999. By the mid-2000s, gigabit ports had become standard on desktop computers and servers, and gigabit switches had displaced 100-megabit equipment in most network backbones.

Gigabit Ethernet retains the Ethernet frame format and the CSMA/CD heritage of earlier standards, though in practice it is almost always used in full-duplex switched mode. It was succeeded by 10 Gigabit Ethernet, standardized in 2002, but remains ubiquitous at the network edge.
