Idea of progress

The idea of progress is the theory that advances in science, technology, modernization, economic development, and social organization are vital to improve the human condition. It conceives of history as moving in an upward, cumulative direction, such that each generation stands on the achievements of the last and can surpass them.

History

Although notions of improvement existed earlier, the idea of progress took shape during the Enlightenment. Thinkers such as Turgot, the Marquis de Condorcet — whose Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind (1795) presented history as the steady perfecting of human capacities — and later Auguste Comte treated progress as a law of history.

The Industrial Revolution seemed to confirm the doctrine through visible material improvement, and it became intertwined with ideologies such as liberalism and Marxism. Philosophers such as Hegel understood history as the unfolding of freedom, while Herbert Spencer extended progress to biological and social evolution.

Criticism

The catastrophes of the twentieth century — two world wars, the Holocaust, and nuclear weapons — led many to question whether technological advance guaranteed moral advance. Critics such as Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer argued in Dialectic of Enlightenment that instrumental reason could turn progress into domination. Environmental concerns have added the objection that material progress may be unsustainable.
