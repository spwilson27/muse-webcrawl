Equal temperament

Equal temperament is a system of musical tuning in which every pair of adjacent notes has an identical frequency ratio. In the dominant modern system, twelve-tone equal temperament (12-TET), the octave is divided into twelve equal semitones, each a frequency ratio of 21/12 (about 1.0595), or 100 cents. Because all intervals of the same size are then identical, music can modulate freely into any key — at the cost that no interval except the octave is perfectly in tune with the natural harmonic series.

Comparison with just intonation

In 12-TET the fifth is nearly pure (700 cents vs. the just 701.955), while thirds deviate more audibly: the major third is 400 cents against a just 386.3, almost 14 cents sharp. Earlier systems such as meantone kept thirds sweet in common keys but rendered remote keys unusable (with a "wolf" interval), while various well temperaments compromised unevenly by key. Equal temperament distributes the error uniformly, which suits fixed-pitch instruments such as the piano and fretted strings.

History

The principle was computed in China by Zhu Zaiyu (1584) and in Europe by Simon Stevin and Marin Mersenne in the 16th–17th centuries, but it was adopted only gradually: fretted instruments had long approximated it, while keyboard practice shifted through the 18th and 19th centuries as piano manufacture standardised. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier was written for a well temperament, not modern equal temperament. By the 20th century 12-TET had become the near-universal standard of Western music and the reference for electronic instruments. Other equal divisions (19, 31, 53 tones) are explored in microtonal music.
