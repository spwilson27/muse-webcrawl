STOBAR ("Short Take-Off But Arrested Recovery") is a system used for the launch and recovery of fixed-wing aircraft from the deck of an aircraft carrier. It combines elements of STOVL (short take-off and vertical landing) and CATOBAR (catapult-assisted take-off but arrested recovery): aircraft take off under their own power using a ski-jump ramp at the bow, but recover by landing on an angled deck fitted with arrestor wires, catching them with a tailhook.

Description

Unlike CATOBAR carriers, STOBAR ships have no aircraft catapults, which makes them cheaper and mechanically simpler to build and maintain. The upward-curved ski-jump converts some of the aircraft's forward speed into lift and gives it additional height and time to accelerate after leaving the deck. The trade-off is payload: because take-off relies only on the aircraft's engines and the ramp, STOBAR aircraft launch with reduced fuel and weapons loads compared with catapult launches, and the system cannot operate heavy fixed-wing airborne early warning aircraft such as the E-2 Hawkeye, so STOBAR navies rely on AEW helicopters instead.

Operators

The configuration was pioneered by the Soviet Navy with the Admiral Kuznetsov-class carriers and their Sukhoi Su-33 fighters. Russia's Admiral Kuznetsov remains in Russian service. China operates the ex-Soviet carrier Liaoning and the domestically built Shandong, flying the Shenyang J-15; its third carrier, Fujian, however, uses catapults (CATOBAR). India's INS Vikramaditya (the converted Soviet carrier Admiral Gorshkov) and the indigenous INS Vikrant operate MiG-29K fighters in the STOBAR configuration.
