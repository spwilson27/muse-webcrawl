Lebensraum

Lebensraum (German for "living space") is a German concept of expansionism and Völkisch nationalism. Developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, it held that Germany required additional territory — principally in Eastern Europe — for settlement and resources. Adolf Hitler adopted and radicalised the idea, making the conquest of Lebensraum in the East a central aim of Nazi ideology and policy, with catastrophic consequences for the peoples of the occupied territories.

Origins

The term was used by geographer Friedrich Ratzel in a biogeographical sense and was popularised after World War I by Karl Haushofer and the school of geopolitics. In the aftermath of Germany's defeat and territorial losses, the idea merged with resentment of the Versailles settlement, anti-Slavic racism, and ambitions for German agrarian settlement in the East, building on older notions of a German Drang nach Osten ("drive toward the East").

Nazi policy

Hitler set out the doctrine in Mein Kampf (1925), arguing that Germany's future lay in conquering land from the Soviet Union and its neighbours, whose Slavic and Jewish inhabitants were to be expelled, enslaved, or exterminated. After 1933 the concept guided rearmament and, from 1939, the invasions of Poland and the Soviet Union. Its implementation included the Hunger Plan, the expulsion and murder of millions, and the Generalplan Ost, the blueprint for the demographic transformation of Eastern Europe under German colonisation. Heinrich Himmler and the SS administered much of this programme through the Reich Commissariat for the Strengthening of Germandom.

Legacy

The pursuit of Lebensraum is regarded by historians as a principal driver of the Second World War in Europe and of Nazi crimes against humanity, including the Holocaust and the deaths of millions of Soviet civilians and prisoners of war. The term survives mainly as a historical concept and, in occasional polemical use, as shorthand for aggressive territorial expansion.
