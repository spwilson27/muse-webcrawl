Minkowski–Bouligand dimension

In fractal geometry, the Minkowski–Bouligand dimension, also known as the Minkowski dimension or box-counting dimension, is a way of determining the fractal dimension of a set S in a Euclidean space. To calculate it, the set is covered with boxes (or balls) of side length ε, and one counts how the number of boxes N(ε) needed grows as ε shrinks. The dimension is the exponent in the scaling law N(ε) ~ ε−dim, defined formally as a limit of log N(ε) / log(1/ε).

Computation and properties

Because the exact limit may not exist, upper and lower box dimensions are defined using limit superior and inferior. In practice the dimension is estimated by plotting log N(ε) against log(1/ε) for a range of box sizes and fitting a straight line — the box-counting method, widely applied to coastlines, images, and experimental data. The box dimension is always at least the Hausdorff dimension and can be strictly greater: for example, the rational numbers in [0,1] have Hausdorff dimension 0 but box dimension 1, since they are dense. For well-behaved fractals such as the Koch curve (dimension log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.26) the two notions agree. The concept is named for Hermann Minkowski and Georges Bouligand, and is a key tool of fractal analysis.
