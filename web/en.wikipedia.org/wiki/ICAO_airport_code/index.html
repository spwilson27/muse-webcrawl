ICAO airport code

ICAO airport code
Also called: ICAO location indicator
Length: 4 letters
Issued by: International Civil Aviation Organization
Published in: ICAO Doc 7910
Example: EGLL (London Heathrow), KJFK (New York JFK)

The ICAO airport code, or location indicator, is a four-letter code designating aerodromes around the world, defined by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and published in ICAO Document 7910, Location Indicators. These codes are used by air traffic control and airline operations such as flight planning, and unlike the three-letter IATA codes familiar to passengers, ICAO codes have a geographic structure and also cover small airfields, weather stations, and navigation facilities without IATA codes.

Structure

The first letter denotes a broad region (e.g., E for Northern Europe, K for the contiguous United States, C for Canada, Y for Australia, S for South America). The second letter usually identifies the country within the region: EGLL is in the E region, G for Great Britain. The remaining letters identify the specific airport, often derived from the city or airfield name. In the contiguous United States, ICAO codes are generally the IATA code prefixed with K (JFK → KJFK); Alaskan airports begin with PA and Hawaiian with PH. Canadian codes begin with C, and many are the IATA code with a Y or other letter prefix (YYZ → CYYZ).

Comparison with IATA codes

IATA codes (three letters) are used in passenger-facing contexts: tickets, baggage tags, and schedules. ICAO codes serve operational and professional users — pilots, controllers, meteorologists — and are used in flight plans, NOTAMs, and METAR weather reports. Not all airports have both codes: major airports typically have both, while tens of thousands of small airfields have only ICAO codes (or national equivalents).
