Electroplating


An electroplating bath with parts suspended as cathodes

Electroplating, also known as electrochemical deposition or electrodeposition, is a process for producing a metal coating on a solid substrate through the reduction of cations of that metal by means of a direct electric current. The part to be coated acts as the cathode (negative electrode) of an electrolytic cell; the electrolyte contains dissolved metal salts of the metal to be deposited; and the anode (positive electrode), usually of the same metal, dissolves or simply completes the circuit.

Process

The workpiece is cleaned and prepared, then immersed in the plating bath. When current flows, metal ions in solution gain electrons at the cathode surface and deposit as a thin metallic film. The thickness of the coating is governed by Faraday's laws of electrolysis — proportional to the current density and the time in the bath. Additives in the bath act as brighteners, levelers, and wetting agents to produce smooth, adherent deposits.

Common platings

Widely used coating metals include chromium (decorative and hard chrome), nickel, zinc and zinc alloys for corrosion protection of steel, copper as an undercoat and for printed circuit boards, tin, and precious metals such as gold and silver for electronics and jewelry. Hot-dip galvanizing is a related but distinct non-electrolytic coating process.

History

Luigi Valentino Brugnatelli performed early electrodeposition experiments in 1805, and commercial electroplating of silver and gold was patented in England around 1840 by the Elkington cousins, founding an industry for plated tableware.

Applications and concerns

Electroplating improves corrosion resistance, wear resistance, solderability, electrical conductivity, reflectivity, and appearance. Plating shops must manage hazardous bath chemistries, including historically hexavalent chromium and cyanide baths, and treat metal-bearing wastewater.
