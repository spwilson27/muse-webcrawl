Oath of allegiance

An oath of allegiance is an oath whereby a subject or citizen acknowledges a duty of allegiance and swears loyalty to a monarch, a state, or its constitution. Such oaths are commonly required of new citizens at naturalization, of persons entering military service, and of officials taking up public office.

History

Oaths of allegiance descend from the feudal bond of fealty, in which a vassal swore fidelity to his lord. In England, oaths to the Crown became instruments of religious and political conformity after the Reformation: the Oath of Supremacy (1534) required recognition of Henry VIII as head of the Church of England, and refusal — most famously by Thomas More — could be punished as treason. Successive statutes imposed oaths on officeholders, clergy, and members of Parliament, long excluding Catholics and nonconformists from public life.

Modern practice

United States

Immigrants becoming citizens take the Oath of Allegiance at a naturalization ceremony, renouncing former sovereignties and swearing to support and defend the Constitution. Federal and state officials, and members of the armed forces, take oaths of office in terms derived from Article VI of the Constitution. Several states also require loyalty oaths of public employees, some of which were struck down by the Supreme Court during the twentieth century as unconstitutionally vague or as infringing freedom of association.

United Kingdom and Commonwealth

Members of Parliament, judges, and members of the armed forces swear (or affirm) allegiance to the monarch and the monarch's heirs and successors. Naturalizing citizens likewise swear allegiance to the sovereign. The form of the British oath has periodically been debated by republicans and by those who object to swearing to a hereditary head of state.

Other countries

Many states, including Canada, Australia, Germany, and Singapore, require a citizenship oath or pledge. In some, allegiance is sworn to the constitution or the state rather than to a person.

Criticism

Oaths of allegiance have been criticized as compelled speech, as tests of belief rather than conduct, and, in monarchical systems, as anachronistic. Conscientious objectors, religious groups such as Quakers who refuse all oaths, and republican movements have all challenged their use; most modern systems provide for a secular affirmation instead of a religious oath.
