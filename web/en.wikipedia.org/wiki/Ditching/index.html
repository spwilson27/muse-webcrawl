Ditching

Ditching is a controlled emergency landing of an aircraft on water. Unlike seaplanes, which are designed to land on water, ordinary landplanes that ditch are rarely able to take off again and often break up or sink within minutes to hours; nevertheless, with good technique, a suitable sea state and prompt rescue, survivable ditchings have been achieved even by large airliners.

Technique and survival factors

Flight crews are trained to configure the aircraft (flaps set, gear retracted), approach at minimum speed along the swell in light winds, and touch down tail-low to avoid the nose or engines digging in and cartwheeling. Cabin preparation — securing loose items, briefing passengers, donning life jackets — greatly improves outcomes. Certifications require airliners to carry life rafts and jackets, and extended-range twin-engine operations (ETOPS) rules limit how far twinjets may fly from diversion airports.

Notable ditchings

The best-known case is US Airways Flight 1549, which on 15 January 2009 lost both engines to a bird strike after departing New York LaGuardia and ditched in the Hudson River; all 155 aboard survived — the "Miracle on the Hudson". In 1963 an Aeroflot Tupolev Tu-124 ditched in the Neva River in Leningrad with no fatalities. Other events ended less well: Ethiopian Airlines Flight 961 (1996), hijacked and out of fuel, broke up off the Comoros with heavy loss of life, showing the danger of touching down in rough seas with engines striking the water at different times. Wartime aviation produced many ditchings, and life-raft survival after them drove advances in rescue beacons and survival equipment.
