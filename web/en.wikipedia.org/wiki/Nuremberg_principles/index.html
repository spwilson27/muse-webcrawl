Nuremberg principles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Nuremberg principles are a set of guidelines for determining what constitutes a war crime, formulated by the UN International Law Commission in 1950 from the Charter and judgment of the Nuremberg Tribunal (1945–46). The seven principles affirm: individual criminal responsibility under international law; that domestic legality is no defence; that official position (including head of state) does not relieve responsibility; that superior orders are no defence where a moral choice was possible; the right to a fair trial; the definitions of crimes against peace (planning and waging aggressive war), war crimes and crimes against humanity; and complicity as a crime.

The principles converted Nuremberg from victors' justice into general law: they underpin the Genocide Convention (1948), the Geneva Conventions (1949), the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and the Rome Statute (1998) of the International Criminal Court, whose definitions they substantially anticipate. Principle IV's treatment of superior orders (a partial defence going to mitigation) remains the doctrinal hinge of military law worldwide.
