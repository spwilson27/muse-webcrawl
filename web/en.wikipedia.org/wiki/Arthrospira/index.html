Arthrospira is a genus of filamentous cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae") whose cells form characteristic open helices, or spirals. Species of Arthrospira, principally Arthrospira platensis and Arthrospira maxima, are cultivated worldwide as a food and dietary supplement and sold under the name spirulina. (The name "spirulina" is also used for the unrelated genus Spirulina in the strict taxonomic sense, which is not the organism of commerce.)

Biology and habitat

Arthrospira grows naturally in warm, highly alkaline and saline lakes, mainly in tropical and subtropical regions. It is an oxygenic photosynthetic bacterium: its filaments consist of cylindrical cells arranged in a left-handed helix, and it reproduces by fragmentation of the filaments. Notable natural sources include Lake Texcoco in Mexico, Lake Chad in Africa, and alkaline lakes of the East African Rift Valley, where it forms a major food of lesser flamingos.

History of use

The Aztecs harvested Arthrospira from Lake Texcoco, drying it into cakes called tecuitlatl, a practice described by Spanish chroniclers. Around Lake Chad, the Kanembu people have traditionally harvested it as dihé, dried into cakes for sauces. Commercial production in open raceway ponds began in the 1970s, notably in Mexico and later at large facilities in California and Hawaii.

Nutrition and uses

Dried Arthrospira is roughly 55–70 per cent protein by weight and contains B vitamins, iron, carotenoids and the blue pigment phycocyanin, which is also used as a natural food colouring. It is sold as powder, flakes and tablets and used in aquaculture and livestock feed. Claims of therapeutic effects have been widely promoted, but systematic reviews find the evidence for most health claims limited; the organism is generally regarded as safe, though supplements can be contaminated if produced in uncontrolled conditions.
