Public works

Public works are infrastructure projects built, financed or maintained by government for the use and benefit of the general public. The category includes roads, bridges, railways, airports and ports; water supply, sewerage and flood control systems; public buildings such as schools, hospitals and courthouses; parks and other civic amenities. Public works departments — at municipal, regional and national levels — plan, procure and oversee such projects, typically contracting construction to private firms.

History

States have undertaken public works since antiquity: Roman roads and aqueducts, imperial Chinese canals and flood works, and state irrigation systems are classic examples. In the nineteenth century, railway building, urban sanitation and water supply transformed cities. The term acquired special resonance during the Great Depression, when governments used public works spending to create employment: in the United States, the Public Works Administration (1933) funded large projects such as dams, bridges and schools, while the Works Progress Administration employed millions on smaller local projects. The Tennessee Valley Authority combined power generation, flood control and regional development.

Economic rationale

The standard justification for public provision is that infrastructure has characteristics of public goods and natural monopolies: networks are capital-intensive, benefits are diffuse and long-lived, and markets alone may under-provide them. Public works spending is also used counter-cyclically as fiscal stimulus, based on the multiplier argument that construction employment generates further demand. Critics point to cost overruns, politically driven project selection ("pork barrel" spending), corruption risks in procurement, and maintenance backlogs when new construction is favoured over upkeep.

Modern practice

Modern public works are delivered through a mix of direct government works departments, public corporations, concessions and public–private partnerships, in which private consortia finance, build and operate facilities for a period in return for tolls or availability payments. Life-cycle costing, competitive tendering rules and audit institutions aim to secure value for money, and environmental and social impact assessments are now standard for major projects in most jurisdictions.
