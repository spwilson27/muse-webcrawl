Zaporozhian Cossacks

The Zaporozhian Cossacks were a Cossack community that arose in the steppe lands beyond the Dnieper rapids — za porohamy, "beyond the rapids" — in what is now central and southern Ukraine. Organised in the Zaporozhian Sich, a fortified camp and self-governing host, they became a major military and political force in Eastern Europe from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century.

Origins and organisation

The Sich was governed by a general assembly (rada) of all Cossacks, which elected its officers, including the kosh otaman. New members, often fugitive serfs, townspeople and adventurers, joined as free warriors. The host combined military campaigns — by boat on the Black Sea and by land against Tatar and Ottoman forces — with farming, fishing and trade in the steppe.

Relations with neighbouring powers

The Zaporozhians served at various times the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, which registered some of them in its service, and later entered the orbit of the Tsardom of Russia after the uprising led by Bohdan Khmelnytsky in 1648 and the Pereiaslav agreement of 1654. Tensions with both Polish and Russian authorities were recurrent.

Destruction of the Sich

As the Russian Empire consolidated control over the steppe and the Ottoman frontier receded, the Sich came to be seen as an obstacle to centralised rule. In June 1775, troops of Catherine the Great surrounded and destroyed the Sich; its lands were annexed and many Cossacks fled to Ottoman territory, forming the Danubian Sich, while others were later resettled as the Black Sea and Kuban Cossacks.

Legacy

The Zaporozhians occupy a central place in Ukrainian historical memory and literature, celebrated in folk songs (dumy), in Nikolai Gogol's novel Taras Bulba, and in Ilya Repin's famous painting Reply of the Zaporozhian Cossacks (1891), depicting their defiant letter to the Ottoman sultan.
