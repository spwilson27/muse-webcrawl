Serbo-Croatian (srpskohrvatski, српскохрватски) is a South Slavic language and the primary language of Serbia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Montenegro. It is a pluricentric language with four standard varieties—Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian, and Montenegrin—which are all based on the Shtokavian dialect and are mutually intelligible. Speakers can generally communicate without difficulty across the varieties; the differences lie mainly in vocabulary preferences, some phonological features (notably the reflex of the vowel jat), and writing systems.

History

The modern standard language began to take shape in the nineteenth century. The Vienna Literary Agreement of 1850, signed by Serbian and Croatian linguists and writers including Vuk Karadžić and Ljudevit Gaj, established a common literary language based on the Eastern Herzegovinian Shtokavian dialect. During the period of Yugoslavia (1918–1992), Serbo-Croatian functioned as a single official language, codified jointly in the Novi Sad Agreement of 1954, though with recognised "variants" (Eastern and Western) and two scripts.

Following the breakup of Yugoslavia in the 1990s, the constituent republics asserted separate standard languages, and the name "Serbo-Croatian" fell out of official use in the region, though it remains common in linguistics and international usage. In 2017, linguists and writers from all four countries signed the Declaration on the Common Language, affirming that a single polycentric language is in use.

Dialects and features

The language's dialects are traditionally classified by their interrogative pronoun ("what"): Shtokavian (što), Kajkavian (kaj), and Chakavian (ča). Shtokavian subdivides by the reflex of Old Slavic jat into Ekavian (Serbia), Ijekavian (Bosnia, Montenegro, most of Croatia's standard), and Ikavian varieties.

Serbo-Croatian is highly inflected, with seven cases for nouns, three genders, and a rich verbal system including a pitch-accent system preserved in the standard. It is written in both the Latin alphabet (used in Croatian, Bosnian, and Montenegrin) and Cyrillic (official in Serbia, and co-official in Bosnia and Montenegro); Serbian usage is genuinely digraphic.
