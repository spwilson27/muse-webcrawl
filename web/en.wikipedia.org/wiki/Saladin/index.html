Saladin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Equestrian statue of Saladin before the Citadel of Damascus]

Saladin (c. 1137 – 4 March 1193), known in Arabic as Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb, was a Kurdish Muslim ruler who founded the Ayyubid dynasty, united Egypt and Syria, and recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187. Vizier of Fatimid Egypt from 1169, he abolished the Fatimid caliphate, then spent a decade bringing Muslim Syria under his rule, before destroying the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at Hattin (1187) and taking Jerusalem — after a negotiated surrender notably more merciful than the Crusaders' capture of the city in 1099. His chivalrous reputation in both Muslim and Christian tradition survived his wars with Richard the Lionheart in the Third Crusade.

Rise and unification

Born in Tikrit and raised in Syria, Saladin served the Zengid ruler Nur al-Din, accompanying his uncle Shirkuh's expeditions to Egypt. As vizier he ended the Shi'a Fatimid caliphate (1171), restored Sunni rule, and after Nur al-Din's death (1174) took Damascus, Aleppo (1183) and Mosul's allegiance, surrounding the Crusader states. His empire rested on a military household of mamluks and Kurdish and Turkmen troops, and on careful diplomacy.

Hattin, Jerusalem and the Third Crusade

In July 1187 Saladin annihilated the Crusader field army at the Horns of Hattin, executing Reynald of Châtillon personally and capturing King Guy and the True Cross; Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October 1187, its inhabitants ransomed rather than massacred. The fall of the city provoked the Third Crusade (1189–1192): Richard I of England retook Acre and won at Arsuf but could not retake Jerusalem; the Treaty of Jaffa (1192) left the coast to the Crusaders and pilgrim access to Jerusalem. Saladin died months later in Damascus, his personal fortune famously small for a sultan.
