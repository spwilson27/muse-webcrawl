New York Convention

The Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, commonly known as the New York Convention, is an international treaty adopted by the United Nations in 1958 that requires the courts of contracting states to recognise and enforce arbitration agreements and foreign arbitral awards. With more than 170 contracting states, it is widely regarded as the foundational instrument of modern international commercial arbitration and the principal reason arbitration is preferred over litigation for cross-border commercial disputes.

History

The Convention was adopted on 10 June 1958 at a UN conference in New York and entered into force on 7 June 1959. It replaced the Geneva Convention of 1927, whose "double exequatur" requirement — leave to enforce had to be obtained first in the country where the award was made — had made enforcement slow and uncertain. The new instrument shifted the burden: an award is presumptively enforceable, and it is for the party resisting enforcement to prove a ground for refusal.

Main obligations

Article II requires courts to recognise written arbitration agreements and to refer parties to arbitration when sued on a matter covered by such an agreement, unless it is null and void or incapable of being performed. Article III requires recognition of arbitral awards as binding and their enforcement under local procedural rules without substantially more onerous conditions than apply to domestic awards. Article IV sets out the documents a party must supply — the authenticated award and the arbitration agreement, with translations.

Grounds for refusal

Article V lists exhaustive grounds on which recognition may be refused: incapacity of a party or invalidity of the agreement; lack of proper notice or inability to present one's case; an award exceeding the scope of the submission; improper composition of the tribunal or procedure; an award not yet binding or set aside at the seat; non-arbitrability of the subject matter; and conflict with the public policy of the enforcing state. Courts have generally construed these grounds narrowly, and the public-policy defence is confined to violations of the forum's most basic notions of morality and justice.

Significance

Because a New York Convention award can be enforced in most trading nations — while a foreign court judgment often cannot — the Convention underpins the global system of international arbitration administered by institutions such as the ICC, LCIA and SIAC, and by ad hoc tribunals under the UNCITRAL Rules. It applies only to commercial awards and is complemented by separate regimes, notably the ICSID Convention for investor-state dispute settlement, whose awards are self-enforcing within that system.
