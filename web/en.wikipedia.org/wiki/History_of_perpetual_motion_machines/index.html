History of perpetual motion machines

ConceptPerpetual motion (perpetuum mobile)
StatusRuled impossible by the laws of thermodynamics

The history of perpetual motion machines records centuries of attempts to build a machine that works forever without an energy source — devices the laws of thermodynamics show to be impossible, yet which continue to fascinate inventors.

Early designs

Indian astronomer Bhāskara II sketched a mercury-filled perpetual wheel around 1150. Villard de Honnecourt (c. 1235) drew an overbalanced wheel, the classic design in which shifting weights supposedly keep one side always heavier. Renaissance engineers, including Leonardo da Vinci — who denounced the quest — examined such schemes.

Scientific verdict

Simon Stevin's statics and, decisively, the formulation of energy conservation (1840s) and the second law of thermodynamics (Clausius, Kelvin) proved both perpetual motion of the first kind (creating energy) and the second kind (perfect heat-to-work conversion) impossible. Patent offices, burned by frauds such as the Redheffer and Keely machines, began demanding working models.

Modern era

"Free energy" claims persist on the fringes, sometimes as investment scams, while legitimate near-frictionless devices (flywheels, superconducting bearings) merely minimise losses. The idea survives as a teaching example of why conservation laws constrain engineering.
