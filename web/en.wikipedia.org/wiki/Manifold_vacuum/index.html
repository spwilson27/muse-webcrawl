Manifold vacuum

Manifold vacuum, or engine vacuum, in an internal combustion engine is the difference in air pressure between the intake manifold and ambient atmospheric pressure. It occurs in a petrol (spark-ignition) engine because the flow of air-fuel mixture is restricted by the throttle plate, causing a pressure drop below atmospheric on the manifold side while the pistons draw the charge into the cylinders.

Overview

At idle with the throttle nearly closed, manifold vacuum is highest — typically 18 to 22 inches of mercury (inHg). As the throttle opens under load, vacuum decreases; at wide-open throttle it approaches zero. Diesel engines, which generally lack a throttle plate, produce little manifold vacuum and use mechanical vacuum pumps where vacuum is needed.

Uses

  • Operating accessories: historically vacuum-powered windscreen wipers, and today brake boosters (servos), which use stored manifold vacuum to reduce pedal effort.
  • Engine management: a manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor measures the inverse quantity for fuel injection and ignition control.
  • Diagnostics: a vacuum gauge reading reveals valve, ignition, exhaust-restriction, and vacuum-leak problems from the pattern of needle movement.
  • Crankcase ventilation (PCV) and evaporative-emission purge systems draw on manifold vacuum.

Measurement

Manifold vacuum is conventionally measured in inches or millimetres of mercury below atmospheric pressure. Manifold absolute pressure, used in modern engine control, expresses the same condition as an absolute value and equals atmospheric pressure minus the vacuum reading.
