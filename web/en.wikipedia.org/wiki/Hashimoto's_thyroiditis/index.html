Hashimoto's thyroiditis, also known as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and Hashimoto's disease, is an autoimmune disease in which the thyroid gland is gradually destroyed by the immune system. It is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in developed countries. The condition is named after the Japanese physician Hakaru Hashimoto, who first described it in 1912.

Signs and symptoms

The disease typically progresses slowly over years. Early on, antibody-mediated attack may cause painless enlargement of the gland (goitre) and sometimes a transient phase of excess hormone release. As functional tissue is lost, hypothyroidism develops, with fatigue, weight gain, sensitivity to cold, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, depression and menstrual irregularities. It affects women far more often than men (at least 10:1) and usually presents between ages 30 and 50.

Causes and diagnosis

Risk factors include genetic susceptibility and other autoimmune disorders such as type 1 diabetes and coeliac disease; iodine excess may be an environmental trigger. Diagnosis rests on elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with low or normal free T4, and the presence of anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies; ultrasound may show a heterogeneous, hypoechoic gland.

Treatment

Hypothyroidism is treated with daily levothyroxine, titrated to normalise TSH; treatment is usually lifelong. There is no therapy that reverses the underlying autoimmune process, though selenium and vitamin D correction are sometimes discussed. Patients have a small increased risk of thyroid lymphoma.
