Kenneth Neal Waltz (8 June 1924 – 12 May 2013) was an American political scientist and founder of neorealism, or structural realism, in international relations theory. He taught at Swarthmore, Brandeis, and the University of California, Berkeley.

Work

Waltz's first book, Man, the State, and War (1959), organised explanations of war into three "images": human nature, the internal structure of states, and the anarchic structure of the international system. His Theory of International Politics (1979), one of the most influential books in the field, developed the third image into a systematic theory: because the system is anarchic — lacking any authority above states — and states seek survival, outcomes are driven by the distribution of capabilities across units, above all by polarity, the number of great powers. Waltz argued that bipolar systems are comparatively stable and that states balance against power rather than bandwagoning with it.

He famously contended, in "The Spread of Nuclear Weapons: More May Be Better" (1981), that gradual nuclear proliferation could enhance stability by raising the costs of war — a position debated ever since. His theory, criticised for neglecting domestic politics, institutions, and change, remains the benchmark against which rivals in international-relations theory define themselves.
