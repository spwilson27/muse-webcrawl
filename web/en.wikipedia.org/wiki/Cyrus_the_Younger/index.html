Cyrus the Younger (Old Persian: Kūruš; c. 424 – 401 BC) was an Achaemenid prince and general, the younger son of King Darius II of Persia and his wife Parysatis. As satrap of the western provinces of Asia Minor he intervened in the Peloponnesian War on the Spartan side, and after his father's death he raised a revolt against his elder brother, King Artaxerxes II. His expedition — famous through Xenophon's Anabasis, which recounts the march of his Greek mercenaries, the "Ten Thousand" — ended with his death at the Battle of Cunaxa in 401 BC.

Satrap of Asia Minor

Appointed in 408/407 BC as karanos (commander) and satrap of Lydia, Greater Phrygia and Cappadocia, Cyrus was sent to the coast with instructions and money — 500 talents of his own — to support Sparta against Athens. He cultivated the Spartan admiral Lysander, funding the Spartan fleet that won the war, and formed lasting ties with Greek soldiers and exiles. He also quarrelled bitterly with Tissaphernes, satrap of Caria, an enmity that later proved fatal.

Revolt

When Darius died in 404 BC, Parysatis — who favoured Cyrus, her younger but preferred son — could not secure him the throne; Artaxerxes II succeeded. Tissaphernes denounced Cyrus for plotting against the king's life, but Parysatis's intervention saved him and he returned to his satrapy. There he quietly assembled an army, using the pretext of expeditions against Pisidian tribes and a dispute over the Ionian cities, and recruited Greek mercenaries through agents such as Clearchus of Sparta. In spring 401 BC he marched inland from Sardis with perhaps 20,000 men, including some 13,000 Greek hoplites and peltasts.

Battle of Cunaxa

At Cunaxa, north of Babylon, Cyrus met Artaxerxes' much larger army in the late summer of 401 BC. The Greek mercenaries on Cyrus's right routed the troops opposite them, but Cyrus, seeing his brother in the centre, charged directly at him with his bodyguard of 600 horsemen. He wounded Artaxerxes but was himself struck — by a javelin, according to Xenophon — and killed. His head and right hand were cut off and displayed. The victorious but leaderless Greeks then began their celebrated fighting retreat to the Black Sea, the "March of the Ten Thousand" recorded in Xenophon's Anabasis.

Character and sources

Xenophon, who served in the expedition, gives a glowing obituary: Cyrus was, he says, the most kingly and worthy-to-rule Persian since Cyrus the Great, generous to friends, harsh to enemies, and scrupulously faithful to his word — "no one ever deserted Cyrus for the King, but many deserted the King for Cyrus." Other accounts, such as the fragments of Ctesias, the king's physician who treated Artaxerxes' wound, are less partisan. Cyrus's revolt exposed the empire's dependence on Greek infantry and foreshadowed the vulnerability that Alexander the Great would exploit seventy years later.
