Jacob, later given the name Israel, is regarded as a patriarch of the Israelites and is an important figure in Abrahamic religions, including Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and the Baháʼí Faith. He first appears in the Book of Genesis as the son of Isaac and Rebekah, the grandson of Abraham, and the younger twin brother of Esau.

Biblical narrative

According to Genesis, Jacob obtained his brother Esau's birthright in exchange for food and, with Rebekah's help, received the blessing intended for Esau from the blind Isaac. Fleeing Esau's anger, Jacob travelled to Haran, where he dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven at Bethel. He worked for his uncle Laban for many years, marrying Laban's daughters Leah and Rachel, and fathered twelve sons and a daughter.

Wrestling and the name Israel

On his return to Canaan, Jacob is said to have wrestled through the night with a mysterious figure—described variously as God, an angel, or a man—at the ford of the Jabbok. He was given the name Israel, traditionally interpreted as "he who strives with God", and the nation descended from his sons took that name. His twelve sons are presented as the ancestors of the Twelve Tribes of Israel.

Later life

Jacob's favoured son Joseph was sold into slavery in Egypt by his brothers but rose to become a high official. During a famine Jacob and his household migrated to Egypt, where he died; his body was returned to Canaan for burial in the Cave of Machpelah.

In Islam and scholarship

In Islam, Jacob (Yaʿqūb) is honoured as a prophet. Modern biblical scholarship treats the Jacob narratives as composite texts whose historical basis is debated, while recognising their central role in Israelite identity and theology.
