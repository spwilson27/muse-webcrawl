Cyberpunk derivatives

The popularity of cyberpunk in science fiction has given rise to a large family of derivative genres, often collectively called the "-punk" genres. These subgenres typically combine a distinctive technology or aesthetic with a countercultural, rebellious attitude, transplanting cyberpunk's "high tech, low life" spirit into other historical periods, technological paradigms, or moods.

Steampunk

The best-known derivative, steampunk, reimagines Victorian-era or Edwardian settings powered by steam technology, brass machinery, airships, and clockwork computers. The term was coined by author K. W. Jeter in 1987. Works such as The Difference Engine (1990) by William Gibson and Bruce Sterling helped define it, and it has grown into a broader aesthetic and maker subculture.

Other derivatives

Dieselpunk draws on the interwar and World War II era: diesel engines, riveted steel, art deco, and pulp adventure. Biopunk focuses on biotechnology and genetic engineering. Nanopunk centres on nanotechnology. Clockpunk uses Renaissance-era clockwork and spring-driven devices, while Atompunk celebrates the Atomic Age of the 1950s and 1960s — rayguns, rockets, and nuclear optimism. Stonepunk, Bronzepunk, and similar coinages push the formula into earlier technological eras.

Later and optimistic derivatives

A newer wave reacts against the dystopian tone of the earlier genres. Solarpunk imagines sustainable, ecologically integrated futures powered by renewable energy, emphasising community and optimism. Hopepunk, a term coined in 2017, frames hope and kindness as acts of resistance. Lunarpunk offers a nocturnal, mystical counterpart to solarpunk's sunlit aesthetic.

Criticism

Critics have noted a proliferation of "-punk" labels, sometimes applied as marketing shorthand where the rebellious "punk" element is thin. Others defend the derivatives as a flexible toolkit for exploring how technology shapes society in different eras and for reimagining the future.
