The tortilla de patatas, also known as tortilla española or Spanish omelette, is an omelette made with eggs and potatoes, optionally including onion, usually slow-fried in olive oil. It is one of the best-known dishes in Spanish cuisine, eaten across Spain as a tapa, a light meal, or in a sandwich (bocadillo de tortilla).

Preparation

Potatoes are peeled, thinly sliced or diced, and gently poached in abundant olive oil until tender — confit rather than crisped — often with onion. The drained potato is mixed with beaten, salted eggs and rested, then the mixture is cooked in a frying pan until set on one side and deftly flipped using a plate to cook the other. The finished tortilla is thick and compact; cooks debate whether the centre should be firm (cuajada) or slightly runny (jugosa, as famously served in Betanzos, Galicia).

The onion debate

Whether tortilla should include onion splits Spain into concebollistas (with onion) and sincebollistas (without). Surveys generally favour onion, but purists argue the original contained none. The debate is a perennial of Spanish food culture.

History

Potatoes arrived from the Americas in the 16th century; the dish is documented by the early 19th century in Navarre. A popular legend credits General Tomás de Zumalacárregui with inventing or popularising it during the First Carlist War (1830s) as cheap, filling food for troops; another attributes it to an anonymous Navarrese housewife. By the early 20th century it was established nationwide and appears in classic accounts of Spanish cooking.

Variations and serving

Regional and modern variants add chorizo, peppers, spinach, cod or mushrooms. It is served in wedges as a tapa, at room temperature at picnics and bars, and in sandwiches. In Spanish, the word tortilla means "little cake"; the dish is unrelated to the Mexican corn or wheat tortilla of the same name (from the same word).
