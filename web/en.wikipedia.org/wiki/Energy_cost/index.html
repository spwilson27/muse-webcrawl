Energy cost

Energy cost refers to the price paid for energy in its various forms — electricity, natural gas, petroleum products, coal and heat — and, in engineering and economics, to the total cost of producing or delivering a unit of energy, including capital, fuel, operating, maintenance, network and environmental components. Energy costs are a major component of household budgets, industrial competitiveness and national inflation, and their level and volatility shape energy policy worldwide.

Levelised costs have fallen dramatically for wind and solar power since 2010.

Levelised cost of energy

The levelised cost of energy (LCOE) is the average net present cost of electricity generation for a plant over its lifetime, expressed per unit of electricity generated. It allows consistent comparison between sources with very different cost structures: nuclear and fossil plants have high capital but low running costs per unit fuel, while wind and solar have near-zero marginal cost but higher upfront investment per unit of expected output. Annual surveys by Lazard and others documented steep declines in utility-scale solar and onshore wind LCOE during the 2010s, making them the cheapest sources of new bulk electricity in many regions.

Retail prices

The price paid by end users differs from generation cost because it includes transmission and distribution charges, retail supply costs, taxes and policy levies. Residential electricity prices vary widely between countries — from a few US cents per kWh in some energy-rich states to over 30 cents in much of Europe — and fuel prices spiked globally during the 2021–2023 energy crisis following the COVID-19 recovery and Russia's invasion of Ukraine, prompting government price caps and subsidies in many countries.

Energy costs and households

When energy absorbs a large share of income, households experience energy poverty (or fuel poverty), defined in the UK as spending more than 10% of income on keeping a home adequately warm. Rising energy costs disproportionately affect low-income households, which spend a higher fraction of income on heating and electricity, and they feed directly into consumer price indices, making energy a key driver of inflation and monetary policy.
