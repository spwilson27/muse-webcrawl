Colossus was a set of computers developed by British codebreakers in the years 1943–1945 to help in the cryptanalysis of the Lorenz cipher. Colossus used thermionic valves (vacuum tubes) to perform Boolean and counting operations. It is thus regarded as the world's first programmable, electronic, digital computer, although it was programmed by switches and plugs and not by a stored program.

Colossus was designed by General Post Office (GPO) research telephone engineer Tommy Flowers to solve a problem posed by mathematician Max Newman at the Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS) at Bletchley Park.

Purpose and operation

The prototype, Colossus Mark 1, was shown to be working in December 1943 and was in use at Bletchley Park by early 1944. An improved Colossus Mark 2 that used shift registers to quintuple the processing speed first worked on 1 June 1944, just in time for the Normandy landings on D-Day. Ten Colossi were in use by the end of the war, decrypting messages enciphered with the German Lorenz SZ40/42 teleprinter cipher, codenamed "Tunny" by the British.

Secrecy and legacy

The existence of the Colossus machines was kept secret until the 1970s; most were dismantled and their blueprints destroyed after the war, which delayed recognition of their place in computing history. A functional rebuild of a Mark 2 Colossus was completed in 2007 by Tony Sale and a team of volunteers at The National Museum of Computing.
