Tryptamine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

IUPAC name2-(1H-indol-3-yl)ethanamine
Chemical formulaC10H12N2
Molar mass160.22 g/mol
AppearanceWhite to orange crystalline powder
Melting point113–116 °C
PrecursorTryptophan (by decarboxylation)

Tryptamine is an indolamine metabolite of the essential amino acid tryptophan. Its chemical structure is defined by an indole ring — a fused benzene and pyrrole ring system — bearing an amino group via a two-carbon side chain at the third carbon of the indole. Tryptamine is the structural backbone of a large family of compounds known collectively as tryptamines, which include important neurotransmitters and psychedelic drugs.

Tryptamine itself is found in trace amounts in the mammalian brain and is thought to act as a neuromodulator or neurotransmitter, as well as a trace amine. It is produced by decarboxylation of tryptophan by the enzyme aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, and is rapidly metabolized by monoamine oxidase.

Substituted tryptamines

Many biologically significant compounds are tryptamine derivatives. The neurotransmitter serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) and the hormone melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) are both substituted tryptamines with central roles in mood, sleep, and circadian regulation. Plants contain a wide variety of tryptamine alkaloids; the Ipomoea and many other plant families produce tryptamine derivatives with various ecological functions.

A number of substituted tryptamines are psychedelic: psilocybin and psilocin (from "magic mushrooms"), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), 5-MeO-DMT, and bufotenin, whose effects are attributed mainly to agonist activity at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. Synthetic tryptamines, including alpha-methyltryptamine and many designer compounds, have been explored in research and, in some cases, controlled as drugs of abuse.

Biosynthesis and metabolism

In animals, tryptophan is decarboxylated to tryptamine, though most dietary tryptophan flows instead through the kynurenine pathway or toward serotonin synthesis. In plants and microorganisms, tryptamine is a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of indole alkaloids, including the monoterpene indole alkaloids such as those in Catharanthus roseus. In the gut, microbial decarboxylation of dietary tryptophan produces tryptamine that can influence gastrointestinal motility through trace amine-associated receptors.

Pharmacology

Tryptamine is an agonist of trace amine-associated receptor 1 (TAAR1) and interacts with serotonin receptors, particularly 5-HT2A, at higher concentrations. Because it is rapidly broken down by monoamine oxidase, orally administered tryptamine has little central effect unless combined with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor.
