Heat equation

Heat equation
TypeParabolic partial differential equation
Introduced byJoseph Fourier, 1822
Form∂u/∂t = α∇²u
ApplicationsHeat conduction, diffusion, finance, image processing

The heat equation is a partial differential equation that describes how the distribution of some quantity (such as heat) evolves over time in a solid medium, as it spontaneously flows from higher-temperature regions to lower ones. In one spatial dimension it reads ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x², and in general ∂u/∂t = α∇²u, where α is the thermal diffusivity of the material.

Derivation and meaning

The equation follows from Fourier's law of heat conduction (heat flux is proportional to the negative temperature gradient) combined with conservation of energy. Intuitively, the rate of change of temperature at a point is proportional to the difference between that point's temperature and the average temperature of its immediate surroundings, expressed by the Laplacian ∇²u; peaks cool and troughs warm, so the distribution "smooths out" over time.

Origin and Fourier series

Joseph Fourier introduced the equation in his Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), solving for the temperature in a rod by expanding the initial distribution in a series of sines and cosines — the origin of Fourier series and of modern harmonic analysis.

Properties and generalisations

The heat equation is the prototype parabolic PDE: solutions are instantly smoothed, obey a maximum principle (no new hot spots can arise), and are not reversible in time — disturbances effectively propagate at infinite speed, a non-physical feature remedied in the relativistic Cattaneo model. With u interpreted as a concentration or probability density, the same equation describes diffusion and Brownian motion (the diffusion equation), and it underlies the Black–Scholes equation in finance and Gaussian blurring in image processing. Fundamental solutions are given by the heat kernel, a Gaussian whose width grows as the square root of time.
