Standard deviation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A bell curve marked with one, two and three standard deviations]

In statistics, the standard deviation is a measure of the amount of variation of the values of a variable. It is the square root of the variance: low standard deviation means values cluster near the mean; high standard deviation means they spread over a wider range.

The term was introduced by Karl Pearson in 1894, replacing earlier names such as 'mean error'; the symbol σ (sigma) is standard for a population, s for a sample. For a normal distribution, about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of values lie within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean respectively.

Use and misuse

Standard deviation quantifies uncertainty in measurement, volatility in finance (annualised σ), quality in manufacturing (Six Sigma programmes), and typical spread in test scores (IQ tests are scaled to mean 100, SD 15). It is sensitive to outliers; the interquartile range is a robust alternative for skewed data.

Confusion between standard deviation (spread of data) and standard error (spread of a statistic, shrinking as √n) is a common reporting error corrected in research-methods teaching.
