Fourth wall

The fourth wall is a performance convention in which an invisible, imagined wall separates actors from the audience. While the audience can see through this "wall", the convention assumes the actors act as if they cannot. In a proscenium theatre the three physical walls of a box set are joined by this fourth, imaginary wall facing the spectators. The phrase "breaking the fourth wall" describes a character acknowledging the audience, the camera, or the fictional nature of the work.

Origin

The critic and philosopher Denis Diderot articulated the convention in his Discourse on Dramatic Poetry (1758), urging actors to imagine a great wall at the front of the stage separating them from the audience, and to act as if the curtain had never risen. The concept took hold with 19th-century realism and naturalism — the plays of Ibsen and Chekhov and the box sets of André Antoine — where the audience seemed to eavesdrop on real rooms and real lives.

Breaking the fourth wall

Direct address is far older than the convention itself: Greek choruses, Plautine prologues, Shakespearean asides and soliloquies all speak to the audience. In modern practice, breaking the wall is used for comedy (the Marx Brothers, Ferris Bueller's Day Off, Deadpool), for Brechtian alienation, and for intimate mockumentary confession (television series such as Fleabag and The Office). Bertolt Brecht deliberately shattered the illusion to make audiences think critically rather than empathise. Literary examples include the narrators of Tristram Shandy and Jane Eyre ("Reader, I married him").

Related conventions

The fourth wall is related to the willing suspension of disbelief: audiences accept the convention so long as it is consistently maintained, and a deliberate breach can be used to comic, unsettling or metafictional effect. In video games, the term extends to characters addressing the player directly, and to interfaces that blur the boundary between game world and player.
