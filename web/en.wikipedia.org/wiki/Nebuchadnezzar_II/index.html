Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned c. 605–562 BC) was the greatest king of the Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) Empire, who transformed Babylon into the wonder city of the ancient world and destroyed Jerusalem, carrying Judah's elite into the exile that reshaped Judaism. Son of Nabopolassar, who had destroyed Assyria with Medes' help, Nebuchadnezzar as crown prince crushed Egypt at Carchemish (605 BC), then spent decades campaigning in the Levant to hold Syria-Palestine as Babylon's frontier.

Jerusalem, Babylon and the legend

When Judah's kings rebelled against Babylonian overlordship, Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem twice: in 597 BC he deported King Jehoiachin and the prophet Ezekiel's generation, and in 586 BC, after Zedekiah's second revolt, his army burned Solomon's Temple and the city and exiled much of the population — the Babylonian captivity of Psalm 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon…"), ended only when Cyrus of Persia took Babylon in 539 BC. At home, Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt Babylon on a colossal scale: the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate and Processional Way (now in Berlin's Pergamon Museum), temples, walls Herodotus ranked among wonders, the ziggurat Etemenanki (a model for the Tower of Babel), and the Hanging Gardens — attributed by later tradition to him, built for his Median queen Amytis, though no Babylonian text mentions them and some scholars relocate them to Nineveh. The Book of Daniel remembers him as the proud pagan king humbled by God — thrown down to eat grass like an ox for seven years (a story whose Babylonian kernel may lie with his successor Nabonidus). In Verdi's Nabucco (1842) he became an Italian national icon by accident of chorus. Archaeology confirms the builder and the conqueror; the gardens remain antiquity's most famous unattested wonder.
