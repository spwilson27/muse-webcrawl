Yu the Great (Chinese: 大禹; pinyin: Dà Yǔ) is a legendary figure in ancient Chinese history, celebrated as the hero who tamed the waters of the Great Flood and founded the Xia dynasty, traditionally regarded as China's first dynasty. He is counted among the sage-kings of antiquity and is a central exemplar of diligence and selflessness in the Confucian tradition.

The flood myth

According to the received accounts in texts such as the Book of Documents and Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, devastating floods afflicted China during the reign of the sage-emperor Yao. Yao appointed Yu's father, Gun, to control the floods; Gun spent nine years building dams and dykes to block the waters and failed, and was executed by Yao's successor Shun. Yu took up the task with the opposite strategy: rather than blocking the water, he spent thirteen years dredging channels, deepening riverbeds, and cutting through mountains so the waters could drain to the sea. Legend holds that Yu passed his own home three times during the work without entering, so devoted was he to the task.

Founder of Xia

In reward for his success, Shun yielded the throne to Yu. Yu is credited with organising the realm into nine provinces and casting the Nine Tripod Cauldrons as symbols of sovereignty. On his death, the throne passed to his son Qi — an event traditionally seen as the beginning of hereditary dynastic rule in China, replacing the abdication system of the sage-kings.

Historicity and legacy

No contemporary evidence confirms Yu as a historical individual, and modern scholars generally treat him as a culture hero or deified figure of flood mythology, though the Xia dynasty itself remains a subject of archaeological debate. The Yu Mausoleum near Shaoxing in Zhejiang is a traditional site of veneration, and emperors of later dynasties performed sacrifices there.
