Crooner

A crooner is a male singer, especially one who sings in a soft, intimate, sentimental style. The term and the style emerged in the United States in the late 1920s and 1930s with the spread of the electrical microphone and radio broadcasting, which for the first time allowed a singer to be heard clearly without projecting to the back of a hall. Crooners sang love songs and standards in a relaxed, conversational manner, quite unlike the declamatory style of vaudeville.

History

Rudy Vallée was an early radio crooner; Bing Crosby perfected the form, using microphone technique to sing as if addressing one listener, and became one of the best-selling recording artists of all time. The style was enormously popular with young female audiences — initially provoking moral panic and even sermons denouncing crooning as effeminate and decadent, a controversy historians regard as an early example of media-driven generational conflict.

Frank Sinatra, who acknowledged Crosby's influence, took crooning to new artistic heights in the 1940s and 1950s, generating the first modern teen-idol hysteria among "bobby-soxers". Other notable crooners include Perry Como, Dean Martin, Nat King Cole, Tony Bennett, Mel Tormé and Andy Williams. The crooner's repertoire — the Great American Songbook of Gershwin, Porter, Berlin and others — became the foundation of traditional pop.

Later usage

Rock and roll displaced crooning in the charts after the mid-1950s, though singers such as Harry Connick Jr., Michael Bublé and others have revived the style. Female singers with a similarly intimate delivery — from Peggy Lee to Norah Jones — are often described with the word "crooner" today, although it was originally reserved for men.
