Buffalo Bill

William Frederick Cody (February 26, 1846 – January 10, 1917), known as "Buffalo Bill", was an American soldier, bison hunter, and showman. He became world-famous for his traveling Wild West shows, which dramatized cowboy life and frontier scenes for audiences across the United States and Europe.

Early life

Cody was born in Iowa Territory and raised in Kansas. As a boy he rode for the Pony Express (by his own account) and worked as a teamster and trapper. During the American Civil War he served with the Union's 7th Kansas Cavalry.

Army scout and the nickname

After the war Cody scouted for the U.S. Army during the Plains Wars and won the Medal of Honor in 1872. He earned the nickname "Buffalo Bill" for supplying Kansas Pacific Railroad workers with buffalo (American bison) meat — he claimed to have killed more than 4,000 bison in about eighteen months in 1867–68, a figure emblematic of the near-extermination of the herds.

Wild West show

In 1883 Cody founded Buffalo Bill's Wild West, an outdoor spectacle featuring roping, riding, sharpshooting by Annie Oakley, and staged battles with Native American performers including, briefly, Sitting Bull. The show toured Europe, performing for Queen Victoria, and appeared at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair, becoming one of the most successful entertainment ventures of its era.

Later life and legacy

Cody founded the town of Cody, Wyoming, in 1896. Despite his show's success, poor investments left him nearly broke by his death in 1917. He remains a defining figure of the mythologized American West, though his record — including his later advocacy for Native American rights and women's suffrage — is more complex than the legend.
