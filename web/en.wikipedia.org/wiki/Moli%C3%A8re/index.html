Molière

Molière (born Jean-Baptiste Poquelin; baptised 15 January 1622 – 17 February 1673) was a French playwright, actor and poet, widely regarded as one of the greatest writers in French literature and the world canon. He specialised in comedy and wrote some of the most durable masterpieces of the genre, including Tartuffe, The Misanthrope, The School for Wives, Don Juan and The Imaginary Invalid.

Life and career

The son of a prosperous upholsterer to the royal household, Poquelin abandoned a legal career for the theatre, co-founding the Illustre Théâtre in 1643 and adopting the stage name Molière. After years touring the provinces, his troupe returned to Paris in 1658 and won the patronage of Louis XIV. His comedies satirised the hypocrisies and follies of contemporary society — religious hypocrisy in Tartuffe, which was banned for several years after its 1664 premiere, as well as misers, misanthropes, quack doctors and social climbers.

Death and legacy

Molière collapsed on stage in February 1673 while playing the hypochondriac Argan in The Imaginary Invalid, and died that night. French is often called "the language of Molière", and his plays remain central to the repertoire of the Comédie-Française.
