Illusory correlation

Illusory correlation
FieldCognitive and social psychology
First describedChapman & Chapman (1967)
Related biasesStereotyping, availability heuristic, confirmation bias

In psychology, an illusory correlation is the phenomenon of perceiving a relationship between variables — typically people, events, or behaviours — even when no such relationship exists. A common example is a person believing that members of a minority group are unusually likely to behave negatively, because the pairing of two statistically distinctive events (minority membership and negative behaviour) stands out in memory and is over-weighted in judgement.

Origins of the concept

Loren and Jean Chapman introduced the term in 1967 in studies of clinical judgement: experienced clinicians reported correlations between patients' test responses (such as Rorschach signs) and diagnoses that were not present in the data, because the associations matched their theoretical expectations.

Distinctiveness-based illusory correlation

David Hamilton and Robert Gifford (1976) showed experimentally that illusory correlations arise from paired distinctiveness: when participants read statements about a large group A and a smaller group B, with the same proportion of desirable and undesirable behaviours in both, they nevertheless judged group B — the distinctive minority — as more negative. The rarer combination (minority member + negative act) is doubly distinctive and thus especially memorable. This mechanism is a leading cognitive explanation for the formation and persistence of stereotypes.

Expectancy-based illusory correlation

A second route is prior expectation: people notice and remember events that confirm what they already believe, and overlook disconfirming ones, manufacturing a perceived correlation that supports their beliefs. Illusory correlations have been invoked in explanations of superstitions, beliefs about full moons and behaviour, and mistaken judgements in medicine and personnel selection.
