Tu BiShvat (Hebrew: ט״ו בשבט) is a Jewish holiday occurring on the 15th day of the Hebrew month of Shevat, usually in late January or February. The name is simply the date — Tu is the Hebrew letters ṭet and vav, which have the numerical value 15. The Mishnah calls the day the New Year of the Trees (Rosh HaShanah La'Ilanot), one of four "new years" in the Jewish calendar. Originally an agricultural date for calculating the age of fruit trees and the tithing of their produce, it has become a day celebrating trees, fruit, and humanity's relationship with the natural world — sometimes described as a Jewish Arbor Day or Earth Day.

Origins

In ancient Israel, the age of a tree determined when its fruit could be eaten (fruit of the first three years, orlah, was forbidden) and which year's tithe it belonged to; the 15th of Shevat, roughly when the earliest almond trees blossom and the rainy season has delivered most of its water, was fixed as the boundary date. The Mishnah records a dispute between the schools of Shammai (1 Shevat) and Hillel (15 Shevat), with Hillel's view prevailing. Because the day was a fiscal rather than festive occasion, few traditional prohibitions attach to it, though the penitential prayer Tachanun is omitted.

Customs

The best-known custom is eating fruit, especially the Seven Species of the Land of Israel — wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and dates — and dried fruits such as almonds, carob, and raisins, which allowed Jews in cold climates to partake of the land's produce. In the 16th century, the Kabbalists of Safed, in the circle of Isaac Luria, created the Tu BiShvat seder, a ritual meal with four cups of wine (progressively redder, symbolizing the seasons) and fruits in categories reflecting the Kabbalistic worlds, accompanied by readings; the seder was popularized by the booklet Pri Etz Hadar. In modern Israel and the diaspora, the Jewish National Fund and schools made tree planting the hallmark of the day, and contemporary observance often emphasizes ecology and environmental stewardship.
