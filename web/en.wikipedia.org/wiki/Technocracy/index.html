Technocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Technocracy is a form of government, or a tendency within one, in which decision-makers are chosen for their technical expertise and background, or in which a society is governed by administrators selected on the basis of knowledge rather than electoral or political processes. The idea's roots run from Plato's philosopher-kings through Saint-Simon's industrial administrators to the organised technocracy movements of the 1930s (proposing energy-based economics) and the scientistic strands of modernisation theory.

Modern forms

Technocratic governance appears today in diluted forms: independent central banks and regulatory agencies staffed by experts; "technical governments" appointed in crises (Italy's Dini (1995) and Monti (2011) cabinets; Draghi (2021) paradigmatically); China's increasingly credentialed bureaucratic career state; and the EU's reliance on expert commissions and agencies (the "technocracy" charge is a staple of Eurosceptic rhetoric). Critics counter that expert rule displaces democratic accountability and disguises political choices as technical necessities — while defenders reply that modern problems (monetary stability, pandemics, climate engineering of incentives) require insulation and competence. The standing tension — expertise versus consent — is one of the defining arguments of contemporary democracy.
