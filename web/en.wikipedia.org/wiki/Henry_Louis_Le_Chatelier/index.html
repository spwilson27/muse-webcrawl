Henry Louis Le Chatelier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry Louis Le Chatelier (French pronunciation: [ɑ̃ʁi lwi lə ʃɑt(ə)lje]; 8 October 1850 – 17 September 1936) was a French chemist most famous for devising Le Chatelier's principle, used by chemists to predict the effect of a change in conditions on a chemical equilibrium.

Life and career

Born in Paris, Le Chatelier studied at the École Polytechnique and the École des Mines, becoming a mining engineer before turning to teaching chemistry. He became professor at the Collège de France and later at the Sorbonne. His research covered cement chemistry, metallurgy, combustion, and high-temperature measurement; he developed a platinum–rhodium thermocouple widely used for measuring high temperatures.

Le Chatelier's principle

In 1884 Le Chatelier stated his principle: if a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to counteract the change. The principle is a cornerstone of chemical education, governing predictions about concentration, temperature, and pressure effects in systems such as the Haber process.

Later years

Le Chatelier was elected to the French Academy of Sciences and became one of the most influential French chemists of his era, also promoting scientific organisation of industry. He died in Miribel-les-Échelles in 1936.
