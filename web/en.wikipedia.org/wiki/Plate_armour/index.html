Plate armour is a type of body armour made from large plates of iron or steel, worn over padding and (earlier) mail, that reached its highest development in late medieval and Renaissance Europe. A complete articulated suit — a "harness" — covered the wearer from head to foot while still permitting movement for fighting on foot or horseback.

Development

Metal cuirasses were worn in the ancient Greek and Roman worlds, but plate largely disappeared in the early Middle Ages, when mail predominated. From the late 13th century, plates were added over mail — first for the torso (coat of plates), then for limbs; by about 1420 the "white harness" of full plate had emerged. Italian (Milanese) armour favoured smooth, rounded plates to glance blows, while German "Gothic" armour used fluting and sharp ridges for both strength and style; Maximilian armour of the early 1500s carried fluting to an extreme. Specialised forms included tournament armours, heavily reinforced and later highly decorated parade armours, and the Greenwich armours made in Henry VIII's workshops.

Effectiveness and decline

Good plate of hardened steel was proof against most swords and arrows; shaped surfaces deflected points, and articulated lames and sliding rivets preserved mobility. A full field harness weighed about 20–25 kg — distributed over the body, comparable to a modern infantryman's load. Firearms gradually eroded plate's value: breastplates were thickened and "proofed" against pistols and muskets (leaving a dent as a proof mark), and three-quarter armour persisted for cuirassiers into the 17th century. By the later 1600s full plate was abandoned on the battlefield, surviving in helmets and cuirasses worn by heavy cavalry into the First World War era and in modern ceremonial dress.

Construction

A harness comprised a helmet (sallet, armet, great bascinet, later close helm), gorget, pauldrons, rerebraces, couters, vambraces and gauntlets for the arms; cuirass (breast- and backplate), faulds and tassets for the torso; and cuisses, poleyns, greaves and sabatons for the legs, worn over a padded arming doublet to which pieces were laced.
