Paideia

Paideia (Greek: παιδεία, "education", "rearing of a child") is, in ancient Greek culture, the ideal of the rearing and education of the ideal citizen — the process of educating a person into their true form, which combined intellectual, moral and physical training. The word later broadened to mean culture or civilisation itself as transmitted through education.

Classical conception

In the classical city-state, paideia joined gymnastics and music (in the broad sense of the Muses' arts: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, mathematics and philosophy) to produce a citizen fit for participation in the polis. Isocrates and Plato both theorised paideia: Plato's Republic presents an elaborate scheme of education as the foundation of the just city, while the sophists offered rhetorical training as a route to political success. Werner Jaeger's monumental study Paideia: The Ideals of Greek Culture (1933–1947) treated the concept as the key to Greek civilisation.

Hellenistic and Roman periods

In the Hellenistic world, paideia came to denote the standard curriculum of Greek learning — the enkyklios paideia, precursor of the liberal arts — through which Greek identity was maintained under foreign rule. Rome absorbed the ideal as humanitas, and Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria adapted the Greek programme to the training of the Roman orator.

Later uses

Early Christian writers used paideia for divine instruction and discipline. In the twentieth century the term was revived by Mortimer Adler's Paideia Proposal (1982), an American school-reform programme advocating a common, rigorous curriculum of didactic instruction, coaching and Socratic discussion for all students.
