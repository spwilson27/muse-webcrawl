The han (Japanese: 藩, "domain") were the feudal domains of Edo-period Japan ruled by daimyo under the Tokugawa shogunate. Together with the shogun's own territories, the han formed the bakuhan system of government, in which the shogunate (bakufu) exercised central authority while daimyo administered their domains with considerable local autonomy.

Structure

A domain was officially recognised as a han when its lord's assessed rice production (kokudaka) exceeded 10,000 koku. At the height of the system there were roughly 270 han, varying enormously in size: the largest, Kaga Domain, was assessed at over one million koku, while many domains barely met the minimum. Daimyo were classified as shinpan (relatives of the Tokugawa), fudai (hereditary vassals) or tozama (outside lords), which determined their proximity to power.

Obligations

Daimyo owed military service to the shogunate and were bound by the Laws for the Military Houses (buke shohatto). Under the sankin-kōtai system, they were required to reside in Edo in alternate years and to leave their families there as hostages, a heavy financial burden that also served as a mechanism of control.

Abolition

Following the Meiji Restoration, the han were abolished in 1871 in the haihan-chiken reform and replaced by prefectures, completing the centralisation of the Japanese state.
