Common knowledge

[Image: Levels of mutual knowledge between agents]

Common knowledge is a special kind of knowledge for a group of agents. There is common knowledge of p in a group of agents G when all the agents in G know p, they all know that they know p, they all know that they all know that they know p, and so on ad infinitum.

The concept was first introduced by the philosopher David Lewis in his master's thesis, later published as Convention (1969). The sociologist Morris Friedell defined it in 1969, and it was given a mathematical formulation by Robert Aumann in 1976, who used set theory to define it in terms of a partition of a state space: a fact is common knowledge if it holds in every state reachable through iterated knowledge operators.

Common knowledge versus mutual knowledge

Common knowledge is stronger than mutual knowledge, in which every agent merely knows p without necessarily knowing that the others know it. A classic illustration is the muddy children puzzle, in which a group of children can only solve a logic problem once a fact they all individually observe is announced publicly, converting mutual knowledge into common knowledge.

Applications

Common knowledge is central to game theory, where solution concepts such as the Nash equilibrium often assume that the rules of the game and the rationality of the players are common knowledge. In computer science, the concept underlies the analysis of distributed systems and the Two Generals' Problem, which shows that common knowledge cannot be attained over an unreliable communication channel: because each acknowledgement itself requires acknowledgement, no finite exchange of messages suffices. In economics, common knowledge of rationality is a key assumption in the analysis of auctions, coordination games and speculative bubbles; Robert Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" theorem of 1976 shows that agents with a common prior cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior beliefs.

Examples

Public announcements, ceremonies and broadcasts are social mechanisms for creating common knowledge: when a result is announced to a crowd that hears it together, everyone present knows it, knows the others heard it, and so on. Michael Chwe's book Rational Ritual (2001) argues that rituals, royal weddings and mass-media events such as the Super Bowl function as common-knowledge generators that make large-scale coordination possible.
