Raceway pond

Placeholder image: aerial view of algae raceway ponds.

A raceway pond is a shallow artificial pond used in the cultivation of algae. The pond is divided into a rectangular grid, with each channel resembling the shape of an oval running track (hence "raceway"). A paddlewheel circulates the algal broth around the loop, typically at velocities around 15–30 cm per second, keeping the cells suspended and exposed alternately to light at the surface and darkness below, and preventing sedimentation.

Raceway ponds are the most widely used technology for large-scale commercial production of microalgae, including Spirulina, Chlorella, Dunaliella salina and Haematococcus, grown for nutritional supplements, pigments, animal feed, wastewater treatment and experimental biofuel production.

Design and operation

Ponds are usually 0.2 to 0.5 m deep — a compromise between allowing light penetration and maintaining manageable volumes — and are built of concrete, compacted earth lined with plastic, or other low-cost materials. Mixing by paddlewheel is the most energy-efficient circulation option, though it still dominates operating costs. Carbon dioxide may be sparged into the culture to maintain pH and supply carbon, since algal growth rapidly depletes dissolved CO2.

Advantages and limitations

Compared with closed photobioreactors, raceways are far cheaper to build and operate and are simpler to scale, but they give less control over culture conditions: evaporation losses are high, temperature follows the weather, cultures are open to contamination by wild algae, grazers and bacteria, and biomass concentrations are lower, making harvesting more expensive. Productivity is typically reported in the range of 10–25 grams of dry biomass per square metre per day in favourable climates such as Hawaii, California, Australia and Israel, where the major commercial facilities are concentrated.

History

The raceway design was developed in the 1950s and 1960s, notably through work at the University of California, Berkeley, and was commercialised from the 1970s and 1980s onward, including by companies such as Earthrise in California and Cyanotech in Hawaii.
