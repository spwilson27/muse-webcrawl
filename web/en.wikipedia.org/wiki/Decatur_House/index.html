Decatur House

Decatur House, a Federal-style townhouse facing Lafayette Square near the White House.

Decatur House is a historic house museum in Washington, D.C., facing Lafayette Square across from the White House. Completed in 1819, it was built for the naval hero Stephen Decatur and his wife Susan and was designed by Benjamin Henry Latrobe, the architect of the United States Capitol. It is one of the oldest surviving homes in the city and was among the first private residences on the square.

History

Commodore Stephen Decatur, celebrated for his exploits in the Barbary Wars and the War of 1812, lived in the house for only fourteen months before he was mortally wounded in a duel with Commodore James Barron in March 1820. His widow Susan let the house to a succession of prominent tenants, including French and Russian ministers and three Secretaries of State — Henry Clay, Martin Van Buren and Edward Livingston — making it a social and diplomatic centre of the young capital.

In 1836 the house was bought by John Gadsby, a hotelier and tavern keeper; Gadsby built an adjoining slave quarters behind the house, now a rare surviving example of an urban slave dwelling, which is preserved and interpreted as part of the museum. Later owners included Vice President George M. Dallas and the California mining magnate William Henry Aspinwall. The house was acquired in 1872 by Edward Fitzgerald Beale, a frontiersman and diplomat, whose family owned it for the next eight decades.

Museum

Beale's daughter-in-law, Marie Beale, bequeathed the house to the National Trust for Historic Preservation in 1956. It is now operated as the Decatur House Museum in conjunction with the White House Historical Association, hosting exhibitions, educational programmes and events, and was designated a National Historic Landmark.
