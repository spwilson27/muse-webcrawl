Enheduanna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The alabaster disc of Enheduanna showing the priestess at an offering]

Enheduanna (c. 2285–2250 BC) was an Akkadian princess and high priestess (en) of the moon god Nanna at Ur, appointed by her father Sargon of Akkad as part of his unification of Sumer and Akkad. She is the earliest known author in world history whose name is attached to her work: the hymns she composed to the goddess Inanna — including the Exaltation of Inanna, a strikingly personal poem of exile and restoration — and a collection of temple hymns. Her works were copied in scribal schools for five centuries and shaped Mesopotamian religious literature.

Priestess and poet

Sargon installed his daughter at Ur's Giparu temple to bind conquered Sumer to Akkadian rule — a precedent followed by kings for a thousand years. Her hymns, written in Sumerian though she was an Akkadian speaker, exalt Inanna above the other gods, foreshadowing later syncretism. In the Exaltation she speaks in the first person of being driven from Ur by the rebel Lugal-Ane and restored by Inanna — the first personal voice in surviving literature, and the first recorded complaint of an author about the labour of composition ('I have given birth, O my lady, to this song for you').

Rediscovery

Enheduanna's alabaster disc portrait, found at Ur by Leonard Woolley's excavations (1927), shows her presiding over offerings. Assyriologist recognition of her authorship grew through the 20th century (Hallo and van Dijk's edition of the Exaltation, 1968). She is now widely cited as history's first named author — earlier works being anonymous — and as a rare female voice from the Bronze Age.
