Osmotic pressure

SymbolΠ

Osmotic pressure is the minimum pressure which needs to be applied to a solution to prevent the inward flow of its pure solvent across a semipermeable membrane. It is also defined as the measure of the tendency of a solution to take in a pure solvent by osmosis. Potential osmotic pressure is the maximum osmotic pressure that could develop in a solution if it were separated from its pure solvent by a semipermeable membrane.

Osmosis occurs when two solutions containing different concentrations of solute are separated by a selectively permeable membrane. Solvent molecules pass preferentially through the membrane from the low-solute to the high-solute solution. The transfer of solvent can be stopped by applying a pressure to the solution side; that pressure equals the osmotic pressure.

Theory

Jacobus van 't Hoff first proposed a relationship analogous to the ideal gas law for dilute solutions:

Π = i · M · R · T

where Π is osmotic pressure, i is the dimensionless van 't Hoff index accounting for dissociation of the solute, M is molar concentration, R is the ideal gas constant, and T the absolute temperature. The relation is a limiting law valid for dilute solutions; more concentrated solutions require corrections, described by the Morse equation and by virial expansions. Measuring osmotic pressure (osmometry) is a classical method for determining the molar masses of macromolecules, and "osmotic stress" techniques measure forces between macromolecules in solution.

Applications

Reverse osmosis applies a pressure exceeding the osmotic pressure to purify water, desalinate seawater and concentrate solutions. In medicine, the osmotic (oncotic) pressure exerted by plasma proteins — about 25–30 mmHg — retains fluid within capillaries; its disturbance contributes to oedema. Cells respond to the tonicity of their surroundings: in hypertonic media they shrink (crenation, plasmolysis in plants), in hypotonic media they swell and may lyse, while plant turgor pressure — the hydrostatic pressure generated against the cell wall by osmosis — keeps non-woody tissues rigid. Osmotic pressure gradients also drive the action of osmometers, osmotic power generation ("blue energy") at river mouths, and forward-osmosis processes.
