Tax evasion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tax evasion is the illegal non-payment or underpayment of taxes owed, accomplished by concealing income, inflating deductions, hiding assets (including offshore) or simply not filing — as distinct from tax avoidance, the lawful arrangement of affairs to minimise tax (the line drawn by Judge Learned Hand's dicta that no one owes a duty to pay more than the law demands, and patrolled by anti-avoidance rules and doctrines). Tax fraud is its criminal core.

Scale and enforcement

The "tax gap" — the difference between tax owed and tax collected — runs to hundreds of billions annually in the United States by IRS estimates, and globally includes a vast shadow economy in cash economies. Enforcement history is colourful (Al Capone fell to tax charges in 1931 when his crimes eluded prosecutors) and increasingly data-driven: whistleblower bounties, leaks (Swiss Leaks, the Panama and Pandora Papers) exposing offshore evasion networks, automatic exchange of bank information between tax authorities (the OECD Common Reporting Standard, US FATCA), and beneficial-ownership registers. Policy debate pits enforcement funding and information reporting against privacy and burden concerns; economists meanwhile model evasion as a gamble on audit probability and penalties (Allingham–Sandmo) and study why compliance is higher than the gamble predicts — tax morale, trust and the fiscal contract again.
