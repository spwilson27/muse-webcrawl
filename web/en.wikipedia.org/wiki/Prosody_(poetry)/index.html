Prosody (poetry)

In poetry, prosody is the study of metre, rhythm, intonation, and the sound patterns of verse — the principles governing how the audible shape of language is organised into lines. The term derives from the Greek prosōidia, originally the tone or accent with which a syllable was sung. Prosody analyses such features as syllable count, stress patterns, metrical feet, caesuras, line endings, and rhyme, and asks how these interact with meaning.

Scansion: marking the metrical pattern of a line

Systems of versification

Different languages organise verse by different phonological features:

  • Syllabic verse counts syllables per line, as in French poetry (the twelve-syllable alexandrine) and Japanese forms such as haiku.
  • Accentual verse counts stresses, typical of Old English verse and nursery rhymes.
  • Accentual-syllabic verse, the dominant English system since Chaucer, counts both: lines are built from metrical feet such as the iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapaest, and dactyl, grouped as dimeter, trimeter, tetrameter, pentameter, and so on.
  • Quantitative verse, used in classical Greek and Latin, patterns long and short syllables, as in the dactylic hexameter of epic.

Elements

Scansion is the practical analysis of a line's metre. Within a line, a caesura is a strong pause; lines may be end-stopped or run on by enjambment. Sound patterning beyond metre — rhyme, alliteration, assonance, and consonance — also belongs to prosody, as does the interaction of the metrical template with the variable rhythms of natural speech, which skilled poets exploit for emphasis and expressive tension. Free verse dispenses with a regular metrical scheme but still depends on prosodic resources such as lineation, cadence, and parallelism.

History

Classical prosody was codified by Greek and Roman grammarians; Sanskrit metrics (as in Piṅgala's Chandaḥśāstra) developed an independent and mathematically sophisticated tradition, and Arabic prosody was systematised by al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad. In English criticism, prosody became a central concern from the Renaissance through the nineteenth century, and modern linguistics has reframed it within generative and phonological theory.
