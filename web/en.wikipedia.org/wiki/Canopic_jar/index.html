Canopic jar

Canopic jar
CultureAncient Egypt
PeriodNew Kingdom to Late Period
UseStoring viscera of the deceased

Canopic jars are containers that were used by the ancient Egyptians during the mummification process, to store and preserve the viscera of their owner for the afterlife. They were commonly either carved from limestone or made of clay, and were in use from the New Kingdom until the Late Period or Ptolemaic Period.

The four jars

The viscera were not kept in a single jar: each organ of the deceased was placed into a jar of its own. The classic set comprised four jars, each protected by one of the Four Sons of Horus and containing a specific organ: Imsety (human-headed) guarded the liver; Hapy (baboon-headed) the lungs; Duamutef (jackal-headed) the stomach; and Qebehsenuef (falcon-headed) the intestines. The heart, considered the seat of the soul, was left inside the body.

Development

Early canopic equipment was a simple chest; jars with human-headed stoppers appeared in the New Kingdom, and the animal-headed forms became standard from the Third Intermediate Period. In later periods the organs were sometimes returned to the body cavity, while solid "dummy" jars were still placed in the tomb for ritual completeness. The term "canopic" derives, by a misunderstanding of early Egyptologists, from Canopus, a town in the Nile Delta where jars representing Osiris were worshipped.

Famous examples include the alabaster canopic chest of Tutankhamun, whose stoppers bear portrait heads of the king.
