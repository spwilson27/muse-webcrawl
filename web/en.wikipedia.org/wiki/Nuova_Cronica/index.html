The Nuova Cronica (Italian for "New Chronicle") is a history of Florence written by the Florentine banker and official Giovanni Villani (c. 1276–1348). Begun around 1300, it grew into one of the most important narrative sources for the social, economic, and political history of medieval Italy, and it is regarded as a foundational work of vernacular Italian historiography.

Composition

Villani wrote that he conceived the project while attending the Jubilee of Pope Boniface VIII in Rome in 1300, reasoning that Florence, then at the height of its prosperity, deserved a chronicle as Rome had received. Unusually for a medieval chronicle, the work begins not with creation but with the Tower of Babel, moving through ancient history to the legendary founding of Florence before settling into detailed contemporary reporting.

The Cronica is divided into twelve books. The first six cover legendary and ancient history; the last six, which occupy the bulk of the work, treat Florentine history from about 1265 onward in close year-by-year detail. Giovanni's narrative breaks off in 1348, when he died of the Black Death that the chronicle itself describes so memorably. His account of the plague's arrival and devastation in Florence is among the most famous eyewitness descriptions of the catastrophe.

Continuations

Giovanni's brother Matteo Villani continued the chronicle from 1348 until his own death from plague in 1363. Matteo's son Filippo Villani carried the work a little further and also added a celebrated series of biographical notices of famous Florentines, including poets and artists such as Dante and Giotto.

Significance

Villani's chronicle is prized for its wealth of concrete information: prices, wages, population figures, tax yields, building projects, and the operations of Florence's great banking companies, several of which — including the Peruzzi company, for which Villani worked — collapsed in the 1340s. His famous statistical snapshot of Florence around 1338, listing schools, churches, workshops, and food consumption, is a staple source for historians of medieval urban life. The work set a model for later Florentine chroniclers and humanists and influenced writers well into the Renaissance.
