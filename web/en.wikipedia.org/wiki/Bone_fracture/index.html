Bone fracture

Bone fracture
Other namesBroken bone, bone break

A bone fracture (abbreviated FRX or Fx, Fx, or #) is a medical condition in which there is a partial or complete break in the continuity of any bone in the body. In more severe cases, the bone may be broken into several fragments, known as a comminuted fracture. A bone fracture may be the result of high force impact or stress, or a minimal trauma injury as a result of certain medical conditions that weaken the bones, such as osteoporosis, osteopenia, bone cancer, or osteogenesis imperfecta, where the fracture is then properly termed a pathologic fracture.

Signs and symptoms

Although bone tissue contains no pain receptors, a bone fracture is painful for several reasons: the surrounding periosteum is richly supplied with nociceptors; adjacent soft tissues are injured and bleed; and involuntary muscle spasms attempt to hold bone fragments in place. Symptoms typically include pain, swelling, bruising, deformity of the limb, and loss of function.

Causes

Most fractures result from trauma — falls, motor vehicle collisions, sports injuries, or direct blows. Stress fractures arise from repetitive mechanical loading that outpaces the bone's ability to repair itself, common in athletes and military recruits. Pathological fractures occur in bone weakened by disease with little or no trauma.

Classification

Fractures are described by pattern and displacement: transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted (multiple fragments), segmental, greenstick (incomplete, in children), and compression. A fracture is open (compound) if the bone or hematoma communicates with the outside through a skin wound, and closed (simple) otherwise. Open fractures carry a high risk of infection.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by history and physical examination, confirmed by imaging. Plain radiographs in two planes are standard; CT is used for complex or intra-articular fractures, and MRI for occult or stress fractures.

Treatment

Treatment aims to restore alignment and maintain it while the bone heals. It includes analgesia, reduction (realignment, closed or open), and immobilization with splints, casts, traction, or surgical fixation with plates, screws, or intramedullary nails. Bone healing proceeds through hematoma formation, soft callus, hard callus, and remodeling, typically over weeks to months.
