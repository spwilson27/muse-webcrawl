Adverse event

FieldPharmacovigilance, clinical research
DefinitionAny untoward medical occurrence in a subject administered a treatment, whether or not causally related
GradingCommon Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), grades 1–5

An adverse event (AE) is any untoward medical occurrence in a patient or clinical-trial subject who has been administered a pharmaceutical product, medical device, or other intervention, which does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the treatment. The term is central to clinical trials and to pharmacovigilance, the science of detecting, assessing, and preventing adverse effects of medicines.

Definitions and classification

An adverse event becomes an adverse drug reaction (ADR) when a causal relationship to the treatment is suspected. A serious adverse event (SAE) is one that results in death, is life-threatening, requires or prolongs hospitalisation, causes persistent or significant disability, or is a congenital anomaly; serious events must be reported to regulators on an expedited basis. Severity is graded separately, commonly with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), which grades events from 1 (mild) to 5 (death related to the event).

Reporting

In trials, investigators record all adverse events whether or not they appear related to the study drug, allowing safety signals to emerge from aggregated data. After a medicine reaches the market, spontaneous reporting systems such as the FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and the international VigiBase maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre collect reports from clinicians and patients. Expected events listed in the product information are handled differently from unexpected ones, which may trigger label changes or further study.

In healthcare generally

The term is also used more broadly for harm associated with medical care itself — for example surgical complications and hospital-acquired infections — a usage prominent in patient-safety research since the Institute of Medicine's report To Err Is Human (1999).
