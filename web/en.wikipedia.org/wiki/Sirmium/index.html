Sirmium

Sirmium Modern site: Sremska Mitrovica, Serbia
Location: South bank of the Sava river, Pannonia
Status: Roman city; imperial residence
Peak period: 3rd–4th centuries AD

Sirmium was a city in the Roman province of Pannonia, located on the river Sava at the site of modern Sremska Mitrovica in Vojvodina, Serbia. It grew from an Illyrian and Celtic settlement into one of the most important cities of the late Roman Empire — a frontier fortress, commercial hub and, in the third and fourth centuries, a frequent imperial residence, reckoned by contemporaries among the great cities of the Roman world.

History

Rome conquered the settlement during the Pannonian wars of Augustus's reign, and under the Flavians it received colonial status as Colonia Flavia Sirmiensium. Standing where roads from Italy, the Danube frontier and the Balkans met, Sirmium became a base for Danubian campaigns and a mint and arms-manufacturing centre. During the third-century crisis and the Tetrarchy it was regularly used by emperors campaigning on the Danube: Galerius and Licinius were associated with the city, and a series of emperors — including Decius, Claudius Gothicus, Quintillus, Aurelian, Probus, Maximian, Constantius II and Gratian — were born in or near Sirmium, earning the region a reputation as a nursery of soldier-emperors. Constantius II held major church councils there in the 350s, whose Arian-leaning creeds (the "Sirmian formulas") were controversial across the Christian world. The city was also an early martyrdom site; Saint Irenaeus of Sirmium was executed in 304.

Decline

Sirmium was attacked repeatedly in the migration period — by Huns, then contested by Goths and Gepids, and besieged by the Avars, who captured it in 582 after a celebrated three-year siege, an episode described by contemporary historians. The population was deported and the city faded, though the name survived in the medieval theme of Sirmium and in the modern Serbian region of Srem (Syrmia).

Archaeology

Excavations beneath Sremska Mitrovica have revealed an imperial palace with floor mosaics and a circus (hippodrome), baths, granaries, walls and early Christian churches; finds are displayed in the Museum of Srem. Because the modern town overlies the ancient one, excavation has been piecemeal, and much of the Roman city remains unexcavated.
