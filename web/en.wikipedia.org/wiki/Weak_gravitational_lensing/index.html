Weak gravitational lensing

Weak gravitational lensing is one of the three regimes of gravitational lensing, in which the deflection of light from a distant source by intervening mass is too small to produce multiple images or obvious arcs, but subtly distorts the shapes of background galaxies. Because the effect on any single galaxy is far smaller than its intrinsic ellipticity, weak lensing is measured statistically over millions of galaxies, revealing the distribution of matter — most of it dark — along the line of sight.

Shear and magnification

Weak lensing is described by convergence (isotropic magnification) and shear (anisotropic stretching). Galaxy images are stretched tangentially around massive foreground structures by typically only one or two percent. Galaxy–galaxy lensing measures the average halo mass of foreground galaxies; cluster lensing weighs galaxy clusters independently of assumptions about their dynamical state; and cosmic shear — correlations in galaxy shapes across the sky — probes the growth of large-scale structure.

Cosmological importance

Because it is sensitive to the total matter density and the amplitude of matter fluctuations (often combined as S₈), cosmic shear from surveys such as the Dark Energy Survey, KiDS and Hyper Suprime-Cam is a key test of the ΛCDM model, complementary to the cosmic microwave background. A persistent mild "S₈ tension" between lensing and early-Universe measurements is an active research topic. Future surveys — the Vera Rubin Observatory's LSST and ESA's Euclid — will measure shapes for billions of galaxies. Systematic challenges include intrinsic alignments of galaxies, photometric redshift errors and modelling of baryonic effects.
