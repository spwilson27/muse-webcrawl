The cycle of directed evolution: diversification, screening or selection, and amplification of improved variants.

Directed evolution is a method used in protein engineering that mimics the process of natural selection to steer proteins or nucleic acids toward a user-defined goal. It consists of subjecting a gene to iterative rounds of mutagenesis (creating a library of variants), selection (expressing those variants and isolating members with the desired function), and amplification, enabling the researcher to evolve the properties of a protein in the laboratory much faster than natural evolution would.

The approach was pioneered in the 1990s, notably by Frances Arnold at Caltech, who demonstrated the evolution of enzymes that function in organic solvents, and by Willem Stemmer, who developed DNA shuffling. In 2018, Arnold received half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the directed evolution of enzymes.

Principles

Directed evolution does not require detailed knowledge of a protein's structure or mechanism. Instead, it relies on three elements: a diverse library of genetic variants, a screen or selection that links genotype to a measurable phenotype, and iteration. Because most random mutations are neutral or deleterious, effective screens must be able to search through large libraries — often millions of variants — to find rare beneficial mutations.

Methods of diversification

Random mutagenesis

Error-prone PCR introduces point mutations at a controlled rate across a gene. Site-saturation mutagenesis randomises a specific codon to explore all twenty amino acids at a chosen position.

Recombination

DNA shuffling and related methods recombine homologous genes, combining beneficial mutations from different parents and purging deleterious ones, in analogy to sexual reproduction.

Applications

Directed evolution has produced enzymes for industrial biocatalysis (including enzymes used in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals such as sitagliptin), biofuels, detergents stable at high temperatures, and laboratory tools such as improved fluorescent proteins and polymerases. It is also used to engineer antibodies and metabolic pathways.

Comparison with rational design

Unlike rational protein design, which modifies a protein based on structural hypotheses, directed evolution explores sequence space empirically. The two approaches are increasingly combined: rational predictions narrow the library, and laboratory evolution optimises within it, a strategy sometimes called semi-rational design.
