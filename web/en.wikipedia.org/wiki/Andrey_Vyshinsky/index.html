Andrey Yanuarevich Vyshinsky (Russian: Андрей Януарьевич Вышинский; 10 December 1883 – 22 November 1954) was a Soviet jurist, prosecutor and diplomat. He is best known as the state prosecutor at the Moscow Trials of the 1930s, in which he demanded death sentences for former leaders of the Communist Party, and later as the Soviet Union's Minister of Foreign Affairs.

Early life

Born in Odessa to a Polish Catholic family, Vyshinsky studied law at Kiev University. He began his career as a Menshevik but joined the Communist Party in 1920. In 1917, as a police official in Moscow, he had signed an order for the arrest of Vladimir Lenin — a fact he later had to live down.

Prosecutor

Vyshinsky rose through the Soviet legal system, serving as prosecutor of the RSFSR and, from 1935, Prosecutor General of the USSR. At the Moscow show trials (1936–1938) he acted as chief prosecutor against defendants including Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and Radek, denouncing them in vitriolic language as "mad dogs" and "vile scum".

His legal theory held that a confession was the "queen of evidence" and that in cases involving state crimes the burden of proof could effectively rest on the accused — doctrines used to legitimise the Great Purge.

Diplomat

From 1940 Vyshinsky was a deputy foreign minister under Vyacheslav Molotov. He supervised the incorporation of Latvia into the USSR in 1940 and the installation of a communist government in Romania in 1945. In 1949 he succeeded Molotov as Minister of Foreign Affairs, and from 1953 he was the Soviet ambassador to the United Nations, where he became known for his combative speeches during the early Cold War. He died in New York in 1954.
