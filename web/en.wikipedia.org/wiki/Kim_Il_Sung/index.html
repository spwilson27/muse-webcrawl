Kim Il Sung (April 15, 1912 – July 8, 1994) was a North Korean politician and dictator who founded the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) and led it from its establishment in 1948 until his death in 1994. He developed the state ideology of Juche (self-reliance), built a pervasive personality cult, and established the hereditary succession continued by his son Kim Jong Il and grandson Kim Jong Un. North Korea's constitution posthumously names him the country's "Eternal President".

Early life and guerrilla career

Born Kim Song-ju in Mangyongdae, near Pyongyang, he emigrated with his family to Manchuria, where he joined Korean anti-Japanese guerrilla units and then the Chinese Communist armed forces, taking the name Kim Il Sung. In 1940 he withdrew to the Soviet Union, was trained near Khabarovsk and served as a captain in the Red Army's 88th Independent Rifle Brigade. He returned to Korea in September 1945 after Japan's defeat, presented by the Soviets as a national hero at a Pyongyang rally.

Rule

Backed by the USSR, Kim became premier of the new North Korean state in 1948. In June 1950 he launched an invasion of South Korea, beginning the Korean War; after early success, UN forces drove his army nearly to the Chinese border before Chinese intervention restored a stalemate, and the war ended with the 1953 armistice.

Kim consolidated absolute power through purges of rival factions (notably after the August Faction Incident of 1956) and promoted Juche from 1955 onwards, asserting independence from both Moscow and Beijing. In the 1950s–60s the North's planned economy initially outgrew the South's, but by the 1970s it stagnated; Kim had himself made President in 1972 and groomed his son Kim Jong Il as successor. He died suddenly of a heart attack on July 8, 1994, amid negotiations over North Korea's nuclear programme and a planned summit with South Korea's president.

Legacy

Kim's birthday, April 15, is North Korea's most important holiday, the Day of the Sun. His embalmed body lies in the Kumsusan Palace of the Sun in Pyongyang, and his cult — statutes, portraits in every home and institution, and the attribution of near-divine wisdom — remains the foundation of the state's ideology.
