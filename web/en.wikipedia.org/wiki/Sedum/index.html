Sedum is a large genus of flowering plants in the family Crassulaceae, members of which are commonly known as stonecrops. The genus, described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753, has at various times encompassed up to 600 species of leaf succulents native to temperate and mountainous regions of the Northern Hemisphere and extending into dry parts of South America. Molecular studies have led many botanists to split off groups into separate genera such as Hylotelephium, Phedimus and Rhodiola, so the circumscription of Sedum remains unsettled.

Description

Sedums are annuals, perennials, herbs and subshrubs with fleshy, water-storing leaves arranged alternately, oppositely or in whorls, and typically star-shaped flowers with five petals in terminal clusters, in colours from white and yellow to pink and red. Like other Crassulaceae they use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), opening their stomata at night to limit water loss, which makes them exceptionally drought-tolerant. Most species grow in thin, poor, rocky soils — hence "stonecrop", a crop that grows on stone.

Cultivation and uses

Hardy, low-maintenance and tolerant of heat, drought and poor soil, sedums are widely planted as ground cover, in rock gardens, in containers and on green roofs, where extensive sedum mats are a standard system because the shallow-rooted plants survive with minimal irrigation and substrate. Popular garden plants include Sedum acre (goldmoss stonecrop), S. spurium, S. kamtschaticum, and the taller autumn-flowering forms now often classified as Hylotelephium, such as 'Autumn Joy' (Herbstfreude). Some species have traditional medicinal uses, and a few are edible in small amounts, though others are mildly toxic. Sedums are also valued by pollinators for their late-season nectar.
