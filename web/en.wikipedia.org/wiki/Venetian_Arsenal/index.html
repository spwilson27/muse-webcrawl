The Venetian Arsenal (Italian: Arsenale di Venezia) is a complex of former shipyards and armories clustered together in the city of Venice in northern Italy. Owned by the state, the Arsenal was responsible for the bulk of the Venetian Republic's naval power during the middle part of the second millennium AD. It was one of the earliest large-scale industrial enterprises in history, employing standardised parts and assembly-line methods centuries before the Industrial Revolution.

History

A Byzantine-style arsenal may have existed in Venice as early as the 8th century, but the complex is generally said to have been founded around 1104 under Doge Ordelafo Faliero. It was repeatedly expanded — the Arsenale Nuovo from 1320 and the Arsenale Nuovissimo from 1473 — until it occupied about 45 hectares, roughly 15 percent of Venice. At its peak in the early 16th century the Arsenal employed up to 16,000 workers (the arsenalotti) and could fit out a fully equipped war galley in a single day, using prefabricated components moved past a sequence of workshops. Dante used its boiling pitch cauldrons as an image in the Inferno.

The Arsenal built and maintained the galleys that sustained Venetian trade and naval dominance in the Mediterranean, including the fleet that fought at the Battle of Lepanto in 1571. Its fortunes declined with the Republic, and after Venice fell to Napoleon in 1797 much of its equipment was removed or destroyed. Parts of the complex remain in use by the Italian Navy and as exhibition spaces for the Venice Biennale.

The gate and the lions

The Arsenal's land entrance, the Porta Magna (built 1460), was one of the earliest works of Renaissance architecture in Venice. It is flanked by marble lions looted from Greece, including the Piraeus Lion, which bears runic graffiti carved by Varangian (Viking) mercenaries.
