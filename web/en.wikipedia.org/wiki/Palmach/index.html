The Palmach (Hebrew: פלמ״ח, an acronym for Plugot Mahatz, "Strike Companies") was the elite combined strike force and commando unit of the Haganah, the underground army of the Yishuv (Jewish community) during the period of the British Mandate for Palestine. It was established on 15 May 1941 and by 1948 had grown into a force of three infantry brigades and auxiliary units.

History

The Palmach was founded by the Haganah High Command in May 1941 with the aim of defending the Jewish community against two possible threats: a local Arab revolt, and an invasion of Palestine by the Axis powers, in particular a German advance through North Africa or Syria. Its first commander was Yitzhak Sadeh, and its early companies cooperated with British forces, including during the Syria–Lebanon campaign of June–July 1941.

Because funding was limited, Palmach members combined military training with agricultural labour: they lived and worked on kibbutzim, training for only part of each month. This arrangement produced a distinctive esprit de corps and a strong connection to the Labour Zionist movement.

Role in 1948

During the 1947–1949 Palestine war the Palmach played a central role. Its brigades — the Yiftach, Harel and Negev brigades — fought in Jerusalem, the Galilee and the Negev. Palmach units carried out operations such as the opening of the road to Jerusalem and the capture of Safed and Eilat. After the establishment of the State of Israel in May 1948, the Palmach was integrated into the Israel Defense Forces; its separate headquarters was disbanded in November 1948 as part of David Ben-Gurion's effort to create a unified, non-partisan army.

Legacy

The Palmach produced a generation of Israeli military and political leaders, including Yigal Allon, Yitzhak Rabin and Moshe Dayan. Its culture — songs, hikes, campfires and a rough, egalitarian ethos — became a founding myth of Israeli society, commemorated in the Palmach Museum in Tel Aviv.
