The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, officially the Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, was a neutrality pact signed on 23 August 1939 by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union. It is named after its signatories, Soviet foreign minister Vyacheslav Molotov and German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop. The pact guaranteed that neither country would attack the other, and a secret protocol divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence.

Molotov signing the pact in Moscow, with Ribbentrop and Stalin looking on.

Background

Following the Munich Agreement of 1938 and the failure of Anglo-French-Soviet negotiations in the summer of 1939, Stalin pursued an agreement with Hitler. Germany sought to avoid a two-front war while invading Poland; the USSR sought time to rebuild its military after the Great Purge and to gain territory in Eastern Europe.

Terms and secret protocol

Publicly, the treaty pledged each side to neutrality if the other was attacked by a third party, and to refrain from joining alliances directed against the other. The secret protocol assigned Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Bessarabia to the Soviet sphere, Lithuania to the German sphere, and divided Poland roughly along the lines of the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers. A second secret protocol of 28 September 1939 (the German–Soviet Frontier Treaty) moved Lithuania into the Soviet sphere.

Consequences

On 1 September 1939 Germany invaded Poland, beginning the Second World War; the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September. The two powers partitioned Poland, and the USSR went on to annex the Baltic states and Bessarabia and to attack Finland in the Winter War. Germany and the USSR also expanded economic cooperation. The pact ended on 22 June 1941 when Germany launched Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union.

Legacy

The Soviet Union denied the existence of the secret protocols until 1989, when they were formally condemned by the Congress of People's Deputies. The pact remains a central grievance in the historical memory of Poland and the Baltic states, and 23 August is commemorated in Europe as a day of remembrance for victims of Stalinism and Nazism.
