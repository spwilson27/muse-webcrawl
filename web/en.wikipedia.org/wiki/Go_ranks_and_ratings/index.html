In the game of Go, ranks and ratings measure a player's strength. The traditional system, inherited from the Japanese dan/kyu ranking of martial arts (itself derived from an earlier Chinese system), divides players into kyu grades for learners and dan grades for masters. Handicap stones used in games between unequal players are calibrated directly against this rank difference.

Amateur ranks

Amateur players progress from about 30 kyu (a complete beginner) down to 1 kyu, then ascend the dan scale from 1 dan (shodan) to typically 7 dan (some associations award 8 dan). Adjacent ranks differ by roughly one handicap stone: a 1-kyu player would give a 2-kyu player one stone, and a 1-dan player would receive one stone from a 2-dan player. The European Go Federation and national associations maintain rating systems (based on Elo-like calculations) that are mapped onto these ranks; online servers use similar kyu/dan scales with their own calibrations.

Professional ranks

Professional players in Japan, China, Korea and Taiwan hold professional dan ranks from 1 dan to 9 dan, awarded by their professional associations (such as the Nihon Ki-in, Kansai Ki-in, Hanguk Kiwon and China Qiyuan) largely through promotion rules based on tournament wins and career results rather than directly measured playing strength. Historically, the gap between professional ranks was smaller than one handicap stone per rank — roughly one stone per three ranks at the top level — and top professionals compete on even terms in title matches for honours such as Meijin, Kisei and Honinbo.

Rating systems

Modern statistical ratings, including Elo and its derivatives (such as the European Go Database ratings and the AGA rating system), estimate strength numerically and can be converted approximately to kyu/dan ranks. Computer programs since AlphaGo have been assigned honorary professional dan ranks by associations in recognition of their strength.
