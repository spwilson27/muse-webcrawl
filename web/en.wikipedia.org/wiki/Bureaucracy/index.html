Bureaucracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bureaucracy is a system of organisation and administration based on specialised departments, hierarchy, formal rules and written records, staffed by appointed officials. In social-science usage — shaped by Max Weber — it denotes the rational-legal administrative apparatus of modern states and large organisations; in everyday usage it is often a pejorative for red tape, rigidity and unaccountable officialdom.

Weber's model

Weber characterised bureaucracy by fixed jurisdictions, hierarchy of offices, management through written files, expert training, full-time salaried officials appointed on merit, and rule-bound, impersonal decision-making — in his analysis, the technically most efficient form of administration and an irreversible feature of modernity (with the danger of an "iron cage" of rationalisation). Historical bureaucracies include imperial China's mandarinate (recruited by examination, a model later admired in Europe) and the Prussian and French state services.

Modern civil services and critique

Reform movements — the Northcote–Trevelyan reforms (1854) and the Pendleton Act (1883) — replaced patronage with merit recruitment in Britain and the US. Twentieth-century critiques examined bureaucratic self-interest (public choice theory), goal displacement and trained incapacity; management reforms ("New Public Management") imported market techniques into government, with mixed evaluations. Bureaucracy remains essential to taxation, welfare delivery, regulation and elections themselves, and its size and quality correlate strongly with state capacity and development outcomes.
