Insulin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Insulin
TypePeptide hormone (51 amino acids, two chains)
Produced inBeta cells of the pancreatic islets
FunctionRegulates blood glucose; promotes glucose uptake and storage
DiscoveryIsolated 1921, University of Toronto

Insulin is a peptide hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets. It regulates the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats and protein by promoting the absorption of glucose from the blood into liver, fat and skeletal muscle cells, and its failure — through absence or resistance — causes diabetes mellitus. Insulin is the principal anabolic hormone of the body: it encourages storage of energy as glycogen, fat and protein, and suppresses their breakdown.

Discovery

In 1921, at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting and medical student Charles Best, working in J.J.R. Macleod's laboratory with biochemist James Collip purifying the extract, isolated insulin from dog pancreases and used it to treat diabetic dogs. In January 1922 the extract was given to Leonard Thompson, a 14-year-old boy dying of type 1 diabetes, who recovered dramatically — before insulin, the diagnosis was a death sentence within months or a few years. Banting and Macleod received the 1923 Nobel Prize; Banting shared his prize money with Best. Insulin's amino-acid sequence was determined by Frederick Sanger in the 1950s (the first protein ever sequenced), and in 1978 insulin became the first human protein produced by genetic engineering, using recombinant DNA in bacteria.

Action and therapeutic use

After a meal, rising blood glucose triggers insulin release; insulin binds receptors on cells, causing glucose transporters (GLUT4) to move to the cell surface and take up glucose, and signalling the liver to store glycogen and stop releasing glucose. People with type 1 diabetes must inject insulin (or use a pump) for life; many with type 2 use it as the disease progresses. Modern formulations include rapid-acting and long-acting analogues, metered by blood-glucose monitoring or continuous glucose sensors. Too much insulin causes hypoglycaemia — dangerously low blood sugar — the main acute risk of therapy.
