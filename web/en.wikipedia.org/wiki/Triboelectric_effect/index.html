The triboelectric effect (also known as triboelectric charging) is a type of contact electrification in which a material becomes electrically charged after it is separated from a different material it had been in contact with. Rubbing the two materials together increases the contact between their surfaces and thus increases the charge that can be transferred. The phenomenon is the everyday cause of most static electricity: rubbing a balloon on hair, shuffling across a carpet, or removing a synthetic sweater can all build up triboelectric charge, which may discharge as a small spark.

History

The effect has been known since antiquity: the Greek philosopher Thales of Miletus is said to have observed around 600 BCE that amber rubbed with fur attracts light objects — the English word "electricity" derives from elektron, Greek for amber. Systematic study began in the 18th century, and researchers compiled empirical triboelectric series: ordered lists of materials by their tendency to gain or lose charge. In the 20th century the effect was often treated as a nuisance of poorly understood physics, but research since the 2010s — spurred by energy-harvesting applications — has clarified many of its mechanisms.

Mechanism

When two dissimilar materials touch, charge carriers move across the interface until an equilibrium is reached; when the materials separate, some of the transferred charge remains, leaving one surface positive and the other negative. Depending on the material pair, the carriers may be electrons, ions, or even microscopic fragments of material itself. The sign and magnitude of charging depend on surface chemistry, roughness, temperature, humidity, and the mechanical history of the contact — which is why triboelectric behaviour is famously difficult to predict from first principles, and why identical materials can sometimes charge each other when their surfaces differ in curvature or strain.

The triboelectric series

Materials near the top of a typical triboelectric series (such as rabbit fur, glass, human hair, nylon, and wool) tend to become positively charged; those near the bottom (such as amber, rubber, polyester, polyurethane, and polytetrafluoroethylene/Teflon) tend to become negative. The further apart two materials are in the series, the greater the charge typically transferred on contact.

Hazards and applications

Uncontrolled triboelectric discharge is a serious industrial hazard: sparks can ignite flammable vapours during fuel handling, damage sensitive electronics (electrostatic discharge, ESD), and cause dust explosions in grain silos and powder plants. Conversely, the effect is exploited in xerography and laser printing (toner transfer), electrostatic painting and powder coating, air filtration, and electrostatic separators. Since 2012, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs), developed prominently by Zhong Lin Wang's group, have converted ambient mechanical energy — footsteps, waves, vibration, wind — into electricity using controlled contact electrification, opening a route to self-powered sensors and wearable devices.
