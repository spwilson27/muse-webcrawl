Liquid crystal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liquid crystals (LCs) are a state of matter which has properties between those of conventional liquids and those of solid crystals. For instance, a liquid crystal may flow like a liquid, but its molecules may be oriented in a crystal-like way. Liquid crystal materials are typically long, rod-like organic molecules, and their optical properties change markedly in an electric field — the basis of the liquid-crystal display (LCD).

Phases

The most important mesophases are: the nematic phase, in which the molecules share a common orientation (the director) but no positional order; the smectic phases, in which the molecules also arrange in layers; and the cholesteric (chiral nematic) phase, in which the director twists in a helix, reflecting selected colours of light and giving strong temperature-dependent colour — exploited in liquid-crystal thermometers. Thermotropic liquid crystals change phase with temperature, while lyotropic ones, formed by amphiphiles in solvents, include the lipid bilayers of cell membranes.

History

In 1888 the Austrian botanist Friedrich Reinitzer observed that cholesteryl benzoate appeared to have two melting points, turning into a cloudy fluid before clearing. With the physicist Otto Lehmann — who coined the term "liquid crystal" — the field was founded, but applications waited until the 1960s–70s, when the dynamic scattering effect and the twisted nematic field effect (patented by Schadt and Helfrich in 1970) made practical displays possible.

Applications

LCDs dominate display technology in televisions, monitors, watches, calculators and instruments. Liquid crystals are also used in tunable lenses and optical shutters, temperature sensors, and in research on self-assembly and biological structures.
