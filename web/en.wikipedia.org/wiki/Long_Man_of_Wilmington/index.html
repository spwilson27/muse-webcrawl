Long Man of Wilmington

The Long Man of Wilmington is a hill figure on the steep slope of Windover Hill near Wilmington in East Sussex, England. At 72 metres (235 ft) tall, it is one of only two major surviving human hill figures in England and depicts a man holding two staves. Long thought to be prehistoric or early medieval, archaeological evidence now suggests it may have been created in the 16th or 17th century.

Description

The figure is drawn in proportion as if seen from directly overhead, so that from the ground below it appears elongated. It holds a staff in each hand, longer than the figure itself. The outline was originally marked by shallow trenches showing the chalk; in 1874 the figure was restored and outlined with yellow bricks, replaced in 1969 by concrete blocks, which are painted white and periodically maintained.

Dating controversy

Antiquarians variously attributed the Long Man to Neolithic ritual, a Roman or Saxon god, or medieval monks from the nearby priory. However, the earliest record is a drawing of 1710, notably different in details from the modern figure, and optically stimulated luminescence dating of buried soil, published in 2003, suggested the figure was cut in the early modern period, around 1545 or the 16th–17th centuries — making it possibly a Tudor or Stuart creation, perhaps religious or political in intent.

Folklore and modern use

The figure is a focus for neo-pagan and Druid celebrations, particularly at Lammas and the summer solstice, and features in local folklore and in fiction. In 2010 a giant image of a woman was temporarily laid out beside the Long Man as a publicity stunt, drawing criticism for its effect on the protected site.
