The Isles of the Blessed (Ancient Greek: μακάρων νῆσοι, makárōn nêsoi) are a paradisiacal region in Greek mythology, an afterlife realm to which heroes and other favoured mortals were conveyed by the gods to live in eternal ease. Located at the western edge of the earth, beyond or within the encircling stream of Oceanus, they were sometimes identified with, or merged into, the Elysian Fields, and in Roman geography they were the origin of the name of the real Fortunate Isles (Latin: Fortunatae Insulae), identified with the Canary Islands and Madeira.

In Greek literature

Hesiod, in Works and Days, describes the demigod heroes of the fourth age as dwelling "untouched by sorrow in the islands of the blessed along the shore of deep-swirling Ocean, happy heroes for whom the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit flourishing thrice a year, far from the deathless gods", with Cronus as their king. In Homer's Odyssey (Book 4), Menelaus is told he will not die but be sent to the Elysian plain at the ends of the earth, where Rhadamanthus rules and no snow or storm ever comes. Pindar's Second Olympian Ode gives the fullest vision: those who have kept their souls free from injustice through three lifetimes reach the tower of Cronus, where ocean breezes blow around the island, golden flowers blaze, and the righteous spend their time in games, music and sacrifice alongside Cadmus, Peleus and Achilles. Plato refers to the Isles in the eschatological myth of the Gorgias, where the truly philosophical dead are sent.

Geography and identification

As Greek geographical knowledge expanded westward, the mythical isles were attached to real Atlantic islands. Plutarch, in his life of Sertorius, tells of sailors who had reached islands of mild climate and gentle breezes in the Atlantic — identifiable with the Canaries — and Roman geographers such as Pliny the Elder describe the Fortunatae in detail, with individual island names (Canaria, Ninguaria, Capraria and others). The Roman poet Horace, in Epode 16, urged his fellow citizens to abandon civil-war Rome and seek the "arva beata", the blessed fields of the fortunate isles.

Later usage

The concept persisted through classical tradition into Renaissance cartography and utopian literature, feeding the Atlantic-island imagination that also produced St Brendan's Isle, Antillia and Hy-Brasil. Macaronesia — the modern biogeographical grouping of the Canaries, Madeira, the Azores and Cape Verde — derives its name from the same Greek word for "blessed", as does the Canary/Madeira designation of the Fortunate Isles preserved in their classical epithets. The phrase "Isles of the Blessed" remains a poetic byword for a remote, blissful afterlife paradise.
