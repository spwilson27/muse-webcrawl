Railgun

A railgun or rail gun is a linear motor device that uses electromagnetic force to launch projectiles at very high velocity. It consists of two parallel conducting rails along which a sliding armature is accelerated by the Lorentz force from a very large electric current — typically millions of amperes — passed through the rails and the armature.

Principles

Current flows down one rail, across the armature and back along the other rail. The magnetic fields generated between the rails interact with the current in the armature to produce a Lorentz force that pushes it, and any projectile in front of it, along the rails. Because no chemical propellant is used, muzzle velocities above 2 km/s are theoretically achievable, far beyond conventional guns.

Challenges

Practical railguns face severe engineering problems: rail erosion and damage from the enormous currents and friction, the need for compact pulsed-power supplies, and barrel life measured in tens of shots. Efficiency is low because much of the input energy becomes waste heat.

Development

The concept was patented by French inventor André Louis Octave Fauchon-Villeplée in 1917–18 and studied in Germany during the Second World War. In the 2000s and 2010s the US Navy developed a prototype intended for long-range naval bombardment, demonstrating shots of 32 megajoules, though the programme was shelved in favour of guided projectile work. China, Japan, India and other countries have also conducted railgun trials.
