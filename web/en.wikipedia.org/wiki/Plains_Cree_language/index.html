Plains Cree (nēhiyawēwin) is a dialect of the Cree language, an Algonquian language spoken by the Plains Cree people of the Canadian Prairies and northern Montana. It is the most widely spoken of the Cree dialects and the dialect on which standard written Cree is largely based.

Dialect features

Cree dialects are classified by how the Proto-Algonquian sound *l is reflected. In Plains Cree it is /j/ (written y), making it a "y-dialect" — hence nēhiyaw, "Cree person", where Swampy Cree has nēhinaw (n-dialect) and Atikamekw nehirowisiw (r-dialect). Plains Cree also merges the phonemes /ʃ/ (š) with /s/ in some positions in certain sub-dialects, and it preserves the distinction between /i/ and /ɪ/ more fully than northern dialects.

Grammar

Like other Algonquian languages, Plains Cree is polysynthetic: whole sentences can be expressed in a single complex verb word. Nouns are classified as animate or inanimate, verbs are conjugated for transitivity and animacy of their arguments, and the language uses a direct–inverse system and obviation to track third persons in discourse. Word order is flexible and largely pragmatic.

Writing and status

Plains Cree is written both in Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, a system devised in the 1840s by missionary James Evans, and in a Latin-based Standard Roman Orthography developed by linguists and Cree educators. Although Cree overall has the largest number of speakers of any Indigenous language in Canada, Plains Cree is considered vulnerable: most fluent speakers are older, and revitalisation programs include immersion schools, the nehiyawewin curriculum in Saskatchewan, and growing digital resources.
