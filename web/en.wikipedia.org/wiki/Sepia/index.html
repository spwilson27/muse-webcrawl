Sepia is a genus of cuttlefish in the family Sepiidae, encompassing some of the best-known and most widespread cuttlefish species. The genus includes the common cuttlefish (Sepia officinalis), one of the most intensively studied of all cephalopods. Species of Sepia are found in coastal waters across the Atlantic Ocean, the Mediterranean Sea, and the Indo-Pacific region.

Description

Members of Sepia have broad, flattened bodies bordered by narrow fins that run along most of the body's length. Like all cuttlefish, they possess an internal calcified shell known as the cuttlebone, a chambered, gas-filled structure that the animal uses to regulate buoyancy. They have eight arms and two retractile tentacles used to capture prey.

Cuttlefish of this genus are masters of camouflage, able to change the colour, pattern and texture of their skin within fractions of a second using chromatophores — pigment-containing cells controlled directly by the brain. This ability is used both to hide from predators and to communicate with other cuttlefish.

Human use

The cuttlebone of Sepia species has long been collected for use as a calcium supplement for caged birds and as a mould-making material in jewellery casting. The dark pigment secreted by these animals, also called sepia, was historically harvested and used as an ink and artists' pigment. Several species are important in commercial fisheries, particularly in the Mediterranean and East Asia.
