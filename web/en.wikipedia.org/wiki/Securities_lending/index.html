Securities lending

Placeholder image: transferable securities that may be lent between market participants.

Securities lending is the practice of temporarily transferring securities — such as stocks or bonds — from a lender to a borrower, who provides collateral and returns equivalent securities at the end of the loan. The lender earns a fee or a share of the return on reinvested cash collateral. Legal title passes to the borrower for the duration of the loan, allowing the borrower to deliver the securities to a third party, most commonly to settle a short sale.

How it works

Large institutional investors such as pension funds, mutual funds, and insurance companies hold vast portfolios of securities that agent lenders (often custodian banks) lend out on their behalf. Borrowers — typically broker-dealers and hedge funds — post collateral of slightly more than the market value of the borrowed securities (commonly 102–105 percent), marked to market daily. In cash-collateral arrangements, the lender reinvests the cash and pays the borrower a rebate rate; the spread is the lender's revenue. The borrower also makes payments equivalent to any dividends or interest paid on the securities during the loan.

Purposes and risks

Securities lending supports market liquidity, settlement, and price discovery by allowing short selling and preventing failed deliveries. However, it carries risks: the borrower may default when the securities have risen in value, and reinvestment of cash collateral can generate losses, as occurred during the 2008 financial crisis when some lenders' cash pools held illiquid mortgage-backed securities. Regulators have since increased transparency and capital requirements for the practice.
