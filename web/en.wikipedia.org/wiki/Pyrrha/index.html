Pyrrha (Ancient Greek: Πύρρα, "flame-coloured, red") is a figure in Greek mythology, the daughter of Epimetheus and Pandora and the wife of Deucalion, son of Prometheus. With Deucalion she is the survivor of the great flood sent by Zeus, and the mother—directly and symbolically—of the renewed human race.

The flood myth

Warned by Prometheus of Zeus's plan to destroy the corrupt Bronze Age of man with a deluge, Deucalion built a chest (larnax) in which he and Pyrrha floated for nine days and nights until they came to rest on Mount Parnassus. When the waters receded, the couple sacrificed to Zeus the Escape-giver, and Deucalion consulted the oracle of Themis on how to restore humankind.

The oracle famously replied that they should "cover their heads and throw the bones of their mother behind them." Understanding "mother" as Gaia, Mother Earth, and her "bones" as stones, Deucalion and Pyrrha gathered stones and cast them over their shoulders: those thrown by Deucalion became men, and those thrown by Pyrrha became women. The tale is retold most fully in Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 1) and Apollodorus's Library.

Offspring and legacy

Pyrrha's children by Deucalion included Hellen, eponymous ancestor of the Hellenes (the Greeks), Amphictyon, and Protogeneia. The myth parallels other flood traditions of the ancient Near East, including those of Noah and of Utnapishtim. In a separate usage, "Pyrrha" was also the name under which Achilles was disguised as a girl at the court of Lycomedes on Skyros.
