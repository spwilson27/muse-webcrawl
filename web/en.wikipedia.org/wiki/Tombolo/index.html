Tombolo

Tombolo
DefinitionDeposition landform: a sand bar connecting an island to the mainland or another island
EtymologyItalian tombolo, from Latin tumulus, "mound"

A tombolo, from Italian tombolo, derived from the Latin tumulus meaning "mound", is a deposition landform by which an island becomes attached to the mainland by a narrow piece of land such as a spit or bar. Once attached, the island is then known as a tied island. The word tombolo sometimes refers to the formation of a spit by longshore drift; several bars may join to enclose a lagoon, as in the Venetian lidi.

Formation

Tombolos form where wave refraction around an island creates a zone of reduced wave energy in its lee, causing longshore drift currents converging from either side to deposit sediment. Over time the accumulating ridge of sand or shingle grows above sea level and joins the island to the shore. A submerged or partly awash ridge connecting an island is called a salience if it does not fully emerge. Tombolos are sensitive to changes in sediment supply and can migrate, breach, or erode with storms and sea-level change.

Examples

Well-known tombolos include Chesil Beach, which connects the Isle of Portland to mainland Dorset, England; the tombolo joining St Ninian's Isle to Shetland, the largest active tombolo in the United Kingdom; Monte Argentario in Tuscany, Italy, connected to the mainland by two (formerly three) tombolos enclosing the Orbetello Lagoon; the tombolo of Giens near Hyères, France; and Punta del Fangar and other features in Spain. The Rock of Gibraltar is connected to Spain by a sandy isthmus of related origin.

Significance

Tied islands have often acquired strategic and settlement importance: ports and fortresses such as Tyre (connected by Alexander the Great's siege causeway, later widened by sedimentation) and Cádiz owe their geography to tombolo-like connections, and such landforms remain important in coastal engineering and habitat conservation.
