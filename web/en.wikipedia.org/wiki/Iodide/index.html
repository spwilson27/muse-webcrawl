Iodide


Formula: I−
Type: Halide anion

An iodide ion is an iodine atom with a −1 charge (I−). Compounds containing iodine in the −1 oxidation state are called iodides. Iodide is one of the largest monatomic anions, with a radius of about 206 picometres; consequently its salts tend to be more soluble and less lattice-stabilised than those of the smaller halides. Common iodides include potassium iodide (KI), sodium iodide (NaI), and silver iodide (AgI).

Occurrence and uses

Iodide occurs naturally in seawater and brines and is concentrated by kelp. It is an essential trace nutrient: the thyroid gland takes up iodide to make the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), and iodised salt — table salt supplemented with potassium iodide or iodate — is used worldwide to prevent iodine deficiency disorders such as goitre and cretinism. Potassium iodide is also used medically to block thyroid uptake of radioactive iodine after nuclear accidents, in expectorants, and in the chemical synthesis of organoiodine compounds; silver iodide is used in photography and cloud seeding.

Chemistry

Iodide is a mild reducing agent and is readily oxidised to elemental iodine (I2), which gives the familiar brown colour in solution and the blue-black starch–iodine test. In analytical chemistry, iodometry exploits this redox behaviour.
