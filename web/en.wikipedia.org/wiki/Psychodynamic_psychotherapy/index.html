Psychodynamic psychotherapy

Origins: Psychoanalysis (Freud, Breuer)
Key concepts: Unconscious processes, transference, defence mechanisms, early relationships
Formats: Long-term and brief/manualized forms

Psychodynamic psychotherapy is a form of talk therapy derived from psychoanalysis that aims to help patients understand how unconscious mental processes and unresolved conflicts — especially those rooted in early relationships — shape current feelings, behaviour, and relationships. It is typically less intensive than classical psychoanalysis, with the patient sitting up and meeting the therapist once or twice a week.

Technique

Central techniques include free association, exploration of patterns in relationships, attention to transference (the patient's feelings toward the therapist), interpretation of defences and resistance, and working through recurring themes. Schools within the tradition include ego psychology, object relations, self psychology, and relational psychoanalysis.

Evidence

Meta-analyses have found psychodynamic therapy effective for depression, anxiety, somatic symptom disorders, and personality disorders, with some studies suggesting benefits that continue to grow after treatment ends. Brief manualized versions such as mentalization-based and transference-focused therapy have been developed for borderline personality disorder.
