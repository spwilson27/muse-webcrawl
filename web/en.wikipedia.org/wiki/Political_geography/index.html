Political geography

Political geography is a subfield of human geography concerned with the study of both the spatially uneven outcomes of political processes and the ways in which political processes are themselves affected by spatial structures. Conventionally, political geography adopts a three-scale structure for the purposes of analysis, investigating the state at the centre, above it the international sphere of geopolitics, and below it localities and places.

History

The discipline's origins lie in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Friedrich Ratzel developed an organic theory of the state, arguing that states grow and compete for Lebensraum (living space). The British geographer Halford Mackinder advanced the "Heartland theory" in 1904, holding that control of Eastern Europe and the Eurasian interior was key to world power. The Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén coined the term geopolitics. After the Second World War, the association of German Geopolitik with Nazi expansionism discredited parts of the field, and political geography rebuilt itself around electoral geography, federalism, and the spatial analysis of political behavior.

Subfields

Electoral geography

Electoral geography studies voting patterns in space, including the effects of constituency boundaries, gerrymandering, and the geography of party support.

Geopolitics

Modern geopolitics analyzes how geographic factors shape international relations and state strategy. From the 1980s, critical geopolitics emerged, examining how geopolitical knowledge is produced through discourse — in speeches, maps, media, and popular culture — rather than treating geography as an objective given.

Geographies of the state and territory

Scholars investigate borders and boundaries, state territoriality, federal systems, secession, and the rescaling of political authority to supranational bodies such as the European Union and to cities and regions.

Notable concepts

  • Territory — an area of space claimed or occupied under political control.
  • Boundary — the line delimiting political units; borders are increasingly studied as processes ("bordering") rather than fixed lines.
  • Place — localities as arenas where national and global politics take concrete form.
  • Scale — the organization of politics across local, national, and global levels.
