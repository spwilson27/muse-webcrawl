The New Forest is one of the largest remaining tracts of unenclosed pasture land, heathland, and forest in Southern England, covering southwest Hampshire and southeast Wiltshire. It was proclaimed a royal forest by William the Conqueror, featuring in the Domesday Book of 1086. The area was designated a national park in 2005. It is famed for the New Forest pony and other livestock that roam freely under the traditional system of commoning.

History

William I created the forest around 1079 for royal hunting of deer, imposing forest law that restricted local inhabitants' use of the land. The Rufus Stone near Minstead marks the spot where, according to tradition, William II ("William Rufus") was killed by an arrow while hunting in 1100. Timber from the Forest supplied the Royal Navy for centuries. The New Forest Act 1877 confirmed commoners' rights and established the Court of Verderers in its modern form; further acts followed in 1949, 1964, and 1970. In March 2005 the area became England's newest (at the time) national park.

Commoning and livestock

Some 700 commoners exercise rights of common — including pasture for ponies, cattle, donkeys, and pigs (the autumn "pannage" turn-out, when pigs eat fallen acorns) — attached to properties in and around the Forest. Around 3,000–5,000 ponies roam the open forest; they are owned by commoners, monitored by five Agisters employed by the Verderers, and rounded up in annual "drifts" for health checks and sale. Free-roaming animals have right of way on Forest roads, and speed limits and campaigns address animal accidents.

Ecology

The New Forest is one of Europe's most important sites for nature conservation, designated as a Special Area of Conservation, Special Protection Area, Ramsar site, and Site of Special Scientific Interest. Its mosaic of lowland heath, mire, ancient pasture woodland (with veteran beech and oak pollards), and grassland supports rare species including the Dartford warbler, sand lizard, smooth snake, southern damselfly, and all six native British reptiles. Fallow, red, roe, and sika deer are present.

Governance

Most of the Crown land is managed by Forestry England; the New Forest National Park Authority oversees planning and conservation for the park area, and the Verderers' Court protects commoners' interests and the Forest's character. Principal settlements include Lyndhurst (the traditional "capital"), Brockenhurst, Lymington, Ringwood, and Fordingbridge.
