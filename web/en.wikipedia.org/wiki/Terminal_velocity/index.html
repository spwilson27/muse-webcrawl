Terminal velocity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Typical values
Skydiver (belly-to-earth)~53 m/s (190 km/h; 120 mph)
Skydiver (head-down)~90 m/s (320 km/h; 200 mph)
Raindrop (2 mm)~6.5 m/s
Baseball~33 m/s (74 mph)

Terminal velocity is the maximum speed attainable by an object as it falls through a fluid (air being the most common example). It is reached when the sum of the drag force and buoyancy equals the downward force of gravity acting on the object. Since the net force is then zero, the object ceases to accelerate and falls at constant speed.

Physics

Using the drag equation, terminal velocity vt is given by

vt = √( 2mg / (ρ A Cd) )

where m is the mass of the falling object, g is the acceleration due to gravity, Cd is the drag coefficient, ρ is the density of the fluid, and A is the projected area of the object. Terminal velocity therefore increases with mass and decreases with drag and frontal area; it also increases at higher altitude, where the air is thinner.

Examples

A human skydiver in a belly-to-earth position reaches about 53 m/s (120 mph) after roughly 12 seconds of fall; adopting a head-down posture, reducing the projected area, raises this to around 90 m/s or more. Small creatures have low terminal velocities relative to their strength, which is why a mouse or an ant can survive falls from heights lethal to a human — a scaling effect discussed by J. B. S. Haldane in his essay "On Being the Right Size". Raindrops fall at a few metres per second, while a hailstone can exceed 40 m/s.

Approach to terminal velocity

An object dropped from rest approaches terminal velocity asymptotically: its speed follows a hyperbolic-tangent curve in time for quadratic drag, reaching 90% of terminal velocity in a time comparable to vt/g scaled by a factor of order unity. The same concept applies to objects rising in a fluid (such as bubbles) and to sedimentation, where the small terminal velocity of particles is exploited in centrifuges and settling tanks.
