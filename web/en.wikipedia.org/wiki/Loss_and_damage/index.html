Loss and damage

Loss and damage, in the context of climate change policy, refers to the harms caused by climate change that cannot be — or have not been — avoided through mitigation and adaptation. It encompasses economic losses (destroyed infrastructure, crops, property) and non-economic losses (lives, health, culture, territory, biodiversity), arising from both extreme events such as hurricanes and floods and slow-onset processes such as sea-level rise and desertification.

In international negotiations

The concept was championed for decades by small island developing states through the Alliance of Small Island States (AOSIS), which proposed an insurance mechanism as early as 1991. The Warsaw International Mechanism for Loss and Damage was established under the UNFCCC in 2013, and Article 8 of the 2015 Paris Agreement recognised the importance of averting, minimising and addressing loss and damage, while expressly disclaiming liability and compensation. At COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh (2022) parties agreed to create a dedicated Loss and Damage Fund for vulnerable developing countries; the fund was operationalised at COP28 in Dubai (2023) with initial pledges of several hundred million dollars and the World Bank as interim host.

Debates

Estimates of annual loss and damage in developing countries run to hundreds of billions of dollars by 2030. Contentious issues include who pays, sources of finance, eligibility, and the relationship with adaptation funding. Attribution science — linking specific extreme events to anthropogenic warming — increasingly informs the debate.
