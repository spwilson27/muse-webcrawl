SlutWalk

SlutWalk is a transnational movement of protest marches calling for an end to rape culture, including victim blaming and slut shaming of sexual assault victims. Participants protest against explaining or excusing rape by referring to any aspect of a woman's appearance, and call for an end to the culture of placing blame on victims of sexual violence.

History

The movement began on April 3, 2011, in Toronto, Ontario, Canada, after Toronto Police Service officer Michael Sanguinetti told a group of York University students on January 24, 2011, that "women should avoid dressing like sluts in order not to be victimized." In response, co-founders Sonya Barnett and Heather Jarvis organized the first SlutWalk march, which drew roughly 3,000 to 4,000 participants.

The name was chosen deliberately to reclaim the word "slut" and challenge its use as a tool for policing women's behaviour. Organizers argued that a person's clothing, sexual history, or perceived promiscuity is never an invitation to assault, and that responsibility for sexual violence rests entirely with perpetrators.

Spread of the movement

Within months, SlutWalk marches spread to dozens of cities worldwide, including New York City, London, Sydney, Berlin, Chicago, Los Angeles, Amsterdam, Mexico City, Buenos Aires, New Delhi, and Cape Town. The marches typically feature participants dressed in a wide range of clothing — from everyday attire to deliberately provocative outfits — carrying signs with slogans such as "My clothes are not my consent" and "Don't tell us how to dress, tell men not to rape."

Criticism and debate

The movement has attracted criticism from several directions. Some feminists objected to reclaiming the word "slut," arguing it reinforces the term's degrading connotations. Others, including Black feminist organizations, criticized SlutWalk for centring the experiences of white, middle-class women and for the historical weight of sexual stereotypes applied to women of colour. Some critics also questioned whether the marches' confrontational style advanced concrete policy change. Supporters responded that the movement's core message — that no one asks to be raped — resonated across diverse communities and helped ignite broader public conversation about consent and rape culture.

Legacy

SlutWalk is widely regarded as an early landmark of fourth-wave feminism and of social-media-era protest organizing. Its emphasis on consent and against victim blaming influenced later movements and campaigns addressing sexual harassment and assault, and annual marches continue in a number of cities.
