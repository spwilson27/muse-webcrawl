Organic compound

An organic compound is a chemical compound containing carbon–hydrogen bonds; more broadly, organic chemistry is the study of compounds of carbon. The class includes almost all compounds found in living organisms — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids — as well as hydrocarbons, fossil fuels, plastics, pharmaceuticals, dyes, and solvents. A few simple carbon compounds, such as carbon dioxide, carbonates, and carbides, are conventionally treated as inorganic.

History

"Organic" originally meant derived from living organisms, and early chemists believed a "vital force" was required to make such substances. Friedrich Wöhler's synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate in 1828 undermined that doctrine and opened the way to modern synthetic organic chemistry, which has since produced millions of characterised compounds — far more than for all other elements combined.

Properties and classification

Carbon's ability to form four covalent bonds and to bond to itself in chains and rings (catenation) gives organic compounds their enormous diversity. They are classified by functional groups — alcohols, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, amines, and so on — which determine characteristic reactivity. Organic compounds are typically molecular, with relatively low melting and boiling points, flammability, and low solubility in water unless polar or ionisable.
