Sensibility

Sensibility refers to an acute perception or responsiveness toward something, such as the emotions of another person or the qualities of a work of art. In eighteenth-century thought it named a prized capacity for refined feeling — physical responsiveness to sensation combined with emotional and moral responsiveness to the suffering and joys of others — and it shaped the literature and philosophy of the period.

In eighteenth-century thought

Philosophers such as the Earl of Shaftesbury, David Hume, and Adam Smith grounded morality in feeling rather than reason alone, arguing that sympathy depends on sensibility. Physicians simultaneously understood sensibility as a property of the nerves, the body's responsiveness to stimuli, debated alongside "irritability" in the physiology of Albrecht von Haller. The concept therefore linked medicine, moral philosophy, and aesthetics.

The cult of sensibility

In literature, the "cult of sensibility" flourished from the 1760s to the 1790s. Novels of sensibility — such as Laurence Sterne's A Sentimental Journey (1768) and Henry Mackenzie's The Man of Feeling (1771) — celebrated characters of exquisite emotional responsiveness, whose tears and trembling demonstrated their virtue. Jane Austen's Sense and Sensibility (1811) satirized the excesses of the mode while engaging seriously with its questions about feeling and restraint.

Later criticism

By the end of the eighteenth century, sensibility attracted backlash: critics associated it with effeminacy, indulgence, and political radicalism, and writers such as Mary Wollstonecraft argued both for women's rational education and against false sensibility. In modern usage the word has broadened to mean a person's characteristic outlook or aesthetic temperament, as in "modernist sensibilities".
