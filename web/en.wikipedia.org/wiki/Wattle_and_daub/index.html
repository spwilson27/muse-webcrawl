Wattle and daub

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wattle and daub infill between oak timbers.

Wattle and daub is a composite building method used for making walls, in which a woven lattice of wooden strips called wattle is daubed with a sticky material usually made of some combination of wet soil, clay, sand, animal dung, and straw. It is one of the oldest known building techniques, with evidence of its use dating back at least 6,000 years to the Neolithic period, and it has been employed on every inhabited continent.

Construction

The wattle is typically formed from thin branches — commonly hazel, willow, or oak — woven between upright staves fixed into a timber frame or into the ground. The daub is mixed by treading or stirring, often with chopped straw or hair added to reduce cracking, and is pressed by hand into and over the wattle. Once dry, the wall is often limewashed to improve its appearance and weather resistance. Because the materials shrink as they dry, several coats may be applied.

History

Wattle and daub was the standard infill for timber-framed buildings across medieval and early modern Europe, including the half-timbered houses of England and Germany. It was also used throughout Africa, Asia, and the Americas, where local variants employed bamboo, reeds, or branches with mud plaster. In colonial North America and Australia it was a common technique for early settler buildings.

Although superseded by brick, lath and plaster, and modern drywall in industrialised countries, wattle and daub survives in vernacular building and has seen renewed interest in natural building movements for its low cost, local materials, and low embodied energy.

Properties

Wattle and daub walls have good thermal mass and moderate insulation, are breathable, and can last for centuries when protected from driving rain by wide eaves or render. Their chief weakness is moisture damage and vulnerability to fire once the daub has cracked away, though in practice timber-framed buildings have proven surprisingly durable.
