Müller-Lyer illusion

The Müller-Lyer illusion is an optical illusion consisting of a set of arrow-like figures. When viewers judge the length of the central line, they find that a line with arrowheads pointing outward (like the tail fins of an arrow) looks shorter than an identical line with arrowheads pointing inward, even though the two lines are exactly the same length. The illusion was devised by the German sociologist and psychiatrist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer in 1889.

Explanation

Several explanations have been proposed. One holds that the visual system misapplies size-constancy mechanisms: the inward-pointing fins are interpreted as the far corner of a room or a receding edge (suggesting a longer extent), while the outward-pointing fins resemble a near corner (suggesting a shorter extent) — the "carpentered world" hypothesis, which predicts stronger effects in cultures accustomed to rectangular buildings. Other accounts invoke low-level spatial filtering or confusion between the length of the shaft and the overall extent of the figure.

Cultural variation

The strength of the illusion varies across populations: studies in the 20th century found that some non-Western groups, such as San hunter-gatherers, were far less susceptible, making the Müller-Lyer illusion a famous example in discussions of whether perception is shaped by visual environment and culture.
