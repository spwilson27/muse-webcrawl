Waveform

Common typesSine, square, triangle, sawtooth, pulse
Described byAmplitude, frequency, phase, harmonic content
Displayed withOscilloscope; audio-editing software

The waveform of a signal is the shape of its graph as a function of time, regardless of its time and magnitude scales and of any displacement in time. In electronics, the term is usually applied to periodically varying voltages, currents or electromagnetic fields. In acoustics and music production it describes the variation of air pressure (or its sampled representation) over time; the waveform determines the timbre of a sound for a given pitch and loudness.

Common waveforms

  • Sine wave — the smooth fundamental oscillation of simple harmonic motion; the only waveform containing a single frequency.
  • Square wave — alternates abruptly between two levels with equal dwell times; contains odd harmonics.
  • Triangle wave — rises and falls linearly; rich in odd harmonics that fall off faster than a square wave's.
  • Sawtooth wave — ramps linearly in one direction and resets abruptly; contains all harmonics.
  • Pulse wave — like a square wave but with an asymmetric duty cycle.

Analysis and synthesis

By Fourier analysis, any periodic waveform can be decomposed into a sum of sine waves at the fundamental frequency and its harmonics, with characteristic amplitudes and phases. Conversely, additive synthesis builds complex tones by summing sinusoids — the principle behind function generators, synthesisers and Fourier-based signal processing. Deviations of a practical waveform from the ideal (for example the distortion of mains-voltage sine waves by non-linear loads) are quantified by measures such as total harmonic distortion.

Applications

Waveform shape is central to power engineering (AC supply), telecommunications (modulated carrier waveforms), medical monitoring (the ECG and EEG traces are clinically interpreted largely by their shape), and digital audio workstations, which display audio files as waveform overviews for editing.
