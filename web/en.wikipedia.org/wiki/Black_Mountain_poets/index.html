Black Mountain poets

Black Mountain College, North Carolina, home of the Black Mountain poets

The Black Mountain poets, sometimes called the projectivist poets, were a group of mid-20th-century American avant-garde poets associated with Black Mountain College in North Carolina. The group's approach to poetry was defined by Charles Olson's influential 1950 essay "Projective Verse," and its members came to define an important alternative tradition in postwar American poetry, in opposition to the academic formalism then dominant.

Projective verse

In "Projective Verse," published in Poetry New York in 1950, Charles Olson proposed a poetics of "open field" composition: the poem as a transfer of energy from the poet to the reader, governed by the breath and by perception rather than by inherited meter and stanza forms. Olson summarized the method as "one perception must immediately and directly lead to a further perception." Form, in his famous phrase, "is never more than an extension of content." Robert Creeley, with whom Olson corresponded intensively, contributed the complementary formulation that form is never more than a revelation of content.

Principal figures

The core of the group consisted of Charles Olson, Robert Creeley, and Robert Duncan. Other poets associated with the school include Denise Levertov, Paul Blackburn, Hilda Morley, John Wieners, Ed Dorn, Larry Eigner, Jonathan Williams (who was also the group's publisher through the Jargon Society), and Joel Oppenheimer. Although Levertov and Duncan were not in residence at the college, their correspondence and shared aesthetic placed them within the movement.

The Black Mountain Review

The group's ideas were disseminated through the Black Mountain Review (1954–1957), edited by Creeley from Mallorca, which published work by the Black Mountain poets alongside that of early Beat writers such as Allen Ginsberg and Jack Kerouac. In 1960 Donald Allen's anthology The New American Poetry 1945–1960 grouped the Black Mountain poets as one of five schools defining the new poetry, securing their place in literary history.

Influence

The Black Mountain poets influenced the Beat Generation, the San Francisco Renaissance, the New York School, and the Language poets. Their insistence on the poem as a scored breath and a field of action reshaped American poetics in the second half of the twentieth century.
