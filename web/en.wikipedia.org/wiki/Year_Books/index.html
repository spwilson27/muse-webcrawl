The Year Books are the earliest surviving series of law reports in England, recording cases heard in the royal courts — principally the Court of Common Pleas — from about 1268 (or, in continuous form, the reign of Edward I) to 1535. Organised by regnal year and legal term (Michaelmas, Hilary, Easter, and Trinity), from which their name derives, they are the primary source for the development of the English common law in the medieval and early Tudor periods.

Content and character

Unlike modern law reports, the Year Books focus less on the facts and outcome of a case than on the oral argument: the exchanges between serjeants-at-law and judges over pleadings and points of law, often recorded in lively dialogue. They were probably compiled by apprentices and junior lawyers as learning exercises. Written mainly in Anglo-Norman (Law French) with Latin headings, they remained in manuscript until printed editions began to appear shortly after the introduction of printing; the standard collected edition is the "Vulgate" of 1678–1680.

Legacy

Cases from the Year Books were mined by later lawyers and institutional writers, including the compilers of abridgments such as Statham's, Fitzherbert's, and Brooke's, through which medieval case law influenced the early modern common law. After 1535 the Year Books were succeeded by the nominate reports of individual reporters such as Edmund Plowden and Sir Edward Coke. The Selden Society has published scholarly editions and translations of many early Year Books, making them accessible to legal historians.
