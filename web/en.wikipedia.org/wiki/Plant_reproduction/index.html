Plant reproduction

Plant reproduction is the production of new individual plants, achieved either sexually, through the fusion of gametes, or asexually, by vegetative growth. All land plants exhibit an alternation of generations: a multicellular haploid gametophyte generation alternates with a multicellular diploid sporophyte generation.

Sexual reproduction

In flowering plants (angiosperms), the sporophyte is the familiar plant, and the gametophytes are reduced to the pollen grain (male) and the embryo sac within the ovule (female). Pollination — transfer of pollen to a stigma by wind, water, or animals — is followed by a uniquely angiosperm event, double fertilisation: one sperm cell fuses with the egg to form the zygote, while another fuses with the central cell to form the endosperm that nourishes the seed. The ovary then develops into fruit, aiding seed dispersal. Gymnosperms such as conifers bear ovules on cones and lack double fertilisation; ferns and mosses release free spores, and mosses and ferns still require water for their motile sperm to reach the egg.

Asexual reproduction

Many plants clone themselves from runners, rhizomes, tubers, bulbs, and other vegetative structures, and some produce seeds asexually (apomixis). Asexual reproduction lets a successful genotype colonise a site rapidly and reliably, at the cost of genetic uniformity; combining both strategies, as many weeds do, spreads risk between stability and variation. Human propagation of plants exploits both pathways, from sowing seed to grafting orchards.
