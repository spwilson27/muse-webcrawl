Memoir

A memoir (from French mémoire, from Latin memoria, "memory") is a nonfiction narrative written from the perspective of the author about experiences in their own life. Unlike an autobiography, which typically recounts the author's entire life story chronologically, a memoir usually focuses on a particular period, theme or set of experiences, and is concerned as much with emotional truth and reflection as with factual completeness.

Characteristics

Memoirs are written in the first person and centre on the author's personal knowledge and recollection. While memoirists draw on memory, journals and letters, the genre accepts a degree of reconstruction — of dialogue, for example — that formal biography does not, though readers and publishers expect memoirs not to be fabricated. Scandals over embellished or invented episodes in commercially successful memoirs have periodically sharpened debate about the genre's obligations to fact.

History

Personal recollections are as old as writing — ancient examples include Julius Caesar's Commentarii — but memoir flourished as a distinct form in early modern Europe, where courtiers, soldiers and statesmen recorded the events and personalities of their times. The twentieth century saw the rise of the literary memoir by novelists and poets, and from the 1990s a marked publishing boom in memoir, including coming-of-age, illness, addiction, travel and family narratives by previously unknown authors.

Subgenres

Recognisable subgenres include the war memoir, the political memoir, the childhood memoir, the travel memoir, the illness or "misery" memoir, the culinary memoir, and the celebrity memoir, the last frequently written with a ghostwriter. The graphic memoir — such as works in comics form — has become prominent since the late twentieth century.

Memoir vs autobiography

The two forms overlap, and publishers sometimes use the terms loosely, but the conventional distinction is one of scope and emphasis: autobiography aspires to a comprehensive, factual account of a whole life, often by a notable figure; memoir selects and shapes experience around themes, privileging voice, scene and subjective meaning over documentary completeness.
