Late Bronze Age collapse

Invasions, destruction, and the abandonment of cities, c. 1200 BC

The Late Bronze Age collapse was a period of societal collapse in the Mediterranean basin during the 12th century BC, roughly 1200–1150 BC. Within a few decades, nearly every major city between Greece and Gaza was destroyed, many never reoccupied: the Mycenaean palatial civilisation vanished, the Hittite Empire fell, and Egypt's New Kingdom declined sharply.

Evidence

Ugarit, Hattusa, Mycenae, and numerous Levantine cities show destruction layers dated to this period. Egyptian inscriptions of Merneptah and Ramesses III record invasions by the mysterious "Sea Peoples". Linear B writing disappeared from Greece, which entered a "Dark Age" lasting centuries.

Proposed causes

Scholarly explanations combine multiple stressors: drought and famine (supported by pollen and climate data), earthquakes, systems collapse of interdependent palace trade networks, internal rebellion, and invasions including the Sea Peoples. Eric Cline's 1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed argues for a "perfect storm" of simultaneous crises overwhelming a hyper-connected world.

Aftermath

Survivors and successors included Phoenician city-states, Israel and Judah, Philistine cities, Neo-Hittite states, and eventually Assyria; ironworking spread widely, giving the succeeding Iron Age its name.
