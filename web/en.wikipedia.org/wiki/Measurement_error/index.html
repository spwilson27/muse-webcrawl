Measurement error


Accuracy versus precision: random and systematic error

Measurement error (also called observational error) is the difference between a measured value of a quantity and its true value. In statistics, an error is not necessarily a "mistake": variability is an inherent part of the results of measurements and of the measurement process.

Random and systematic error

Measurement errors can be divided into two components: random error and systematic error.

  • Random errors are errors in measurement that lead to measurable values being inconsistent when repeated measurements of a constant attribute or quantity are taken. They arise from unpredictable fluctuations in the readings of a measurement apparatus, or in the experimenter's interpretation. Random error can be reduced by taking the average of many repeated measurements.
  • Systematic errors are errors that are not determined by chance but are introduced by an inaccuracy inherent in the system, such as a miscalibrated instrument or a biased sampling method. They push measurements consistently in one direction and cannot be reduced simply by repetition.

Sources

Sources of error include imperfect calibration, environmental interference, limitations of the instrument's resolution (including digitisation), the observer's reading of analogue scales, and imperfect methods of observation. In survey research, measurement error also encompasses invalid responses, question wording effects, and interviewer effects.

Consequences

In regression analysis, measurement error in an independent variable generally biases coefficient estimates toward zero (attenuation bias), reducing statistical power and potentially invalidating conclusions. Errors-in-variables models and instrumental variable techniques are used to address this problem.
