Trivialism

Trivialism
FieldPhilosophical logic
ClaimAll statements, including contradictions, are true
ContrastDialetheism (some contradictions are true)
Notable proponent (explored by)Graham Priest

Trivialism is the logical theory that all statements—and their negations—are true. Equivalently, it holds that every contradiction is true. A theory or belief system is "trivial", in this technical sense, if it endorses every possible statement.

Relation to the principle of explosion

In classical logic, the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet) states that from a single contradiction anything follows. Trivialism can thus be seen as the view that this process is sound and that its conclusion—the truth of everything—should be accepted rather than treated as a reductio of the premises.

Discussion

Almost no philosopher has seriously held trivialism as a sincere doctrine; it is discussed mainly as a limiting case in the study of paraconsistent logics and dialetheism, the view that some contradictions are true. Graham Priest has examined arguments for and against trivialism, arguing that while dialetheism is defensible, trivialism faces decisive objections—for example, that a trivialist cannot meaningfully deny anything, including trivialism itself. Some commentators have drawn parallels with certain interpretations of Buddhist or mystical texts, though these are usually read metaphorically.
