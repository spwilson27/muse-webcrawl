Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Clergy celebrating the Divine Liturgy

The Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom is the most celebrated Divine Liturgy in the Byzantine Rite. It is named after its core part, the anaphora attributed to Saint John Chrysostom, Archbishop of Constantinople in the 5th century. It reflects the work of the Cappadocian Fathers to both combat heresies and define Trinitarian theology for the Christian Church.

The Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom is celebrated on most days of the year in Eastern Orthodox and Byzantine Catholic churches, except during Great Lent on weekdays, when the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts is used, and on certain feasts when the longer Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great is celebrated instead.

Structure

The liturgy comprises three parts: the Liturgy of Preparation (Prothesis), in which the bread and wine are prepared; the Liturgy of the Word (or of the Catechumens), consisting of antiphons, hymns, readings from the Apostle and Gospel, and a sermon; and the Liturgy of the Faithful, containing the Great Entrance, the Creed, the anaphora with the epiclesis, the Lord's Prayer, and the distribution of Holy Communion.

Music

The liturgy has inspired many composers. Celebrated choral settings include those by Tchaikovsky (Op. 41), Rachmaninoff (Op. 31), and numerous others in the Slavic choral tradition.
