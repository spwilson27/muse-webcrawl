Quintic function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebra, a quintic function is a function of the form

g(x) = ax5 + bx4 + cx3 + dx2 + ex + f,

where a, b, c, d, e and f are constants (usually rational, real or complex numbers) and a is nonzero. In other words, a quintic function is a polynomial function of degree five. The equation obtained by setting a quintic function equal to zero is a quintic equation.

Solvability by radicals

Whereas cubic and quartic equations can be solved by general formulas involving only arithmetic operations and root extractions, the Abel–Ruffini theorem, proved by Paolo Ruffini and Niels Henrik Abel in the early nineteenth century, establishes that there is no general algebraic formula for the roots of a quintic equation in terms of its coefficients. Galois theory later explained this phenomenon: a quintic is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group, which for degree five means the group must be contained in a certain metacyclic group of order 20.

A practical criterion, due to Galois theory, is that an irreducible quintic with rational coefficients is solvable by radicals if and only if it has exactly one pair of non-real complex roots (that is, exactly three real roots) or is otherwise reducible in a specific way; the general solvable quintic can be transformed to the Bring–Jerrard form x5 + px + q = 0.

Solutions beyond radicals

Although radicals do not suffice in general, quintics can be solved using special functions. Charles Hermite showed in 1858 that the general quintic can be solved using elliptic modular functions, and Felix Klein later gave an approach based on the symmetries of the icosahedron. Quintics can also be solved in terms of Bring radicals, hypergeometric functions, or theta functions, and in practice are usually solved numerically.

Applications

Quintic polynomials arise in celestial mechanics, in the geometry of periodic motions, in interpolation (quintic splines give smooth curves with continuous acceleration), and in robotics, where quintic trajectories are used to plan smooth joint motions.
