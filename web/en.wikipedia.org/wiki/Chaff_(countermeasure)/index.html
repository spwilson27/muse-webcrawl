Chaff (countermeasure)

Chaff, originally called Window by the British and Düppel by the German Luftwaffe during World War II, is a radar countermeasure in which aircraft or other targets spread a cloud of small, thin pieces of aluminium, metallised glass fibre or plastic, which either appears as a cluster of false targets on radar screens or swamps the screen with multiple returns. Modern chaff is usually dispensed by military aircraft, ships and vehicles to confuse radar-guided missiles and radar-directed guns.

Principle

Chaff consists of fibres or strips cut to lengths corresponding to half the wavelength of the radar to be countered, so that each fibre acts as a resonant dipole and reflects radar energy strongly. When released, the cloud blooms rapidly and presents a radar cross-section that can rival or exceed that of the dispensing aircraft. A chaff cloud can be used as a decoy to seduce a missile away from its target, as a screen to mask approaching aircraft, or to saturate a radar picture so that operators cannot pick out real targets.

History

The idea of chaff was developed independently in Britain, Germany, the United States and Japan before and during the Second World War, but each side initially refrained from using it for fear the enemy would copy it. The British first used Window operationally during Operation Gomorrah, the raid on Hamburg on the night of 24–25 July 1943, when bombers dropped bundles of aluminised paper strips. The effect was dramatic: German radar screens were saturated and bomber losses fell sharply. The Germans responded with their own Düppel and with new tactics and radar wavelengths less susceptible to the countermeasure.

Post-war development produced chaff cartridges and dispensers integrated into aircraft countermeasure suites, along with rapidly blooming chaff designed to defeat missile seekers. Naval vessels also fire chaff rockets to create false targets for anti-ship missiles, as part of so-called "soft-kill" defences.

Modern use and limitations

Modern fire-control and missile radars use Doppler processing and other techniques to distinguish a slow-drifting chaff cloud from a fast-moving aircraft, reducing chaff's effectiveness when used alone; it is therefore usually combined with evasive manoeuvre and electronic jamming. Specialised chaff types have been developed for infrared deception in combination with flares. Environmental concerns about the vast quantities of fibres released during training have prompted the development of more degradable materials.
