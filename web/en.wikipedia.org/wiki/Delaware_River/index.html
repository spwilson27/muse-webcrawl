Delaware River

The Delaware River is a major river on the Atlantic coast of the United States, about 282 miles (454 km) long. It flows from the Catskill Mountains of New York, along the borders of New York with Pennsylvania, and of Pennsylvania with New Jersey, past the cities of Trenton, Philadelphia and Wilmington, into Delaware Bay and the Atlantic Ocean. It forms part of the borders of five US states and drains an area of about 13,500 square miles (35,000 km²). The river is named for Thomas West, 3rd Baron De La Warr.

The Delaware River at the Delaware Water Gap

Course

The river begins at the confluence of its East and West branches at Hancock, New York. It flows generally southward, cutting through the Appalachian ridges at the Delaware Water Gap, and becomes tidal at Trenton, New Jersey. Below Philadelphia the river widens into the estuary of Delaware Bay. Major tributaries include the Neversink, Lehigh and Schuylkill rivers. New York City draws much of its water supply from reservoirs in the upper Delaware basin under a 1954 Supreme Court decree.

History

The river valley was home to the Lenape people before European colonisation by the Dutch, Swedes and English in the seventeenth century. It is famous in American history for George Washington's crossing of the Delaware on the night of 25–26 December 1776, before the Battle of Trenton. In the nineteenth century the river and its canals carried coal and other freight, and its ports — Philadelphia, Camden, Chester and Wilmington — became major centres of shipbuilding and industry. The Delaware River Basin Commission, created in 1961, manages water resources for the four basin states and the federal government.

Environment

The Delaware is the longest free-flowing (undammed) main-stem river in the eastern United States. Water quality improved markedly after the Clean Water Act of 1972, and the river supports fisheries including American shad, striped bass and sturgeon, as well as the port complex around Philadelphia, among the busiest on the Atlantic coast.
