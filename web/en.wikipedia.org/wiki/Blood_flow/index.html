Blood flow

Blood flow is the movement of blood through the vessels of the circulatory system. In a healthy body, blood circulates continuously, delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues and removing carbon dioxide and metabolic waste products. The study of blood flow is called hemodynamics.

Mechanics

Blood flow is driven by the pressure gradient generated by the pumping of the heart. Flow is governed by relationships analogous to Ohm's law: flow equals the pressure difference divided by vascular resistance. Resistance depends strongly on vessel radius — as described by Poiseuille's law, resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of the radius — as well as on vessel length and blood viscosity.

Velocity and vessel type

Blood moves fastest in the aorta and large arteries, where flow is pulsatile, and slowest in the capillaries, whose enormous total cross-sectional area allows time for exchange of gases, nutrients, and waste across their thin walls. In veins, flow is assisted by skeletal muscle contractions and one-way valves that prevent backflow.

Regulation

Blood flow is regulated locally by autoregulation — vessels dilate or constrict in response to metabolic demand, oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, and signaling molecules such as nitric oxide — and systemically by the autonomic nervous system and hormones that adjust heart rate, stroke volume, and peripheral resistance.

Disorders

Abnormal blood flow underlies many diseases, including ischemia (insufficient flow), thrombosis and embolism (obstruction by clots), aneurysm, and shock. Turbulent flow, which occurs at high velocities or at vessel branches and narrowings, can damage the endothelium and contribute to atherosclerosis.

Measurement

Clinicians measure blood flow using techniques such as Doppler ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging, and plethysmography.
