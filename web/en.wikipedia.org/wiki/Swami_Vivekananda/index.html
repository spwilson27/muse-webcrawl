Swami Vivekananda

Swami Vivekananda (born Narendranath Datta; 12 January 1863 – 4 July 1902) was an Indian Hindu monk, philosopher and author, and the chief disciple of the mystic Ramakrishna. He is chiefly remembered for introducing Vedanta and yoga to the Western world and for his celebrated speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893, which began "Sisters and brothers of America…" and brought Hinduism wide attention in the West.

Life and work

Born in Calcutta (Kolkata) into an aristocratic Bengali family, Narendranath became a disciple of Ramakrishna and, after his guru's death, a wandering monk who travelled the length of India. After his triumph in Chicago he lectured widely in the United States and Britain, founding the Vedanta Society of New York. Returning to India in 1897 to a hero's welcome, he founded the Ramakrishna Math and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897) to combine spiritual teaching with social service — education, healthcare and disaster relief.

Teachings and legacy

Vivekananda taught a practical Vedanta emphasising the divinity of every soul, religious tolerance, and service to humanity as worship of God. His works, including Raja Yoga and his letters and lectures, inspired generations, and he influenced the Indian independence movement. He died at age 39 at Belur Math, the monastery he founded near Kolkata. His birthday, 12 January, is celebrated in India as National Youth Day.
