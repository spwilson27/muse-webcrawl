The Abbey of the Paraclete (French: Abbaye du Paraclet) was a Benedictine nunnery in northern France, near Nogent-sur-Seine in the Aube. It grew out of an oratory founded by the philosopher Peter Abelard around 1122 and became famous as the community ruled for over thirty years by Héloïse, Abelard's former pupil, wife, and correspondent. The abbey survived until its suppression during the French Revolution in 1792.

Foundation

After his condemnation at the Council of Soissons (1121) and a period as a monk of Saint-Denis, Abelard withdrew to a tract of land near Nogent-sur-Seine given by the Count of Champagne, where he built an oratory of reeds and thatch dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Students flocked to him in such numbers that they rebuilt the oratory in stone; Abelard rededicated it to the Paraclete — the Holy Spirit as Comforter — a choice that drew charges of theological novelty, since dedicating a church to the Holy Spirit alone was unusual. Persecuted by his enemies, Abelard left around 1125 to become abbot of Saint-Gildas in Brittany.

Héloïse and the nuns

In 1129 the abbot Suger of Saint-Denis expelled the nuns of Argenteuil, claiming the property for his abbey. Abelard offered the abandoned Paraclete to Héloïse and her displaced nuns; Pope Innocent II confirmed the gift in 1131. Under Héloïse as abbess the Paraclete flourished: it acquired lands and daughter priories, developed its own liturgy — including hymns and sequences some of which Abelard composed for the sisters — and became a respected centre of female religious life. Héloïse's letters to Abelard from the Paraclete, asking for a rule suited to women, prompted his Rule for the community and his history of religious women. Abelard died in 1142 at the priory of Saint-Marcel; Héloïse had his body brought to the Paraclete, and on her death in 1164 she was buried beside him.

Later history and remains

The abbey endured through the Middle Ages and early modern period, its abbesses drawn increasingly from the nobility. Suppressed in 1792, its buildings were sold and largely demolished; the church was destroyed, and only the conventual buildings, a dovecote, and part of the crypt survive. In 1817 the remains of Abelard and Héloïse were transferred to Père Lachaise Cemetery in Paris, where their Gothic tomb — built partly from stones of the Paraclete — remains one of the cemetery's most visited monuments. A small obelisk on the site marks where the lovers were originally buried.
