Photosensitive epilepsy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


EEG photoparoxysmal response during intermittent photic stimulation.

Photosensitive epilepsy (PSE) is a form of epilepsy in which seizures are triggered by visual stimuli that form patterns in time or space, such as flashing lights, bold regular patterns, or regular moving patterns. It affects roughly 3% of people with epilepsy and is more common in children and adolescents, particularly in juvenile myoclonic epilepsy.

Triggers

The most provocative stimuli are flashes at frequencies between about 3 and 30 Hz (most commonly 15–25 Hz), high contrast patterns such as stripes, and deep red flashes. Sources include television and video games, strobe lighting, sunlight flickering through trees or railings, and malfunctioning fluorescent lights. Diagnosis is usually confirmed by EEG with intermittent photic stimulation, which produces a characteristic photoparoxysmal response.

Notable incidents

The condition gained worldwide attention in December 1997 when an episode of the Pokémon anime, "Dennō Senshi Porygon", featuring rapidly flashing red and blue frames, sent hundreds of Japanese children to hospital with seizures, leading to broadcast standards for flashing imagery. Regulators such as Ofcom in the UK and guidelines in the WCAG web accessibility standard limit flash rates in broadcast and web content, and broadcast material is screened with automated analysers such as the Harding test.

Management

Management involves avoiding known triggers, covering one eye when confronted with a provocative stimulus, keeping distance from screens, and, where seizures persist, antiseizure medication such as valproate or levetiracetam.
