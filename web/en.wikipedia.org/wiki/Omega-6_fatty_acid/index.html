Omega-6 fatty acid

Omega-6 fatty acids
TypePolyunsaturated fatty acids
Defining featureFirst double bond between the 6th and 7th carbons counted from the methyl (omega) end
Principal membersLinoleic acid; arachidonic acid; gamma-linolenic acid
Common sourcesVegetable oils (sunflower, soybean, corn), nuts, seeds, poultry, eggs

Omega-6 fatty acids (also referred to as ω-6 fatty acids or n-6 fatty acids) are a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids that have in common a final carbon–carbon double bond in the n-6 position, that is, the sixth bond, counting from the methyl end.

The biological effects of the omega-6 fatty acids are largely mediated by their conversion to omega-6 eicosanoids, which bind to diverse receptors found in every tissue of the body. The family is contrasted with the omega-3 fatty acids, and the balance between the two in the diet has been the subject of extensive nutritional research.

Biochemistry

Linoleic acid (18:2, n-6), the shortest-chain omega-6 fatty acid, is an essential fatty acid: it cannot be synthesised by the human body and must be obtained from food. In the body, linoleic acid is elongated and desaturated to form gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) and ultimately arachidonic acid (20:4, n-6), a precursor of prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes involved in inflammation and cell signalling.

Dietary sources

Omega-6 fatty acids are abundant in many vegetable oils — including sunflower, safflower, soybean, and corn oil — as well as in nuts, seeds, cereals, and animal products such as poultry and eggs. Modern Western diets tend to be rich in omega-6 fatty acids, in part because of the widespread use of seed oils in processed foods.

Health

Linoleic acid is required to prevent essential fatty acid deficiency, whose symptoms include scaly skin and impaired growth. Beyond adequacy, the health effects of high omega-6 intakes — and of the dietary omega-6 to omega-3 ratio — remain debated. Major reviews conclude that replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat, including omega-6 fats, lowers cardiovascular risk, while hypotheses that linoleic acid is broadly pro-inflammatory in humans have not been consistently supported by feeding trials.
