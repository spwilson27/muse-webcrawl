Paulet affair
DateFebruary 10 – July 31, 1843
LocationHonolulu, Kingdom of Hawaii
ParticipantsLord George Paulet; Kamehameha III; Gerrit P. Judd; Richard Charlton; Rear Admiral Richard Darton Thomas
OutcomeHawaiian independence restored by Admiral Thomas; sovereignty restoration celebrated on July 31

The Paulet affair was a five-month occupation of the Kingdom of Hawaii in 1843 by Lord George Paulet, captain of the British Royal Navy frigate HMS Carysfort. It ended when Rear Admiral Richard Darton Thomas, Paulet's commanding officer, repudiated the seizure and restored the kingdom's independence.

Background

In the early 1840s, disputes arose between the Hawaiian government and Richard Charlton, the British consul in Honolulu, over land claims and the treatment of British subjects. Charlton pressed his grievances in London and told approaching British warships that British residents were being oppressed. Meanwhile, King Kamehameha III had sent envoys to seek international recognition of Hawaiian independence from the United States, Britain, and France.

Provisional cession

Paulet arrived in Honolulu on February 10, 1843, and demanded that the king cede the islands provisionally to Britain, threatening to attack the city. Under protest, on February 25, Kamehameha III signed a provisional cession, declaring that he yielded "to the power of the force" while expecting his rights to be restored once the British government reviewed the matter. Paulet hoisted the Union Jack, dissolved the Hawaiian government except for administrative functions, and imposed British law.

Restoration

News of the occupation reached Admiral Richard Thomas, commander of the Pacific Station, at Valparaíso. Thomas sailed to Hawaii aboard HMS Dublin and, after investigating, concluded that Paulet had acted without authority. On July 31, 1843, in a ceremony at what is now Thomas Square in Honolulu, the Hawaiian flag was raised again and sovereignty formally restored. Kamehameha III delivered the speech containing the famous phrase "Ua mau ke ea o ka ʻāina i ka pono" — "The life of the land is perpetuated in righteousness" — which later became the motto of the State of Hawaii.

Aftermath

July 31 is still celebrated in Hawaii as Lā Hoʻihoʻi Ea (Sovereignty Restoration Day). On November 28, 1843, Britain and France jointly issued the Anglo-Franco Proclamation recognizing the independence of the Hawaiian Kingdom.
