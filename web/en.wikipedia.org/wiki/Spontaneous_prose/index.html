Spontaneous prose

Spontaneous prose is a writing method devised by Jack Kerouac, the American novelist and poet of the Beat Generation. Kerouac had set down the basics and specifics of his method in Essentials of Spontaneous Prose (1953) and in the "Belief & Technique for Modern Prose", a list of thirty axioms published in 1958, including "Something that you feel will find its own form" and "No fear or shame in the dignity of your experience, language & knowledge."

Method

The method rejects rewriting and conventional punctuation in favour of an uninterrupted flow of perception, organised by the dash rather than the period: "the vigorous space dash separating rhetorical breathing". Kerouac compared the technique to jazz improvisation — "blowing" on a subject as a saxophonist blows on a theme — and famously typed On the Road in three weeks of April 1951 on a single scroll of taped-together paper, though he revised his work more than legend suggested.

Kerouac credited the immediate inspiration to the long confessional letters of Neal Cassady, especially the "Joan Anderson letter" of 1950, which he called a breakthrough in American writing. Truman Capote's quip that Kerouac's work was "not writing, but typing" became the standard hostile verdict; Beat writers and later scholars defended the method as a deliberate rhetoric of improvisation rooted in jazz, bop prosody and Buddhist notions of first thought.

Influence

Spontaneous prose influenced Allen Ginsberg's long-line poetry ("first thought, best thought"), the teaching of the Jack Kerouac School of Disembodied Poetics, and generations of writers who adopted stream-of-consciousness drafting, freewriting pedagogy and automatic writing.
