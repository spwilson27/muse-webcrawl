Akhmatova was one of the most significant Russian poets of the 20th century.

Anna Andreyevna Akhmatova (Russian: Анна Андреевна Ахматова; 23 June 1889 – 5 March 1966), born Gorenko, was a Russian poet, one of the most acclaimed writers in the Russian canon. She chose the surname Akhmatova, said to derive from a Tatar ancestor, Khan Akhmat, as her literary pseudonym. Her work ranges from short lyric poems to complex cycles such as Requiem (1935–1940), her memorial to the victims of Stalinist terror.

Life and career

Akhmatova was born near Odessa and studied law in Kyiv. In 1910 she married the poet Nikolay Gumilev and became a central figure of Acmeism, a movement reacting against Symbolist mysticism in favour of clarity and precision. Her collections Evening (1912) and Rosary (1914) brought her fame. Gumilev was executed by the Cheka in 1921 for alleged participation in an anti-Bolshevik conspiracy.

Under Stalin, Akhmatova's work was condemned and banned; she was expelled from the Union of Soviet Writers in the Zhdanov decree of 1946, which denounced her as "half nun, half harlot." Her son, the historian Lev Gumilev, was repeatedly imprisoned in the Gulag. During the Great Terror she stood for months in prison queues in Leningrad, an experience recorded in Requiem, which circulated in secret and was published in the USSR only in 1987.

Later years and legacy

During the siege of Leningrad and the war years, Akhmatova wrote patriotic verse that was briefly permitted publication. Her long, enigmatic Poem Without a Hero occupied her for decades. In 1964 she received the Etna-Taormina poetry prize in Italy, and in 1965 Oxford University awarded her an honorary doctorate. She died in Moscow in 1966. Her flat in the Fountain House in St Petersburg is now a museum. The young Joseph Brodsky was among the poets she mentored in her final years.
