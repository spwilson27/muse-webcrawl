Exile

Exile is the state of being barred from one's native country, typically for political or punitive reasons. A person who lives in exile is called an exile. Exile may be imposed by a government as punishment (external exile or banishment), ordered within a country (internal exile), or self-imposed, where a person flees in anticipation of persecution. In ancient Athens, ostracism allowed the citizenry to exile a prominent person for ten years by popular vote; the Roman law of exilium similarly regulated banishment.

History and forms

Exile has been a central experience of political and literary history. Dante Alighieri was exiled from Florence in 1302 and wrote the Divine Comedy in exile. Victor Hugo spent nearly two decades in the Channel Islands under the Second French Empire. In the twentieth century, authoritarian regimes made systematic use of internal exile — the Soviet Union's banishment of dissidents such as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn and Andrei Sakharov to remote regions — while expulsion across borders produced large diasporas of political refugees. Napoleon was exiled successively to Elba and then to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821.

A government in exile is a political group that claims to be the legitimate government of a country from outside its territory, as many European governments did in London during the Second World War. Exile differs from population transfer and deportation in that it classically targets individuals rather than whole populations, though in practice mass expulsions blur the line; compare the deportation of the Acadians. People who flee rather than await formal exile overlap with those seeking political asylum abroad.

Exile in literature

Exile is a major theme of world literature, from Ovid's Tristia, written from Tomis on the Black Sea, to the works of James Joyce, Thomas Mann, and the writers of the postcolonial diaspora. The Palestinian poet Mahmoud Darwish and the concept of saudade in Portuguese culture express the longing for return characteristic of exilic writing.
