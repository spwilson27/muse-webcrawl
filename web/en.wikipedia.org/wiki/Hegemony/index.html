Hegemony

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hegemony is the political, economic and military predominance of one state over others — in international relations, the position of a hegemon (Athens of the Delian League, Victorian Britain, the post-1945 United States) whose power orders the system. In a second, influential sense derived from Antonio Gramsci's Prison Notebooks (written 1929–35), hegemony is the cultural and ideological dominance by which a ruling class secures the consent of the governed to arrangements serving the rulers' interests — leadership (“direzione”) rather than mere domination, exercised through civil society's institutions: schools, churches, media and common sense.

The two senses meet in hegemonic stability theory (Kindleberger, Gilpin): international orders need a leader willing to supply public goods (open markets, liquidity, security), and decline of the hegemon breeds disorder — a thesis aimed successively at interwar leaderlessness and at American decline debates from the 1970s to the rise of China. Gramscian IR (Robert Cox: “theory is always for someone and for some purpose”) treats such orders as hegemony in the first sense produced by the second: consent organised through institutions and ideas, not only through fleets and GDP.
