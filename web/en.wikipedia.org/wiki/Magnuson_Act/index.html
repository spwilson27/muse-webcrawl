Magnuson Act
Long titleAn Act to repeal the Chinese Exclusion Acts, to establish quotas, and for other purposes

The Magnuson Act, also known as the Chinese Exclusion Repeal Act of 1943, was legislation enacted by the United States Congress on December 17, 1943, which repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the laws that had extended it. Named after its sponsor, Representative (later Senator) Warren G. Magnuson of Washington, the act permitted Chinese nationals to immigrate to the United States under an annual quota of 105 persons, and — for the first time — made Chinese immigrants already residing in the country eligible for naturalization as American citizens.

Background

China and the United States were allies against Imperial Japan during the Second World War, and Japanese propaganda exploited the exclusion laws to argue that the United States did not truly regard China as an equal partner. Chinese-American organizations and supporters such as the Citizens Committee to Repeal Chinese Exclusion lobbied for repeal, and President Franklin D. Roosevelt urged Congress to act as a wartime measure of goodwill toward an ally.

Provisions

While symbolically important, the act's quota was minuscule: 105 Chinese immigrants per year, calculated on the basis of the formula used for other nationalities, and persons of Chinese ancestry were charged to the Chinese quota regardless of their country of birth or citizenship. Racial bars to naturalization remained in place for most other Asian groups until the McCarran–Walter Act of 1952, and national-origins quotas survived until the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965.

Legacy

Despite its limits, the Magnuson Act ended six decades of statutory exclusion aimed at a single nationality and marked the beginning of the dismantling of the exclusion regime, a milestone frequently commemorated in Chinese-American history.
