Celastrus orbiculatus is a woody vine of the family Celastraceae, commonly called Oriental bittersweet or Asian bittersweet. Native to East Asia — China, Japan, and Korea — it was introduced into North America around 1879 as an ornamental plant and has since become a serious invasive species in eastern North America.

Description

The plant is a vigorous deciduous climbing vine that can reach 18 metres or more, twining around trees and structures or sprawling over the ground. Its leaves are alternate, glossy, and rounded (the basis of the epithet orbiculatus), typically 5–10 cm long with finely toothed margins. Small greenish flowers are borne in leaf axils. The species is largely dioecious, with separate male and female plants. In autumn the fruit — a three-lobed yellow capsule — splits open to reveal seeds covered by fleshy red arils, an attractive feature that led to its use in floral arrangements and wreaths.

Invasion in North America

Oriental bittersweet invades forest edges, fields, and roadsides. It shades and girdles trees, adds weight that topples them in storms, and uproots them by leveraging wind. Birds disperse the seeds widely. It hybridises with the native American bittersweet, Celastrus scandens, threatening the native species genetically as well as competitively. Distinguishing the two is a standard diagnostic problem: in C. orbiculatus the flowers and fruit are borne in the leaf axils along the stem, whereas in C. scandens they occur in terminal clusters.

Control

Management combines mechanical removal of vines and roots with cut-stem or foliar herbicide treatment, and repeated monitoring because of the persistent seed bank and root suckering. Several U.S. states prohibit its sale.
