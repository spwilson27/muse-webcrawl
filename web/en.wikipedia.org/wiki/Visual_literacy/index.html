Visual literacy

Visual literacy is the ability to interpret, negotiate, and make meaning from information presented in the form of an image, extending the meaning of literacy, which commonly signifies interpretation of a written or printed text. Visual literacy is based on the idea that pictures can be "read" and that meaning can be communicated through a process of reading.

Origins of the term

The term was coined in 1969 by John Debes, co-founder of the International Visual Literacy Association (IVLA), who defined it as a group of vision-competencies a human being can develop by seeing and at the same time having and integrating other sensory experiences. The field drew on earlier work in art education, semiotics, perceptual psychology and media studies, including the writings of Rudolf Arnheim on visual thinking and of Marshall McLuhan on media.

Scope

Visual literacy encompasses the interpretation of photographs, paintings, diagrams, maps, signs, icons, films, advertisements and digital interfaces. It involves recognising visual conventions (such as perspective, framing, cropping, and colour symbolism), understanding how images may persuade or mislead, and producing visual messages oneself. In contemporary education, professional bodies such as the Association of College & Research Libraries have published competency standards for visual literacy in higher education, and it is frequently grouped with media literacy and digital literacy as an essential 21st-century skill.

Debates

Scholars debate whether images constitute a genuine "language" with grammar and syntax, or whether "literacy" functions only as a metaphor when applied to pictures. Critics have also questioned the proliferation of "literacies" in educational discourse, while defenders argue that in a media environment dominated by screens and images, the capacity to read and create visuals deserves explicit teaching.
