Bikkurim (Hebrew: ביכורים, "first fruits") were the first-fruit offerings brought by Israelite farmers to the Temple in Jerusalem in ancient Israel. The mitzvah (commandment) is derived from the Torah: "The choicest of the first fruits of your soil you shall bring to the house of the Lord your God" (Exodus 23:19), and the full ceremony is described in Deuteronomy 26:1–11.

The ceremony

Only the Seven Species for which the Land of Israel was praised — wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and dates — were eligible for the bikkurim offering. When the first fruit of one of these species ripened, the farmer tied a reed around it and declared it bikkurim. The fruits were gathered in baskets — the wealthy used baskets of gold and silver, the poor used woven willow baskets — and carried in procession to Jerusalem, accompanied by music and an ox whose horns were overlaid with gold.

On arrival, the farmer presented the basket to a priest (kohen) and recited the declaration known as Mikra Bikkurim, beginning "My father was a wandering Aramean" (Deuteronomy 26:5–10), a brief recitation of Israelite history from the patriarchs through the Exodus to the gift of the Land.

Tractate Bikkurim

The Mishnah contains a tractate named Bikkurim in the order of Zeraim, consisting of three chapters (four in the Tosefta tradition), which details the laws of the offering: who must bring it, when and where it may be brought, and the rules for the recitation. The tractate also contains famous passages about categories of people who bring but do not recite, and about the convert who both brings and recites.

Modern observance

Since the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the offering cannot be performed. In modern Israel, kibbutzim and agricultural settlements revived a ceremonial first-fruits presentation as part of Shavuot celebrations, and the term bikkurim is used metaphorically in Hebrew for a debut work, such as an author's first book.
