Datura is a genus of nine species of highly poisonous, vespertine-flowering plants belonging to the nightshade family (Solanaceae). They are commonly known as thornapples or jimsonweeds, but also known as devil's trumpets or mad apple. Other English common names include moonflower, devil's weed, and hell's bells. All species of Datura are extremely poisonous and psychoactive, especially their seeds and flowers, which can cause respiratory depression, arrhythmias, fever, delirium, hallucinations, anticholinergic syndrome, psychosis, and death if taken internally.

The name Datura originates from the Hindi and Urdu word dhatūra, for the plant's use in India. Its species were traditionally known throughout the world as powerful deliriants, used in religious and magical rituals in both the Old and New Worlds. The taxonomy of the genus has been difficult: many species once placed in Datura — especially the woody, pendulous-flowered "angel's trumpets" — are now assigned to the related genus Brugmansia.

Description

Datura species are herbaceous, leafy annuals and short-lived perennials, which can reach up to 2 metres in height. The leaves are alternate, 10–20 cm long and 5–18 cm broad, with a lobed or toothed margin. The flowers are erect or spreading, trumpet-shaped, 5–20 cm long and 4–12 cm broad at the mouth; colours vary from white to yellow, pink, and pale purple. The fruit is a spiny capsule, 4–10 cm long and 2–6 cm broad, splitting open when ripe to release the numerous seeds. The seeds disperse freely over pastures, fields and even wasteland locations.

Species

Nine species are generally accepted:

  • Datura discolor Bernh. – desert thorn-apple
  • Datura ferox L. – long-spined thorn-apple
  • Datura innoxia Mill. – sacred datura (New World)
  • Datura leichhardtii F.Muell. ex Benth.
  • Datura metel L. – devil's trumpet
  • Datura quercifolia Kunth – oak-leaf thorn-apple
  • Datura reburra Barclay
  • Datura stramonium L. – jimsonweed
  • Datura wrightii Regel – sacred datura

Toxicity

All parts of Datura plants contain dangerous levels of the tropane alkaloids atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine, which are classified as deliriants and anticholinergics. The risk of fatal overdose is high among uninformed users, and many hospital admissions have been reported for unintentional poisoning. The concentration of toxins varies greatly between plants and even between parts of the same plant, making the potency of any given plant unpredictable and its recreational or ritual use especially hazardous.

Cultivation and uses

Species such as Datura metel and D. innoxia are grown as ornamentals for their large, fragrant, trumpet-shaped flowers, which open in the evening. In traditional medicine systems, particularly Ayurveda, carefully prepared datura has been used in small doses, and the alkaloids derived from the plant family remain important pharmaceuticals. Datura stramonium, widely naturalized, is a common weed of disturbed ground and livestock pastures in temperate regions worldwide.

History and folklore

The Old World species Datura metel was described in ancient Sanskrit texts and associated with the god Shiva. In the Americas, Datura species were used ritually by numerous Indigenous peoples, including in puberty rites and divination. The common name "jimsonweed" derives from "Jamestown weed", after an incident in 1676 in Jamestown, Virginia, in which soldiers who ate the plant in a salad suffered prolonged delirium. The plant also features prominently in the works of Carlos Castaneda and in various accounts of witchcraft and shamanic practice.
