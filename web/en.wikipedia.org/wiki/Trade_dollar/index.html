Trade dollar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trade dollar is a silver coin minted by a country to facilitate trade with foreign countries, usually to a weight standardised against the dominant coin of a trading region. Trade dollars were struck so that merchants could pay for goods in Asia, especially China, where silver was preferred, without relying on foreign coinage. Famous examples include the Spanish dollar (piece of eight), the Maria Theresa thaler, the United States trade dollar, and the British trade dollar.

United States trade dollar

The United States trade dollar was a silver dollar coin issued by the United States Mint from 1873 to 1885. It weighed 27.22 grams (420 grains) and was 0.900 fine, containing slightly more silver than the standard Seated Liberty dollar, so that it could compete with the Mexican peso in the China trade. Designed by Chief Engraver William Barber, the obverse showed a seated figure of Liberty holding an olive branch over the sea, and the reverse an eagle with the weight and fineness stated.

Although intended purely for export, the Coinage Act of 1873 made the trade dollar legal tender in the United States up to five dollars. When the price of silver fell, large numbers of the coins were dumped into domestic circulation at a discount, angering workers paid in them. Congress revoked the coin's legal-tender status in 1876 and limited mintage to export orders in 1878; proof pieces were struck until 1885. Unredeemed trade dollars were finally redeemed by the government in 1887. Many surviving pieces bear "chop marks" — small counterstamps applied by Chinese merchants and bankers who tested and guaranteed the coin's silver.

British trade dollar

The British trade dollar, minted from 1895 to 1935, was struck for use in the Far East, in Hong Kong, the Straits Settlements and Malaya, to compete with the Mexican dollar. It depicted Britannia standing with trident and shield on the obverse, with a reverse design incorporating Chinese and Malay inscriptions. The coins were struck chiefly at the Bombay and Calcutta mints.

Other trade dollars

The French Indochinese piastre de commerce, the Japanese trade dollar of 1875–1877 (a competitor struck for the treaty ports), and the earlier Maria Theresa thaler — restruck with the date 1780 for trade in Africa and the Middle East well into the twentieth century — all belong to the same tradition of standardised silver for foreign trade.
