Implicit Association Test

Implicit Association Test (IAT)
Introduced1998, by Greenwald, McGhee and Schwartz
FieldSocial psychology
MeasuresStrength of automatic associations between concepts
OutputD-score based on response latencies

The Implicit Association Test (IAT) is a computer-based measure within social psychology intended to detect the strength of a person's automatic associations between mental representations of objects (concepts) in memory. It was introduced in 1998 by Anthony Greenwald, Debbie McGhee and Jordan Schwartz, and popularised through the Project Implicit website, founded with Mahzarin Banaji and Brian Nosek.

Procedure

Respondents rapidly categorise stimuli — words or images — using two response keys. In critical blocks, two concepts share a key (for example, "flowers" and "pleasant" on one key, "insects" and "unpleasant" on the other); in reversed blocks the pairings are switched. People are generally faster when strongly associated concepts share a key. The difference in average response latencies is converted into a D-score.

Applications

The best-known versions measure implicit attitudes about race, gender, age, sexuality and other social groups, and millions of tests have been taken online. IAT scores have been used in research on discrimination, stereotyping and consumer and health behaviour, and in diversity training.

Criticism

The test has been widely debated. Critics note modest test–retest reliability, weak and inconsistent prediction of individual behaviour, and uncertainty about whether it measures personal prejudice or familiarity with cultural associations. Meta-analyses disagree on its predictive validity; defenders argue it captures meaningful population-level patterns. The IAT is distinct from broader self-report questionnaires about implicit bias, though the two are often discussed together.
