Urban growth boundary

Urban growth boundary
TypeLand-use planning / zoning regulation
PurposeContain urban sprawl, protect farmland and open space
Notable examplesPortland, Oregon; London Green Belt; Seoul Greenbelt

An urban growth boundary (UGB) is a regional planning boundary intended to control urban sprawl by mandating that the area inside the boundary be used for urban development, and the area outside be preserved in rural uses such as farming, forestry or open space. Development outside the boundary is generally prohibited or severely limited, and public services such as sewers are not extended there.

Examples

The best-known example is Portland, Oregon, where Oregon state law since 1973 has required each city to adopt a growth boundary; the Portland metropolitan boundary is managed by the Metro regional government and must contain a 20-year supply of developable land, being periodically expanded. Related instruments include the Green Belt around London and other British cities, established from the 1930s–1950s, Seoul's development restriction zones, and urban containment policies in Canada, Australia and New Zealand.

Effects and debate

Supporters credit growth boundaries with protecting agricultural land and ecosystems, encouraging infill and higher-density development, reducing infrastructure costs and supporting public transport. Critics argue that constraining land supply raises housing prices inside the boundary and can push "leapfrog" development to unregulated jurisdictions beyond it. Empirical studies of Portland find measurable effects on density and land preservation, while the magnitude of its effect on housing prices remains contested.
