Hybrid speciation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hybrid speciation is a form of speciation in which hybridization between two different species leads to a new species, reproductively isolated from both parents. It contrasts with speciation by geographic isolation or gradual divergence, because the new lineage arises from genomes combined from two distinct species.

Mechanisms

Two principal mechanisms are recognized. In allopolyploid speciation, common in plants, chromosome doubling in a hybrid restores fertility and instantly creates reproductive isolation from both parents, since backcrosses produce offspring with mismatched chromosome numbers. Classic examples include bread wheat, cultivated tobacco, and the cordgrass Spartina anglica, which arose in the 19th century.

In homoploid hybrid speciation, the hybrid lineage has the same chromosome number as its parents. Isolation must therefore evolve through other means: novel combinations of parental traits may fit a new ecological niche, or chromosomal rearrangements and assortative mating may separate the hybrid from its parents. Verified examples are relatively rare but include the butterfly Heliconius heurippa, the Italian sparrow, and the sunflowers Helianthus anomalus, H. deserticola, and H. paradoxus, the last of which has been experimentally resynthesized in the laboratory.

Significance

Hybrid speciation shows that hybridization — once viewed only as a force that erases species differences — can also generate new biodiversity, and that evolutionary histories may be reticulate (net-like) rather than purely tree-like. Advances in genomics have revealed hybrid ancestry in many lineages, including modern humans, who carry DNA from Neanderthals and Denisovans, although such introgression does not by itself constitute hybrid speciation.
