Mirror

A mirror, also known as a looking glass, is an object that reflects an image. Light striking the mirror is reflected so that, for many practical purposes, the eye perceives a virtual image of objects in front of it. The most familiar type, the plane mirror, produces an image that is the same size as the object, laterally inverted, and appears as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. Curved mirrors can magnify, diminish, or focus light and are used in telescopes, headlamps, and optical instruments.

History

The earliest mirrors were pieces of polished obsidian found in Anatolia dating to around 6000 BCE. Polished copper and bronze mirrors were made in Egypt and Mesopotamia by 3000 BCE, and metal mirrors spread through the Greek, Etruscan, and Roman worlds; Chinese craftsmen produced bronze mirrors from about 2000 BCE. Glass mirrors backed with metal foil appeared in Roman times, and in 1835 Justus von Liebig invented the silvered-glass process — depositing a thin layer of metallic silver on glass — that underlies most modern mirrors.

Manufacture

Modern mirrors are made by coating glass with aluminium or silver by vacuum deposition or chemical reduction, protected by paint or copper layers. "First-surface" mirrors, coated on the front face, avoid ghost images and are used in precision optics; dielectric mirrors use stacked thin films for high reflectivity at selected wavelengths.

Uses and symbolism

Beyond grooming and decoration, mirrors serve in scientific instruments (reflecting telescopes, microscopes, lasers), vehicles, signalling, solar power concentration, and periscopes. Two-way mirrors are used for observation and optical illusions. In culture and psychology, mirrors symbolise self-knowledge and vanity; developmental psychology uses the mirror test to study self-recognition in children and animals, and Jacques Lacan placed the "mirror stage" at the centre of his theory of the ego.
