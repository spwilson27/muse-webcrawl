Mongol invasion of Europe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Mongol horse archers on campaign, from a Persian manuscript]

The Mongol invasion of Europe (1236–1242) was the Mongol Empire's great western campaign, launched under Batu Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, with the veteran general Subutai as its strategist. Mongol armies subjugated the steppe peoples and the Rus' principalities (1237–1240, including the destruction of Kiev), then in 1241 shattered Polish and German forces at Legnica and annihilated the Hungarian army at Mohi, reaching the Adriatic and the gates of Vienna. The campaign ended abruptly in 1242 when the armies withdrew east — traditionally explained by the death of the Great Khan Ögedei and the succession — leaving Hungary devastated and eastern Europe permanently reshaped.

The campaign

After securing the Volga Bulgars and Cumans, the Mongols took Ryazan (1237), Vladimir (1238) and, after a siege, Kiev (December 1240). In spring 1241 three columns entered Poland and Hungary in coordinated fashion: at Legnica (9 April) a Polish-German army with Templar and Hospitaller contingents was destroyed; two days later at Mohi (11 April) King Béla IV's host was annihilated in a night river-crossing and feigned retreat. Mongol detachments raided toward Vienna and the Adriatic coast in pursuit of Béla.

Withdrawal and aftermath

In spring 1242 the princes withdrew through the Balkans to the steppe. Ögedei's death (December 1241) and the need to elect a successor is the classic explanation; historians also cite pasture limits, Hungarian castle resistance (stone citadels had held), weather, and strategic priorities. Hungary lost perhaps a quarter to a half of its population and rebuilt with stone castles; the Rus' lands remained under the Golden Horde's suzerainty ('the Mongol yoke') for two centuries, while western Europe, terrified but spared, received the friar Carpini's report from the khan's court (1245–47).
