Rank-size distribution

A rank-size distribution describes a set of observations by ranking them from largest to smallest and examining how size declines with rank. It is the counterpart of a frequency distribution: instead of asking how many items fall in each size class, one asks how big the largest, second-largest, third-largest, and so on, items are. Rank-size distributions are widely used in the study of city sizes, word frequencies, incomes, firm sizes and species abundances.

Zipf's law and the rank-size rule

The most famous rank-size pattern is the rank-size rule associated with Zipf's law: the size of the r-th largest item is approximately proportional to 1/r. For cities this predicts that the second-largest city in a country has about half the population of the largest, the third about a third, and so on:

Pr ≈ P1 / rq, with q ≈ 1.

On a log–log plot of size against rank, a Zipf distribution appears as a straight line of slope −1. The pattern was noted for cities by Felix Auerbach (1913) and popularised by George Kingsley Zipf (1949); it is a special case of a power-law (Pareto) distribution.

Examples

  • Cities: national urban systems in many large countries approximate the rule, though primate-city systems (e.g. where one capital dominates) and very small countries deviate strongly.
  • Words: in natural-language corpora, the frequency of the r-th most common word falls roughly as 1/r — the original Zipf observation.
  • Other phenomena: firm sizes, website visits, earthquake energies and income tails show related rank-size regularities connected to Pareto distributions.

Explanations

Proportional random growth processes (Gibrat's law) with a lower bound can generate Zipf-like city-size distributions, as shown in models by Xavier Gabaix and others. Deviations from the rule — primacy, or a flatter or steeper slope — are used by urban geographers and economists to compare urban systems and their histories.
