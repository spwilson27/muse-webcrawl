Russian America

Russian America (Russian: Русская Америка) was the name for the Russian Empire's colonial possessions in North America, centred on present-day Alaska, from the mid-18th century until 1867, when the territory was sold to the United States in the Alaska Purchase.

Exploration and the fur trade

The 1741 expedition of Vitus Bering and Alexei Chirikov opened Alaska's coasts to Russian promyshlenniki (fur traders), who hunted sea otters and fur seals across the Aleutian Islands, often brutally exploiting and decimating the Aleut and other Alaska Native peoples. Grigory Shelikhov founded the first permanent settlement at Three Saints Bay on Kodiak Island in 1784.

The Russian-American Company

In 1799 Tsar Paul I chartered the Russian-American Company with a monopoly over the trade. Under chief manager Alexander Baranov the capital was moved to New Archangel (Sitka), a fortified port with shipyards, schools, and an Orthodox cathedral. Russian posts extended as far south as Fort Ross in California (1812–1841), established to supply the Alaskan settlements with food. The Russian population never exceeded a few hundred, alongside Creole (mixed Russian–Native) communities and missionaries such as St. Innocent (Ivan Veniaminov), who devised an alphabet for Aleut.

Sale to the United States

Defeat in the Crimean War, the cost of defending a distant colony against Britain, and declining fur returns persuaded Tsar Alexander II to sell. By the treaty signed on 30 March 1867 and the transfer ceremony at Sitka on 18 October 1867, the United States acquired the territory for $7.2 million. Russian heritage survives in Alaska in Orthodox parishes, place names such as Baranof Island, and the Sitka historic sites.
