Deformation (engineering)

In engineering, deformation is the change in the size or shape of an object due to an applied force (deformation energy), or a change in temperature (thermal expansion). In the first case, the deformation may be caused by tension, compression, shear, bending, or torsion. Deformation is quantified locally by the strain, the relative displacement of material points, and is related to the applied stress by the material's constitutive behaviour.

Types of deformation

Elastic deformation

Elastic deformation is reversible: when the load is removed, the object returns to its original shape. For most metals and crystalline materials under small loads, elastic deformation is proportional to stress, as described by Hooke's law, with the proportionality constant given by elastic moduli such as Young's modulus. Atomic bonds are stretched but not broken.

Plastic deformation

Plastic deformation is permanent deformation that remains after the load is removed. It begins when the stress exceeds the material's yield strength and proceeds microscopically by the motion of dislocations, twinning, or sliding of grains and molecular chains. Plastic deformation is the basis of metal-forming processes such as forging, rolling, and extrusion, but in service it can lead to failure.

Other forms

Further forms include creep, the slow time-dependent deformation under sustained load, especially at high temperature; viscoelastic deformation, combining viscous flow and elasticity, typical of polymers; and brittle fracture, in which an object fails with little prior deformation. Thermal deformation results from expansion or contraction with temperature change.

Stress–strain behaviour

The deformation of an engineering material is characterised by its stress–strain curve, obtained from a tensile test. The curve records the elastic region, yielding, strain hardening, ultimate tensile strength, necking, and fracture, and is fundamental to the design of structures and machine components.
