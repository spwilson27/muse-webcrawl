Frontal lobe

The frontal lobe (highlighted) is the largest lobe of the cerebral cortex.

The frontal lobe is the largest of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in mammals, located at the front of each cerebral hemisphere, separated from the parietal lobe by the central sulcus and from the temporal lobe by the lateral sulcus. It contains most of the brain's dopaminergic pathways and is central to voluntary movement, language, reasoning, planning and personality.

Anatomy and function

Key regions include the primary motor cortex in the precentral gyrus, which controls voluntary movement; the premotor and supplementary motor areas, which plan movement; Broca's area in the dominant hemisphere, important for speech production; and the prefrontal cortex, which supports executive functions such as decision-making, working memory, impulse control and social behaviour. The prefrontal cortex is the last brain region to mature, continuing development into a person's mid-twenties.

Damage and clinical significance

Damage to the frontal lobe can cause paralysis, expressive aphasia, and striking changes in personality and social conduct. The most famous case is Phineas Gage, an American railroad worker who in 1848 survived an iron rod being driven through his frontal lobe and reportedly became impulsive and unreliable, providing early evidence that the frontal lobes govern personality. Frontal lobe disorders are implicated in schizophrenia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and frontotemporal dementia.
