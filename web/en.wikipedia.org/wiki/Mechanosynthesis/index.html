Mechanosynthesis

Mechanosynthesis is a term for hypothetical chemical syntheses in which reaction outcomes are determined by the use of mechanical constraints to direct reactant molecules to specific sites. In its modern usage it refers to molecular manufacturing: the proposed fabrication of complex structures by mechanically positioning individual atoms and molecules with atomic precision, typically envisioned using tools resembling scanning probe microscopes.

A probe tip mechanically placing atoms — the basic operation envisioned for mechanosynthesis.

Concept

The idea traces to Richard Feynman's 1959 lecture "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom", and was developed in detail by K. Eric Drexler in Engines of Creation (1986) and Nanosystems (1992), where he proposed molecular assemblers that build diamondoid structures by depositing carbon atoms or dimers at programmed positions. Controlled single-atom manipulation was demonstrated experimentally in 1989, when IBM scientists used a scanning tunnelling microscope to spell "IBM" with 35 xenon atoms on a nickel surface.

Research

Experimental mechanosynthesis remains limited: ultra-high-vacuum scanning probe techniques can move atoms and induce specific reactions on surfaces, and computational studies (notably by Robert Freitas and Ralph Merkle) have modelled tooltip chemistries for diamond mechanosynthesis, including hydrogen abstraction and carbon dimer placement. Progress is constrained by tip wear, error rates, thermal noise, and the difficulty of operating outside vacuum.

Criticism

The feasibility of Drexler-style assemblers was famously debated with Nobel laureate Richard Smalley in 2001–2003 (the "fat fingers"/"sticky fingers" objections): Smalley argued that the required manipulator arms could not fit into the reaction space and that reactive tooltips would stick uncontrollably to the workpiece. Drexler countered that his designs avoided both problems. Mainstream nanotechnology has since focused on chemistry-based and biological approaches, though mechanosynthesis research continues in surface science.
