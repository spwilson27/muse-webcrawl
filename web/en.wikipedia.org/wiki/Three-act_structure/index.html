Three-act structure

The three-act structure is a model used in narrative fiction that divides a story into three parts, often called the Setup, the Confrontation, and the Resolution. The model, popularized by Syd Field in his 1979 book Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting, is widely used in screenwriting and other forms of storytelling. It draws on ideas traceable to Aristotle's Poetics, which observed that a whole story has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and to the structure of classical drama as described by Gustav Freytag.

The three acts

Act I — the Setup (roughly the first quarter of a screenplay) establishes the main characters, their relationships, and the world they live in. An inciting incident disrupts the protagonist's life, and the act ends with the first plot point, which commits the protagonist to the central conflict. Act II — the Confrontation (about half the story) follows the protagonist's attempts to resolve the problem, meeting escalating obstacles and reversals; a midpoint typically raises the stakes or reverses the direction of the story, and the act ends at a low point or crisis. Act III — the Resolution (the final quarter) builds to the climax, in which the central question of the story is answered, followed by a brief denouement tying up remaining threads.

Criticism

The model is highly influential in Hollywood development practice, but it has been criticized as formulaic and prescriptive. Screenwriters and critics have noted that many successful films and novels do not conform to Field's page-count proportions, and alternatives—such as the hero's journey, five-act structure, sequence approaches, and non-linear structures—are widely used. Defenders reply that the model is a descriptive paradigm rather than a formula, reflecting a near-universal pattern of beginning, middle and end.
