Lynching
Definition: Extrajudicial killing by a mob
Etymology: Possibly from Charles Lynch, Virginia, 18th century

Lynching is an extrajudicial killing by a group, most often carried out by hanging. It is used by mobs to punish an alleged transgressor, an already convicted transgressor, or to intimidate a community. Lynching has occurred in many societies, but the term is most strongly associated with the racial terror lynchings of African Americans in the United States, principally in the South, from the late 19th century through the mid-20th century.

United States

The Equal Justice Initiative has documented more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings of African Americans between 1877 and 1950. Victims were often accused of minor offences or of violating racial etiquette; many lynchings were public spectacles attended by large crowds, and photographs were sold as postcards. Lynchings enforced white supremacy during the Jim Crow era, terrorizing Black communities and suppressing economic and political advancement.

Anti-lynching efforts

Activists such as Ida B. Wells documented lynchings and campaigned against them from the 1890s. The NAACP pushed for federal anti-lynching legislation; the Dyer Anti-Lynching Bill passed the House in 1922 but was blocked in the Senate. Although more than 200 anti-lynching bills were introduced in Congress, none passed until the Emmett Till Antilynching Act of 2022 made lynching a federal hate crime. The National Memorial for Peace and Justice opened in Montgomery, Alabama, in 2018 to commemorate victims.

Etymology

The word derives from "Lynch law", associated with Charles Lynch (1736–1796), a Virginia justice of the peace who ordered extralegal punishment of Loyalists during the American Revolutionary War.
