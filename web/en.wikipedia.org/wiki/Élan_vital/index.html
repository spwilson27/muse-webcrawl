Élan vital (French: [elɑ̃ vital]) is a term coined by the French philosopher Henri Bergson in his 1907 book Creative Evolution (L'Évolution créatrice). Usually translated as "vital impetus" or "vital force", it denotes the creative impulse Bergson believed drives the evolution of life, propelling organisms toward ever greater complexity and diversity in a way that neither mechanistic nor finalistic explanations could fully capture.

The concept

Bergson argued that evolution proceeds like a bursting shell: the single impetus of life divides into divergent lines — plants and animals, instinct and intelligence — as it encounters the resistance of inert matter. The élan vital is not a plan or purpose but an original push, continuous with Bergson's wider philosophy of duration (durée), the lived, indivisible flow of time. Intelligence, he claimed, fabricates concepts suited to matter, whereas intuition can grasp the vital impetus from within.

Reception and criticism

Creative Evolution made Bergson internationally famous and helped him win the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1927. Biologists, however, generally rejected élan vital as an untestable, quasi-mystical substitute for explanation; Julian Huxley compared it to explaining a locomotive by an "élan locomotif". The concept is usually classified with vitalism, though Bergson resisted the label. It later influenced process philosophy, Gilles Deleuze, and writers such as George Bernard Shaw, whose play Back to Methuselah dramatised creative evolution.
