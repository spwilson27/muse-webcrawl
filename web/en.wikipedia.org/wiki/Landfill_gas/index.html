Landfill gas

Landfill gas
Composition~50% methane, ~50% CO₂, trace gases
SourceAnaerobic decomposition of organic waste
UsesElectricity generation, heating, upgraded biomethane
HazardsExplosion, asphyxiation, greenhouse-gas emissions

Landfill gas is a mixture of gases produced when microorganisms decompose organic waste inside a landfill under anaerobic conditions. It consists of roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide, with small amounts of nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen sulfide, and non-methane organic compounds.

Production

Gas generation proceeds through phases: initial aerobic decomposition gives way to acidogenesis and then methanogenesis, in which methanogenic archaea convert organic acids to methane. A landfill reaches peak gas production within a few years of waste placement and can generate gas for 20–50 years. One tonne of municipal solid waste typically yields on the order of 100–200 cubic metres of landfill gas over its lifetime.

Collection and use

Regulations in many countries require larger landfills to collect the gas with systems of vertical wells and horizontal collectors under vacuum. Collected gas may be flared or used productively: burned in engines or turbines for electricity, used directly for industrial heat, or upgraded to pipeline-quality biomethane (renewable natural gas) by removing CO₂ and contaminants. The U.S. EPA's Landfill Methane Outreach Program (LMOP) promotes such projects.

Environmental concerns

Uncollected methane is a potent greenhouse gas — landfills are among the largest anthropogenic methane sources — and escaping gas can migrate through soil into buildings, where it poses explosion and asphyxiation risks. Odorous trace compounds and leachate generation are also managed as part of landfill environmental controls.
