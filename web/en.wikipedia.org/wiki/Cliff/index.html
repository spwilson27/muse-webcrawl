Cliff

In geography and geology, a cliff is an area of rock which has a general angle defined by the vertical, or nearly vertical, rock exposure. Cliffs are formed by the processes of weathering and erosion, and are common on coasts, in mountainous areas, along escarpments, and beside rivers.

[Image: Sea cliffs with near-vertical rock faces rising from the ocean]

Formation

Cliffs form where resistant rock is undermined or worn away faster than it can slump. On coasts, wave action cuts a notch at the base of the rock face; the overhanging rock eventually collapses, and the cliff retreats, leaving a wave-cut platform. Inland cliffs may arise from faulting, glacial erosion, river incision, or differential erosion of hard and soft strata. Sedimentary rocks such as sandstone, limestone, chalk, and dolomite commonly form cliffs, as do igneous rocks such as granite and basalt.

Tallest cliffs

The highest cliff on Earth is often considered to be Mount Thor on Baffin Island, Canada, whose west face rises about 1,370 metres (4,500 ft) with a sheer drop of roughly 1,250 metres. The sea cliffs of Kalaupapa, Hawaii, rise over 1,000 metres from the Pacific. In the solar system, Verona Rupes on Uranus's moon Miranda may be the tallest known cliff, estimated at up to 20 kilometres high.

Ecology

Cliff faces provide nesting habitat for seabirds and raptors, and shelter specialised plants in crevices beyond the reach of grazing animals. Because cliffs are inaccessible, they often act as refuges for rare species displaced from surrounding lowlands.

Human use

Cliffs have long served as defensive sites for forts and settlements. Today they are used for rock climbing, BASE jumping, and coasteering, and cliff erosion poses a recognised hazard to coastal property and infrastructure.
