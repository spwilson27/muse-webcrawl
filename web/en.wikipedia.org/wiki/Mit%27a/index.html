Mit'a

TypeLabour tax / corvée
RegionAndes, South America
Used byInca Empire; Spanish colonial administration

Mit'a (Quechua for "turn" or "season") was a system of mandatory public service labour in the Inca Empire, in which communities contributed work in turns as a form of taxation. Because the Inca state had no currency, labour was its principal tax, and mit'a levies built roads, terraces, fortresses, temples and other public works, and staffed state farms and mines.

Inca system

Under the Inca, able-bodied men from each community (ayllu) were drafted for rotating periods of service. In principle the obligation was reciprocal: the state fed, clothed and housed the workers and held feasts, and labour obligations were tied to the state's duty to provide for its subjects in times of need.

Colonial mita

After the Spanish conquest, Viceroy Francisco de Toledo reorganised the system in the 1570s into the colonial mita, which became infamous for its use in the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica. Indigenous communities were required to send a proportion of their adult male population — in the Potosí catchment, roughly one in seven men each year — to labour far from home under harsh and often lethal conditions. The colonial mita became a symbol of exploitation in the Andes and was a grievance behind major rebellions, including that of Túpac Amaru II.
