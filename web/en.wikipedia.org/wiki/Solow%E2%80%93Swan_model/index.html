Solow–Swan model

The Solow–Swan model, or exogenous growth model, is an economic model of long-run economic growth developed independently by Robert Solow and Trevor Swan in 1956. It explains growth through capital accumulation, labor (population) growth, and exogenous technological progress, and became a foundational framework in macroeconomics and growth theory.

Developed1956
AuthorsRobert Solow; Trevor Swan
TypeNeoclassical growth model
Key conceptsSteady state, capital accumulation, diminishing returns

Structure

The model uses a Cobb–Douglas production function with capital and effective labor. Because of diminishing returns to capital, economies converge to a steady state in which output per worker grows only at the rate of technological progress. The model predicts conditional convergence: poorer economies with similar fundamentals should grow faster than richer ones. Solow's 1957 empirical work attributed most U.S. growth to technological progress rather than factor accumulation.
