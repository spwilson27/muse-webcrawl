The gastrointestinal tract (GI tract, digestive tract, or alimentary canal) is the continuous tube of organs through which food passes in animals, where it is digested and absorbed and waste is expelled. In humans it runs from the mouth through the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine to the anus, assisted by accessory glands — the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas.

Structure

The wall of the tract has four layers throughout most of its length: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa (responsible for peristalsis), and serosa or adventitia. The upper GI tract comprises the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, and duodenum; the lower tract comprises most of the small intestine and the large intestine. The small intestine — duodenum, jejunum, and ileum — is the principal site of nutrient absorption, its surface enormously enlarged by folds, villi, and microvilli.

Function

Digestion combines mechanical processes (chewing, churning, segmentation) with chemical breakdown by enzymes and gastric acid. The enteric nervous system, an extensive network of neurons in the gut wall, coordinates motility and secretion and communicates with the central nervous system via the gut–brain axis. The tract also hosts the gut microbiota and a major part of the immune system.

Clinical significance

Common disorders include gastroesophageal reflux, peptic ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, and gastroenteritis. Endoscopy allows direct visualization and biopsy of much of the tract.
