Major chord

In music theory, a major chord is a chord whose root note is a major third above the tonic construction — that is, a chord consisting of a root, a major third, and a perfect fifth. A major triad contains three notes: the root, the note a major third (four semitones) above it, and the note a perfect fifth (seven semitones) above the root. For example, the C major triad consists of the notes C, E, and G.

Structure and notation

Major chords are typically notated with the root note alone (e.g., "C") or with a capital "M" or "maj" (e.g., Cmaj). In figured notation and jazz symbols, extensions build on the major triad to form major seventh, major ninth, and related chords. The major triad's intervals approximate the 4:5:6 frequency ratio of the harmonic series, contributing to its consonant, stable sound.

Character

Major chords are often described in Western musical culture as sounding bright, happy, or resolved, in contrast to minor chords, whose third is lowered by a semitone. The major tonic chord (I) is the foundational sonority of major-key tonal music, and the major scale is built around its structure.
