The M1 Garand is a semi-automatic rifle chambered for the .30-06 Springfield cartridge that was the standard service rifle of the United States during World War II and the Korean War. Designed by Canadian-American engineer John C. Garand at Springfield Armory, it was the first standard-issue autoloading rifle fielded by any major power.

History

The rifle was officially adopted in 1936 and reached troops in quantity by the early 1940s. General George S. Patton famously described it as "the greatest battle implement ever devised." Its semi-automatic action gave American infantry a significant rate-of-fire advantage over opponents armed with bolt-action rifles.

More than five million were produced. The M1 remained in front-line U.S. service until its replacement by the M14 rifle in 1959, and it continued in reserve, training, and ceremonial roles for decades afterwards. Large numbers were supplied to allied nations under military aid programmes.

Design

The M1 is loaded with a distinctive eight-round en bloc clip inserted into a fixed internal magazine. When the last round is fired, the empty clip is ejected with a characteristic metallic "ping" and the bolt locks open. The rifle uses a long-stroke gas piston system and a rotating bolt derived from Garand's earlier designs.

Legacy

The Garand's action directly influenced the M14 and the Mini-14. Surplus rifles distributed through the Civilian Marksmanship Program remain highly prized by collectors and competitive shooters.
