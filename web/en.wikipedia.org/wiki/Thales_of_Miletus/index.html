Thales of Miletus

Thales of Miletus (Ancient Greek: Θαλῆς; c. 626/623 – c. 548/545 BCE) was an Ancient Greek pre-Socratic philosopher from Miletus in Ionia, Asia Minor. Thales was one of the Seven Sages, founding figures of Ancient Greek philosophy. Many regard him as the first philosopher in the Greek tradition, breaking from the prior use of mythology to explain the world and instead using natural philosophy. He is thus otherwise referred to as the first to engage in mathematics, science, and deductive reasoning.

Thales' most famous philosophical position was his cosmological thesis, which held that the archē (principle or origin) of all things is water. Neither Thales' writings nor contemporary accounts survive, and knowledge of him derives from later authors, above all Aristotle, who reported that Thales held that the earth rests on water and that all things are full of gods, and Herodotus and Diogenes Laërtius, who preserve many anecdotes.

Astronomy and mathematics

Herodotus reports that Thales predicted the solar eclipse of 28 May 585 BCE, which occurred during a battle between the Lydians and the Medes and prompted them to make peace. Thales is credited with using geometry to solve practical problems, such as measuring the distance of ships at sea and the height of the pyramids by their shadows, and with several elementary theorems, including that a circle is bisected by its diameter and that the angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle ("Thales's theorem"). Anecdotes also credit him with practical wisdom: Aristotle tells how Thales, foreseeing a bumper olive harvest, leased all the olive presses in Miletus and Chios at a low price and profited greatly, proving philosophers could be wealthy if they wished.

Legacy

Thales founded the Milesian school, whose other members were his younger contemporaries Anaximander and Anaximenes. Bertrand Russell wrote that "Western philosophy begins with Thales." His search for a single material principle behind nature, explained naturalistically, marks the conventional starting point of both Greek philosophy and science.
