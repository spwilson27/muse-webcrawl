Jerome John Garcia (August 1, 1942 – August 9, 1995) was an American musician best known for being the principal songwriter, lead guitarist and a vocalist with the rock band the Grateful Dead, which he co-founded and which came to prominence during the counterculture era of the 1960s. Although he disavowed the role, Garcia was viewed by many as the leader of the band.

As a guitarist, Garcia was known for his distinctive long, melodic improvisations, which often combined elements of rock, folk, bluegrass, blues, country and jazz. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as a member of the Grateful Dead in 1994.

Early life

Garcia was born in San Francisco, California, to a Spanish immigrant father, José Ramón "Joe" Garcia, and a mother of Irish and Swedish descent, Ruth Marie "Bobbie" Garcia. He was named after the composer Jerome Kern. In 1947, while on a family vacation, his father drowned in the Russian River. Garcia was raised thereafter by his mother, who ran a bar near the city's Excelsior District.

At the age of four, Garcia lost the middle finger of his right hand in a wood-chopping accident. He took up the guitar at fifteen, having earlier studied piano, and was influenced by the folk and bluegrass music he encountered in the coffeehouses of the San Francisco Bay Area.

Career

After a brief stint in the United States Army in 1960, Garcia settled in Palo Alto, where he met Robert Hunter, who would become his principal lyricist collaborator. He played in a succession of jug bands and bluegrass groups, including Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, before forming the Warlocks in 1965 with Bob Weir, Ron "Pigpen" McKernan, Phil Lesh and Bill Kreutzmann. The band soon renamed itself the Grateful Dead.

The Grateful Dead became central figures of the San Francisco psychedelic scene, performing at Ken Kesey's Acid Tests and the Human Be-In. Over a thirty-year career the band released thirteen studio albums and built an enormous touring following; their extended concert improvisations, taping-friendly policies and songs such as "Truckin'", "Ripple", "Uncle John's Band" and the 1987 hit "Touch of Grey" made them one of the most successful touring acts in American music.

Side projects

Alongside the Dead, Garcia led the Jerry Garcia Band, played pedal steel guitar with the New Riders of the Purple Sage, and explored his bluegrass roots in Old & In the Way with mandolinist David Grisman and fiddler Vassar Clements. His acoustic duo work with Grisman in the 1990s introduced his playing to a new generation.

Personal life and death

Garcia struggled for much of his adult life with heroin and cocaine addiction, diabetes and the effects of heavy smoking. In 1986 he fell into a diabetic coma that nearly killed him and had to relearn the guitar. He died of a heart attack on August 9, 1995, at a drug rehabilitation facility in Forest Knolls, California, eight days after his fifty-third birthday.

Legacy

Garcia's guitar playing, songcraft and improvisational philosophy made him one of the most influential American musicians of his generation. The flavor of Ben & Jerry's ice cream "Cherry Garcia" was named in his honor, and his custom guitars — including "Wolf" and "Tiger", built by Doug Irwin — are among the most famous instruments in rock history.
