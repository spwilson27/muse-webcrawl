The Grateful Dead was an American rock band formed in 1965 in Palo Alto, California. Known for their eclectic style, which fused elements of rock, folk, country, jazz, bluegrass, blues, rock and roll, gospel, reggae, and world music with psychedelia, the band is famous for their live performances featuring lengthy jams and a devoted fan base known as "Deadheads".

History

Formation (1964–1966)

The band began as the Warlocks, a group including Jerry Garcia, Bob Weir, Ron "Pigpen" McKernan, Phil Lesh, and Bill Kreutzmann, and served as the house band for Ken Kesey's Acid Tests. Taking the name Grateful Dead — from a folk motif found by Garcia in a dictionary — they became central figures of the San Francisco counterculture and the Haight-Ashbury scene.

Recording career

Their albums include Anthem of the Sun (1968), Live/Dead (1969), Workingman's Dead and American Beauty (both 1970), Europe '72, and Terrapin Station (1977). Songs such as "Truckin'", "Casey Jones", "Uncle John's Band", "Ripple", and "Touch of Grey" — their only top-ten single, in 1987 — became standards. Their improvisational centrepiece was "Dark Star".

Touring and taping culture

The band toured almost constantly for thirty years, playing more than 2,300 concerts. Unusually, they permitted fans to record shows and trade tapes, building an archive culture that later extended to official live releases such as the Dick's Picks series. Their concerts featured two sets, the second built around extended improvisation and the "Drums/Space" segment.

End and legacy

Garcia died on 9 August 1995, and the remaining members disbanded the Grateful Dead, continuing in offshoots including The Other Ones, The Dead, Furthur, and Dead & Company. The band was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1994 and received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award; in 2024 the surviving members were Kennedy Center Honorees. The Library of Congress selected their 8 May 1977 Cornell concert recording for the National Recording Registry.
