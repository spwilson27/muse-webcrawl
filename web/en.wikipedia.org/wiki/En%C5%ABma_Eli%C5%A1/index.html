Enūma Eliš (Akkadian: Enūma eliš, "When on high") is the Babylonian creation myth, named for its opening words. It is considered one of the most important sources for understanding Babylonian cosmology and the worldview of ancient Mesopotamia.

Content

The poem describes how, before heaven and earth had names, the primeval waters Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water) mingled together. From their union a series of gods was born, culminating in Marduk, the patron god of Babylon. After a conflict in which the younger gods disturb the primeval peace, Marduk agrees to battle Tiamat on condition that he be made supreme ruler of the gods. He defeats Tiamat, splits her body in two to form the sky and the earth, and creates humankind from the blood of her ally Qingu to serve the gods.

Discovery and publication

The text was recovered in fragmentary form from the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, excavated in the 19th century. George Smith published the first English translation in 1876 as part of The Chaldean Account of Genesis. The myth is recorded on seven clay tablets, each containing between about 115 and 170 lines of cuneiform text.

Significance

The Enūma Eliš was recited during the Babylonian New Year festival (Akitu). Scholars have noted parallels between its account of creation and the Genesis creation narrative, contributing to debate about shared ancient Near Eastern traditions.
