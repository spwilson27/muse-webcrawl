The Itaipu Dam is a hydroelectric dam on the Paraná River located on the border between Brazil and Paraguay. The structure that houses the turbines is roughly half a mile long; the dam, completed in 1984, is operated by the binational company Itaipu Binacional under a 1973 treaty between the two countries. For many years it was the world's largest power station by electricity generation, and it remains, with China's Three Gorges Dam, one of the two largest hydroelectric facilities on Earth.

Construction

Brazil and Paraguay signed the Itaipu Treaty in April 1973. Construction diverted the Paraná — then the world's seventh-largest river — through a 2 km diversion channel blasted from rock; the riverbed was closed in October 1978. The project used some 12.3 million m³ of concrete and employed up to 40,000 workers. The first generating unit entered service in May 1984; the original 18 units were complete by 1991, and two further units added in 2006–07 brought capacity to 14,000 MW. The reservoir flooded the Guaíra Falls (Sete Quedas), then among the largest waterfalls in the world by flow, which were dynamited and submerged in 1982.

Power sharing

Output is divided equally between Brazil and Paraguay, but Paraguay uses only a fraction of its share and sells the surplus to Brazil, a long-standing point of political friction and periodic renegotiation of the tariff. Paraguay's share nevertheless supplies the great majority of its electricity, while Itaipu provides roughly 10–15% of Brazil's consumption. Because the two countries' grids run at different frequencies (Brazil 60 Hz, Paraguay 50 Hz), half the units generate at each frequency, with Paraguay's unused 50 Hz power converted for export.

Recognition

In 1994 the American Society of Civil Engineers named Itaipu one of the Seven Wonders of the Modern World. The project also drew criticism for displacing tens of thousands of people, including Guaraní communities, and for the loss of the Guaíra Falls ecosystem.
