Lithography

Lithography (from Ancient Greek λίθος, lithos, "stone", and γράφειν, graphein, "to write") is a planographic method of printing originally based on the immiscibility of oil and water. The printing is from a stone (lithographic limestone) or a metal plate with a smooth surface. It was invented in 1796 by the German playwright Alois Senefelder as a cheap means of publishing scripts and music, and it became one of the world's most important printing and art media.

Principle

The image is drawn on the flat printing surface with a greasy substance — a lithographic crayon or ink (tusche). The surface is then treated with a solution of gum arabic and mild acid, which makes the non-image areas receptive to water and the image areas receptive to oil-based ink. During printing the surface is kept damp; ink adheres only to the greasy image, and the dampened blank areas repel it. Because the image and non-image areas lie in the same plane, lithography is a planographic process, unlike relief or intaglio printing.

History

Senefelder discovered the process by accident in 1796 while experimenting with printing his own plays; he patented it in England in 1799. Lithography spread rapidly across Europe because it was cheaper and more flexible than copper engraving, and because the artist could draw directly on the stone without learning an engraver's craft. It was used for maps, music, book illustration, labels and advertising. Chromolithography — printing in many colours from a series of stones — produced the elaborate Victorian colour prints, posters and trade cards of the 19th century.

Artists took up lithography enthusiastically: Goya made his Bulls of Bordeaux series by lithography in 1825, and Delacroix, Daumier, Toulouse-Lautrec, Whistler, Munch, Picasso and Miró all produced celebrated lithographs. Toulouse-Lautrec's colour posters of the 1890s remain landmarks of the medium.

Modern process

Contemporary offset lithography, which dominates commercial printing, uses thin aluminium plates instead of stone and transfers the image first to a rubber blanket cylinder, then to paper — an advance developed in the early 20th century that improved quality and speed. Direct lithography still survives in fine-art printmaking workshops. The word also survives in microelectronics: photolithography, the process used to pattern semiconductor wafers, takes its name and its planographic logic from Senefelder's invention.
