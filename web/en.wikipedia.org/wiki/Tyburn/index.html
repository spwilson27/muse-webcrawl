Tyburn

Tyburn was a manor and village in Middlesex, England, near the present-day Marble Arch in London. It is best known as the principal place of public execution for London criminals for several centuries, from at least the twelfth century until 1783, when executions were moved to Newgate Prison. The site took its name from the Tyburn Brook, a tributary of the River Westbourne.

The "Tyburn Tree", erected in 1571, could execute up to 24 prisoners simultaneously.

History

Executions at Tyburn are recorded as early as 1196, when the demagogue William Fitz Osbert was hanged there. The site stood at the junction of major roads west of the city — roughly where Watling Street (Edgware Road) met the road to Uxbridge (Oxford Street) — and execution days drew enormous crowds. In 1571 the permanent gallows known as the Tyburn Tree was erected: a triangular frame supported on three legs, with three horizontal beams from which as many as 24 people could be hanged at once.

Condemned prisoners were transported from Newgate Prison through the streets of London in an open cart, a procession of some three miles that took hours, stopping at alehouses along the way. The last words of the dying were published in broadsheets, and "Tyburn tickets" and execution literature were a popular genre. Famous people executed at Tyburn include Roger Mortimer (1330), Perkin Warbeck (1499), the regicide John Bradshaw's exhumed corpse (1661), highwayman Claude Duval (1670), and Jonathan Wild (1725). During the religious persecutions of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, many Catholic martyrs — among them Edmund Campion (1581) — were executed there; the Tyburn Convent near the site commemorates them.

End of the Tyburn gallows

By the late eighteenth century the disorder of execution days — the "Tyburn Fair" atmosphere, pickpocketing, and riots — was widely condemned. The last execution at Tyburn, of the highwayman John Austin, took place on 3 November 1783. Public executions were then transferred to a scaffold outside Newgate Prison. A small stone tablet in the traffic island near Marble Arch marks the site of the gallows.
