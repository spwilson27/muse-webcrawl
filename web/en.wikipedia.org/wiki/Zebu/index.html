Zebu

The zebu (Bos indicus), sometimes known as indicine or humped cattle, is a species or subspecies of domestic cattle originating in South Asia. Zebu are characterised by a fatty hump on their shoulders, long drooping ears and a large dewlap. Adapted to hot climates, they are raised across Asia, Africa and South America for milk, meat, hides and draught power, and are sacred in Hindu tradition.

Origins and domestication

Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates that zebu were domesticated from the Indian aurochs (Bos primigenius nomadicus) in the greater Indus Valley region roughly 8,000 years ago, independently of the domestication of taurine cattle in the Near East. Seals from Mohenjo-daro and other Indus cities depict humped bulls, showing the antiquity of the zebu in South Asian culture. From India, zebu spread westward into Africa — where they interbred with local taurine cattle — and eastward through Southeast Asia.

Characteristics

The shoulder hump is composed of fat and muscle supported by elongated spinous processes of the vertebrae. Zebu have more sweat glands and a lower metabolic rate than taurine cattle, giving them superior tolerance of heat and drought and greater resistance to many parasites and diseases such as tick-borne fevers. Milk yields per animal are typically lower than those of specialised taurine dairy breeds, though zebu milk is rich in butterfat. Their hides produce prized leather.

Distribution and uses

Major zebu populations are found in India, Pakistan, Brazil — whose Nelore herd is the world's largest beef population of zebu type — and across sub-Saharan Africa, where breeds such as the Boran and Ankole are kept. Crosses with taurine cattle, such as the American Brahman (itself substantially zebu) and the Australian droughtmaster, combine heat tolerance with productivity. Zebu are also used in ploughing and carting throughout Asia.

Cultural significance

In India, zebu cattle are revered in Hinduism; the bull Nandi is the mount of the god Shiva, and cows are protected from slaughter in most Indian states. The animals feature in festivals, temple sculpture and everyday street life in Indian cities.
