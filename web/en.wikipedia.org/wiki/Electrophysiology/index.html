Electrophysiology

Electrophysiology (from Greek ἥλεκτρον, ēlektron, "amber"; φύσις, physis, "nature"; and -λογία, -logia) is the branch of physiology that studies the electrical properties of biological cells and tissues. It involves measurements of voltage changes or electric current on a wide variety of scales from single ion channel proteins to whole organs like the heart. In neuroscience, it includes measurements of the electrical activity of neurons, and, in particular, of the action potential.

Techniques

Classical electrophysiology places electrodes into biological tissue to record voltage or current. Intracellular recording with sharp microelectrodes or patch-clamp pipettes resolves single-cell and even single-channel activity; Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann won the 1991 Nobel Prize for developing the patch clamp. Extracellular techniques include single-unit recording, multi-electrode arrays, and field-potential recording. Clinical and organism-level methods include the electrocardiogram (ECG) for the heart, electroencephalography (EEG) for the brain, and electromyography (EMG) for muscle.

Cardiac electrophysiology

Clinical cardiac electrophysiology studies and treats arrhythmias. In an electrophysiology study, catheters with electrodes are threaded into the heart to map abnormal electrical pathways; identified circuits can often be destroyed by catheter ablation, curing conditions such as supraventricular tachycardia and atrial flutter, and implantation of pacemakers and defibrillators is guided by the same discipline.

History

The field originates with Luigi Galvani's experiments on frog legs in the 1790s and Alessandro Volta's critique of them, and passed through Emil du Bois-Reymond's measurements of nerve current to the Hodgkin–Huxley model of the action potential (1952), which remains the quantitative foundation of cellular electrophysiology.
