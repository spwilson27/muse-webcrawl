Danzon (Spanish: danzón) is the official musical genre and dance of Cuba. It developed in Matanzas in the 1870s from the earlier contradanza and danza, fusing European salon-dance forms and instrumentation with African-derived rhythmic patterns. The first composition generally recognized as a danzon is "Las alturas de Simpson", performed by the Orquesta Faílde in Matanzas in 1879 under Miguel Faílde, who is regarded as the genre's creator.

Form and instrumentation

The traditional danzon is played by a charanga francesa: a French-style wooden flute, violins, piano, double bass, güiro and timbales (originally a pailas criollas/cata combination). Its structure is a rondo of contrasting sections — an introduction, a clarinet or violin trio section, a flute solo section and a lively final section — with characteristic rhythmic cells such as the cinquillo and the tresillo underlying its stately 2/4 metre. Couples dance in an elegant, restrained ballroom style, pausing during the introduction. In the 1910s and 1920s, Orestes López and others extended the form; López's danzon "Mambo" (1938) introduced a syncopated final section, the nuevo ritmo, from which the mambo and, through Enrique Jorrín's danzones of the early 1950s, the cha-cha-chá emerged.

Spread

From Cuba the danzon travelled to Mexico, where it took deep root in Veracruz and Mexico City — the Salón México being a celebrated venue — and remains a living social dance performed by orchestras in public squares. In Cuba it is revered as the national dance, although younger generations largely favour newer genres; composers such as José Urfé ("El bombín de Barreto") and Antonio María Romeu shaped its classic repertory. The danzonete, a sung variant popularized by Aniceto Díaz from 1929, briefly rivalled the instrumental form.
