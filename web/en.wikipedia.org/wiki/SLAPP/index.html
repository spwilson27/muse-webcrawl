Strategic lawsuit against public participation

A strategic lawsuit against public participation (SLAPP), also known as a SLAPP suit or an intimidation lawsuit, is a lawsuit intended to censor, intimidate, and silence critics by burdening them with the cost of a legal defence until they abandon their criticism or opposition. The term was coined in the 1980s by University of Denver professors Penelope Canan and George W. Pring.

Characteristics

A SLAPP plaintiff typically does not expect to win the lawsuit. Rather, the plaintiff's goals are accomplished if the defendant succumbs to fear, intimidation, mounting legal costs, or simple exhaustion and abandons the criticism. In some cases, prolonged litigation meant to drain a defendant's financial resources can drive vulnerable targets into insolvency. A SLAPP may also intimidate others from participating in debate, producing a chilling effect on speech.

Anti-SLAPP legislation

Many jurisdictions have enacted anti-SLAPP laws allowing defendants to file a special motion to strike or dismiss the claim at an early stage, often shifting legal costs to the plaintiff. In the United States, a majority of states have some form of anti-SLAPP statute, with California's (Code of Civil Procedure § 425.16) and the Texas Citizens Participation Act among the most prominent. The European Union adopted an anti-SLAPP directive in 2024 aimed at protecting journalists and human rights defenders from manifestly unfounded or abusive cross-border proceedings.

Criticism of the concept

Critics argue that the label "SLAPP" can itself be used to disparage legitimate claims, such as defamation suits by private individuals, and that broad anti-SLAPP motions may deny genuine plaintiffs access to the courts and impose fee-shifting penalties on them.
