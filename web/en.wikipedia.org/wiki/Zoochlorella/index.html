Zoochlorella (plural zoochlorellae) is a generic term for green algae (typically of the genera Chlorella and related chlorophytes) that live as endosymbionts within the cells or tissues of an animal host. The term contrasts with zooxanthellae, the golden-brown dinoflagellate symbionts found in corals and other marine animals. Zoochlorellae are especially common in freshwater invertebrates.

Hosts and symbiosis

Well-known associations include the green hydra (Hydra viridissima), whose gastrodermal cells harbour Chlorella-like algae; Paramecium bursaria, a ciliate packed with symbiotic algae; freshwater sponges; some sea anemones; and certain flatworms and molluscs. Within the host, the algae photosynthesise and release a share of their fixed carbon — often the sugar maltose — to the host, supplementing its nutrition. In return, the host provides the algae with shelter, carbon dioxide, and nitrogenous waste products, an arrangement generally classed as mutualism.

Transmission and regulation

In asexually reproducing hosts such as hydra, algae are passed directly to offspring in buds. In other associations the symbionts must be acquired from the environment in each generation. Hosts regulate the algal population through digestion of excess cells, control of cell division, and expulsion; under stress, associations may break down, analogous to coral bleaching in zooxanthellate systems.

Taxonomy

"Zoochlorella" is not a formal taxon: the algae once grouped under the name Zoochlorella parasitica have been redistributed among several green algal genera, including Chlorella, Micractinium and Choricystis, on molecular evidence. The term persists as a convenient ecological label for any green algal symbiont of an animal.
