Napoleon II

Napoleon II (Napoléon François Charles Joseph Bonaparte; 20 March 1811 – 22 July 1832) was the son of Emperor Napoleon I and Empress Marie Louise of Austria. Born at the Tuileries Palace in Paris, he was styled King of Rome from birth. He was proclaimed emperor twice, in 1814 and again for a few days in 1815 after his father's second abdication, but never actually ruled France. In Austria he was known as Franz, Duke of Reichstadt.

Early life

Napoleon's marriage to Marie Louise, daughter of Emperor Francis I of Austria, was intended to secure the Bonaparte dynasty. The birth of a son in 1811 was greeted with great celebration; the infant received the title King of Rome, reviving an old imperial dignity. When Napoleon abdicated in April 1814, he sought to abdicate in favour of his son, but the allies insisted on the restoration of the Bourbon monarchy under Louis XVIII. The child was taken to Vienna and raised at the court of his grandfather.

The Hundred Days and 1815

During the Hundred Days, Napoleon returned to power but was defeated at Waterloo. On 22 June 1815 he abdicated for a second time in favour of his son, and a commission of government proclaimed the boy Napoleon II. Louis XVIII was restored before the child, who remained in Austria, could return. Bonapartists nonetheless regarded him as the legitimate emperor, and the numbering of his cousin Louis-Napoleon as Napoleon III in 1848 acknowledged the claim.

Life in Austria

At Schönbrunn Palace the young prince was educated as an Austrian archduke under the supervision of his grandfather. In 1818 he was granted the title Duke of Reichstadt. He grew into a tall, handsome young man, devoted to a military career and fascinated by his father's campaigns, which he studied in detail. He formed a close friendship with Princess Sophie of Bavaria. His health, however, was fragile, and he suffered recurrent chest infections.

Death and legacy

Napoleon II died of tuberculosis at Schönbrunn on 22 July 1832, aged 21. He was buried in the Imperial Crypt in Vienna. His short, melancholy life inspired Edmond Rostand's play L'Aiglon ("The Eaglet"), a nickname by which he became known in France. In December 1940 Adolf Hitler had his remains transferred to Paris, where they now rest in Les Invalides near his father's tomb — though his heart and intestines remained in Vienna, in accordance with Habsburg custom.
