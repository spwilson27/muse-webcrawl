Law school

A law school (also known as a law centre or college of law) is an institution, usually part of a university, specialising in legal education. The pattern of study varies greatly between jurisdictions: in the United States and Canada, law is a postgraduate degree (the Juris Doctor), while in England, Wales, India, and many Commonwealth countries it is commonly studied as an undergraduate degree (the Bachelor of Laws, LLB).

United States

Admission to an ABA-accredited law school normally requires a bachelor's degree and a Law School Admission Test (LSAT) score. The standard degree is the three-year Juris Doctor (J.D.). Teaching historically used the case method pioneered at Harvard Law School under Dean Christopher Columbus Langdell, centring on appellate decisions and Socratic questioning. Graduates must pass a state bar examination to practise.

United Kingdom

In England and Wales, prospective solicitors and barristers typically take a qualifying law degree or a conversion course, followed by vocational training — since 2021 the Solicitors Qualifying Examination (SQE) route for solicitors, and Bar training courses for barristers. In Scotland, the LLB followed by the Diploma in Professional Legal Practice is the standard path.

Other jurisdictions

Many civil law countries integrate legal studies into five- or six-year university programmes. India has both five-year integrated programmes at national law universities and three-year LLB degrees. Australia and Canada follow models influenced by both British and American practice.
