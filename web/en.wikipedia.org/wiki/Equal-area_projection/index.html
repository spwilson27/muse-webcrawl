Equal-area projection

In cartography, an equal-area projection (also called an equivalent or authalic projection) is a map projection that preserves areas: regions on the map have the same relative sizes as the corresponding regions on Earth. Because no flat map can preserve both area and shape, equal-area projections necessarily distort shapes, angles and distances, especially towards the edges of the map.

Properties

Equal-area maps are widely used for thematic mapping — distributions of population, soil, land use or vegetation — wherever comparing the sizes of regions fairly matters. The scale of an equal-area map varies from place to place, but the ratio of areas is everywhere preserved.

Examples

  • The Mollweide projection, an elliptical pseudocylindrical projection popular for world maps.
  • The sinusoidal projection, one of the oldest equal-area projections.
  • The Albers equal-area conic projection, standard for maps of the continental United States.
  • The Lambert azimuthal equal-area projection, used for hemispheric and polar maps.
  • The Gall–Peters projection, promoted in the 1970s as a fairer representation of the relative sizes of continents.
  • The Eckert IV and Equal Earth projections, modern compromises retaining equal area with less shape distortion.
