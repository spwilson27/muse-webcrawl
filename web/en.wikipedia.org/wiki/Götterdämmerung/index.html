Götterdämmerung (German for "Twilight of the Gods"), WWV 86D, is the last of the four music dramas constituting Richard Wagner's cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen. It received its premiere at the Bayreuth Festspielhaus on 17 August 1876, as part of the first complete performance of the Ring. The title is a German rendering of the Old Norse Ragnarök, the prophesied destruction of the gods in Norse mythology.

Composition

Wagner's text was written first among the four Ring librettos: in 1848 he drafted Siegfrieds Tod ("Siegfried's Death"), and over the following years worked backwards, adding the three preceding dramas. The music of Götterdämmerung was composed between 1869 and 1874. It is the longest of the cycle, with a prologue and three acts running to roughly four and a half hours.

Synopsis

Prologue

The three Norns weave the rope of fate, which breaks. Siegfried and Brünnhilde emerge from their cave; Siegfried gives her the ring as a token of love and departs on new adventures, an episode accompanied by the orchestral interlude "Siegfried's Rhine Journey".

Act 1

At the hall of the Gibichungs, Gunther and Gutrune, advised by their half-brother Hagen (son of Alberich), plot to marry Brünnhilde and Siegfried respectively. Siegfried arrives and, given a potion that makes him forget Brünnhilde, agrees to win her for Gunther in exchange for Gutrune's hand. Disguised by the Tarnhelm as Gunther, he overpowers Brünnhilde and takes the ring from her.

Act 2

Brünnhilde, brought to the Gibichung hall as Gunther's bride, sees the ring on Siegfried's finger and denounces him. With Hagen and Gunther she plots revenge: during a hunt, Hagen will kill Siegfried, whose one vulnerable point is his back.

Act 3

Siegfried, separated from the hunting party, meets the Rhinemaidens, who beg in vain for the ring. Hagen gives him a drink that restores his memory; as Siegfried recounts his awakening of Brünnhilde, Hagen spears him in the back. The funeral march accompanies his body's return. In the final scene Brünnhilde orders a funeral pyre, takes the ring, and rides her horse Grane into the flames. The Rhine overflows, the Rhinemaidens recover the ring, Hagen drowns, and Valhalla is seen burning in the sky as a theme of redemption closes the cycle.

Music

The score is famous for the orchestral interludes "Siegfried's Rhine Journey" and "Siegfried's Funeral March", and for a dense web of leitmotifs accumulated across the four operas.
