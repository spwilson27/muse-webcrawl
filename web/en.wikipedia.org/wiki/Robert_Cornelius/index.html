Robert Cornelius

Robert Cornelius (March 1, 1809 – August 10, 1893) was an American pioneer of photography who is credited with taking one of the earliest photographic self-portraits — and one of the first photographs of a human being — in 1839.

Early life

Cornelius was born in Philadelphia to a Dutch immigrant father, Christian Cornelius, who had built a successful business manufacturing lamps. Robert worked in the family firm, Cornelius and Company, specialising in silver plating and metal polishing. His expertise in metals and chemistry brought him into contact with early experiments in the new daguerreotype process, which had been announced in France in 1839.

The 1839 self-portrait

In October or November 1839, in the yard behind his family's lamp store in Philadelphia, Cornelius set up a camera fitted with a lens adapted from an opera glass, removed the lens cap, ran into frame, and sat still for between three and fifteen minutes before replacing the cap. The resulting daguerreotype shows him with arms crossed and hair tousled, slightly off-center — often described as the first "selfie". On the back of the plate he wrote: "The first light Picture ever taken. 1839." The image is now held by the Library of Congress.

Photography career

Cornelius's knowledge of chemistry allowed him to shorten exposure times dramatically — with Joseph Saxton's and John William Draper's circles he was among those improving the daguerreotype process in America. He operated one of the first portrait studios in the United States in Philadelphia from about 1841 to 1843, attracting sitters with sittings that took only seconds thanks to improved plates, fast lenses, and blue-filtered light. Among his sitters was the chemist Martin Hans Boyè.

Later life

Photography was a brief episode in Cornelius's life. He returned to the family lamp business, which became a major manufacturer of lamps and gas fixtures, and grew wealthy. He retired in 1877 and died in Philadelphia in 1893, largely forgotten as a photographic pioneer until historians revived his name in the twentieth century.
