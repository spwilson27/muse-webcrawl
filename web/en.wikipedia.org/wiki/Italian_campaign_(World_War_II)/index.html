The Italian campaign of the Second World War was the Allied campaign in and around Italy from 1943 to 1945. It opened with the invasion of Sicily in July 1943, which precipitated the fall of Mussolini, and continued up the Italian peninsula against determined German defence after Italy's armistice. The joint Allied Forces Headquarters campaign is remembered for gruelling mountain warfare at Monte Cassino and along the Gothic Line.

Course of the campaign

Operation Husky, the invasion of Sicily (10 July 1943), was at the time the largest amphibious operation ever mounted. Mussolini fell on 25 July; Italy's armistice with the Allies was announced on 8 September, whereupon Germany disarmed Italian forces and occupied the north. Landings at Salerno (Operation Avalanche) and Taranto put Allied armies on the mainland, and Naples fell on 1 October. German commander Albert Kesselring conducted a masterful delaying defence through successive fortified lines: the Gustav Line, anchored on Monte Cassino, held through four battles (January–May 1944), while the Anzio landing (January 1944) failed to break the stalemate quickly. Rome was liberated on 4 June 1944, two days before D-Day. In the north, the Gothic Line battles of late 1944 ground to a halt in the Apennine winter; the final Spring 1945 offensive broke through to the Po Valley, and German forces in Italy surrendered effective 2 May 1945.

Assessment

The campaign knocked Italy out of the war, gave the Allies air bases (notably Foggia) for bombing southern and central Europe, and tied down German divisions, but its strategic value was debated even at the time, and after June 1944 it became secondary to the Western Front. Casualties were heavy — each side suffered on the order of 300,000 or more — and Italian civilians endured occupation, civil war between the RSI and the resistance, and Allied bombing.
