Artificial fly
TypeFishing lure for fly fishing
MaterialsHook, thread, feather, fur, hair, silk, synthetic fibres, tinsel
Principal categoriesDry flies, wet flies, nymphs, streamers, terrestrials, saltwater flies

An artificial fly or fly lure is a type of fishing lure, usually very light, used in the sport of fly fishing. Artificial flies are constructed by fly tiers on a bare hook using thread, feather, fur, hair, and other natural and synthetic materials. They are designed to imitate the natural food of fish—most often aquatic and terrestrial insects, but also crustaceans, worms, baitfish, and other prey—or simply to provoke a strike through colour and movement.

History

Descriptions of flies dressed to imitate insects date to antiquity: the Roman author Aelian described Macedonian anglers using red wool and feathers on a hook. The English Treatyse of Fysshynge wyth an Angle (1496) listed a dozen fly patterns, and Izaak Walton's The Compleat Angler (1653) further popularised fly fishing. In the nineteenth century, English chalk-stream practice, codified by Frederic M. Halford, emphasised precise imitation with dry flies, while in North America tiers adapted patterns to freestone rivers and to Atlantic salmon and bass. The twentieth century added synthetic materials, foam, and rubber legs, greatly widening the range of possible imitations.

Categories

Dry flies

Dry flies float on the surface film, imitating adult mayflies, caddisflies, stoneflies, midges, or terrestrial insects such as ants and grasshoppers that have fallen onto the water. Famous patterns include the Adams, the Royal Wulff, and the Elk Hair Caddis.

Wet flies and nymphs

Wet flies are fished beneath the surface, traditionally swung down-and-across, and imitate drowned or emerging insects. Nymph patterns—such as the Pheasant Tail, Hare's Ear, and Copper John—imitate the underwater larval stages of aquatic insects, often weighted with beads or wire to sink.

Streamers

Streamers are larger flies, including the Woolly Bugger and various baitfish patterns, that imitate minnows, leeches, sculpins, or crayfish and are actively retrieved to attract predatory fish.

Other types

Saltwater flies imitate shrimp, crabs, and baitfish for species such as bonefish and tarpon; bass and pike flies include buoyant deer-hair poppers; and salmon and steelhead flies range from traditional feather-wing patterns to simple modern intruder styles.

Construction

Flies are tied with the aid of a vice, bobbin, and other tools. Materials are layered onto the hook shank—typically a tail, a body of dubbed fur or synthetic fibre, ribbing, wings, and hackle (a spiralled feather providing floatation or movement). Good pattern design balances silhouette, size, colour, and behaviour in or on the water, a selection process anglers call "matching the hatch."
