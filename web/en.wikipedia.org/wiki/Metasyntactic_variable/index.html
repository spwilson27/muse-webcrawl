Metasyntactic variable

A metasyntactic variable is a specific word or set of words identified as a placeholder in computer science, used in examples and documentation to stand for whatever the context requires. The classic metasyntactic variables in English-speaking programming culture are foo, bar, baz, and their combinations such as foobar. When a programmer needs a third, fourth, and further names, common continuations include qux, quux, corge, grault, garply, waldo, fred, plugh, xyzzy, and thud — a list codified in the Jargon File.

Etymology

The origin of "foo" is uncertain. It may derive from the Second World War acronym FUBAR ("fouled up beyond all recognition"), from the Chinese word fu ("good fortune") as displayed in Jade Foo Dogs, or from the comic strip Smokey Stover, which used "foo" liberally in the 1930s. The word was in use at MIT by the late 1950s and appears in early hacker culture documents and in the first Request for Comments (RFC 269 drew on the tradition; the canonical Foo/Bar discussion appears in RFC 3092, an April Fools' RFC).

Usage

Metasyntactic variables are used when the identity of a thing is unimportant — in code examples, tutorials, and technical discussion. Equivalent conventions exist in other cultures: French programmers use toto, titi, tata, and tutu; Japanese examples often use hoge and piyo; German programmers may use bla and blub. In mathematics and logic, analogous placeholder conventions use letters such as x and y.

In popular culture

The Foo Fighters took their name from Second World War slang for unidentified flying objects, itself tied to the same "foo" lineage. Google has used "foo" in various Easter eggs, and the programming challenge Google Foobar used the term directly.
