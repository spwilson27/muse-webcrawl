International Standard Book Number

A 13-digit ISBN encoded in an EAN-13 barcode.

The International Standard Book Number (ISBN) is a numeric commercial book identifier that is intended to be unique. Publishers purchase or receive ISBNs from an affiliate of the International ISBN Agency. A different ISBN is assigned to each separate edition and variation of a publication, but a work republished without changes retains the same number; format changes such as a new e-book edition generally require a new ISBN.

Structure

The ISBN has been 13 digits long since 2007, replacing the earlier 10-digit form. The digits are divided into five parts of variable length:

  • the GS1 prefix (978 or 979), aligning ISBNs with the EAN barcode system;
  • the registration group element, identifying the country, region or language area;
  • the registrant element, identifying the publisher;
  • the publication element, identifying the specific title and edition;
  • the check digit, computed by a weighted modulo-10 algorithm for the 13-digit form (the 10-digit form used modulo 11, with "X" representing 10).

History

The system originated in the United Kingdom in 1966 as the nine-digit Standard Book Numbering (SBN) system, created for the bookseller W. H. Smith. The 10-digit ISBN was adopted internationally in 1970 as ISO 2108, and the migration to 13 digits took effect on 1 January 2007 to integrate ISBNs with the global EAN product code system and expand capacity.

Assignment and use

National ISBN agencies assign registrant blocks to publishers, who assign numbers to their titles. ISBNs are printed on the copyright page and, as barcodes, on back covers, enabling ordering, inventory, and sales tracking throughout the book trade. ISBNs do not themselves indicate anything about the content of a book, and they confer no legal or copyright protection.
