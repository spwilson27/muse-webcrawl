American comic book

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

American comic books, the staple-bound periodicals that defined the medium in the United States.

An American comic book is a short magazine or booklet of comics originating in the United States, typically 32 pages in color, published monthly or periodically, and sold through newsstands and, later, specialty comic shops. The format emerged in the 1930s, beginning as reprints of newspaper comic strips before publishers began commissioning original material.

History

Early landmarks include Famous Funnies (1934) and New Fun (1935). The debut of Superman in Action Comics #1 (1938) launched the Golden Age and the superhero genre. After a postwar slump and the moral panic that produced the Comics Code Authority in 1954, the Silver Age revived superheroes at Marvel Comics and DC Comics, followed by the darker Bronze and Modern ages.

Industry and formats

Historically American comics were produced by an assembly-line studio system dividing writing, pencilling, inking, lettering, and coloring among different hands. Alongside the "floppy" single issue, the industry developed the trade paperback and the graphic novel. The rise of the direct market in the 1970s–80s shifted sales to comic shops, and the 1990s saw a speculative boom and bust. Japanese manga and webcomics have since reshaped the market.

Cultural significance

American comic books created enduring popular icons — Superman, Batman, Spider-Man, the X-Men — whose film and television adaptations dominate global popular culture, while alternative and underground comics broadened the medium's artistic ambitions.
