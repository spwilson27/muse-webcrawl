Gwalior Fort (Gwalior Qila) is a great hill fort near Gwalior, Madhya Pradesh, in central India. Occupying an isolated rocky hill about 3 kilometres long and rising some 100 metres above the surrounding plain, the fort has existed in some form since at least the 8th century, and its surviving palaces and temples date mainly from the 15th century. The Mughal emperor Babur famously described it as "the pearl in the necklace of the forts of India".

History

According to local tradition, the fort was founded by a local chieftain named Suraj Sen, who was cured of leprosy by the hermit Gwalipa, after whom the fort and city are said to be named. Historically, the site passed through the hands of many dynasties: the Hunas, the Gurjara-Pratiharas, the Kachchhapaghatas, the Tomaras, the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughals, the Marathas under the Scindias, and finally the British.

The fort's most celebrated buildings were erected under the Tomara dynasty, especially Man Singh Tomar (r. 1486–1516), who built the great Man Mandir palace with its distinctive turquoise, blue and yellow tiled façade. In 1232 the fort withstood a long siege before falling to Iltutmish of Delhi. It was later used by the Mughals as a state prison for political captives; the emperor Aurangzeb imprisoned and executed his brother Murad there.

During the Indian Rebellion of 1857, Gwalior was the scene of fierce fighting. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, one of the rebellion's most famous leaders, was killed in battle near Gwalior in June 1858. The fort passed to the Scindia dynasty, under whom it remained until Indian independence.

Architecture

The fort complex covers about 3 square kilometres and contains palaces, temples, water tanks and Jain rock-cut sculptures. Its defences consist of massive sandstone walls following the contours of the hill, with steep cliffs on the northern side that make the fort nearly impregnable from that direction.

Man Mandir Palace

The Man Mandir, built by Man Singh Tomar between 1486 and 1516, is the fort's most famous structure. Its eastern façade is richly decorated with blue, green and golden tiles forming patterns of ducks, crocodiles, elephants and peacocks around arched windows and friezes. Inside are two open courtyards surrounded by rooms with carved brackets, and below them lie dark underground chambers once used as a prison.

Other structures

  • Gujari Mahal — a palace built by Man Singh for his queen Mrignayani, now an archaeological museum.
  • Sas Bahu Temples — twin 11th-century Hindu temples dedicated to Vishnu.
  • Teli ka Mandir — the tallest structure in the fort, a 9th-century temple about 30 metres high, blending north and south Indian architectural styles.
  • Jain sculptures — colossal rock-cut statues of Jain tirthankaras carved into the cliff faces, dating from the 7th to 15th centuries; some were defaced by Babur's forces in 1527.
  • Chaturbhuj Temple — notable for an inscription recording the use of the symbol for zero, dated to 876 CE, one of the oldest known representations of zero as a numeral.

The zero inscription

The Chaturbhuj Temple within the fort contains an inscription dated Vikrama Samvat 933 (876 CE) that records a land grant and refers to measurements including "270" written with a small circular zero. It is frequently cited as one of the earliest surviving examples of the numeral zero written in a positional decimal system.
