French Renaissance architecture is the style of building that developed in France between the late 15th and early 17th centuries, when Italian Renaissance ideas — classical orders, symmetry and antique ornament — were imported, chiefly through the French invasions of Italy, and fused with native Gothic traditions of steep roofs, tall chimneys and picturesque silhouettes.


Château de Chambord, emblem of the French Renaissance

Early phase

After Charles VIII's invasion of Italy in 1494–95, Italian artists and craftsmen came to the Loire Valley, where the court built its châteaux. At Amboise and Blois, classical pilasters and round arches were applied to still-Gothic plans. Under Francis I building reached a climax: the Château de Chambord (from 1519), with its double-helix staircase and forest of turrets around a Greek-cross keep, and the transformation of Fontainebleau, where Rosso Fiorentino and Primaticcio created the Gallery of Francis I and founded the First School of Fontainebleau, blending painting, stucco and woodwork.

Classical maturity

From the 1540s French architects such as Pierre Lescot (the Cour Carrée wing of the Louvre, from 1546) and Philibert de l'Orme (Château d'Anet; theorist and inventor of French roof carpentry) mastered classical vocabulary in a distinctly French idiom, emphasising rustication, tall pavilion roofs and refined stone carving. Jean Bullant and the second School of Fontainebleau continued the style under the last Valois kings.

Later phase

The Wars of Religion slowed building after 1560. Under Henry IV, urban projects in Paris — the Place des Vosges, the completion of the Pont Neuf and parts of the Louvre and Tuileries — set the stage for the French classical Baroque of the 17th century.
