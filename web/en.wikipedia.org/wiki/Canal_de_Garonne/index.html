Canal de Garonne

The Canal de Garonne, formerly known as the Canal latéral à la Garonne, is a French canal connecting Toulouse to Castets-en-Dorthe near Bordeaux. Together with the Canal du Midi, which it joins at Toulouse, it forms the Canal des Deux Mers, the 457-kilometre waterway linking the Mediterranean Sea to the Atlantic Ocean. The canal is 193 kilometres (120 miles) long and has 53 locks; it was built in the 19th century as an extension designed, in part, to avoid the unpredictable navigation of the Garonne River.

History

Although Pierre-Paul Riquet, builder of the Canal du Midi in the 17th century, envisaged an extension westward, construction did not begin until 1838 under the engineer Jean-Baptiste de Baudre. The canal was completed in stages and opened fully in 1856, just as the railways were beginning to undermine canal transport. It follows the left bank of the Garonne from Toulouse to Agen and then the right bank to Castets-en-Dorthe, where it rejoins the tidal river for the final reach to Bordeaux.

Features

The canal crosses the Garonne valley on notable structures, most famously the Agen aqueduct (Pont-Canal d'Agen), completed in 1849, a stone aqueduct of 23 arches carrying the canal high above the river. At Montech, barges historically bypassed a flight of locks via the Montech water slope (1974), an experimental inclined plane, now closed. Today the canal is used almost entirely by pleasure craft, hire boats, and hotel barges, and its plane-tree-lined towpaths form part of a popular long-distance cycle route from Bordeaux to Toulouse and onward along the Canal du Midi to the Mediterranean.
