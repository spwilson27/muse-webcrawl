The Czechoslovak Legions (Czech: Československé legie) were volunteer armed forces composed predominantly of Czechs and Slovaks that fought alongside the Allied Powers during the First World War. Their goal was to win Allied support for the independence of Bohemia, Moravia and Slovakia from the Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary, and their military and political weight was a major factor in the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918.

Formation

Legions were formed in Russia, France and Italy, recruited from expatriate communities, emigrants, and — especially in Russia — prisoners of war and deserters from the Austro-Hungarian army. In Russia the first unit, the Czech Company (Česká družina), was formed in 1914. Under the political leadership of Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, Edvard Beneš and Milan Rastislav Štefánik, the Czechoslovak National Council sought recognition of the legions as the army of a future state.

The Legion in Russia

The Russian legion grew to some 60,000 men. It distinguished itself at the Battle of Zborov in July 1917 during the Kerensky Offensive. After the Bolshevik Revolution and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, the legion sought evacuation to France via the Trans-Siberian Railway and Vladivostok. In May 1918, clashes with Bolshevik forces — sparked by the Chelyabinsk incident — escalated into open conflict, and the legion seized control of long stretches of the Trans-Siberian Railway. The legionnaires became a significant force in the Russian Civil War before being evacuated from Vladivostok by 1920.

France and Italy

In France, Czech and Slovak volunteers served from 1914, notably in the Foreign Legion (including the famous "Nazdar" company), and later in an autonomous Czechoslovak army recognised by France in 1917–18. In Italy, a legion of some 20,000 fought on the Italian front, including at the Piave and Monte Grappa, after Italy recognised the Czechoslovak National Council in 1918.

Legacy

The legions' achievements underpinned Allied recognition of Czechoslovak independence in 1918. In the new republic the legionnaires (legionáři) were honoured as founders of the state; the term "legionnaire" carried political and social prestige, and the Legion Bank (Legiobanka) and numerous monuments commemorated them. Their Siberian anabasis remains one of the most celebrated episodes in Czech and Slovak national history.
