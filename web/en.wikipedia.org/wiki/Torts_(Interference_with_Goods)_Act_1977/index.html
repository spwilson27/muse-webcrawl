The Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977 (c. 32) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that reforms the law of England and Wales concerning wrongful interference with goods. It implemented recommendations of the Law Reform Committee and modernised the cluster of torts — conversion, trespass to goods, and negligence resulting in damage to goods — by gathering them under the single statutory concept of "wrongful interference with goods."

Main provisions

Section 1 defines wrongful interference as conversion, trespass to goods, negligence resulting in damage to goods or to an interest in them, and other torts so far as they involve goods. Most notably, the Act abolished the ancient tort of detinue — the action for failure to deliver up goods — though it preserved the court's power to order delivery up of goods in conversion claims. The Act also reformed remedies: it provides for damages and specific restitution of goods, allows for improvements made by an innocent improver to be taken into account, and addresses double liability where more than one person has a claim to the same goods.

Uncollected goods

Part II of the Act (sections 12 and 13 and Schedule 1) gives bailees — such as repairers, warehousers, and dry cleaners — a statutory power of sale over goods that the owner fails to collect. After giving the prescribed notice to the bailor, a bailee may sell uncollected goods and account for the proceeds; the Act imposes a duty to obtain the best price reasonably obtainable. These provisions are widely relied upon in consumer transactions where goods are left uncollected after repair or storage.
