Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis

Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis
Signed2–3 April 1559
LocationLe Cateau-Cambrésis, France
PartiesFrance; Spain; England
EffectEnded the Italian War of 1551–1559 and the Habsburg–Valois wars

The Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis, signed in April 1559, ended the Italian War of 1551–1559 and brought to a close the sixty-five years of Habsburg–Valois conflict over Italy. It was agreed between Henry II of France on one side and Philip II of Spain and Elizabeth I of England on the other, and is regarded as one of the most consequential settlements of the sixteenth century, confirming Spain as the dominant power in Italy and Europe for the next century.

Terms

France renounced its claims to the Italian peninsula: it withdrew from Piedmont and Savoy (restoring the Duke of Savoy, Emmanuel Philibert), returned Corsica to Genoa, and recognised Spanish control of Milan, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia and the State of the Presidi. In the north, France retained the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Toul and Verdun, seized in 1552, and — in the separate Anglo-French agreement — kept Calais, England's last possession in France, which had fallen in 1558, subject to a payment of 500,000 crowns or its return after eight years (a condition never fulfilled).

Marriage alliances and aftermath

The peace was sealed by dynastic marriages: Philip II married Henry II's daughter Elisabeth of Valois, and Emmanuel Philibert of Savoy married Henry's sister Margaret of France. During the tournament held in Paris to celebrate the treaty, Henry II was fatally wounded by a lance splinter on 30 June 1559 and died ten days later — an event that plunged France toward the Wars of Religion.

With its Italian ambitions ended, France turned inward and toward overseas expansion, while Spanish Habsburg predominance in Italy lasted until the War of the Spanish Succession in the early eighteenth century.
