Electromagnetic shielding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conductive enclosures block electromagnetic fields by reflection and absorption

In electrical engineering, electromagnetic shielding is the practice of reducing or redirecting the electromagnetic field in a space with barriers made of conductive or magnetic materials. It is used to isolate devices from their environment — preventing interference with sensitive equipment, protecting people and data, and keeping signals from escaping — and is central to electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) design.

Principles

A shield attenuates a field by three mechanisms: reflection of the wave at the surface (which requires mobile charge carriers, as in metals), absorption as the wave induces eddy currents that dissipate energy as heat (increasing with thickness and with the material's conductivity and magnetic permeability), and multiple internal reflections. Effectiveness is expressed in decibels and depends strongly on frequency: thin foils work well at radio frequencies, while low-frequency magnetic fields require high-permeability alloys such as mu-metal. In practice, seams, doors, cable penetrations and ventilation apertures — which must stay below a fraction of a wavelength, often using honeycomb vents and conductive gaskets — usually limit performance more than the shield material itself.

Applications

The classic example is the Faraday cage. Shielding is used in MRI scanner rooms, anechoic and EMC test chambers, shielded cables and connectors, microwave oven doors, TEMPEST-rated secure facilities, and the metal cans soldered over radio sections of circuit boards.
