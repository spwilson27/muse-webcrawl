Gaussian integral

The Gaussian integral, also known as the Euler–Poisson integral, is the integral of the Gaussian function f(x) = e^(−x²) over the entire real line: ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π. Despite the integrand having no elementary antiderivative, the definite integral can be evaluated exactly, and it underlies the normalisation of the normal distribution in probability theory.

Computation

The classical proof squares the integral and rewrites it as a double integral over the plane in polar coordinates: I² = ∫∫ e^(−(x²+y²)) dx dy = ∫₀^{2π} ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²) r dr dθ = π, hence I = √π. Variant methods use differentiation under the integral sign or the gamma function, since Γ(1/2) = √π.

General forms

More generally, ∫ e^(−ax²) dx = √(π/a), and the integral with a linear term gives √(π/a) e^(b²/4a). Multivariate Gaussian integrals produce determinants and appear throughout statistical mechanics, quantum field theory (path integrals) and signal processing.

History

Abraham de Moivre encountered the result in 1733 in work on the normal approximation to the binomial distribution; Laplace and Poisson later gave influential proofs.
