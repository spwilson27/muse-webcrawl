Bascinet

TypeCombat helmet
Periodc. 1300 – mid-15th century
RegionWestern Europe
FeaturesPointed skull, mail aventail, optional visor

The bascinet, also written bassinet or basinet, was a Medieval European open-faced military helmet that evolved from the small skull-cap worn under the great helm in the late 13th century. It was in use from the early 14th century until it was superseded by the sallet and armet in the mid-15th century.

Development

The early bascinet was a small, light steel cap, sometimes with a slightly pointed apex, worn alone or beneath a larger helm. Around the turn of the 14th century it lengthened to cover the sides and back of the head and neck. Attached to its lower edge by a system of staples (vervelles) was a curtain of mail, the aventail (or camail), protecting the throat and shoulders.

Visored forms

From the later 14th century, bascinets were fitted with hinged visors. The most famous is the "hounskull" or "pig-faced" bascinet, whose conical visor — pierced with breaths and sight slits — deflected blows and gave the wearer a distinctive snouted profile. Breath holes were usually placed on the right side, away from an opponent's lance. The "great bascinet" of the early 15th century added plate defences for the neck and was often worn in the tournament and on campaign.

Decline

By the middle of the 15th century the bascinet had largely fallen from battlefield use in western Europe, replaced by the sallet, armet, and barbute, although it survived longer in some regions and in effigies and art it remains an iconic image of the Hundred Years' War knight.
