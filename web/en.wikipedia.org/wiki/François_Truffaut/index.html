François Truffaut

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: François Truffaut on the set of Day for Night (1973), directing himself directing: the critic-turned-auteur in his element]

François Roland Truffaut (6 February 1932 – 21 October 1984) was a French film director, screenwriter, producer, actor and critic, a founder of the French New Wave. His first feature The 400 Blows (1959) — the semi-autobiographical story of Antoine Doinel, played by Jean-Pierre Léaud from age 12 across five films — won Best Director at Cannes and remains the New Wave's most beloved monument.

Truffaut's other films include Shoot the Piano Player (1960), Jules and Jim (1962), Fahrenheit 451 (1966), The Last Metro (1980) and Day for Night (1973), his love letter to filmmaking, which won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.

From delinquent to critic to director

An unhappy childhood of neglect and reform school; rescue by the critic André Bazin, who became his surrogate father; ferocious criticism at Cahiers du Cinéma — his 1954 essay 'A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema' fired the New Wave's first shot at the 'Tradition of Quality' — then The 400 Blows, dedicated to Bazin, who had died the night shooting began. The Doinel cycle followed Léaud through army, marriage and divorce, cinema's longest collaboration between a director and his alter ego.

The Hitchcock book and legacy

Truffaut's book-length interview Hitchcock/Truffaut (1966) recast Hitchcock in world opinion from entertainer to artist — the auteur theory in action. He played the scientist Lacombe in Spielberg's Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977). Brain cancer killed him at 52; French cinema treats him as its tutelary saint, the filmmaker as cinephile made flesh.
