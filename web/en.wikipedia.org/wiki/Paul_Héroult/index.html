Paul Louis-Toussaint Héroult (10 April 1863 – 9 May 1914) was a French scientist. He was the inventor of aluminium electrolysis and of the first successful commercial electric arc furnace. His name is known worldwide through the Hall–Héroult process, the electrolytic method by which nearly all of the world's primary aluminium is still produced.

Life

Héroult was born in Thury-Harcourt in Normandy. He read Henri Sainte-Claire Deville's book on aluminium as a schoolboy and became fascinated by the metal, which at the time was more expensive than silver because it could only be obtained by costly chemical reduction. He studied at the École des Mines in Paris, graduating in 1882, and began experimenting with electrolysis of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite in his father's tannery workshop.

The 1886 patent

On 23 April 1886, at the age of 23, Héroult filed a French patent for the electrolytic production of aluminium from alumina dissolved in molten cryolite. In the same year, independently and in the United States, Charles Martin Hall filed an almost identical patent. The coincidence of the two inventions led to the process bearing both names. Héroult's patent was initially neglected in France, and he turned to Switzerland, where the Aluminium Industrie Aktien Gesellschaft at Neuhausen, near the Rhine Falls, adopted his process in 1888.

Electric arc furnace

In 1900 Héroult developed the electric arc furnace for steel production, in which an electric arc between carbon electrodes melts scrap and refines steel. The Héroult furnace became a standard design in the steel industry and remains the basis of modern electric arc steelmaking.

Death and legacy

Héroult died in 1914 aboard a ship in the Mediterranean, aged 51. His electrolytic cell design — carbon anodes in a cryolite bath with a carbon-lined cathode — remains, in refined form, the universal technology of the aluminium industry.
