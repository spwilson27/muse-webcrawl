Aphelion

The aphelion is the point in the orbit of a planet, asteroid, or comet that is farthest from the Sun. For objects orbiting Earth, the corresponding term is apogee. The generic term is apoapsis.

Aphelion is the point of greatest separation in the orbit of an astronomical body from the Sun. The word comes from the Greek apò (away from) and hḕlios (sun). Its opposite is perihelion, the point of least separation. More generally, the extremes of distance in an orbit around any body are called apsides; for an arbitrary central body the terms are apoapsis and periapsis, and specialised terms exist for particular bodies, such as apogee and perigee for Earth.

Earth's aphelion

Earth reaches aphelion in early July, about two weeks after the June solstice, at a distance of roughly 152.1 million kilometres (1.017 astronomical units). At perihelion, in early January, Earth is about 147.1 million kilometres from the Sun. The roughly 3% difference in solar distance means Earth receives about 7% more solar radiation at perihelion than at aphelion; the seasons, however, are dominated by axial tilt rather than orbital distance.

Orbital mechanics

By Kepler's second law, a body moves slowest at aphelion. The aphelion distance is given by a(1 + e), where a is the semi-major axis and e the orbital eccentricity. The dates and exact distances of aphelion vary slowly over time because of planetary perturbations and the precession of the orbit.
