Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum

The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), alternatively the Eocene Thermal Maximum 1 (ETM1) and formerly the Late Paleocene Thermal Maximum, is a time period with a more than 5–8 °C global average temperature rise across the event. This climate event occurred at the boundary between the Paleocene and Eocene geological epochs, roughly 56 million years ago, and is associated with a rapid release of carbon into the ocean–atmosphere system.

Evidence and causes

The PETM is marked in marine and terrestrial sediments worldwide by a sharp negative carbon isotope excursion (CIE) of 2–5‰, indicating a massive input of isotopically light carbon. Proposed sources include dissociation of methane clathrates on the ocean floor, North Atlantic volcanism and the intrusions of the North Atlantic Igneous Province into organic-rich sediments, oxidation of peat and permafrost carbon, and some combination of these. The onset occurred over a few thousand years or less, and the whole event lasted approximately 200,000 years before carbon was re-sequestered.

Effects

Global temperatures rose 5–8 °C; the deep ocean warmed and acidified, causing the extinction of 30–50 per cent of benthic foraminifera. On land, mammals underwent rapid dwarfing and major faunal turnovers, the earliest true primates and even-toed and odd-toed ungulates appeared and spread across the northern continents, and plant communities shifted toward tropical floras at high latitudes — crocodilians and palms lived above the Arctic Circle.

Relevance to modern climate change

The PETM is frequently cited as the closest geological analogue to present-day anthropogenic carbon release, though current emissions rates appear to be faster than the inferred PETM onset rate — a point emphasised in studies of ocean acidification and carbon-cycle recovery.
