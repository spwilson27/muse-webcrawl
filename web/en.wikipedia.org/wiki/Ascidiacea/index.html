Ascidiacea, commonly known as the ascidians or sea squirts, is a class in the sac-like marine invertebrate filter feeders subphylum Tunicata. Ascidians are characterised by a "tunic" made of the cellulose-like carbohydrate tunicin. They are found all over the world in shallow seas, either as solitary animals or as colonies of genetically identical zooids. About 2,300 species are described.

Despite their simple adult form, ascidians are chordates: their free-swimming, tadpole-like larvae possess a notochord, dorsal nerve cord and tail. On settling, the larva loses these features, resorbing its tail and most of its nervous system as it metamorphoses into the sessile adult — an extreme case of retrogressive metamorphosis.

Description

An adult ascidian is essentially a filter-feeding barrel. Water enters an oral (branchial) siphon, passes through a perforated pharynx coated with mucus that traps plankton and organic particles, and exits through an atrial siphon. The body is enclosed in the tunic, which in some species contains spicules or blood vessels and may be brightly coloured. If disturbed, many species contract and squirt water from their siphons — hence the name "sea squirt".

Life cycle

Most ascidians are hermaphrodites. Solitary species broadcast eggs and sperm into the sea; the resulting larva swims for hours to days before settling on a hard surface. Colonial species reproduce both sexually and asexually by budding, forming mats, sheets or lobed colonies of zooids embedded in a common tunic; some, like Botryllus schlosseri, exhibit remarkable phenomena of colony fusion and rejection governed by a single genetic locus. The model species Ciona intestinalis and Ciona savignyi have sequenced genomes and are widely used in developmental biology.

Ecology and human relevance

Ascidians are important fouling organisms on ships, docks and aquaculture equipment, and several species have become invasive worldwide. Some are eaten, notably the sea pineapple (Halocynthia roretzi) in Korea and Japan and Microcosmus species in the Mediterranean. Their blood can concentrate the rare metal vanadium at levels millions of times higher than seawater, a long-standing physiological puzzle.
