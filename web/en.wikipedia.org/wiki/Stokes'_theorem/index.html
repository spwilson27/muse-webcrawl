Stokes' theorem

Stokes' theorem, also known as the Kelvin–Stokes theorem, is a fundamental result in vector calculus. It states that the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over a surface equals the line integral of the vector field around the boundary curve of that surface:

∬S (∇ × F) · dS = ∮∂S F · dr

History

The theorem was communicated by Lord Kelvin to George Gabriel Stokes in a letter of 1850 and appeared as a question on the Smith's Prize examination at Cambridge in 1854, from which its association with Stokes derives. It is a special case of the generalised Stokes theorem of differential geometry, which unifies the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the classical Stokes theorem and the divergence theorem.

Applications

Stokes' theorem is used throughout physics, notably in expressing Maxwell's equations: Faraday's law of induction and the Ampère–Maxwell law in integral form are direct applications, relating circulation of electric and magnetic fields to fluxes of their curls. It is also fundamental in fluid dynamics and in the theory of conservative fields, where a field with zero curl on a simply connected domain is the gradient of a potential.
