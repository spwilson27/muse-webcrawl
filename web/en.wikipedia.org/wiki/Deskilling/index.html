Deskilling is the process by which skilled labour within an industry or economy is eliminated by the introduction of technologies operated by semiskilled or unskilled workers, or by the fragmentation of crafts into simple, repetitive tasks. It is used largely in a pejorative sense, and describes the decline of skilled work and the relative reduction in the wages, autonomy and bargaining power of the previously skilled workforce.

Origins

Karl Marx argued that mechanisation under capitalism tended to degrade work by separating conception from execution. The thesis was revived and popularised by Harry Braverman in Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974), a landmark of labour process theory. Braverman argued that scientific management (Taylorism) and machinery were systematically used by management to strip workers of craft knowledge, transfer it to management's control of the labour process, and cheapen labour.

Debate

Critics of the deskilling thesis point out that technology also creates new skilled jobs (technicians, programmers, maintenance specialists) and that aggregate skill levels in many economies have risen — an "upskilling" trend driven by education and the growth of professional work. Others describe polarisation: growth at the high-skill and low-skill ends with a hollowing out of middle-skill routine work. Deskilling arguments have re-emerged in debates about artificial intelligence and its effects on professions such as law, medicine and programming.
