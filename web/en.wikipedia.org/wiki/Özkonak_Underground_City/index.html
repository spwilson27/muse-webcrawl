The Özkonak Underground City is an ancient multi-level underground city in the town of Özkonak, in Nevşehir Province, Turkey, in the historical region of Cappadocia. Like the better-known complexes at Derinkuyu and Kaymaklı, it was carved into the soft volcanic tuff of the region and served as a refuge where local populations could hide from invaders for extended periods.

Description

The city was discovered in 1972 by a local farmer, Latif Acar, who noticed water disappearing into the ground. Only a portion of the complex has been cleared and opened to visitors: a single main level (with indications of up to ten levels in total, only four of which are accessible) containing stables, wineries, storage rooms, kitchens, living quarters, and a burial area. A ventilation system of long shafts connects the levels, and a distinctive feature of Özkonak is its system of small holes above the tunnels, believed to have been used to pour hot oil on enemies below — a defensive system not found in the other Cappadocian underground cities.

Passageways could be sealed from the inside with large rolling-stone doors, and narrow tunnels forced attackers to proceed one at a time in a stooped position.

History

Cappadocia's underground cities may have begun with the Phrygians or even the Bronze Age Hittites, and were enlarged under Byzantine rule, when Christian communities used them to shelter from Arab raids between the 7th and 11th centuries, and later from Seljuk and Mongol incursions. Özkonak is thought to have accommodated several thousand people at its peak.
