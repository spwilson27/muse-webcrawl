Tonic sol-fa

Tonic sol-fa
OriginEngland, 19th century
Developed byJohn Curwen (building on Sarah Ann Glover's system)
PurposeSight-singing and music education notation

Tonic sol-fa (or tonic sol-fah) is a pedagogical technique for teaching sight-singing, invented by Sarah Ann Glover of Norwich, England, and popularised by John Curwen. It uses a movable "do" system in which the syllables doh, ray, me, fah, soh, lah, te name the degrees of the major scale relative to the tonic, allowing music to be sung at sight without reference to absolute pitch.

Notation

In Curwen's system, music is written with the initial letters of the syllables (d, r, m, f, s, l, t) arranged in columns, with punctuation marks indicating rhythm: bar lines as vertical strokes, colons dividing beats, and dots and dashes for subdivisions and sustained notes. Octaves are shown by superscript or subscript marks, and chromatic alterations change the vowel (e.g. de for raised doh, te becoming ta when flattened). Curwen also adapted hand signs for each syllable, later incorporated into the Kodály method.

Use and legacy

Tonic sol-fa spread rapidly through British Sunday schools, choral societies, and mission schools in the 19th century, and became deeply rooted in the choral traditions of Wales and South Africa, where sol-fa notation is still widely used for hymnody and choral music. Many British hymnals were printed with the tunes in both staff and sol-fa notation.
