Nucleic acid hybridization

A labelled probe hybridizes to its complementary sequence in a target sample.

Nucleic acid hybridization is a fundamental technique in molecular biology in which single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules anneal to complementary sequences through base pairing. Because a strand will bind stably only to a (nearly) exact complement, hybridization allows researchers to detect, locate and quantify specific sequences within complex mixtures of nucleic acids.

Principle

Double-stranded DNA can be denatured ("melted") into single strands by heat or alkali, and the strands will re-anneal when conditions permit. The temperature and salt conditions — the stringency — determine how perfect a match must be: high stringency permits only near-exact matches. A short, labelled fragment of known sequence, called a probe, is used to reveal where its complement occurs.

Applications

Hybridization underlies a family of classic and modern methods. In Southern and Northern blotting, separated DNA or RNA fragments immobilised on a membrane are probed to identify specific sequences. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) uses fluorescent probes to locate sequences on chromosomes or in tissue sections. DNA microarrays hybridize samples to thousands of immobilised probes at once to profile gene expression, and hybridization capture is used to enrich target regions before DNA sequencing. Historically, DNA–DNA hybridization between whole genomes was also used to estimate relatedness between species.
