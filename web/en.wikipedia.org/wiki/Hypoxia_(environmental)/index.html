Hypoxia (environmental)

Hypoxia
DefinitionDissolved oxygen below ~2 mg/L in water
Main causesEutrophication, stratification, respiration of organic matter
EffectsFish kills, "dead zones", ecosystem shifts

Hypoxia, in environmental science, is a condition in which dissolved oxygen in a body of water falls to levels too low to support most aquatic life — conventionally below about 2 milligrams per litre. Severely hypoxic areas are often called dead zones. The extreme case, with essentially no oxygen, is anoxia.

Causes

The most common driver is eutrophication: excess nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser runoff, sewage, and atmospheric deposition fuel algal blooms. When the algae die and sink, bacterial decomposition consumes oxygen faster than it can be replenished, especially where thermal or salinity stratification prevents mixing with surface water.

Notable dead zones

Large seasonal dead zones recur in the northern Gulf of Mexico at the mouth of the Mississippi River, in the Baltic Sea — among the largest hypoxic areas in the world — and in the Chesapeake Bay, as well as off the coasts of China and in many estuaries worldwide. The number of reported coastal dead zones has grown steadily since the mid-20th century.

Ecological effects

Mobile animals flee hypoxic water, but sessile organisms such as mussels and worms suffocate. Repeated hypoxia restructures food webs, favours jellyfish and microbes, and can cause mass fish kills when oxygen-depleted water is suddenly mixed upward. Climate warming worsens the problem by reducing oxygen solubility and strengthening stratification, and oxygen loss is also expanding in the open ocean's oxygen minimum zones.
