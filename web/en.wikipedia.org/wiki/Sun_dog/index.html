Sun dog

A sun dog (scientific name parhelion, plural parhelia, from Greek παρήλιον, "beside the sun") is an atmospheric optical phenomenon that consists of a bright spot to one or both sides of the Sun. Two sun dogs often flank the Sun within a 22° halo. The sun dog is a member of the family of halos caused by the refraction of sunlight by ice crystals in the atmosphere. Sun dogs typically appear as a pair of subtly coloured patches of light, around 22° to the left and right of the Sun, and at the same altitude above the horizon as the Sun.

Formation

Sun dogs are formed by hexagonal plate-shaped ice crystals drifting in high, cold cirrus or cirrostratus clouds — or, in very cold weather, by low-level ice crystals known as diamond dust. The crystals act as prisms: light entering one face and exiting another is bent by a minimum of 22°. Because plate crystals fall with their flat faces nearly horizontal, the refraction is concentrated horizontally, producing the two bright spots. Refraction disperses the light slightly, so the edge nearest the Sun is reddish and the outer edge bluish, though when the Sun is higher the colours can blend to a bright white.

Appearance and occurrence

Sun dogs are best visible when the Sun is low on the horizon, at which point they sit on the 22° halo; as the Sun climbs, the parhelia move outward beyond 22° and fade. They can occur anywhere in the world and in any season where suitable ice clouds are present, but are most striking in the winter half-year at higher latitudes. The corresponding phenomenon beside the Moon is a moon dog (paraselene), which is rarer and fainter.

History

Parhelia have been recorded since antiquity: Aristotle mentions "two mock suns" in his Meteorology, and Cicero and Pliny describe them in Roman times. One of the most famous depictions is the Vädersolstavlan ("Sun Dog Painting") of 1535, showing halos and sun dogs over Stockholm — the earliest known colour depiction of the phenomenon. René Descartes analysed halos and parhelia in Les Météores (1637), and the elaborate display over Rome in 1629 was documented by Christoph Scheiner. In folklore, sun dogs were read as omens — the three suns seen before the Battle of Mortimer's Cross (1461) were reputedly taken by the future Edward IV as a sign of the Holy Trinity and later inspired the "sun in splendour" badge.
