Spinor

In geometry and physics, spinors are elements of a complex vector space that can be associated with Euclidean space. Like geometric vectors and more general tensors, spinors transform in a certain way when the coordinate system is rotated. However, unlike geometric vectors and tensors, a spinor transforms to its negative when the space is rotated through a complete turn from 0° to 360°; it takes a rotation of 720° for a spinor to return to its original value.

Introduction

Spinors were introduced by Élie Cartan in 1913 and later found to be essential to quantum mechanics, where they describe the state of particles with spin, such as electrons. In the Dirac equation, the wavefunction of a relativistic electron is a four-component spinor. The requirement that spinors change sign under a 360° rotation corresponds physically to the fact that particles of half-integer spin, called fermions, obey Fermi–Dirac statistics.

Mathematical structure

Spinors are constructed as elements of representations of the spin group, which is the double cover of the rotation group. In three dimensions, the relevant group is the special unitary group SU(2), which double-covers SO(3). The simplest spinors in three dimensions have two complex components and transform under rotations via 2×2 unitary matrices of determinant one.

More generally, spinors are tied to the Clifford algebra of a quadratic form on the underlying vector space. In spaces of arbitrary dimension and signature, spinor representations can be periodic in dimension, a phenomenon reflected in the theory of Clifford algebras known as Bott periodicity.

Applications

Spinors appear throughout theoretical physics: in the relativistic quantum theory of the electron, in the Standard Model of particle physics, in supersymmetry, and in differential geometry, where spin manifolds admit spinor fields used to define the Dirac operator. The Dirac equation remains the canonical equation of motion for spin-½ particles.
