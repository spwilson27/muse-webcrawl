Salesmanship

Salesmanship is the art and practice of persuading a prospective customer to buy goods or services. It encompasses the interpersonal skills, product knowledge and persuasive techniques used by salespeople in presenting an offer, handling objections and closing a sale. The term came into wide use in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, as mass production created a need for people who could move large volumes of manufactured goods.

History

Peddlers and drummers of the 19th century gave way to professionalised selling in the early 1900s, when companies such as National Cash Register introduced formal sales training, scripts and conventions. Dale Carnegie's How to Win Friends and Influence People (1936) popularised interpersonal techniques for salespeople. After the Second World War, "high-pressure" tactics fell into disrepute, and methods shifted toward needs-based, consultative and relationship-oriented approaches. Academic study of selling grew within marketing departments, examining buyer behaviour, negotiation and personal selling's role in the marketing mix.

Techniques

Classic models include AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action) and feature–advantage–benefit presentation. Common elements of the sales process are prospecting, approach, needs assessment, presentation, objection handling, closing and follow-up. Modern salesmanship emphasises listening, trust-building and problem solving over pressure; research consistently associates customer-oriented selling with long-term performance. Ethics in salesmanship concerns honesty in claims, fair treatment of competitors and the avoidance of manipulative techniques.
