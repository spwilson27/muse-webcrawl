Shoeshine (1946 film)

Shoeshine (Italian: Sciuscià, pronounced [ʃuʃˈʃa]) is a 1946 Italian film directed by Vittorio De Sica. One of the first films of Italian neorealism, it tells the story of two shoeshine boys in post-war Rome whose friendship is destroyed by the juvenile justice system. The film received an Academy Honorary Award in 1948, an award created specially for it that became the forerunner of the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. The title derives from the Neapolitan pronunciation of the English "shoeshine", the occupation of boys who polished the boots of American soldiers.

Plot

Giuseppe Filippucci and Pasquale Maggi, two friends who shine shoes for American GIs in Rome, save their earnings to buy a horse, Bersagliere, which they board at a stable. Drawn into selling stolen American blankets by Giuseppe's older brother, the boys are arrested and sent to a juvenile prison. Manipulated by the authorities into informing against each other, the boys' friendship collapses amid prison violence; Giuseppe dies during an escape attempt staged amid a fire, and Pasquale, discovering his friend's body, weeps over him as the horse wanders away.

Production and reception

De Sica cast non-professional child actors — Franco Interlenghi and Rinaldo Smordoni were themselves shoeshine boys — and shot in real Roman locations and a reconstructed prison. A companion film to Rossellini's Rome, Open City (1945), Shoeshine was a major international success; Orson Welles praised it extravagantly, remarking that in De Sica's hands "the camera has disappeared" and only life remained. Its honorary Oscar citation praised "the high quality of this motion picture, brought to eloquent life in a country scarred by war".
