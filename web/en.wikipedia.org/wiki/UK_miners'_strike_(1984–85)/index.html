UK miners' strike (1984–85)

The UK miners' strike of 1984–85 was a major industrial action affecting the British coal industry. It was led by the National Union of Mineworkers (NUM), under its president Arthur Scargill, against the National Coal Board's plans to close a number of pits, which the union argued would destroy mining communities. The Conservative government of Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher prepared extensively for the confrontation, and the strike became one of the defining episodes of her premiership.

Background

British coal mining had been in long decline, and previous miners' strikes in 1972 and 1974 had won substantial victories for the NUM, contributing to the fall of Edward Heath's government in 1974. The Thatcher government, elected in 1979, drew up plans to reduce the industry and sought to avoid repeating the earlier defeats, building up coal stocks and coordinating police responses. In March 1984, the Coal Board announced the closure of Cortonwood colliery in Yorkshire, and strike action spread.

The strike

The NUM called a national strike without holding a national ballot, a decision that proved controversial and led many miners in Nottinghamshire to keep working. The strike lasted a full year and involved mass picketing, including the confrontation between thousands of pickets and police at the Orgreave coking plant on 18 June 1984. The government used roadblocks, mass arrests, and the courts, which sequestered the NUM's funds. Mining families endured severe hardship but received support from solidarity groups across the country and abroad, including the Lesbians and Gays Support the Miners campaign.

Outcome and legacy

By early 1985 the number of miners returning to work had grown, and on 3 March 1985 the NUM voted narrowly to end the strike without an agreement. In the years that followed, most of Britain's deep coal mines were closed and the industry contracted to a fraction of its former size, devastating many mining communities. The strike remains a landmark in modern British history, studied for its effects on trade union power, policing, and the politics of deindustrialization.
