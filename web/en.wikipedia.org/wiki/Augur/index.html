Augur

An augur was a priest of ancient Rome who practised augury, interpreting the will of the gods by studying the flight, calls, and feeding of birds. Augurs belonged to one of the four great colleges of priests and held office for life. No major public business — elections, legislation, or war — was supposed to be undertaken without taking the auspices, and unfavourable signs could suspend public proceedings.

The augural discipline

The augur marked out a templum, a consecrated region of the sky, and watched for signs within it. Birds seen on the left (east) were generally favourable in Roman practice, while those on the right were unfavourable — the reverse of Greek custom. Augury also included signs from quadrupeds, celestial phenomena, and the sacred chickens whose appetite before battle was eagerly watched by generals; the consul Publius Claudius Pulcher, who threw the chickens into the sea before the Battle of Drepana (249 BC) when they refused to eat, was said to have lost the fleet for his impiety.

Political role

Augurs formed a self-electing college whose members included leading senators; Cicero and Mark Antony both held the office. Because adverse auspices could obstruct legislation — as when Bibulus attempted to block Caesar's laws in 59 BC by "watching the sky" — augury became an instrument of senatorial politics as well as religion. The emperor eventually became chief augur, and the institution faded with the Christianisation of the empire.

The lituus and vocabulary

The augur's distinctive instrument was the lituus, a curved wand shown on coins and monuments. Latin augural vocabulary survives in modern words such as "inaugurate" and "auspicious" (from auspicium, watching birds).
