Maxwell's equations

Maxwell's equations, published by James Clerk Maxwell in the 1860s, are a set of four partial differential equations that form the foundation of classical electromagnetism. Together with the Lorentz force law, they describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated by charges and currents and how they propagate.

The four equations

∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss's law)
∇·B = 0 (Gauss's law for magnetism)
∇×E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday's law)
∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t (Ampère–Maxwell law)

Gauss's law relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge. Gauss's law for magnetism states that there are no magnetic monopoles. Faraday's law describes how a changing magnetic field induces an electric field, and the Ampère–Maxwell law describes how currents and changing electric fields produce magnetic fields.

Electromagnetic waves

In free space the equations predict self-propagating waves of oscillating electric and magnetic fields travelling at the speed of light, c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). Maxwell identified light itself as an electromagnetic wave, unifying optics with electricity and magnetism.

Significance

Maxwell's equations underlie technologies from radio and radar to power generation. Their invariance under Lorentz transformations was a key motivation for Einstein's special theory of relativity, and they remain exact in the classical limit of quantum electrodynamics.
