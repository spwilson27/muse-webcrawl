Jōdo-shū (Japanese: 浄土宗, "Pure Land School") is the main school of Japanese Pure Land Buddhism, founded by the monk Hōnen in 1175. It teaches that in the present degenerate age (mappō), beings cannot attain liberation by their own efforts, but may be born in the Pure Land of the Buddha Amitābha (Amida) by relying on his Primal Vow and reciting his name — the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu".

Hōnen and the Senchakushū

Hōnen, ordained on Mount Hiei in the Tendai school, concluded after years of study that the nembutsu was the practice selected by Amida for the salvation of ordinary people. In 1175 he began teaching openly in Kyoto, attracting disciples from all classes, including women and outcasts excluded from elite religious institutions. His treatise Senchaku hongan nembutsushū (1198) systematically argued that the nembutsu alone should be practised (senchaku, "selection"), a teaching that provoked backlash: in 1207 his school was suppressed, disciples executed, and Hōnen exiled, though he was pardoned in 1211, shortly before his death.

Doctrine and practice

Jōdo-shū emphasises "other-power" (tariki) — salvation through Amida's grace rather than self-power (jiriki). Hōnen nevertheless encouraged sustained, lifelong recitation and ethical conduct, and his followers codified the "five right practices", headed by chanting the nembutsu. The goal is birth in the Pure Land at death, from which enlightenment is assured. Major branch temples include Chion-in in Kyoto, the school's head temple, and Zōjō-ji in Tokyo, mausoleum of several Tokugawa shoguns.

Branches and relations

Hōnen's disciple Shinran founded the related but distinct Jōdo Shinshū, now the largest Pure Land school, which dispensed with monastic discipline. Other disciples developed the Chinzei and Seizan branches of Jōdo-shū itself. Today Jōdo-shū counts some seven thousand temples in Japan and maintains missions abroad.
