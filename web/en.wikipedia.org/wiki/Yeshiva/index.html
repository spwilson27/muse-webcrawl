A yeshiva (Hebrew: ישיבה, lit. "sitting"; pl. yeshivot) is a traditional Jewish educational institution focused on the study of Rabbinic literature, primarily the Talmud and the Torah. Study takes place in a hall called a beit midrash, and much of the learning is done in pairs known as chavruta, in which two students argue and analyse a text together.

History

The institution descends from the academies of late antiquity, including the great Babylonian yeshivas of Sura and Pumbedita, which produced the Babylonian Talmud. In medieval and early modern Europe, yeshivas flourished in Spain, France, Germany and Poland-Lithuania. The modern yeshiva system was reshaped by the Volozhin Yeshiva (founded 1803), the "mother of yeshivas", whose model of an independent, full-time residential academy for advanced Talmud study was copied across Lithuania and beyond.

After the Holocaust destroyed the yeshiva world of Eastern Europe, its centres were re-established in Israel — notably Ponevezh, Hebron, Slabodka and Mir — and in the United States, where Beth Medrash Govoha in Lakewood, New Jersey, became one of the largest yeshivas in the world. The Mir Yeshiva in Jerusalem is among the largest in Israel.

Structure of study

The yeshiva day is divided into sessions (sedarim) of paired study, interspersed with lectures (shiurim) given by the rosh yeshiva or senior teachers. The curriculum centres on Talmud study with the commentaries of Rashi and the Tosafists, supplemented by halakha (Jewish law), mussar (ethics) in the Lithuanian tradition, or Hasidic thought in Hasidic yeshivas. Students typically enter in their teens, after a mesivta (high-school yeshiva), and may continue into a kollel, a yeshiva for married men.

Women's yeshivas

Although traditional yeshivas are male institutions, parallel advanced Torah academies for women — often called midrashot (singular midrasha) — have developed since the 20th century, particularly in Israel, offering intensive textual study programmes.
