An electrical grid (or electricity network) is an interconnected network for delivering electricity from producers to consumers. It consists of power stations that generate electrical power, high-voltage transmission lines that carry power over long distances, and distribution lines that deliver it to final users. Often called the largest machine in the world, a grid must balance supply and demand continuously, because electricity is consumed essentially the moment it is generated.

Structure and operation

Generators produce power at moderate voltages, which transformers step up (to 220–765 kV or higher) for efficient bulk transmission, then step down through substations for distribution and final use. Within a synchronous grid all generators rotate in lock-step at the system frequency — 50 Hz in most of the world, 60 Hz in the Americas and parts of Asia — and grid operators dispatch generation, manage reserves and trade power in electricity markets to keep frequency and voltage within limits. The world's largest synchronous areas include the Continental Europe grid and the grids of China, India and North America's Eastern and Western Interconnections; asynchronous regions are linked by high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) converters.

Reliability and change

Grids are designed to survive the loss of any single major component (the N-1 criterion), but cascading failures have caused historic blackouts, such as the 2003 Northeast blackout in North America and the 2003 Italian blackout. Ageing infrastructure, extreme weather, cyber threats, and the rapid growth of variable wind and solar generation, batteries, electric vehicles and demand response are driving investment in smarter, more flexible grids. Microgrids and islanding allow parts of the network to operate independently during disturbances.
