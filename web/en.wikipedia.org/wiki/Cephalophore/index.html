Cephalophore

[Image: Statue of Saint Denis carrying his own head, Notre-Dame de Paris]

A cephalophore (from the Greek κεφαλή kephalḗ, "head", and φορεύς phoreús, "bearer") is a saint depicted carrying their own severed head. Cephalophory is the act, in hagiography, of a martyr picking up their head after decapitation and carrying it, often while continuing to preach. The motif is especially common in French medieval tradition; more than a hundred cephalophore saints are known.

Famous cephalophores

The most famous is Saint Denis of Paris, patron saint of France, who according to legend was beheaded on Montmartre in the 3rd century, then picked up his head and walked several kilometres north, preaching a sermon the whole way, to the site of the present Basilica of Saint-Denis. Other well-known examples include Saint Valerie of Limoges, Saint Nicaise of Reims, Saint Austremoine, Saint Solange, Saint Alban (in some traditions), and Saint Osyth. In Welsh tradition, Saint Winefride was said to have been beheaded and restored to life by Saint Beuno.

In art

Cephalophores are a striking feature of Gothic sculpture, notably on the portals of Notre-Dame de Paris and the Basilica of Saint-Denis, where the head-carrying figure makes the saint instantly identifiable. The image has also inspired modern writers and folklorists as an emblem of speech surviving death and of sanctity persisting beyond martyrdom.
