Julian of Norwich (c. 1343 – after 1416), also known as Juliana of Norwich or Dame Julian, was an English anchoress and mystic. Her writings, now known as Revelations of Divine Love, are the earliest surviving English-language works known to have been written by a woman.

Life

Little is known of Julian's early life, including her real name. In May 1373, at the age of thirty and apparently near death from a serious illness, she received a series of sixteen visions or "showings" of the Passion of Christ and the Virgin Mary. She recovered and later became an anchoress — a religious recluse enclosed for life in a cell attached to the church of St Julian in Norwich, from which she probably took her name. From her cell's window she gave spiritual counsel to visitors; the mystic Margery Kempe recorded visiting her around 1413.

Revelations of Divine Love

Julian wrote two accounts of her visions: a short text soon after 1373, and a much longer, theologically developed version some twenty years later. Her theology is noted for its optimism and its vision of God as revealed in love rather than wrath. She described God as both Father and Mother, and famously wrote:

"All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well."

She also described seeing God in a "point", and the whole of creation as a ball the size of a hazelnut held in the palm of her hand, remarking that "it lasts and ever shall, for God loves it."

Veneration

Julian is venerated in the Anglican Communion and in Lutheran churches, and is commemorated in the Roman Catholic Church. Her cell at St Julian's Church, destroyed in the Second World War, was rebuilt, and the Julian Centre in Norwich preserves her legacy. The theologian Thomas Merton called her one of the greatest English mystics.
