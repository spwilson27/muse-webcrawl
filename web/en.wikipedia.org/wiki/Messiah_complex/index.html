Messiah complex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The messiah complex is a mental state in which a person believes they are a messiah or prophet and will save or redeem others or humanity. The term saviour complex is also used, especially in everyday speech, for a person who feels responsible for saving or helping others even at cost to themselves. It is not listed as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-5, but messianic beliefs can appear as a form of grandiose delusion, in religious delusions in psychosis, or in mania, and the term is frequently used figuratively in politics and popular culture.

Characteristics

Affected individuals may believe they are chosen by God or destiny to perform a world-changing mission: to save humanity, found a new religion, heal the sick, or redeem a community. In clinical settings such beliefs are usually classified under grandiose or religious delusions, which occur in schizophrenia, bipolar disorder in manic phases, and some other psychotic disorders. Famous historical cases discussed in the literature include people who identified themselves as Jesus Christ in asylums, the subject of Milton Rokeach's 1964 study The Three Christs of Ypsilanti.

Related concepts

The messiah complex is related to Jerusalem syndrome, a group of phenomena involving religiously themed obsessive ideas or delusions triggered by a visit to the city of Jerusalem. It differs from the "white saviour" concept used in social criticism, and from the god complex, an unshakable belief in one's own infallibility and privilege that is more closely associated with narcissistic personality disorder. In a milder, non-clinical sense, "saviour complex" describes helpers — including some counsellors, teachers, and activists — who compulsively rescue others.

In popular culture

The archetype appears widely in literature and film, from Dostoevsky's characters to modern treatments of cult leaders. The term is routinely applied, often pejoratively, to politicians, entrepreneurs, and celebrities perceived as presenting themselves as indispensable saviours.
