Animal testing, also known as animal experimentation or animal research, is the use of non-human animals in experiments that seek to control the variables affecting behaviour or biological systems under study. It is conducted in universities, medical schools, pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies, farms, and government laboratories for purposes including basic biological research, developing and testing medicines and vaccines, safety-testing chemicals and consumer products, and education.

Scale and species

Estimates of global use range from roughly 50 million to over 190 million animals per year, depending on which species are counted; vertebrates such as mice and rats make up the vast majority (over 90% in most national statistics), followed by fish and birds. A much smaller number are rabbits, guinea pigs, farm animals, dogs, cats, and non-human primates, the last used mainly in neuroscience and infectious-disease research. Most laboratory rodents are purpose-bred, and genetically modified mice have become a central tool of biomedical research.

History

Dissection and experimentation on animals date to ancient Greek physicians such as Galen. Modern experimental physiology was built on vivisection by figures like William Harvey (blood circulation, 1628) and Claude Bernard in the 19th century. Animal experiments contributed to landmark advances including insulin's isolation in dogs (1921–22), the polio vaccines, antibiotics, organ transplantation, and monoclonal antibodies. The practice has been contested since the 19th century; the modern animal-protection movement drew energy from undercover exposés such as the 1981 Silver Spring monkeys case.

Regulation and the Three Rs

Most research nations regulate animal use through licensing, ethics committees, and inspections — for example the EU Directive 2010/63 and, in the US, the Animal Welfare Act (which excludes purpose-bred rats, mice, and birds). The guiding framework is the "Three Rs" proposed by William Russell and Rex Burch in 1959: Replacement of animals with alternatives (cell cultures, organoids, computer models), Reduction of numbers, and Refinement of procedures to minimise suffering. The European Union banned testing of cosmetics on animals (2004–2009) and the marketing of animal-tested cosmetics (2013); similar bans exist in a number of other jurisdictions.

Debate

Supporters argue animal research remains indispensable for understanding whole-organism biology and for legally required safety testing, and point to benefits for both human and veterinary medicine. Critics argue the practice is ethically unjustifiable given animal sentience, and question its predictive value for human outcomes, citing high failure rates in drug development. Surveys generally find public support conditional on medical purpose, absence of alternatives, and minimisation of suffering.
