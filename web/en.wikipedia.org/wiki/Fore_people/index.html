Fore people

Region: Okapa District, Eastern Highlands, Papua New Guinea
Population: ~20,000 (historically)
Language: Fore (Trans–New Guinea)
Known for: Kuru epidemic

The Fore people are an indigenous group inhabiting the Okapa District of the Eastern Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea. They are a horticultural people whose traditional subsistence centres on sweet potato gardens, supplemented by hunting and, later, coffee growing.

Kuru

The Fore became internationally known through kuru, a fatal neurodegenerative disease that reached epidemic proportions among them in the mid-20th century, killing more than 2,700 people, predominantly women and children. Research by Daniel Carleton Gajdusek and others linked kuru to the Fore practice of ritual endocannibalism — the consumption of deceased relatives as a mortuary rite, in which women and children disproportionately consumed brain tissue. Kuru proved to be a prion disease, transmitted through infected tissue; Gajdusek received the Nobel Prize in 1976 for the work. After the practice ceased in the late 1950s under Australian colonial administration, the epidemic slowly declined, though long incubation periods meant cases continued for decades.

Genetic legacy

Studies of Fore survivors have identified a protective variant of the prion protein gene (G127V) among people exposed to kuru, interpreted as evidence of strong recent natural selection — a widely cited example of human evolution in response to disease.
