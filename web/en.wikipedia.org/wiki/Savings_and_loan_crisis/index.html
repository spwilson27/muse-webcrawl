Savings and loan crisis

The savings and loan crisis of the 1980s and 1990s (commonly dubbed the S&L crisis) was the failure of 1,043 out of the 3,234 savings and loan associations in the United States between 1986 and 1995. A Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation (FSLIC) insured "savings and loan" (or "thrift") institution is a financial institution that accepts savings deposits and makes mortgage, car and other personal loans to individual members<ref> — in practice, thrifts were the backbone of American home mortgage lending in the postwar decades.

Causes

Thrifts were caught in a structural trap: they held long-term, fixed-rate mortgages funded by short-term deposits. When the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker raised interest rates sharply in 1979–1981 to fight inflation, the market value of thrift mortgage portfolios collapsed while their cost of funds soared. Deregulation — notably the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 and the Garn–St Germain Act of 1982 — removed interest-rate ceilings and broadened thrifts' investment powers, allowing them to gamble on commercial real estate, junk bonds and direct equity investments to recover. Many did so recklessly; some, such as Charles Keating's Lincoln Savings and Loan, engaged in outright fraud.

Resolution

The FSLIC itself became insolvent, and in 1989 Congress passed the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (FIRREA), which abolished the FSLIC and the Federal Home Loan Bank Board, created the Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC) to close failed thrifts and sell their assets, and moved deposit insurance to the FDIC. The RTC closed or resolved 747 institutions holding some $394 billion in assets before completing its work in 1995.

Cost and aftermath

The total cost of the crisis is generally estimated at about $160 billion, of which roughly $124 billion was borne directly by taxpayers. The scandal had political aftershocks, including the "Keating Five" affair involving five U.S. senators. Regulatory responses included stricter capital requirements for thrifts and risk-based deposit insurance premiums, and the episode remains a standard case study in how deposit insurance plus deregulation can produce moral hazard.
