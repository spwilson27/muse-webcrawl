Selection bias

Selection bias is the bias introduced when the members of a population selected for study are not representative of the population intended to be analysed, so that the sample is systematically different in ways relevant to the conclusions. It arises in statistical sampling, clinical trials, epidemiology, survey research and machine learning whenever the process of selecting individuals, groups or data for analysis depends on variables related to the outcome under study.

Types

Sampling bias occurs when some members of the population are more likely to be included in the sample than others — for example, a telephone poll that systematically misses people without phones. Survivorship bias arises when only surviving examples are considered, as in assessing the armour needed by returning aircraft from the places where the survivors were not hit. Berkson's paradox appears when a study samples from a subpopulation defined by a collider variable, such as studying diseases only among hospital patients, creating spurious correlations. Other forms include attrition bias (differential dropout from a trial), volunteer or self-selection bias, and publication bias, in which positive results are more likely to be published.

Examples

The famous failure of the Literary Digest poll of 1936, which predicted a landslide defeat for Franklin D. Roosevelt based on millions of responses from car owners and telephone subscribers, is a textbook case: the sampling frame over-represented wealthier voters. In medicine, studies recruited from specialist clinics can overestimate the severity or change the apparent associations of a disease compared with the general population. In observational astronomy and economics, effects such as Malmquist bias and the "healthy worker effect" are domain-specific forms of selection bias.

Mitigation

The primary defence is sound design: defining the target population clearly, using probability sampling where possible, minimising and documenting non-response and attrition, and adjusting analyses with weighting or sensitivity analyses. In causal inference, directed acyclic graphs are used to identify and avoid conditioning on colliders that induce selection bias.
