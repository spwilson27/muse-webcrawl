The Palmer Raids were a series of raids conducted in November 1919 and January 1920 by the United States Department of Justice under the administration of President Woodrow Wilson to capture and arrest suspected socialists, especially anarchists and communists, and deport them from the United States. The raids are named for Alexander Mitchell Palmer, the United States Attorney General. During the First Red Scare, fear of communist revolution — inflamed by the Russian Revolution of 1917 and a series of anarchist bombings, including one that damaged Palmer's own home in June 1919 — drove the government to act against radical organisations.

Background

In April 1919 authorities intercepted a series of mail bombs addressed to prominent officials and businessmen; on 2 June 1919 bombs exploded in eight cities, one outside Palmer's Washington home, killing the bomber, the Italian anarchist Carlo Valdinoci. Palmer obtained funding from Congress to combat radicalism and appointed the young J. Edgar Hoover to head the Bureau of Investigation's new General Intelligence Division, which compiled files on some 200,000 individuals and organisations.

The raids

On 7 November 1919, the second anniversary of the Bolshevik Revolution, federal agents raided the Union of Russian Workers in a dozen cities, arresting hundreds. On 2 January 1920 the largest operation swept more than 30 cities and towns in 23 states: at least 3,000 people were arrested, and many more were held incommunicado, denied counsel, or beaten. Targets included members of the newly formed Communist Party of America and Communist Labor Party. In Boston alone nearly 1,000 detainees were held in an improvised facility on Deer Island.

Deportations

Under the Immigration Act of 1918, non-citizens who advocated anarchism or belonged to proscribed organisations could be deported. In December 1919 the transport USAT Buford — dubbed the "Soviet Ark" — carried 249 deportees, including the anarchists Emma Goldman and Alexander Berkman, to Soviet Russia. In total roughly 550 people were deported under the raids.

Criticism and aftermath

Assistant Secretary of Labor Louis Freeland Post, who reviewed deportation cases, cancelled more than 1,500 warrants for lack of evidence. Palmer's prediction of a massive May Day 1920 uprising failed to materialise, badly damaging his credibility and his bid for the Democratic presidential nomination. A May 1920 report by twelve prominent lawyers, including future Supreme Court justice Felix Frankfurter, charged the Justice Department with widespread illegal acts. The raids became a lasting symbol of government overreach during a period of national panic, and the episode contributed to the founding of the American Civil Liberties Union in 1920.
