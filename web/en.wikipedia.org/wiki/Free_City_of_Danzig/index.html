The Free City of Danzig (German: Freie Stadt Danzig; Polish: Wolne Miasto Gdańsk) was a semi-autonomous city-state on the Baltic coast that existed between 1920 and 1939, created by the Treaty of Versailles and placed under the protection of the League of Nations. It comprised the port city of Danzig (present-day Gdańsk, Poland) and its surrounding district, separated from Germany so that the reborn Polish state would have access to the sea.

The Long Market and Motława waterfront of Danzig, the Free City's commercial heart.

Origins

Article 100–108 of the Treaty of Versailles detached Danzig from Germany and made it a free city, neither German nor Polish, though Poland conducted its foreign relations, ran its railways and maintained a military depot on the Westerplatte peninsula. A League High Commissioner supervised disputes between the city and Poland. The arrangement satisfied neither side and made Danzig one of the most persistent flashpoints of interwar Europe.

Politics

The Free City had its own constitution, parliament (the Volkstag) and senate. Its population was overwhelmingly German-speaking, and from 1933 the local Nazi Party under Albert Forster and Arthur Greiser progressively took control, aligning the city with Adolf Hitler's Germany and persecuting its small Jewish community. Danzig's status became one of Nazi Germany's central demands in 1939: the "return" of Danzig and an extraterritorial corridor across the Polish Corridor.

End

On 1 September 1939 the German battleship Schleswig-Holstein opened fire on the Polish garrison at Westerplatte, beginning the Second World War. The same day, Germany annexed the Free City. Its Polish post office defenders and other Poles were murdered or deported. After the war the city became Polish Gdańsk, and its German population was expelled westward.
