Korean independence movement

Demonstrations during the March First Movement, 1919 (image placeholder)

The Korean independence movement was a series of diplomatic, cultural and military efforts by Koreans, at home and in exile, to achieve independence from the Empire of Japan between the late 19th century and 1945. It encompassed the "righteous army" guerrillas who fought the protectorate and annexation, the mass non-violent uprising of the March First Movement in 1919, the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea in exile in Shanghai and Chongqing, independence armies operating from Manchuria and China, and diplomacy by Korean missions in the United States and at international conferences. The movement ended with Japan's defeat in 1945, though the peninsula was then divided between Soviet and American occupations rather than unified under the Provisional Government.

Origins: righteous armies

Resistance began before annexation. After Japan imposed the protectorate (1905) and disbanded the Korean army (1907), former soldiers, Confucian scholars and peasants formed uibyeong ("righteous armies") that waged guerrilla war; the Japanese "pacification" campaign of 1907–1910 killed thousands. In 1909 An Jung-geun assassinated Itō Hirobumi, Japan's Resident-General, at Harbin station — an act celebrated in Korea and condemned in Japan. Annexation in 1910 drove much resistance underground or abroad, to Manchuria, the Russian Maritime Province, Hawaii and the continental United States.

March First Movement and Provisional Government

On 1 March 1919, 33 religious and cultural leaders proclaimed Korean independence in Seoul, sparking nationwide demonstrations involving perhaps two million people. Japanese suppression killed several thousand and imprisoned tens of thousands. The uprising galvanised the movement: in April 1919 activists in Shanghai proclaimed the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, with Syngman Rhee as its first president, uniting (imperfectly) exile factions. Inside Korea the movement shifted to cultural and educational resistance in the 1920s — newspapers, schools, the Korean Language Society's defence of Hangul, and campaigns for Korean products.

Armed struggle

In Manchuria, independence armies won notable victories in 1920 at the Battle of Qingshanli (Cheongsanri), where Korean forces under Kim Jwa-jin defeated Japanese troops, before punitive expeditions and the Jiandao incident drove them into Siberia and decline. Guerrilla activity continued in the 1930s, including the Korean People's Association in Manchuria and, in China proper, the Korean Patriotic Organization's operations such as Yun Bong-gil's 1932 bombing in Shanghai's Hongkou Park. In 1940 the Provisional Government, by then based in Chongqing with Nationalist China, organised the Korean Liberation Army, which later cooperated with Allied forces in China and Burma.

Diplomacy and the war years

Exile leaders lobbied Western powers for recognition: Syngman Rhee and the Korean Commission in Washington, and figures such as Kim Kyu-sik, pressed Korea's case at the League of Nations and, during the Second World War, sought Allied commitments. The 1943 Cairo Declaration promised that Korea would become free and independent "in due course" — a phrase that disappointed activists but first put Korean independence into Allied war aims. Liberation came in August 1945 with Japan's surrender, though partition followed; both Korean states today claim the independence movement, and especially the Provisional Government, as part of their founding legitimacy.
