Driven grouse shooting


A line of shooting butts on a managed grouse moor.

Driven grouse shooting is a form of game shooting, practised chiefly in the uplands of northern England and Scotland, in which red grouse are driven by beaters over a line of shooters ("guns") concealed in small turf or stone shelters called butts. It is one of the most prestigious and expensive forms of shooting in Britain.

The season and the shoot

The grouse season opens on 12 August — the "Glorious Twelfth" — and closes on 10 December in Great Britain. On a driven day, teams of beaters walk across the heather moorland in line, flushing grouse toward the butts. Red grouse fly low and extremely fast, and shooting them driven is considered a demanding test of skill. Dogs, especially retrievers and spaniels, are used to pick up shot birds. The number of birds shot is counted in braces (pairs).

Moor management

Grouse moors are intensively managed to produce a surplus of wild birds. Keepers burn heather in rotation to create a mosaic of young nutritious shoots and older cover, control predators such as foxes, stoats and carrion crows, and provide medicated grit against the strongyle worm. Driven shooting requires high grouse densities, and estates argue that the income — a day's shooting can cost thousands of pounds per gun — funds this management and rural employment.

Controversy

The sport is criticised by conservation and animal-welfare groups over the illegal persecution of birds of prey such as hen harriers on some moors, the environmental effects of heather burning on peatlands and carbon storage, lead shot, and the use of mountain hare culls. Defenders counter that moorland management preserves heather habitat and supports populations of curlew, lapwing and golden plover. The debate has prompted government reviews and licensing measures in Scotland.
