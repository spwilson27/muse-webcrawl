Tariqa

A tariqa (Arabic: طريقة, ṭarīqah, plural ṭuruq) is a school or order of Sufism, the mystical dimension of Islam, and by extension the spiritual path followed by its adherents. The word means "way" or "path". In classical Sufi thought, a seeker (murid) progresses under the guidance of a master (shaykh or pir) along the tariqa, the middle stage between outward observance of the law (sharia) and ultimate reality (haqiqa).

Structure

Initiates join an order through an oath of allegiance (bay'ah) to the shaykh, who transmits the order's litanies (wird), techniques of remembrance of God (dhikr), and spiritual genealogy (silsila) — a chain of masters traced back, in most orders, to the Prophet Muhammad, often through Ali ibn Abi Talib. Members gather in lodges known by names such as zawiya, khanaqah, or tekke for prayer, dhikr ceremonies, teaching, and charitable work.

Major orders

Hundreds of tariqas exist. Among the oldest and most widespread are the Qadiriyya, founded in the name of Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (d. 1166) in Baghdad; the Naqshbandiyya, associated with Baha al-Din Naqshband (d. 1389) of Bukhara and notable for silent dhikr; the Chishtiyya of South Asia; the Suhrawardiyya; the Rifa'iyya; and the Mevleviyya of Anatolia, founded by the followers of Jalal al-Din Rumi and famous for the whirling ceremony (sema). Later orders such as the Tijaniyya and the Senegalese Muridiyya spread widely in Africa in the 19th and 20th centuries.

History and influence

The orders crystallised in the 12th and 13th centuries and became the principal vehicle by which Islam spread across Africa, Central and South Asia, and Southeast Asia, adapting to local cultures. Sufi orders also shaped politics, founding dynasties (the Safavids began as a tariqa) and leading resistance movements against colonial powers. In the modern period, reformist and Salafi critics have attacked some orders' saint veneration and shrine practices, while the tariqas remain central to the religious life of much of the Muslim world.
