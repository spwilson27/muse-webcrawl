Air mass

In meteorology, an air mass is a large body of air with relatively uniform temperature and humidity at any given altitude. Air masses can cover hundreds of thousands of square kilometres and acquire their characteristics from prolonged contact with an underlying surface — ocean, ice cap, desert or forest — called a source region. The concept was developed by the Bergen School, notably Vilhelm and Jacob Bjerknes and Tor Bergeron, in the early 20th century.

Classification

In the Bergeron system, air masses are labelled by a lower-case letter for moisture (c for continental, m for maritime) and an upper-case letter for temperature (A arctic, P polar, T tropical, E equatorial). Thus continental polar (cP) air from northern Canada is cold and dry, while maritime tropical (mT) air from the Gulf of Mexico is warm and humid. A third letter may indicate stability relative to the surface (k colder than surface, w warmer).

Weather effects

As an air mass moves away from its source region it is gradually modified by the surfaces it crosses. The boundaries between air masses are weather fronts, along which much mid-latitude storminess occurs. Air-mass thunderstorms, lake-effect snow, heat waves and cold outbreaks are all expressions of particular air masses dominating a region. Air mass analysis remains a standard tool of synoptic weather forecasting.
