The Ramadan Revolution, also called the 8 February Revolution, was a coup d'état in Iraq in February 1963 carried out by the Iraqi branch of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party together with Arab nationalist officers. It overthrew the government of Brigadier Abd al-Karim Qasim, who had himself come to power in the 14 July Revolution of 1958 that ended the Hashemite monarchy.

Background

Qasim's rule had become increasingly isolated: he had alienated Arab nationalists by resisting union with Egypt, angered the West and oil companies by asserting control over unexploited oil concessions, and leaned at times on the Iraqi Communist Party, which alarmed both nationalists and anti-communist powers. Planning for the coup involved Ba'athist leaders including Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and Ali Salih al-Sa'di, and nationalist officers such as Abd al-Salam Arif.

The coup

On 8 February 1963 — 14 Ramadan in the Islamic calendar — rebel forces attacked, and after two days of fighting in Baghdad, including the bombardment of the Ministry of Defence where Qasim held out, Qasim surrendered. He was given a summary trial and executed, and photographs and footage of his corpse were broadcast to prove his death. Estimates of those killed in the coup and the subsequent purge of communists run into the thousands; the Ba'athist National Guard militia conducted mass arrests and executions of suspected communists, an anti-communist purge in which declassified reporting has indicated a role for CIA-supplied lists, a matter of continuing historical debate.

Aftermath

Abd al-Salam Arif became president and al-Bakr prime minister, but the Ba'athist government fractured and was overthrown by Arif in November 1963. The Ba'ath returned to power definitively in the coup of July 1968, beginning three and a half decades of Ba'athist rule that culminated in the regime of Saddam Hussein.
