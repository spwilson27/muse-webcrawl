Hodge conjecture

Hodge conjecture
Proposed byW. V. D. Hodge
Proposed1950 (announced), published 1950s
StatusOpen
PrizeMillennium Prize Problem

The Hodge conjecture is a major unsolved problem in algebraic geometry, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute, each carrying a US$1,000,000 prize. It asks, roughly, whether certain topological invariants of a smooth complex projective variety — the so-called Hodge classes — can always be built from the geometry of algebraic subvarieties.

Statement

Let X be a non-singular complex projective manifold. Its cohomology groups H2p(X, Q) decompose, by Hodge theory, into pieces Hp,q. A Hodge class is a rational cohomology class lying in the (p,p) piece. Any algebraic subvariety of codimension p defines such a class (its cycle class). The conjecture, formulated by the British mathematician W. V. D. Hodge, asserts the converse: every Hodge class is a rational linear combination of the classes of algebraic cycles.

Known cases

The case p = 1 was proved by Solomon Lefschetz (1924): Hodge classes of degree 2 correspond to divisors, a result equivalent to the existence of ample line bundles. For fourfolds and in many special families of varieties (abelian varieties of certain types, K3 surfaces in the analogous setting, Fermat varieties) the conjecture is known. The related integral version of the conjecture is false — counterexamples were found by Atiyah and Hirzebruch — so the formulation with rational coefficients is essential. The conjecture is widely expected to be true but is considered exceptionally deep, because it links transcendental topology (Hodge decomposition, defined via analysis) with algebraic geometry.

Significance

A proof would give a powerful bridge between the topology and the algebra of complex varieties and would likely resolve many subsidiary questions about algebraic cycles, motives, and periods. Claire Voisin and others have developed much of the modern theory of Hodge structures partly in pursuit of it.
