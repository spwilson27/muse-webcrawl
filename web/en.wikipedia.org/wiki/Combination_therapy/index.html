Combination therapy

Combination therapy
FieldMedicine, pharmacology
ContrastMonotherapy

Combination therapy or polytherapy is therapy that uses more than one medication or modality. Typically the term refers to using multiple therapies to treat a single disease, often in a single fixed-dose combination pill, in contrast to monotherapy, in which a single drug is used.

Rationale

Drugs used in combination may act on different targets or by different mechanisms, producing additive or synergistic effects, allowing lower doses of each agent, or reducing the chance that a pathogen or tumour develops resistance to any single drug.

Examples

Combination therapy is standard in the treatment of HIV/AIDS (antiretroviral therapy combining at least three drugs), tuberculosis, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and many cancers, where chemotherapy regimens combine agents with differing toxicities and mechanisms.

Fixed-dose combinations

Fixed-dose combination products combine two or more active ingredients in a single dosage form, simplifying regimens and improving adherence. Their disadvantages include less flexibility in adjusting individual doses.
