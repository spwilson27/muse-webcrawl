Herbert Charles Brown (22 May 1912 – 19 December 2004) was an American chemist who won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1979, shared with Georg Wittig, for his development of boron-containing compounds into important reagents in organic synthesis.

Life

Brown was born in London to Ukrainian Jewish immigrants and emigrated with his family to Chicago as a small child. Determinedly self-educated, he raced through school, studied at Wright Junior College and the University of Chicago, and received his PhD in 1938 under H. I. Schlesinger. After positions at Chicago and Wayne University, he joined Purdue University in 1947 and remained there for the rest of his career.

Work

Brown's research began with diborane and the metal hydrides; wartime work led to a practical synthesis of sodium borohydride. Studying the addition of diborane to alkenes, he discovered hydroboration (1956–59): the boron adds to the less substituted carbon, and oxidation then gives an alcohol with anti-Markovnikov orientation. The reaction, and the family of organoboranes it opened up, transformed synthetic organic chemistry and is still a standard method for building carbon skeletons. Brown also conducted classic studies of steric effects and waged a famous decades-long controversy over whether the 2-norbornyl cation is best described as a "non-classical" bridged ion. He trained a large school of chemists at Purdue and wrote the memoir Boranes in Organic Chemistry and an autobiography. He died in Lafayette, Indiana, in 2004.
