The Monmouth Rebellion, also known as the Pitchfork Rebellion, the Revolt of the West, or the West Country Rebellion, was an attempt in 1685 to depose King James II of England. James had succeeded his brother Charles II on 6 February 1685. James Scott, 1st Duke of Monmouth — the eldest illegitimate son of Charles II and a Protestant — claimed the throne, arguing that his parents had secretly married, and landed in Dorset with a small force of exiles.

The Battle of Sedgemoor, the last battle fought on English soil, ended the rebellion.

The rising

Monmouth landed at Lyme Regis on 11 June 1685 with about 150 men, expecting a general Protestant rising. Thousands of West Country working people — cloth workers, labourers, and Nonconformists, many armed only with farm tools such as pitchforks and scythes — flocked to his banner, and he was proclaimed king at Taunton. The expected support from the gentry and from a simultaneous Scottish rising under the Earl of Argyll largely failed to materialise.

Battle of Sedgemoor

After skirmishes at Bridport, Axminster, and Norton St Philip, Monmouth attempted a night attack on the royal camp on Sedgemoor on 6 July 1685. His untrained force was detected crossing the moor, and in the ensuing battle the rebels were routed by royal infantry and cavalry under the Earl of Feversham and John Churchill. Around 1,300 rebels were killed; Monmouth fled but was captured two days later hiding in a ditch near Ringwood.

Aftermath: the Bloody Assizes

Monmouth was beheaded on Tower Hill on 15 July 1685. The suppression that followed — the Bloody Assizes presided over by Judge Jeffreys — saw some 320 people executed and around 850 transported to the West Indies. Though brutal, the assizes did not end opposition to James, whose Catholicising policies led to his overthrow in the Glorious Revolution three years later.
