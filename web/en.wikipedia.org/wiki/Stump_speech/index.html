Stump speech

A stump speech is a standard speech used by a politician running for office. Typically, a candidate who schedules many appearances prepares a short standardized speech to be given on most occasions, covering their biography, platform, and core arguments. The name derives from the practice, in the 19th-century United States, of candidates standing on a sawed-off tree stump to deliver addresses in rural areas where no platform was available.

History

The phrase "on the stump" entered American political vocabulary during the era of frontier campaigning, when candidates such as Davy Crockett traveled from settlement to settlement speaking from tree stumps cleared from newly settled land. By extension, a campaign tour itself came to be called "stumping," and the verb "to stump" still describes traveling to deliver campaign speeches.

Use

Candidates repeat the stump speech at rallies, town halls, and fundraisers throughout a campaign, refining it in response to audience reaction and events. Because the speeches are delivered many times before news media, journalists often compare successive versions to detect shifts in message. Modern stump speeches typically run from fifteen to forty-five minutes and are supplemented by shorter "five-minute" versions for brief appearances.

Notable examples

Historically famous stump speeches include William Jennings Bryan's addresses during his 1896 presidential campaign, delivered to enormous crowds across the country, and the whistle-stop speeches of Harry S. Truman's 1948 campaign. In contemporary politics, stump speeches are frequently broadcast and clipped for news and social media, making consistency of delivery an important skill.
