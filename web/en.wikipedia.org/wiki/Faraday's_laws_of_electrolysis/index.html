Faraday's laws of electrolysis

Faraday's laws of electrolysis are two quantitative laws published by Michael Faraday in 1833 describing the amounts of substances altered at an electrode during electrolysis. They rest on the concept that the chemistry at electrodes is driven by discrete units of electric charge.

First law

The mass of a substance altered at an electrode during electrolysis is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity transferred at that electrode. In symbols, m ∝ Q, where Q is the total electric charge passed.

Second law

For a given quantity of electricity, the mass of an elemental material altered at an electrode is directly proportional to the element's equivalent weight — its molar mass divided by the number of electrons involved in the reaction. Combining both laws gives m = (Q × M) / (F × z), where F is the Faraday constant, approximately 96,485 coulombs per mole of electrons, z the valence of the ion, and M its molar mass.

Significance

The laws provided early evidence for the quantization of electric charge and the particulate nature of matter, foreshadowing the discovery of the electron. They remain fundamental to electrochemistry and industrial processes such as electroplating, aluminium smelting and chloralkali production.
