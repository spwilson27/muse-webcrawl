Grain size

Grain sizes range from sub-micrometre clay particles to boulders several metres across.

Grain size (or particle size) is the diameter of individual grains of sediment, or the lithified particles in clastic rocks. The term may also be applied to other granular materials. This is different from the crystallite size, which refers to the size of a single crystal inside a particle or grain.

Scales

The most widely used scale in sedimentology is the Wentworth scale, devised by Chester K. Wentworth in 1922, which divides sediment into classes based on diameter:

ClassDiameter range
Boulder> 256 mm
Cobble64–256 mm
Pebble / gravel2–64 mm
Sand1/16–2 mm
Silt1/256–1/16 mm
Clay< 1/256 mm

Because these ranges span many orders of magnitude, geologists often use the phi (φ) scale introduced by W. C. Krumbein, defined as φ = −log2(d), where d is the grain diameter in millimetres. On this scale, each class boundary falls on a whole number.

Measurement

Grain size distributions are measured by sieving (for coarse material), by settling-tube or pipette analysis (for fine material, exploiting Stokes' law), and increasingly by laser diffraction and image analysis. Results are commonly plotted as histograms or cumulative curves, from which statistical parameters such as the mean, median, sorting (standard deviation) and skewness are calculated.

Significance

Grain size is a fundamental descriptor of sediments and sedimentary rocks. It records information about the energy of the transporting medium (wind, water or ice), the distance of transport, and the depositional environment. Coarse, poorly sorted deposits typically indicate high-energy or proximal conditions, whereas fine, well-sorted sediments suggest quieter or more distal settings.
