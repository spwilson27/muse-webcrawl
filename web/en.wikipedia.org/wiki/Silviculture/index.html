Silviculture

Silviculture is the practice of controlling the growth, composition, structure, and quality of forests to meet diverse needs and values. The name comes from the Latin silvi- ("forest") and culture ("growing"). The study of forests and woods is termed silvology. Silviculture also focuses on making sure that the treatment(s) of forest stands are used to conserve and improve their productivity.

Scope

Silviculture is the applied, ecological core of forestry. Where forestry is the broader science and business of creating, managing, and conserving forests, silviculture concerns the specific treatments applied to a stand: how trees are regenerated after harvest, how young stands are tended (weeding, cleaning, thinning, pruning, and fertilising), and how mature stands are harvested and renewed.

Regeneration methods

Silvicultural systems are classified by their regeneration method and resulting stand structure. Even-aged systems include clearcutting, seed-tree, and shelterwood methods; uneven-aged systems include single-tree and group selection. Coppice systems regenerate stands from stump sprouts or root suckers. The choice depends on the shade tolerance and ecology of the desired species, site conditions, and management objectives such as timber production, wildlife habitat, watershed protection, carbon storage, and recreation.

History

Systematic silviculture developed in Germany and France in the 18th and 19th centuries, associated with foresters such as Heinrich Cotta, and was carried to North America and the British Empire by professionally trained foresters including Dietrich Brandis and Gifford Pinchot.
