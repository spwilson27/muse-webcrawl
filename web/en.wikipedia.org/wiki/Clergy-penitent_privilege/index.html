Clergy-penitent privilege

Clergy-penitent privilege, also known as the confessional privilege or priest-penitent privilege, is a rule of evidence that forbids judicial inquiry into communications made to a member of the clergy in their professional capacity as spiritual adviser. In many jurisdictions, clergy may not be compelled to testify about matters confided to them during confession or pastoral counselling.

Scope

The privilege is recognized in the law of all U.S. states, though its scope varies: in some jurisdictions it belongs to the penitent, in others to the clergy member, and some statutes extend it to communications made to any religious functionary in confidence. In Roman Catholic canon law, the seal of the confessional is absolute: a priest who directly violates it incurs automatic excommunication.

Controversies

The privilege has been debated in connection with mandatory reporting laws for child abuse. Several jurisdictions have considered or enacted laws requiring clergy to report abuse even when learned in confession, proposals opposed by the Catholic Church and some other religious bodies as infringements on religious freedom.
