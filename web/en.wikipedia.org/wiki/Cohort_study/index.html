Cohort study

A cohort study is a particular form of longitudinal study that samples a cohort (a group of people who share a defining characteristic, typically those who experienced a common event in a selected period, such as birth or graduation), performing a cross-section at intervals through time. It is a type of observational study used in medicine, epidemiology, social science, and ecology, in which the researcher follows exposed and unexposed groups forward to compare the incidence of an outcome.

Design

The investigator identifies a population, classifies participants by exposure (for example, smokers and non-smokers), and follows them over time to record who develops the disease or outcome of interest. Because exposure is measured before the outcome occurs, cohort studies establish temporal sequence and can estimate incidence directly. In a retrospective (historical) cohort study, exposure and outcome have already occurred and are reconstructed from records. Analysis typically reports the relative risk or, with time-to-event data, the hazard ratio, adjusting for confounding factors.

Famous examples

The Framingham Heart Study, begun in 1948 in Massachusetts, identified the major cardiovascular risk factors. The British Doctors' Study, led by Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill from 1951, followed some 34,000 male doctors and provided landmark evidence linking cigarette smoking to lung cancer and premature death. The Nurses' Health Study (founded 1976) has followed more than 100,000 female nurses, and the "British birth cohort" studies have tracked everyone born in a single week in 1946, 1958, and 1970 across their lives.

Strengths and limitations

Cohort studies can examine multiple outcomes of one exposure and rare exposures, and they avoid some biases of case–control designs. However, they are expensive, slow for diseases with long latency, inefficient for rare diseases, and vulnerable to loss to follow-up and to changes in exposure over time. As observational studies, they cannot by themselves prove causation as firmly as randomised controlled trials.
