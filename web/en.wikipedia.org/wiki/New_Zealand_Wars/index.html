New Zealand Wars
Date1845–1872 (main campaigns 1860–1864)
LocationNorth Island, New Zealand
BelligerentsBritish Empire and colonial forces (with Māori allies, kūpapa) vs. Māori iwi and the Kīngitanga (Māori King movement)
ResultBritish/colonial victory; extensive confiscation of Māori land (raupatu)
Casualties~2,100–2,200 Māori and ~750 British/colonial killed (estimates vary)

The New Zealand Wars (Māori: Ngā pakanga o Aotearoa) were a series of armed conflicts in New Zealand between 1845 and 1872, fought between the British Crown and colonial government — supported by Māori allies known as kūpapa — and various Māori groups resisting European settlement and the sale and confiscation of their land. The heaviest fighting, in the 1860s, pitted imperial troops against the Kīngitanga (Māori King movement) in Waikato and Taranaki. The wars reshaped New Zealand, accelerating European settlement and leaving a legacy of land confiscations addressed, in part, through Treaty of Waitangi settlements from the 1990s onward.

Causes

After the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), settler demand for land grew rapidly. Disputes over dubious land purchases and sovereignty — symbolized by the founding of the Kīngitanga in 1858 to unite Māori and halt land sales — produced armed confrontation. Governor George Grey determined to break the King movement's power.

Major campaigns

  • Flagstaff War (1845–46): Hōne Heke and Kawiti resisted in the Bay of Islands, repeatedly felling the flagstaff at Kororāreka (Russell).
  • First Taranaki War (1860–61): Sparked by the disputed Waitara purchase.
  • Invasion of the Waikato (1863–64): The largest campaign, with battles at Rangiriri, Pāterangi, and Ōrākau, where Rewi Maniapoto's defenders fought to the end.
  • Second Taranaki War and Tauranga campaign (1863–66): Including the famous Māori victory at Gate Pā (1864) and the subsequent defeat at Te Ranga.
  • Later conflicts (1865–72): Fighting against Pai Mārire (Hauhau) adherents, Tītokowaru's campaign in south Taranaki (1868–69), and the pursuit of Te Kooti across the central North Island.

Tactics and consequences

Māori forces made highly effective use of the modern pā — sophisticated trench-and-bunker fortifications that resisted artillery and inflicted heavy casualties, as at Ruapekapeka and Gate Pā. At its peak, the British deployed some 18,000 troops, the largest military force ever assembled in New Zealand. Following the Waikato war, the government confiscated about 1.2 million hectares of Māori land under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 — confiscations (raupatu) later acknowledged by the Crown as unjust.

Legacy

Long marginalized in national memory, the wars have been increasingly recognized since the late 20th century, aided by historian James Belich's influential 1986 study The New Zealand Wars. A national day of commemoration, He Rā Maumaharatanga, is observed on 28 October, and the wars are now taught in the national school curriculum.
