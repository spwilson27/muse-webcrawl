Cabinet collective responsibility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cabinet collective responsibility is a constitutional convention in parliamentary systems under which members of the cabinet must publicly support all governmental decisions made in cabinet, even if they do not agree with them privately. A minister who cannot support a decision must resign, as British ministers have done over Iraq (Robin Cook, 2003), Brexit (multiple resignations, 2018–19) and other issues; the convention also entails that cabinet deliberations remain confidential and that the government falls (or at least the ministry is reconstituted) if it loses the confidence of the legislature on a matter of confidence.

The doctrine — traceable in Britain to the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — serves party cohesion and accountable government: voters face a single government position, and parliament knows whom to hold responsible. Formal exceptions are agreed suspensions: the 1932 “agreement to differ” on tariffs, the 1975 referendum on EC membership, and coalition arrangements (2010–15) where collective responsibility was qualified. Critics argue that in an age of leaks, special advisers and prime-ministerial government, the convention binds the weak and is waived for the strong; defenders reply that it remains the mechanism by which governments speak — and answer — with one voice.
