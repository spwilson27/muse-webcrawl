Tusi

Tusi (Chinese: 土司; pinyin: tǔsī), often rendered in English as "native chieftains", were tribal leaders recognised by the imperial Chinese government who ruled ethnic minority regions, principally in the southwestern provinces of Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, Guangxi and parts of Tibet and Hunan. The tusi system was a form of indirect rule in which hereditary local chiefs administered their territories on behalf of the emperor in exchange for recognition, titles and a degree of autonomy.

History

The system had roots in the Tang dynasty jimi ("loose rein") system and was formalised under the Yuan, Ming and Qing dynasties. Under the Ming, tusi were graded into ranks and required to pay tribute, furnish troops and maintain order, while succession to office needed imperial confirmation. In the eighteenth century the Qing court pursued the policy of gaitu guiliu — replacing native chieftains with regular circulating officials — gradually abolishing many tusi domains, a process completed under the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China in the twentieth century.

Legacy

In 2015, three tusi sites — Laosicheng, Tangya and Hailongtun — were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the Tusi Sites, recognising the system as a distinctive method of governance in imperial China's frontier regions.
