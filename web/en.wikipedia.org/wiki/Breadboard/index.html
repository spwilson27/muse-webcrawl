Breadboard

A breadboard, or protoboard, is a construction base for prototyping of electronics. Originally the word referred to a literal bread board — a polished piece of wood used for slicing bread — on which early radio hobbyists mounted components. In modern usage, the term most often refers to the solderless breadboard: a plastic board with many spring-clip contact points into which component leads and jumper wires can be inserted and removed, allowing circuits to be built, tested, and modified without soldering.

Solderless breadboard layout

A typical solderless breadboard consists of terminal strips and distribution (bus) strips. In the terminal strips, clips connect points in groups of five along rows, with the two halves of the board separated by a central notch sized to straddle a dual in-line package (DIP) integrated circuit. The bus strips along the edges, usually marked with red (+) and blue (−) lines, distribute power. Hole spacing is standardized at 0.1 inch (2.54 mm), matching DIP pin spacing.

History

In the 1920s and 1930s, amateur radio builders screwed vacuum tube sockets and terminal strips to wooden breadboards. Point-to-point wiring on wooden or metal chassis followed. The solderless plastic breadboard in its familiar form emerged around 1971, when Ronald Portugal of EI Instruments developed the design that became the modern standard. Related prototyping media include perfboard, stripboard (Veroboard), and wire-wrap, which trade convenience for durability.

Limitations

Breadboards are unsuitable for high-frequency circuits because of parasitic capacitance and inductance between contact strips, and contacts can become unreliable with age or heavy current. They are intended for low-power, temporary circuits in education and development; designs are typically transferred to a printed circuit board for production.
