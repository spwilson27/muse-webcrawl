Florio family

The Florio family is a prominent Italian business dynasty that rose to great wealth and influence in Sicily in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Originally from Calabria, the family settled in Palermo and built an empire spanning shipping, wine, tuna fishing and canning, sulfur, banking and metallurgy, becoming one of the richest families in Italy and symbols of the Sicilian Belle Époque.

Origins

The family's Palermo enterprise was founded by Paolo Florio (1772–1807), who moved from Bagnara Calabra and opened a shop trading in spices and colonial goods, including quinine. His son Vincenzo Florio Sr. (1799–1868) greatly expanded the business: in 1832 he founded the Marsala wine concern that still bears his name (Cantine Florio), in 1840 he established a steamship company that grew into a major Mediterranean fleet, and he invested in sulfur mining, banking (he co-founded the Banco di Sicilia), insurance and the foundries of the Oretea works.

The golden age

Under Vincenzo's son Ignazio Florio Sr. (1838–1891) and his grandson Ignazio Florio Jr. (1869–1957), the family reached its apogee. The Navigazione Generale Italiana, formed in 1881 by merging the Florio and Rubattino fleets, was Italy's largest shipping company. The Florio tuna fisheries and canneries (tonnare), especially at Favignana in the Aegadian Islands, industrialised the traditional mattanza tuna catch and marketed canned tuna throughout Europe. The family hosted royalty and aristocracy at the Villa Igiea in Palermo and sponsored the Targa Florio, the famous road race founded in 1906 by Vincenzo Florio Jr., Ignazio's younger brother.

Decline

Financial difficulties, heavy borrowing and the economic dislocations of the First World War eroded the fortune; by the 1930s most of the family's enterprises had passed to creditors and the state holding company IRI. The Marsala wine business survives under the Florio name, and the family's villas, tonnara and racing heritage remain landmarks of Palermo and western Sicily.
