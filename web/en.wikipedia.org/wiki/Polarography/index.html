Polarography


Dropping mercury electrode

Polarography is a type of voltammetry where the working electrode is a dropping mercury electrode (DME) or a static mercury drop electrode (SMDE), which are useful for their wide cathodic ranges and renewable surfaces. It was invented by the Czech chemist Jaroslav Heyrovský, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1959 for its discovery and development. Polarography is an electrochemical method of analysis used to determine the concentration and identity of electroactive species in solution.

Principle

A slowly increasing (more negative) potential is applied to mercury drops growing at the tip of a capillary immersed in the sample solution. As each reducible species reaches its characteristic half-wave potential it is reduced at the electrode, producing a step-shaped increase in current; the height of the wave is proportional to concentration (the Ilkovič equation) and its position identifies the species. Successive drops continually renew the electrode surface, giving reproducible results, though mercury's toxicity has led to its replacement in many modern applications by other electrode materials and by pulse techniques such as differential pulse polarography.

Applications

Polarography has been applied to the analysis of metal ions, dissolved oxygen, and many organic and pharmaceutical compounds, and to the study of electrode reaction mechanisms, complex formation, and adsorption phenomena.
