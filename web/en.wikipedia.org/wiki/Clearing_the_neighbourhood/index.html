Clearing the neighbourhood

"Clearing the neighbourhood" (or clearing its neighbourhood) is one of the three criteria used by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) to define a planet. Under the definition adopted by the IAU in 2006, a planet is a celestial body that (a) orbits the Sun, (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, and (c) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit.

Meaning

A body is said to have cleared its neighbourhood when it has become gravitationally dominant in its orbit — accreting, ejecting, or otherwise removing most smaller bodies that share its orbital zone, so that no comparably sized bodies remain there (other than its own satellites and bodies under its gravitational influence, such as trojans and resonant populations like Pluto's fellow plutinos). The eight recognised planets satisfy the criterion by orders of magnitude; dwarf planets such as Pluto, Ceres, and Eris do not, because they share their orbital regions with the Kuiper belt and asteroid belt populations.

Quantitative measures

Several scientists have proposed numerical measures of orbital dominance. Alan Stern and Harold Levison's parameter Λ (lambda) measures a body's ability to scatter smaller bodies out of its orbital zone over the age of the universe; values greater than 1 indicate clearing. Steven Soter's planetary discriminant µ compares a body's mass with the total mass of everything else sharing its orbital zone; the planets have µ values in the thousands or more, while Ceres has µ ≈ 0.33 and Pluto ≈ 0.077. Jean-Luc Margot proposed a criterion based on the time a body needs to clear its orbit, which can be applied to exoplanets as well.

Criticism

The criterion has been criticised by some planetary scientists, including Alan Stern, who note that Earth itself has not fully "cleared" its neighbourhood — thousands of near-Earth asteroids cross its orbit — and that a strict reading would be context-dependent, since a body's clearing ability depends on its distance from its star and on the age of the system. Defenders reply that the criterion concerns comparable mass in the orbital zone, not the literal absence of small bodies, and that by any such measure the recognised planets stand apart from dwarf planets by several orders of magnitude.
