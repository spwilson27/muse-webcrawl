Joseph Rudyard Kipling (30 December 1865 – 18 January 1936) was an English novelist, short-story writer, poet, and journalist. Born in British India, which inspired much of his work, he is best known for the children's books The Jungle Book (1894) and Just So Stories (1902), the novel Kim (1901), and the poems "Mandalay" (1890), "Gunga Din" (1890), "The Gods of the Copybook Headings" (1919), "The White Man's Burden" (1899), and "If—" (1910).

Kipling is regarded as a major innovator in the art of the short story, and his children's books are enduring classics. In 1907, at the age of 41, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature — the first English-language writer to receive it and still its youngest recipient.

Early life

Kipling was born in Bombay to Alice Kipling (née MacDonald) and John Lockwood Kipling, a sculptor and pottery designer who became professor and curator at the Mayo School of Industrial Art in Lahore. At age five he and his sister were sent to England to live with a foster family in Southsea, an unhappy period he recalled in the story "Baa Baa, Black Sheep" (1888). He was educated at the United Services College in Devon, an experience fictionalised in Stalky & Co. (1899).

In 1882 he returned to India and worked as a journalist in Lahore and Allahabad, producing the poems of Departmental Ditties (1886) and the short stories of Plain Tales from the Hills (1888), which made his reputation.

Career

Return to England and fame

Kipling travelled back to England in 1889 via Japan and the United States and quickly became one of the most popular writers in English. Barrack-Room Ballads (1892) and The Jungle Book (1894) and The Second Jungle Book (1895) — written during four years in Vermont after his marriage to Caroline Balestier — established him internationally.

Later work

Kim (1901), set against the Great Game of Anglo-Russian rivalry in Central Asia, is widely considered his masterpiece. His later collections include Puck of Pook's Hill (1906) and Actions and Reactions (1909). Deeply affected by the death of his son John at the Battle of Loos in 1915, Kipling worked with the Imperial War Graves Commission and wrote regimental histories and epitaphs, including the phrase "Known unto God" for unknown soldiers' graves.

Views and reception

Kipling's works express a complex attitude toward British imperialism: celebratory themes of duty, sacrifice, and empire made him a symbol of imperial Britain to some, while stories emphasising the dignity of ordinary soldiers and Indian characters, and his scepticism about politicians, complicate the picture. George Orwell called him a "prophet of British imperialism" yet conceded he contained passages of enduring power. His poem "Recessional" (1897) warned against imperial pride. T. S. Eliot edited a selection of his verse in 1941, arguing for his technical mastery. In 1995 "If—" was voted the United Kingdom's favourite poem in a BBC poll.

Death

Kipling died of a perforated duodenal ulcer on 18 January 1936. His ashes were interred in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey. His home from 1902, Bateman's in East Sussex, is preserved by the National Trust.
