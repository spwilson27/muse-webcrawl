The Mōri clan (毛利氏, Mōri-shi) is a Japanese samurai clan descended from Ōe no Hiromoto, a courtier and adviser of the Kamakura shogunate. From their base in Aki Province the Mōri rose during the Sengoku period, under Mōri Motonari (1497–1571), to become one of the most powerful daimyō houses in western Honshu, ruling much of the Chūgoku region. In the Edo period they were the lords of Chōshū Domain, which played a leading role in the overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate during the Meiji Restoration.

History

Origins

The clan's founder, Mōri Suemitsu, was a son of Ōe no Hiromoto and served as a retainer of the Kamakura shogunate; the family took its name from Mōri estate in Sagami Province and later settled in Aki Province, where they were minor local lords for several centuries.

Sengoku period

Under Mōri Motonari the clan defeated its powerful neighbours the Amago and Ōuchi clans; the victory over Sue Harukata at the Battle of Itsukushima in 1555 secured Mōri supremacy in the region. Motonari's sons — including Kikkawa Motoharu and Kobayakawa Takakage, adopted into allied houses — extended the family's influence (the "Two Rivers" system). At its height under Motonari's grandson Mōri Terumoto, the clan controlled some ten provinces with an assessed income of about 1.2 million koku and fielded the principal western army at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, where Terumoto was nominal commander of the Western Army.

Edo period and Meiji

After Sekigahara, the victorious Tokugawa Ieyasu reduced the Mōri to the provinces of Nagato and Suō — Chōshū Domain, based at Hagi Castle, assessed at 369,000 koku. The domain nursed its grievance against the shogunate for two and a half centuries; in the 1860s Chōshū, together with Satsuma, led the movement that toppled the Tokugawa regime. Prominent Meiji statesmen, including the first prime minister Itō Hirobumi, came from Chōshū and the Mōri retainer band.
