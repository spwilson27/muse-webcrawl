Solemn League and Covenant

The Solemn League and Covenant was an agreement between the Scottish Covenanters and the leaders of the English Parliament in 1643, during the First English Civil War. Drafted principally by Alexander Henderson, it was adopted by the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland and the Convention of Estates in August 1643, and sworn by the English Parliament and the Westminster Assembly on 25 September 1643 at St Margaret's, Westminster.

Terms

In return for Scottish military assistance against Charles I, the English Parliament promised the preservation of the reformed religion in Scotland and the reformation of religion in England and Ireland "according to the Word of God and the example of the best reformed churches" — language the Scots read as a commitment to Presbyterianism, though many English signatories, including Independents, understood it more loosely. The covenant also pledged the extirpation of popery and prelacy, the preservation of the king's person and authority, and the defence of the rights and liberties of the parliaments of the three kingdoms.

Consequences

A Scottish army of some 21,000 men under Alexander Leslie, Earl of Leven, entered England in January 1644 and played a major part in the Parliamentarian victory at the Battle of Marston Moor. The Westminster Assembly, which included Scottish commissioners, produced the Westminster Confession of Faith and other standards in line with the covenant's aims. Relations soured when the English New Model Army proved dominated by Independents unwilling to impose Presbyterianism, and the Scots' later Engagement with Charles I in 1647 effectively broke the alliance.

Later history

After the Restoration, the covenant was declared unlawful and burned by the common hangman in London in 1661; subscription to it was made treasonable. Nevertheless it remained a foundational document for Presbyterian and Reformed Presbyterian churches, whose members — the Covenanters — suffered persecution in Scotland during the later seventeenth century.
