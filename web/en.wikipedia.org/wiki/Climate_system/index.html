The climate system is the interacting system that produces Earth's climate. As defined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), it consists of five major components — the atmosphere, the hydrosphere (oceans, lakes, rivers), the cryosphere (ice and snow), the lithosphere (land surface and upper earth), and the biosphere (living organisms) — interacting with each other and with external influences such as solar radiation and volcanic activity.

How it works

The system is driven by energy from the Sun. Earth absorbs shortwave solar radiation and re-emits longwave infrared radiation to space; greenhouse gases (water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and others) slow the escape of infrared energy, warming the surface — the greenhouse effect. The atmosphere and oceans transport heat from the tropics toward the poles, and exchange water, carbon, and momentum across their boundaries. Components respond on vastly different timescales: the atmosphere adjusts in weeks, the upper ocean in years, deep oceans and ice sheets over centuries to millennia.

Feedbacks and change

Interactions within the system create feedbacks that amplify or dampen change: warming melts reflective ice (positive ice–albedo feedback), while increased water vapor — itself a greenhouse gas — further amplifies warming; clouds exert both warming and cooling effects and remain a major source of uncertainty. Human activity, principally fossil-fuel combustion increasing greenhouse gas concentrations, is now the dominant driver of observed warming, and the climate system's behavior is studied with coupled global climate models (Earth system models) that simulate all five components.
