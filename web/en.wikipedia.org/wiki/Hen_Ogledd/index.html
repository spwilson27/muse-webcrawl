Hen Ogledd

Hen Ogledd (Welsh pronunciation: [heːn ˈɔɡlɛð]), meaning "the Old North", is the term used in Welsh tradition for the Brittonic-speaking parts of what is now northern England and southern Scotland in the post-Roman period, roughly the fifth to seventh centuries AD. The region's kingdoms — including Rheged, Gododdin, Elmet, Strathclyde, and Bernicia's Brittonic neighbours — were gradually conquered or absorbed by the expanding Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and by the Scots and Picts.

Kingdoms and literature

The Hen Ogledd is remembered above all through early Welsh poetry. The bards Taliesin and Aneirin are associated with its courts: Aneirin's Y Gododdin laments a Brittonic warband from the kingdom of Gododdin destroyed at the Battle of Catraeth (around 600), and Taliesin praised Urien of Rheged. The genealogies of the "Men of the North" (Gwŷr y Gogledd) were preserved in Welsh manuscripts, and figures such as Coel Hen ("Old King Cole") and the warrior culture of the north fed into later Arthurian tradition.

Decline

Defeats such as the Battle of Chester (c. 616) and the fall of Elmet, Edinburgh (Eidyn), and other strongholds broke up the Brittonic north. The kingdom of Strathclyde, centred on Dumbarton Rock (Alt Clut), survived longest, into the eleventh century. The Cumbric language, closely related to Welsh, died out during the medieval period, leaving traces in place-names across Cumbria and southern Scotland.
