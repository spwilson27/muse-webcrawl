A sail is a tensile structure — made from fabric or other membrane materials — that uses wind power to propel sailing craft, including sailing ships, sailboats, windsurfers, iceboats, and even land vehicles (sail-powered land vehicles) and rotor ships. Sails may be made from a combination of woven materials — including canvas or polyester cloth, laminated membranes or bonded filaments — usually in a three- or four-sided shape, with a point at the apex.

A sail provides propulsive force via a combination of lift and drag, depending on its angle of attack — its angle with respect to the apparent wind. The apparent wind is the wind as experienced by an observer in motion and is the vector sum of the true wind and the headwind due to the object moving against its own source. As sailing craft velocities have increased beyond that of the apparent wind (as on high-performance catamarans and iceboats), most foils provide only lift, not drag.

History

Sails were probably used for the first time around 3500–3200 BC, when the Egyptians built ships with masts and square sails on the Nile. Over the centuries, sail plans and rigs evolved: the lateen sail of the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean enabled sailing closer to the wind, while the full-rigged ships of the Age of Sail carried dozens of sails on three or more masts. Steam power displaced sail commercially during the 19th century, though working sail survived into the early 20th century and sail remains ubiquitous in recreation.

Types

Sails are commonly classified as square sails, set perpendicular to the vessel's length on yards, and fore-and-aft sails, set along the line of the keel. Fore-and-aft sails include the triangular Bermuda (Marconi) rig, gaff sails, spritsails, lugsails, and the lateen. Headsails such as jibs and genoas are set on stays forward of the mast, while downwind sails include spinnakers and gennakers.

Parts of a sail

A triangular sail has three corners: the head (top), the tack (lower forward) and the clew (lower aft). Its edges are the luff (forward edge), leech (aft edge) and foot (bottom edge). Sails are shaped with broad seaming, giving them a curved airfoil form, and are controlled by sheets and adjusted with telltales.

Materials

Until the mid-20th century sails were made of natural fibres — flax, hemp and cotton canvas. Modern sails use woven polyester (Dacron), nylon for spinnakers, and high-modulus fibres such as aramid (Kevlar), carbon fibre and ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene in laminated racing sails.
