Manganese nodule

Manganese nodules, also called polymetallic nodules or ferromanganese nodules, are mineral concretions on the deep ocean floor formed of concentric layers of iron and manganese hydroxides around a core. They vary in size from tiny particles visible only under a microscope to pellets several centimetres across, most commonly 3 to 10 cm in diameter, roughly like potatoes in shape.

Formation

Nodules grow extremely slowly — a few millimetres per million years — by the precipitation of metals from seawater or from sediment pore waters around a nucleus such as a shark tooth, a fragment of basalt, or an older nodule fragment. They are found in abundance on abyssal plains at depths of about 4,000 to 6,000 metres, most famously in the Clarion–Clipperton Zone of the Pacific Ocean.

Composition

Besides manganese (around 27–30%) and iron, nodules contain commercially interesting amounts of nickel, copper, and cobalt, together making up roughly 3% of nodule mass, plus traces of many other elements including rare earths, lithium, titanium, and molybdenum.

Discovery

Manganese nodules were first dredged up during the voyage of HMS Challenger (1873–1876), and John Murray's reports documented their widespread distribution on the deep sea floor.

Mining interest and environment

Since the 1960s the nodules have attracted interest as a potential source of critical metals for batteries and steel. Deep-sea mining is regulated in international waters by the International Seabed Authority under UNCLOS. Environmental scientists warn that nodule fields support slow-growing, poorly known ecosystems, and that sediment plumes and habitat removal could cause long-lasting damage; these concerns remain a major subject of debate.
