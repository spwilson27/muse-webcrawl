Quantum sensor

A quantum sensor is a device that exploits quantum correlations, such as entanglement, squeezing, or superposition, to achieve sensitivity or resolution exceeding what classical sensors can reach. Quantum sensors measure physical quantities including time, magnetic and electric fields, gravity, rotation, and acceleration.

Types

Atomic clocks are the most mature quantum sensors, using hyperfine transitions in atoms such as caesium and rubidium to define the second. Atom interferometers measure gravity and acceleration by splitting and recombining matter waves. Nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond detect magnetic fields at nanometre scales. SQUIDs (superconducting quantum interference devices) measure extremely weak magnetic fields using superconducting loops. Optomechanical and squeezed-light sensors improve gravitational-wave detectors such as LIGO.

Applications

Applications include navigation without GPS, mineral exploration and civil engineering via gravity gradiometry, medical imaging of the brain and heart, tests of fundamental physics, and Earth observation. Quantum sensing is often considered the most commercially advanced branch of quantum technology after atomic clocks.

Quantum metrology

The underlying theory, quantum metrology, studies fundamental limits on precision: uncorrelated probes obey the standard quantum limit (scaling as 1/√N), while entangled probes can in principle reach the Heisenberg limit (scaling as 1/N).
