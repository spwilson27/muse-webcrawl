The Kagyu school of Tibetan Buddhism, also romanised as Kagyü, is one of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism, alongside the Nyingma, Sakya, and Gelug. The term Kagyu means "oral lineage" or "command transmission", reflecting the school's emphasis on instructions passed directly from master to disciple, particularly the tantric practices of Mahamudra and the Six Yogas of Naropa.

Lineage and history

The Kagyu lineage traces itself to the Indian mahasiddha Tilopa (988–1069), who is said to have received the teachings from the Buddha Vajradhara, and to his student Naropa, abbot of Nalanda. Naropa's Tibetan disciple Marpa Lotsawa ("the Translator") travelled three times to India and brought the transmissions back to Tibet. Marpa's most famous student was the yogi and poet Milarepa, celebrated for attaining enlightenment in a single lifetime after years of solitary retreat; his collected songs remain among the most beloved works of Tibetan literature. Milarepa's disciple Gampopa, a monk of the Kadam school, systematised the teachings, combining monastic discipline and sutra study with the tantric oral instructions, and founded the monasteries from which the Kagyu sub-schools descend.

Sub-schools and practice

Gampopa's disciples founded the "four great" Kagyu lineages, from which the "eight lesser" schools derive. The most prominent surviving sub-schools include the Karma Kagyu, headed by the Karmapa — the earliest recognised reincarnate lama lineage in Tibet, dating to the 12th century — the Drikung Kagyu, and the Drukpa Kagyu, which is the dominant school of Bhutan. Core practices include Mahamudra meditation on the nature of mind, the Six Yogas of Naropa (including tummo, or inner heat), guru yoga, and extensive retreat practice, traditionally including a three-year, three-month, three-day retreat.
