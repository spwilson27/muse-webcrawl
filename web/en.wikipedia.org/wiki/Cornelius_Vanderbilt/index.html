Cornelius Vanderbilt (May 27, 1794 – January 4, 1877), nicknamed "the Commodore", was an American business magnate who built his wealth in railroads and shipping. Born to a modest family on Staten Island, he borrowed money as a teenager to buy a ferry boat, and over six decades assembled one of the largest fortunes in American history, dominating steamboat traffic on the Hudson River and the east coast, opening a transit route across Nicaragua during the California Gold Rush, and later consolidating the New York Central Railroad system.

At his death he left an estate of roughly $100 million — larger, as a share of the economy, than almost any American fortune before or since. His philanthropy was comparatively slight, but included a $1 million endowment that founded Vanderbilt University in Nashville, Tennessee, in 1873.

Early life

Vanderbilt was born on Staten Island to Cornelius van Derbilt, a farmer and ferryman of Dutch descent, and Phebe Hand. At sixteen, with $100 borrowed from his mother, he bought a two-masted sailing vessel (a periauger) and began ferrying passengers and freight between Staten Island and Manhattan. During the War of 1812 he held government contracts to supply forts around New York.

Steamboats

From 1817 Vanderbilt worked for steamboat pioneer Thomas Gibbons, managing his ferry between New Jersey and Manhattan in defiance of the steamboat monopoly granted by New York to Robert Fulton and Robert Livingston. The resulting litigation produced the landmark Supreme Court decision Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), which struck down the monopoly and established a broad reading of federal power over interstate commerce. Vanderbilt then built his own fleet, driving competitors from the Hudson River and Long Island Sound routes with ruthless price-cutting, and became known as "Commodore" — the highest rank then used in the United States Navy.

Nicaragua transit and the Accessory Transit Company

After the discovery of gold in California in 1848, Vanderbilt organised a steamship route to California via Nicaragua — across the isthmus by river, lake and stagecoach — which undercut the Panama route in time and fares. His Accessory Transit Company made him still richer, though he lost control of it to rivals Charles Morgan and Cornelius Garrison while on an extended European voyage in 1853 aboard his enormous steam yacht North Star. He regained control amid the upheavals surrounding William Walker's filibustering regime in Nicaragua, and famously wrote to his rivals: "Gentlemen: You have undertaken to cheat me. I won't sue you, for the law is too slow. I'll ruin you. Yours truly, Cornelius Vanderbilt."

Railroads

In the 1860s Vanderbilt sold most of his shipping interests and turned to railroads. He gained control of the New York and Harlem Railroad, the Hudson River Railroad, and in 1867 the New York Central, combining them into a route from New York to Buffalo and, through further acquisitions, to Chicago. His attempt to corner the Erie Railroad in the "Erie War" of 1868 against Daniel Drew, Jay Gould and James Fisk ended in a compromise after Gould and Fisk printed floods of new shares and fled to New Jersey. In 1869–1871 Vanderbilt built Grand Central Depot in Manhattan, the predecessor of today's Grand Central Terminal.

Personal life and death

Vanderbilt married his cousin Sophia Johnson in 1813; they had thirteen children. After Sophia's death in 1868 he married Frank Armstrong Crawford, a much younger cousin from Alabama. He died at his home at 10 Washington Place, Manhattan, on 4 January 1877, after months of illness. The bulk of his fortune passed to his son William Henry Vanderbilt, who roughly doubled it before his own death in 1885.

Legacy

Vanderbilt is a central figure of the so-called "robber baron" era: biographers remain divided between admiration for his self-made energy and low fares, and criticism of his monopolistic practices and indifference to labour and safety. The Vanderbilt dynasty he founded became synonymous with Gilded Age wealth, building the "summer cottages" of Newport and Biltmore House in North Carolina, before the fortune dissipated across later generations.
