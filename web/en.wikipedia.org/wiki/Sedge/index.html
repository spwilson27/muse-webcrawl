Sedge

FamilyCyperaceae
Principal genusCarex
HabitatWetlands, grasslands, tundra, woodland

Sedges are grass-like flowering plants of the family Cyperaceae, a large family of monocotyledonous plants that also includes the papyrus sedge and the water chestnut. The largest genus of sedges is Carex, with roughly 2,000 species. Sedges are found worldwide, and are especially abundant in wetlands, moist grasslands, and arctic and temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

Identification

Sedges are often confused with grasses (Poaceae) and rushes (Juncaceae). A traditional mnemonic for distinguishing them is "sedges have edges": the stems of most sedges are triangular in cross-section and usually solid, whereas grass stems are typically round and hollow at the nodes, and rush stems are round and filled with pith. Sedge leaves are arranged in three vertical ranks, another distinguishing feature.

Ecology and uses

Sedges are ecologically important as food and cover for wildlife, as stabilizers of wet soils, and as dominant vegetation in many tundra and fen communities. Some species are cultivated as ornamentals or used as ground cover in gardens, while others are troublesome weeds in lawns and rice paddies. The ancient Egyptians used papyrus sedge (Cyperus papyrus) to make a writing material.
