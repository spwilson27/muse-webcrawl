Jan van Eyck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Arnolfini Portrait (1434): a merchant and his wife in a Bruges interior, reflected in a convex mirror inscribed 'Jan van Eyck was here']

Jan van Eyck (c. 1390 – 9 July 1441) was a Flemish painter active in Bruges, one of the founders of Early Netherlandish painting and the most famous Northern Renaissance artist of the fifteenth century. Court painter and valet de chambre to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, he perfected the oil medium — translucent glazes over precise underdrawing — and filled his panels with jewels of light, texture and symbolism.

His masterpieces are the Ghent Altarpiece (completed 1432, with his brother Hubert), the Arnolfini Portrait (1434), the Madonna of Chancellor Rolin and the Lucca Madonna; his motto, inscribed on frames, was Als Ich Can — 'As I can'.

Court painter in Bruges

Van Eyck entered the Burgundian court in 1425 and undertook diplomatic missions for the duke, including journeys to Portugal to paint the Infanta Isabella's portrait. In Bruges, then northern Europe's richest trading city, he ran a workshop producing altarpieces and portraits for courtiers, Italian merchants and the duke himself.

Technique and fame

Vasari wrongly credited van Eyck with inventing oil painting, but his layered oil glazes achieved an unprecedented luminosity and microscopic detail — single hairs, reflections in armour, the tiny reflected self-portrait in the Arnolfini mirror. The Ghent Altarpiece, looted repeatedly through history, is among the most frequently stolen artworks in the world, and its 'Just Judges' panel, taken in 1934, has never been recovered.
