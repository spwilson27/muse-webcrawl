John Owen (1616 – 24 August 1683) was an English Puritan theologian, Congregational church leader and academic administrator at the University of Oxford. He served as chaplain to Oliver Cromwell and is regarded by many as the foremost British theologian of the seventeenth century.

Life

Owen was educated at Queen's College, Oxford, but left the university during the Laudian reforms. After the outbreak of the English Civil War he became a parish minister in Essex and a prominent preacher before Parliament. He moved from Presbyterian to Congregational (Independent) convictions and became Cromwell's chaplain, accompanying him on campaigns in Ireland and Scotland.

Oxford

In 1651 Owen became Dean of Christ Church, Oxford, and in 1652 Vice-Chancellor of the university, posts he held through the Commonwealth. He defended academic standards, opposed the imposition of a national Presbyterian church settlement, and promoted the cause of religious toleration for Protestant dissenters within limits. After the Restoration of Charles II in 1660 he was ejected from his deanery and spent much of his remaining life preaching to a London congregation and writing.

Works

Owen's vast output — his collected works run to many volumes — includes The Death of Death in the Death of Christ (1648), a defence of definite atonement; Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers (1656); Of Communion with God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost (1657); and the enormous commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews. His writings on the Holy Spirit and on indwelling sin have remained influential in Reformed theology.
