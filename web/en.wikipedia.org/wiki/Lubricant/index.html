Lubricant

Lubricant
PurposeReduce friction and wear between moving surfaces
Common formsOils, greases, dry films, gases
Base stocksMineral, synthetic, vegetable oils

A lubricant is a substance used to reduce friction between surfaces in mutual contact, which ultimately reduces the heat generated when the surfaces move. It may also transmit forces, transport foreign particles, or heat or cool the surfaces. The property of reducing friction is known as lubricity.

Types

Liquid lubricants include engine oils, hydraulic fluids, and cutting oils, formulated from mineral or synthetic base stocks with additives such as detergents, dispersants, anti-wear agents (for example ZDDP), and viscosity-index improvers. Greases are oils thickened with soaps or other agents for applications where oil would drain away. Solid lubricants such as graphite, molybdenum disulfide, and PTFE serve at extreme temperatures or in vacuum.

Lubrication regimes

Depending on film thickness relative to surface roughness, lubricated contacts operate in the boundary, mixed, elastohydrodynamic, or full hydrodynamic regimes, summarized by the Stribeck curve. In hydrodynamic lubrication a pressurized film fully separates the surfaces, as in journal bearings.

Applications

Lubricants are essential to internal combustion engines, gearboxes, turbines, compressors, machine tools, and countless industrial machines. Specialty lubricants include food-grade products, biodegradable oils, and personal lubricants.
