Bremsstrahlung

Bremsstrahlung (German for "braking radiation") is electromagnetic radiation produced by the deceleration of a charged particle when it is deflected by another charged particle, typically an electron passing near an atomic nucleus. The moving particle loses kinetic energy, which is converted into a photon, satisfying energy conservation. The spectrum of bremsstrahlung is continuous up to a maximum photon energy equal to the initial kinetic energy of the particle.

Mechanism and spectrum

When a fast electron passes close to a nucleus, the Coulomb attraction accelerates it, and an accelerating charge radiates. Because the deflection can be large or small, the emitted photon energies form a continuum from zero up to the Duane–Hunt limit, beyond which single-step stopping cannot occur. The emitted intensity increases with the atomic number (Z) of the target material — roughly as Z² — which is why heavy elements such as tungsten are used as X-ray tube targets. Thermal bremsstrahlung from hot plasmas (free–free emission) is an important cooling and diagnostic process in astrophysics, for example in the intracluster medium of galaxy clusters.

Applications and significance

Bremsstrahlung is the principal source of X-rays in X-ray tubes: electrons accelerated onto a metal anode radiate a continuous spectrum with characteristic lines superimposed. In radiation physics it is also a dose and shielding concern — energetic beta particles and electron beams generate penetrating bremsstrahlung in surrounding materials, so low-Z materials are preferred as the first shielding layer. In astrophysics, inverse bremsstrahlung and free–free absorption affect radio propagation through ionized gas.

Related processes

Synchrotron radiation and cyclotron radiation are produced by charged particles accelerated in magnetic fields, while Cherenkov radiation arises from particles exceeding the phase velocity of light in a medium. In quantum electrodynamics, bremsstrahlung appears as radiation accompanying scattering processes, and soft-photon emission plays a role in resolving infrared divergences.
