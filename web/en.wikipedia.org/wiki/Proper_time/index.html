Proper time

In relativity, proper time (from the Latin proprius, "one's own") along a timelike world line is defined as the time as measured by a clock following that line. It is independent of coordinates and is a Lorentz scalar. The proper time interval between two events on a world line is the time between the events as measured in the rest frame of an observer who is present at both — in other words, the time shown by a single clock that passes through both events.

Proper time is measured along an object's own world line

Mathematical definition

In special relativity, for an object moving with velocity v(t) in an inertial frame, the infinitesimal proper time is dτ = dt √(1 − v²/c²), and the total proper time between two events is the integral of this quantity along the world line. In general relativity, proper time is defined through the spacetime metric: dτ² = −(1/c²) gμν dxμ dxν (with sign convention varying by author), integrated along the world line. Because of this definition, proper time is sometimes described as the invariant "length" of a timelike path in spacetime.

Consequences

Because a moving clock accumulates proper time more slowly than the coordinate time of the frame in which it moves, proper time explains time dilation and the twin paradox: the travelling twin's world line between departure and reunion has a shorter proper time than the stay-at-home twin's, so the traveller returns younger. Experimental confirmations include the extended lifetimes of fast-moving muons and the clock corrections applied to satellites of the Global Positioning System, which must account for both special- and general-relativistic effects on proper time.

History

The concept was introduced by Hermann Minkowski in his 1908 work on spacetime, where he called it Eigenzeit ("own time"). It became central to Einstein's general theory of relativity (1915), in which freely falling objects follow geodesics that locally maximise proper time between events.
