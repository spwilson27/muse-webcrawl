The Hawker Siddeley Harrier, known informally as the "Jump Jet", is a British-designed military jet aircraft capable of vertical/short takeoff and landing (V/STOL). Developed in the 1960s, it was the first of the Harrier family and entered service with the Royal Air Force in 1969 — the first operational fixed-wing aircraft anywhere with V/STOL capability. Its single Rolls-Royce Pegasus turbofan engine directs thrust through four rotating nozzles, allowing the aircraft to hover, take off vertically, or roll along a short runway with a heavier load.

Development

The Harrier grew out of the Hawker P.1127 experimental aircraft, which first hovered (tethered) in 1960, and its refined successor, the Kestrel FGA.1, evaluated by a tripartite British–American–West German squadron in 1965. Hawker Siddeley developed the design into the production Harrier GR.1, which first flew on 28 December 1967 and entered RAF service on 1 April 1969, equipping No. 1 Squadron at RAF Wittering. The US Marine Corps, impressed by the concept for close air support from small ships and forward sites, ordered the type as the AV-8A.

Design

The Pegasus engine's fan flow and exhaust are ducted to four swivelling nozzles that rotate together from horizontal (for forward flight) to slightly past vertical (for jet-borne flight and braking). At low speeds, where aerodynamic controls are ineffective, the Harrier uses reaction-control "puffer" valves at the nose, tail, and wingtips fed by engine bleed air. The bicycle-type main landing gear with wingtip outriggers is another distinctive feature. The first-generation Harrier was subsonic, carried two 30 mm ADEN cannon, and could lift about 2,300 kg of stores on short takeoff.

Operational history

RAF Harriers operated from dispersed sites in West Germany during the Cold War, ready to survive a first strike on airfields. The navalised Sea Harrier derivative flew from Royal Navy carriers and fought in the 1982 Falklands War, where Sea Harriers shot down 20 Argentine aircraft without air-to-air loss. First-generation Harriers were progressively replaced by the larger, more capable second-generation Harrier II (AV-8B / GR.5–9) developed with McDonnell Douglas. British Harriers were retired in 2010.
