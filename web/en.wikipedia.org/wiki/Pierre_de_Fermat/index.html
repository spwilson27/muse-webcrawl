Pierre de Fermat (French: [pjɛʁ də fɛʁma]; between 31 October and 6 December 1607 – 12 January 1665) was a French mathematician and lawyer at the Parlement of Toulouse. He is best known for his work in number theory, in particular for Fermat's Last Theorem, a conjecture written in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that remained unproved until 1994.

Life and career

Fermat was born in Beaumont-de-Lomagne, France, the son of a wealthy leather merchant. He studied law at the University of Orléans and pursued mathematics as an amateur throughout his career as a jurist. He corresponded with many leading mathematicians of his day, including Blaise Pascal, with whom he founded the theory of probability, and René Descartes, with whom he independently developed analytic geometry.

Contributions to mathematics

Fermat made foundational contributions to number theory, including Fermat's little theorem and the method of infinite descent, which he used to prove results about sums of squares and polygonal numbers. His work on maxima and minima anticipated the differential calculus, and in optics his principle of least time explained the laws of refraction. He also developed methods for computing areas under curves and tangents to curves.

Fermat's Last Theorem

Around 1637, Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of the Arithmetica that he had discovered a proof that no three positive integers a, b, and c satisfy the equation an + bn = cn for any integer n greater than 2. The claimed proof was never found, and the statement resisted all attempts at proof for more than 350 years, until Andrew Wiles published a proof in 1994–95 making heavy use of the modularity theorem for elliptic curves.

Legacy

Although he published almost nothing in his lifetime, Fermat's letters and marginal notes profoundly shaped the development of modern mathematics. He is regarded as the founder of modern number theory, and numerous theorems, numbers, and concepts bear his name.
