Korean Demilitarized Zone

The Korean Demilitarized Zone (DMZ; Korean: 한반도 비무장 지대) is a strip of land running across the Korean Peninsula that serves as a buffer zone between North Korea (DPRK) and South Korea (ROK). Established by the Korean Armistice Agreement of 27 July 1953, it is about 250 km (160 mi) long and 4 km (2.5 mi) wide, and is — despite its name — one of the most heavily militarised borders in the world.

Structure

The Military Demarcation Line runs through the centre of the zone; each side withdrew 2 km from it in 1953. The Joint Security Area at Panmunjom, where the armistice was negotiated and where the blue conference buildings straddle the line, is the only place where North and South Korean forces stand face to face. Two villages were permitted to remain in the zone: Daeseong-dong in the south and Kijŏng-dong in the north, the latter long alleged in the South to be an uninhabited propaganda façade. Both sides erected giant flagpoles in a 1980s rivalry sometimes called the "flagpole war".

Incidents and tunnels

The DMZ has seen hundreds of incidents since 1953, including the 1968 Blue House raid, the 1976 axe murder incident in the Joint Security Area, and repeated exchanges of fire. South Korea discovered four North Korean infiltration tunnels dug under the zone between 1974 and 1990; the Third Tunnel, near Panmunjom, is now a tourist attraction. Defectors have occasionally crossed the zone on foot, as in the dramatic 2017 dash by a North Korean soldier at the JSA.

An unintended nature reserve

Almost untouched by humans for seven decades, the DMZ and the adjoining Civilian Control Zone have become a de facto wildlife sanctuary sheltering red-crowned cranes, musk deer, Asiatic black bears and possibly Amur leopards, and are often proposed as a future peace park.
