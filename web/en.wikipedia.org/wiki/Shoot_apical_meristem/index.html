Shoot apical meristem

The shoot apical meristem (SAM) is the population of undifferentiated, dividing cells at the tip of a plant shoot. It generates all the above-ground organs of the plant — stems, leaves and flowers — while maintaining itself as a reservoir of stem cells. Together with the root apical meristem, it drives the plant's primary growth.

Structure

The SAM is typically a small dome of cells, often less than 0.3 mm across. In many seed plants it is organised into layers: the outer tunica (L1 and L2 layers, dividing mostly anticlinally) over an inner corpus (L3). Zones can also be distinguished by function — a slowly dividing central zone of stem cells, a peripheral zone where leaf primordia are initiated, and a rib zone producing stem tissue.

Regulation

Stem-cell identity in the SAM is maintained by a feedback loop between the homeodomain transcription factor WUSCHEL, expressed in an organising centre beneath the stem cells, and the CLAVATA signalling pathway (CLV1, CLV2 and the CLV3 peptide), which restricts WUSCHEL expression. Plant hormones — especially cytokinin, which promotes meristem activity, and auxin, whose local maxima trigger organ initiation — coordinate growth and patterning. Leaves arise in regular geometric patterns (phyllotaxis) at the meristem flanks.

Phase change

At flowering, signals such as the mobile protein florigen convert the vegetative SAM into an inflorescence meristem, which then produces floral meristems — a switch governed by genes including LEAFY and APETALA1.
