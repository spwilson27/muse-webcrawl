Pileus (hat)

The brimless felt pileus of the ancient world

The pileus (Greek: πῖλος, pilos) was a brimless, conical felt cap worn in the ancient Greek and Roman world. Made of felt, leather or wool, it was the characteristic headgear of sailors, fishermen, travellers and workmen, and is frequently shown on figures such as Odysseus in ancient art.

Symbolism

In Rome the pileus acquired a powerful political meaning as the cap of freedom. At the ceremony of manumission, a slave being freed had his head shaved and was presented with a pileus, and the expression servos ad pileum vocare ("to call the slaves to the cap") meant to summon them to arms with a promise of liberty. After the assassination of Julius Caesar, Brutus issued coins showing a pileus between two daggers, celebrating the liberation of the Republic.

Legacy

The pileus is an ancestor of the Phrygian cap, the soft conical cap later worn as a symbol of liberty in the American and French revolutions, and echoed in the red "liberty cap" of revolutionary iconography. The shape also survives in the Albanian traditional felt cap, the qeleshe or plis.
