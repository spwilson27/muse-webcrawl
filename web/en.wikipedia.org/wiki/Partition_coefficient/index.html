Partition coefficient

In the physical sciences, a partition coefficient (P) or distribution coefficient (D) is the ratio of concentrations of a compound in a mixture of two immiscible solvents at equilibrium. Most commonly, one solvent is water and the other is 1-octanol, and the resulting octanol–water partition coefficient, usually quoted as its logarithm log P, is a measure of the compound's lipophilicity — its preference for lipid over aqueous environments.

Measurement and significance

Log P can be measured by the classical shake-flask method, by reverse-phase HPLC retention, or estimated computationally. For ionisable compounds the distribution coefficient D, which accounts for all ionic species at a given pH, is more relevant; log D at pH 7.4 is standard in drug discovery.

Applications

Partition coefficients govern drug absorption and distribution: Lipinski's rule of five suggests oral drugs generally have log P below 5. The same quantity predicts environmental fate — bioaccumulation in fatty tissues, sorption to soils, and the behaviour of pollutants in groundwater — and is used in pharmacology, toxicology, analytical chemistry and the formulation of agrochemicals.
