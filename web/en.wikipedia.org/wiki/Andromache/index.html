Andromache (Ancient Greek: Ἀνδρομάχη, "battle of men") is a figure of Greek mythology, the devoted wife of the Trojan prince Hector. She appears most memorably in Homer's Iliad, where her farewell scene with Hector at the Scaean Gates is one of the poem's most celebrated episodes.

In the Iliad

Andromache was the daughter of Eetion, king of Thebe in Cilicia, whose city was sacked by Achilles, who killed her father and seven brothers. In Book 6 of the Iliad she pleads with Hector not to return to battle, foreseeing his death and her own enslavement; Hector gently refuses, and the couple's infant son Astyanax is frightened by his father's plumed helmet.

After the fall of Troy

After Troy fell, Astyanax was hurled from the city walls, and Andromache was given as a war prize to Neoptolemus (Pyrrhus), son of Achilles, who took her to Epirus. Their children were said to include Molossus, ancestor of the Molossian kings. After Neoptolemus's death she married Hector's brother Helenus, who had become king in Epirus.

In later literature

Andromache is the central figure of Euripides' tragedy Andromache and appears in his Trojan Women. Racine's Andromaque (1667) made her story a classic of French theatre. She has come to symbolise the suffering of women in war and the fate of the conquered.
