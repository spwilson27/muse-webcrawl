Single-cell sequencing

Single-cell sequencing examines the sequence information from individual cells with optimised next-generation sequencing technologies, providing a higher resolution of cellular differences and a better understanding of the function of an individual cell in the context of its microenvironment. Whereas conventional ("bulk") sequencing averages the genomes or transcriptomes of millions of cells, single-cell methods reveal heterogeneity within tumours, tissues, embryos and microbial communities that bulk methods conceal.

Methods

The field began with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), first demonstrated in 2009: a cell is isolated — by micromanipulation, fluorescence-activated sorting, microfluidic droplets or microwells — its RNA is reverse-transcribed and amplified, with molecular barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) added so that reads can be traced back to their cell of origin. Commercial droplet platforms made it routine to profile thousands to millions of cells per experiment. Parallel methods sequence the genome of single cells (for mutation and copy-number analysis), assay chromatin accessibility (scATAC-seq), DNA methylation, and combinations of these ("multi-omics"), while spatial transcriptomics preserves each cell's location in the tissue.

Applications

Single-cell sequencing underlies large reference projects such as the Human Cell Atlas, which maps the cell types of the human body. It is used to trace developmental lineages, identify rare cell populations such as cancer stem cells, study immune repertoires (with paired T-cell and B-cell receptor sequencing), and characterise unculturable microbes from environmental samples.

Challenges

Because a single cell contains only picograms of material, amplification introduces noise and bias; RNA data are sparse, with many "dropout" zeros. Analysis therefore depends heavily on computational methods for normalisation, dimensionality reduction, clustering and trajectory inference.
