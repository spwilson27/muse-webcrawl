Julian's Persian expedition was a major invasion of the Sasanian Empire launched in 363 CE by the Roman emperor Julian. Intended to secure Rome's eastern frontier and win Julian a triumph to match his defence of Gaul, the campaign began with spectacular success — the army reached the Persian capital, Ctesiphon — but ended in retreat, Julian's death in battle, and a humiliating peace treaty.

Background

Since the 330s the Sasanians under Shapur II had pressed Rome's Mesopotamian frontier, capturing the fortresses of Amida, Singara and Bezabde. Julian, sole emperor from 361 after the death of Constantius II, determined to settle the eastern question by a decisive invasion, emulating Alexander the Great. In the winter of 362–363 he concentrated at Antioch an army of roughly 65,000–95,000 men, the largest Roman field army of the century, with a fleet of more than 1,000 supply ships and a river flotilla.

The advance

Julian divided his forces: some 30,000 men under Procopius and Sebastianus were sent to join the Armenian king Arsaces and advance from the north, while Julian led the main body down the Euphrates in March 363. The Romans stormed the frontier fortresses of Pirisabora and Maiozamalcha, crossed to the Tigris by canal, and defeated the Persians before the walls of Ctesiphon in late May. But the city was too strong to storm, the northern army failed to arrive, and Persian reinforcements were gathering. Julian's council decided to retreat up the Tigris — and the emperor ordered the great supply fleet burned, a decision that has been debated ever since.

Retreat and the death of Julian

Harried by Persian cavalry, scorched-earth tactics and summer heat, the army retreated northward through hostile country with dwindling supplies. On 26 June 363, at the Battle of Samarra, Julian rushed to counter a Persian attack without donning his breastplate and was struck by a spear. He died that night in his tent. The army acclaimed the guard commander Jovian as emperor.

Aftermath

Trapped with the army's survival at stake, Jovian accepted a thirty-year peace on Shapur's terms: Rome ceded the five Transtigritine provinces won in 298, the great fortresses of Nisibis and Singara, and abandoned its Armenian ally. Nisibis, a Christian city, was handed over and its population evacuated. The treaty was regarded in antiquity and since as a disgrace, and it defined the Roman–Persian frontier for generations.

Sources

The principal account is that of the soldier-historian Ammianus Marcellinus, who marched on the expedition and admired Julian. Libanius, Gregory of Nazianzus and later church historians offer sharply contrasting judgements.
