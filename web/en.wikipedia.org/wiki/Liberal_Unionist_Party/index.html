The Liberal Unionist Party was a British political party that was formed in 1886 by a breakaway faction of the Liberal Party led by the Marquess of Hartington (later the 8th Duke of Devonshire) and Joseph Chamberlain. The party split over William Gladstone's proposal to give Ireland its own parliament under "Home Rule". Liberal Unionists allied with the Conservative Party to oppose Home Rule and to maintain the political union between Great Britain and Ireland under the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.

History

When Gladstone introduced the First Home Rule Bill in June 1886, 93 Liberal MPs voted against it, defeating the bill and splitting the party. The rebels — Whig aristocrats around Hartington on the right and Chamberlain's radicals on the left — formed a separate organization and immediately entered an electoral alliance with Lord Salisbury's Conservatives. The alliance won the 1886 election and governed, with one interruption (1892–1894), until 1905; from 1895 the Liberal Unionists formally joined the "Unionist" government, with Chamberlain as Colonial Secretary driving an activist imperial policy.

Merger

Cooperation with the Conservatives deepened over tariffs and imperial questions, and local associations increasingly fused. In May 1912 the Liberal Unionist Party formally merged with the Conservative Party, which thereafter styled itself the Conservative and Unionist Party — a name retaining the Unionist legacy to this day. Historians see the split as a key stage in the Liberal Party's long decline and in the realignment of British politics around class rather than Ireland and religion.
