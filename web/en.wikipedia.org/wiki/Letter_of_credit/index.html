Letter of credit

Parties to a documentary letter of credit
PartiesApplicant (buyer), issuing bank, beneficiary (seller), advising/confirming bank
Governing rulesUCP 600 (ICC Uniform Customs and Practice)
Used inInternational trade finance

A letter of credit (LC), also known as a documentary credit, is a payment mechanism used in international trade in which a bank, acting on the instructions of a buyer (the applicant), makes an irrevocable commitment to pay a seller (the beneficiary) a stated sum when the seller presents specified documents — typically a bill of lading, commercial invoice, and packing list — that comply strictly with the credit's terms.

How it works

The buyer asks its bank to issue the credit in favour of the seller. The issuing bank sends the credit, usually through an advising bank in the seller's country, which may add its own confirmation. The seller ships the goods, assembles the stipulated documents, and presents them within the credit's validity. If the documents conform on their face, the bank must pay regardless of any dispute about the goods themselves — banks deal in documents, not goods. This principle of strict compliance protects sellers against buyer default and buyers against paying for unshipped goods.

Types

Credits may be revocable (rare since UCP 600 makes all credits irrevocable), confirmed or unconfirmed, payable at sight or at a future date (usance), transferable to a second beneficiary, revolving, or issued as standby letters of credit, which function like guarantees and are drawn only on default.

Legal framework

Most letters of credit worldwide are issued subject to the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits published by the International Chamber of Commerce, currently UCP 600 (2007). Standby credits are commonly issued under the ICC's ISP98 rules.
