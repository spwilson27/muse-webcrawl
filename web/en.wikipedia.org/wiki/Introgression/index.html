Introgression

Introgression, also known as introgressive hybridization, in genetics is the transfer of genetic material from one species into the gene pool of another by the repeated backcrossing of an interspecific hybrid with one of its parent species. Introgression is a long-term process, as it may take many hybrid generations for an appreciable amount of gene flow to occur, and it differs from simple hybridization in that foreign alleles become permanently incorporated—often adaptively—into the recipient population.

Repeated backcrossing transfers a small fraction of one species' genome into another.

Mechanism and detection

Introgression requires a fertile hybrid that backcrosses to one parent; selection then retains some introgressed segments while most of the donor genome is purged. Genomic methods—such as the ABBA–BABA (Patterson's D) statistic, admixture graphs, and analyses of linkage disequilibrium—detect introgressed tracts and estimate their age and adaptive significance.

Examples

  • Archaic humans — non-African modern humans carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry from interbreeding ~50,000–60,000 years ago, and some Oceanian populations carry additional Denisovan ancestry; introgressed alleles affect immunity, skin, and altitude adaptation (the Denisovan-derived EPAS1 variant in Tibetans).
  • Animals — wolf alleles in domestic dogs (e.g. the black coat K locus), coyote–wolf admixture, and adaptive introgression among Heliconius butterflies' wing-pattern genes.
  • Plants — crop–wild relative gene flow; wheat, maize and sunflower show extensive histories of introgression, which breeders also exploit deliberately to move disease-resistance genes into cultivars.

Significance

Introgression is now recognized as a significant source of genetic variation and rapid adaptation, complicating strictly bifurcating models of speciation. In conservation it may threaten rare species through genetic swamping (e.g. hybridization with abundant relatives), or, controversially, be harnessed in "genetic rescue".
