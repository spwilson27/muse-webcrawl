The American Scholar

"The American Scholar" is an oration delivered by Ralph Waldo Emerson on August 31, 1837, to the Phi Beta Kappa Society at Harvard College. Regarded as a foundational text of American intellectual independence, it called on American thinkers and writers to free themselves from European models and to trust their own minds. Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. famously called it America's "intellectual Declaration of Independence."

Content

Emerson divides the scholar's education into three influences: nature, the mind of the past (books), and action. He warns against book-worship and mere imitation, arguing that books are valuable only insofar as they inspire original thought — "Books are the best of things, well used; abused, among the worst." He urges the scholar to be "Man Thinking," a complete and active soul rather than "a mere thinker, or still worse, the parrot of other men's thinking." The address closes with a hopeful prophecy of an American culture yet to come: "We will walk on our own feet; we will work with our own hands; we will speak our own minds."

Context and legacy

The oration was published in pamphlet form in 1837 and later included in Emerson's Nature; Addresses, and Lectures (1849). Delivered together with his Divinity School Address of 1838, it helped define Transcendentalism and set the agenda for a distinctly American literature, influencing figures such as Henry David Thoreau and Walt Whitman.

The quarterly magazine The American Scholar, published by the Phi Beta Kappa Society since 1932, takes its name from the address.
