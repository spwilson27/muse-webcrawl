Self-incrimination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Self-incrimination is the act of exposing oneself to criminal prosecution by statement or testimony. The privilege against self-incrimination — the right of a person not to be compelled to testify against themselves — is a fundamental protection in modern legal systems, expressed in the maxim nemo tenetur se ipsum accusare ("no one is bound to accuse himself"), the US Fifth Amendment ("nor shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself"), the ICCPR (Article 14(3)(g)) and the case law of the European Court of Human Rights.

History and scope

The right emerged from resistance to the oath ex officio in the English ecclesiastical and Star Chamber courts — John Lilburne's refusals in the 1630s–40s are the celebrated origin story — and was entrenched by the late 17th century. In the US it grounds the Miranda warnings (1966) for custodial interrogation and may be invoked in any proceeding where answers could incriminate (hence "pleading the Fifth" before Congress); adverse inferences from silence are barred in US criminal trials, while England and Wales since 1994 permit specified inferences — a divergence much debated. The privilege does not generally protect documents' contents created voluntarily, physical evidence (blood, lineups), or corporate records — boundaries litigated in the digital era over compelled decryption and passwords.
