Controversies surrounding Yasukuni Shrine

Yasukuni Shrine controversies
LocationKudankita, Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan
Founded1869, by Emperor Meiji
EnshrinedAbout 2.46 million war dead, including 1,068 convicted war criminals, of whom 14 are Class A

The controversies surrounding Yasukuni Shrine concern the Japanese Shinto shrine in Tokyo that commemorates those who died in service of Japan from the Boshin War (1868) onward. The shrine's enshrinement in 1978 of 14 Class-A war criminals convicted by the International Military Tribunal for the Far East, its on-site museum's revisionist presentation of Japan's wartime history, and official visits by Japanese prime ministers and cabinet members have repeatedly provoked diplomatic protests from China, South Korea and other countries invaded by Imperial Japan.

The enshrinement of war criminals

In October 1978, the shrine's chief priest Nagayoshi Matsudaira enshrined 14 Class-A war criminals, including wartime prime minister Hideki Tojo, in a secret ceremony made public in 1979. Under Shinto doctrine, once enshrined a spirit cannot be removed or separated, which the shrine cites in refusing demands to remove them. Emperor Hirohito, who had visited the shrine eight times after the war, stopped visiting after the enshrinement; a memorandum by Imperial Household official Tomohiko Tomita published in 2006 recorded the emperor's displeasure at the enshrinement as the reason. Neither Emperor Akihito nor Emperor Naruhito has visited the shrine.

Prime ministerial visits

Official visits by prime ministers have been flashpoints since Yasuhiro Nakasone's 1985 visit on the 40th anniversary of the war's end. Junichiro Koizumi visited annually from 2001 to 2006, prompting China and South Korea to freeze summit meetings; each visit was upheld in practice despite court findings touching on the constitutional separation of religion and state (Articles 20 and 89 of the Constitution). Shinzo Abe's visit on 26 December 2013 drew criticism not only from Beijing and Seoul but a rare statement of "disappointment" from the United States. Successive leaders have often instead sent ritual offerings (masakaki) while abstaining from visiting.

The Yūshūkan museum

The shrine's museum, the Yūshūkan, presents exhibits that critics describe as glorifying Japan's militarist past — for example describing the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War as wars of self-defense and Asian liberation, and minimizing or omitting atrocities such as the Nanjing Massacre. The shrine also enshrines colonial subjects, including tens of thousands of Koreans and Taiwanese who served in the Imperial forces, some against the wishes of surviving families, raising further legal and diplomatic disputes.
