Dramatic structure

Dramatic structure (also called dramatic form) is the structure of a dramatic work such as a book, play or film. It concerns how a story is organized into acts and movements of exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution.

Dramatic structure
Field: Narratology, theatre
Key models: Aristotle three unities / acts, Freytag's pyramid, three-act structure

Aristotle and early theory

Aristotle's Poetics held that a tragedy has a beginning, middle and end forming a unified whole, with peripeteia (reversal) and anagnorisis (recognition). Horace later prescribed five acts.

Freytag's pyramid

Gustav Freytag, in Die Technik des Dramas (1863), analysed five-part structure: introduction/exposition, rise, climax, return/fall, and catastrophe. Modern screenwriting commonly condenses the pattern into the three-act structure — setup, confrontation, resolution — with turning points and midpoints, described by Syd Field and others, while alternatives include the hero's journey, kishōtenketsu, and non-linear forms.
