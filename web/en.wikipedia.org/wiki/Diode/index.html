A diode is a two-terminal electronic component that conducts current primarily in one direction (asymmetric conductance); it has low (ideally zero) resistance in one direction and high (ideally infinite) resistance in the other. A diode vacuum tube or thermionic diode is a vacuum tube with two electrodes, a heated cathode and a plate, in which electrons can flow in only one direction, from cathode to plate.

A semiconductor diode, the most commonly used type today, is a crystalline piece of semiconductor material with a p–n junction connected to two electrical terminals. Semiconductor diodes were the first semiconductor electronic devices. The discovery of asymmetric electrical conduction across the contact between a crystalline mineral and a metal was made by German physicist Ferdinand Braun in 1874.

Function

The most common function of a diode is to allow an electric current to pass in one direction (called the diode's forward direction), while blocking it in the opposite direction (the reverse direction). As such, the diode can be viewed as an electronic version of a check valve. This unidirectional behavior is called rectification and is used to convert alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC), as in rectifiers, and to extract information from radio signals in demodulators.

Current–voltage characteristic

A semiconductor diode's current–voltage characteristic can be tailored by varying the semiconductor materials and doping. Forward conduction begins in earnest at the knee voltage (about 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium). If the reverse voltage exceeds the breakdown voltage, the diode may conduct heavily in avalanche or Zener breakdown — an effect that is destructive in rectifier diodes but exploited deliberately in Zener diodes for voltage regulation.

Types

  • Rectifier diodes — for power conversion.
  • Zener diodes — voltage reference and regulation using reverse breakdown.
  • Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) — emit light under forward bias.
  • Photodiodes — convert light to current.
  • Schottky diodes — metal–semiconductor junctions with low forward drop and fast switching.
  • Tunnel diodes — exhibit negative resistance.
  • Varactor diodes — voltage-variable capacitance for tuning.

History

Thermionic diodes were invented by John Ambrose Fleming in 1904 as detectors for radio receivers. Crystal detectors (cat's whisker diodes) followed, and solid-state semiconductor diodes became standard after the development of purified germanium and silicon in the 1940s.
