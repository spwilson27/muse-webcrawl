Aniline

Aniline (from Portuguese anil, "indigo plant", + -ine) is an organic compound with the formula C6H5NH2. Consisting of a phenyl group attached to an amino group, aniline is the simplest aromatic amine. It is an industrially significant commodity chemical, used as a precursor to many materials, and a versatile building block in organic synthesis.

Properties

Aniline is a colourless to slightly yellow oily liquid that darkens on exposure to air and light. It has a characteristic amine odour and burns with a smoky flame. It is only slightly soluble in water but miscible with most organic solvents. As a weak base, it forms salts with strong acids; its basicity is greatly reduced compared with aliphatic amines because the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the aromatic ring.

Production and uses

Aniline is produced industrially by the catalytic hydrogenation of nitrobenzene, itself made by nitration of benzene. The great majority of aniline output is converted to methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) for polyurethane foams. Other uses include rubber-processing chemicals, herbicides, dyes and pigments. Historically, aniline launched the synthetic dye industry: in 1856 William Henry Perkin oxidised impure aniline while attempting to synthesise quinine and produced mauveine, the first synthetic organic dye, and August Wilhelm von Hofmann's students went on to develop the magenta and other "aniline dyes".

Safety

Aniline is toxic, readily absorbed through the skin, and causes methaemoglobinaemia, in which haemoglobin is oxidised and cannot carry oxygen. Chronic exposure in dye workers was historically linked to bladder cancer, an association that spurred early occupational-health regulation.
