Corbel

Corbel
TypeArchitectural structural element
MaterialsStone, brick, timber, metal
RelatedBracket, corbel arch, corbel table, machicolation

In architecture, a corbel is a structural piece of stone, wood or metal jutting from a wall to carry a superincumbent weight, a type of bracket. A corbel is a solid piece of material in the wall, whereas a console is a piece applied to the structure. A piece of timber projecting in the same way was called a "tassel" or a "bragger" in England. The technique of corbelling, where rows of corbels deeply keyed inside a wall support a projecting wall or parapet, has been used since Neolithic times.

Corbel tables and arches

A row of corbels supporting a parapet or the eaves of a roof forms a corbel table, a common feature of Romanesque and Gothic churches, often carved with grotesque heads and figures — the hundreds of carved corbels of Romanesque churches in England and France are prized records of medieval sculpture. The corbel arch (or corbelled vault) is built by offsetting successive courses of masonry inward until they meet, a technique used in Mycenaean tholoi such as the Treasury of Atreus, Egyptian and Maya pyramids and temples, and Neolithic passage tombs such as Maeshowe. Unlike a true arch, a corbel arch does not convert load into lateral thrust in the same way and is weaker for a given span.

Decorative use

Corbels support oriels, balconies, chimney breasts, mantelpieces and clocks, and are frequently ornamented — carved in Gothic work with foliage, angels, or grotesques, and in classical orders with scroll-like profiles. In timber framing, corbelled jettying projected upper storeys over the street. Machicolations — defensive floor openings over castle gateways — were carried on corbels.
