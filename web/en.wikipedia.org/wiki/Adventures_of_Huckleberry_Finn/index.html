Adventures of Huckleberry Finn is a novel by American author Mark Twain (Samuel Clemens), published in the United Kingdom in December 1884 and in the United States in February 1885. It is widely regarded as one of the great American novels and a central work of American literature.

Plot

The novel is narrated in the first person by Huckleberry "Huck" Finn, a boy of about thirteen living in the fictional town of St. Petersburg, Missouri, on the Mississippi River in the antebellum South. To escape his abusive, drunken father, Huck fakes his own murder and flees to Jackson's Island, where he meets Jim, an enslaved man who has run away after learning he is to be sold. Together they raft down the Mississippi, intending to reach the free states, and encounter feuding families, fraudsters (the "Duke" and the "King"), slave hunters and the moral contradictions of a slaveholding society.

The moral climax comes when Huck, believing that helping Jim escape is stealing and a sin, resolves nonetheless to go to hell rather than betray his friend — a decision often read as the novel's critique of the "civilised" conscience formed by a racist society.

Themes and style

Written largely in vernacular English — Missouri dialect rendered in Huck's own voice — the novel combines humour with a serious treatment of race, freedom, friendship and the conflict between society's rules and individual conscience. Ernest Hemingway famously wrote that all modern American literature comes from Huckleberry Finn.

Reception and controversy

The book was controversial from publication — banned in Concord, Massachusetts, in 1885 as "trash" — and remains debated for its frequent use of a racial slur and its depiction of race, though it is also widely taught and praised as an anti-racist work. Its final chapters, in which Tom Sawyer engineers an elaborate "evasion" of Jim, have often been criticised as a tonal retreat from the novel's moral seriousness.
