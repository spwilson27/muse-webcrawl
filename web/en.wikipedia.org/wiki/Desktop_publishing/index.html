Desktop publishing (DTP) is the creation of documents using dedicated software on a personal ("desktop") computer. It was first introduced in the 1980s for creating printed materials such as documents, presentations, and books, using the graphics capability of computers to produce near-typeset quality output. Desktop publishing software allows the user to place text and graphics on a page, see the result on screen as it will appear in print (WYSIWYG), and control typography with a precision previously available only to professional typesetters.

History

The technology came together in 1984–85: the Apple Macintosh provided a graphical interface and screen fonts; Adobe's PostScript page-description language encoded text and graphics resolution-independently; Apple's LaserWriter (1985) was the first widely available PostScript laser printer; and Aldus PageMaker, released in July 1985, gave users a pasteboard-style layout program. Aldus founder Paul Brainerd coined the term "desktop publishing". The combination undercut the cost of traditional typesetting by an order of magnitude, transformed magazine, newsletter and book production, and helped drive the Macintosh into creative industries.

Software and workflow

DTP documents combine text frames, images and vector graphics on pages arranged in spreads, with master pages, styles and colour management for print output (CMYK, spot colours, PDF/X). The field later consolidated around Adobe InDesign (1999), which displaced QuarkXPress as the industry standard; free and lower-cost alternatives include Scribus and Affinity Publisher. Desktop publishing skills overlap with graphic design and prepress, and the technology also enabled print-on-demand and self-publishing.
