Cough

TypeProtective reflex; symptom
PathwayVagus nerve; medullary cough centre
Duration classesAcute (< 3 weeks), subacute (3–8 weeks), chronic (> 8 weeks)
Common causesViral upper respiratory infection, asthma, reflux, post-nasal drip

A cough is a protective reflex that expels air suddenly and noisily from the lungs to clear the airways of mucus, irritants, foreign particles, and microbes. It begins with a deep inspiration, followed by closure of the glottis and contraction of the respiratory muscles, which raises intrathoracic pressure; the glottis then opens and air is expelled at high velocity — up to roughly 800 km/h in the narrowed airways. The reflex arc runs through receptors in the airways and the vagus nerve to a cough centre in the medulla.

Classification and causes

Coughs are described as acute (under three weeks, most often from a viral common cold), subacute (three to eight weeks, often post-infectious), or chronic (over eight weeks in adults). The leading causes of chronic cough are upper airway cough syndrome (post-nasal drip), asthma, and gastroesophageal reflux disease, together with smoking, chronic bronchitis, and ACE-inhibitor medicines. A cough may be productive (bringing up sputum) or dry; blood in the sputum (haemoptysis), whooping sounds in pertussis, and a barking cough in croup are diagnostically distinctive.

Complications

Violent coughing can cause rib fractures, urinary incontinence, hernia, syncope, and exhaustion, and coughing is a principal route for spreading respiratory infections via droplets and aerosols.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Over-the-counter cough medicines have limited evidence of benefit; honey eases cough in children over one year old, and codeine-containing antitussives are restricted because of side effects. Persistent, changing, or blood-stained cough warrants medical evaluation.
