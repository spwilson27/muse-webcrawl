Mandatory access control

Mandatory access control (MAC) is a type of access control in computer security by which the operating system or another authority constrains the ability of a subject (such as a process or user) to access or perform operations on an object (such as a file), based on a system-wide security policy. Unlike discretionary access control, the owner of an object cannot override the policy to grant access to others.

Model

In a MAC system, every subject and object is assigned a security label expressing a clearance or sensitivity level, often organised in a lattice of levels and compartments. A central policy — classically the Bell–LaPadula model, with its rules of "no read up" and "no write down" — determines which accesses are permitted. The policy is administered by a security administrator on behalf of the organisation rather than by individual users.

Implementations

Historically, MAC was associated with multilevel secure systems used by governments and the military to protect classified information. Modern general-purpose implementations include SELinux (Security-Enhanced Linux) and AppArmor on Linux, TrustedBSD on FreeBSD, and Mandatory Integrity Control in Microsoft Windows, which assigns integrity levels to processes and files. MAC frameworks can also enforce finer-grained type-enforcement policies that confine programs to the minimum privileges they require.

Comparison with other models

MAC provides stronger assurance than discretionary schemes because even a compromised or malicious program cannot exceed the policy, but it is more complex to administer. Role-based access control (RBAC) is a related approach in which permissions are attached to organisational roles rather than classification labels.
