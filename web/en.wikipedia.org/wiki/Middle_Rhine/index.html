Middle Rhine

The Middle Rhine (German: Mittelrhein) is the roughly 130 km (81 mi) stretch of the river Rhine in Germany between Bingen am Rhein and Bonn, where the river cuts a deep, winding gorge through the Rhenish Slate Mountains. The section between Bingen and Koblenz, the Upper Middle Rhine Valley, is famous for its dense concentration of castles, steep terraced vineyards, and picturesque wine towns, and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2002.

Geography

At Bingen the Rhine leaves the Upper Rhine Plain and turns northwest through the slate mountains, passing the famous Lorelei rock near Sankt Goarshausen, where the river narrows and its currents were once hazardous to navigation. At Koblenz it is joined by the Moselle at the Deutsches Eck, and continues past Andernach and Remagen toward Bonn, where the Lower Rhine begins. Notable tributaries include the Nahe, Moselle, Lahn, and Wied.

History and culture

The valley was a frontier of the Roman Empire and later a toll-riddled patchwork of ecclesiastical and noble territories; more than 40 castles and fortresses, many built to levy tolls on river traffic, survive along the gorge, along with towns such as Bacharach, Oberwesel, St. Goar, Boppard, and Braubach with its intact Marksburg. The romantic image of the Rhine gorge inspired nineteenth-century Rhine romanticism in poetry, painting, and music, and the region is a classic wine landscape, producing mainly Riesling from steep slate slopes under the Mittelrhein wine region designation.

Transport

The Middle Rhine is an important shipping route, kept navigable by blasting and training works, and both banks carry busy railway lines (the Left and Right Rhine lines) that thread through tunnels below the castles. No bridge crosses the river between Koblenz and Bingen, so ferries remain essential local links — a recurring subject of debate about a proposed Middle Rhine bridge.
