Roberto Calvi (13 April 1920 – 18 June 1982) was an Italian banker, chairman of Banco Ambrosiano, whose close association with the Vatican Bank earned him the nickname "God's Banker" (Il banchiere di Dio) in the Italian press. In June 1982, as Ambrosiano collapsed under the weight of some $1.4 billion in unaccounted debts, Calvi fled Italy and was found hanging from scaffolding beneath Blackfriars Bridge in London. Initially ruled a suicide, his death was later treated by Italian prosecutors as murder; trials of alleged killers ended in acquittals, and the case remains officially unsolved.

Career

Calvi joined Banco Ambrosiano in 1947 and rose under the protection of Michele Sindona, the Sicilian financier later convicted of fraud and murder. Becoming general manager in 1971 and chairman in 1975, Calvi built a web of offshore companies in Panama, the Bahamas, Nicaragua and elsewhere, borrowing heavily through them against Ambrosiano shares. He was close to Archbishop Paul Marcinkus, head of the Vatican Bank (Istituto per le Opere di Religione), Ambrosiano's largest shareholder, and to Licio Gelli, master of the clandestine Propaganda Due (P2) masonic lodge, to which Calvi belonged. In 1981 Calvi was convicted of illegal currency exports and given a suspended sentence; the exposure of the P2 membership lists that year shook the Italian establishment.

Collapse and death

In June 1982 the Bank of Italy demanded that Ambrosiano account for loans of about $1.4 billion made to the offshore shells. Calvi fled Italy on a false passport, reaching London via Austria. On the morning of 18 June 1982 a postal clerk found his body hanging beneath Blackfriars Bridge, his pockets weighted with bricks and cash in several currencies. A first London inquest returned suicide; a second, in 1983, recorded an open verdict. Italian forensic reinvestigations in the 1990s and 2000s concluded he had been strangled and his body suspended to simulate suicide. Five defendants, including Cosa Nostra boss Giuseppe "Pippo" Calò, were tried in Rome for the murder; all were acquitted in 2007, and acquittals were upheld on appeal. Banco Ambrosiano was liquidated; the Vatican Bank paid $244 million to creditors in 1984 while denying wrongdoing.

In popular culture

Calvi's story inspired the character of Frederick Keinszig in The Godfather Part III (1990) and numerous books and documentaries on the Vatican financial scandals; the Italian film I banchieri di Dio (2002) dramatised his life.
