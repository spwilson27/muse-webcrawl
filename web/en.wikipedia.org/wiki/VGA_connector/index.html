A VGA connector is a three-row, 15-pin D-subminiature connector (DE-15, often called HD-15) used to carry analog component video signals. It was introduced with IBM's Video Graphics Array standard on the PS/2 computers of 1987 and became the near-universal analog interface between computers and monitors for nearly three decades.

Signals

The connector carries red, green and blue analog video (0.7 V peak-to-peak into 75 ohms), horizontal and vertical synchronisation signals, and several auxiliary pins. Original implementations used some pins only for monitor ID bits; later VESA Display Data Channel (DDC) standards repurposed pins for a serial link allowing the monitor to report its capabilities (EDID) to the host.

Cable quality and resolutions

Because the signal is analog, image quality depends on cable construction and length; poorly shielded cables cause ghosting, especially at higher resolutions. VGA commonly carried resolutions from 640×480 up to 2048×1536 (QXGA) and beyond on good hardware, though quality degrades as bandwidth rises.

Decline

Digital interfaces — DVI, then HDMI and DisplayPort — offered better quality and thinner cables, and major manufacturers removed VGA ports from laptops and graphics cards during the 2010s. Intel and AMD announced in 2010 that they would phase out VGA support by 2015. The connector nonetheless persists in projectors, industrial equipment and legacy installations.
