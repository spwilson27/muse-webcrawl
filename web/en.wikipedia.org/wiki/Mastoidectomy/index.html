Mastoidectomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mastoidectomy is a surgical procedure in which the mastoid air cells in the mastoid process of the temporal bone, behind the ear, are opened and diseased tissue is removed. It is performed by an otolaryngologist (ear, nose and throat surgeon), usually through an incision behind the ear (postauricular) or within the ear canal, using a surgical drill under an operating microscope.

Indications

The operation is most commonly performed for:

  • Cholesteatoma — an abnormal growth of skin in the middle ear and mastoid that expands and erodes bone; removal of the disease is the mainstay of treatment.
  • Mastoiditis — infection of the mastoid air cells that does not resolve with antibiotics, or that has produced complications.
  • Chronic otitis media with infection or damage not manageable by lesser procedures.
  • Access — as part of the surgical approach for cochlear implantation, removal of acoustic neuromas (translabyrinthine approach), and repair of cerebrospinal fluid leaks or facial nerve decompression.

Types

Procedures vary in how much of the mastoid and ear canal wall is removed. In a canal wall up (closed) mastoidectomy, the posterior wall of the ear canal is preserved, maintaining normal anatomy but carrying a higher risk of residual or recurrent cholesteatoma. In a canal wall down (open, or radical/modified radical) mastoidectomy, the canal wall is removed and the mastoid cavity is exteriorised into the ear canal, creating a single cavity that is easier to inspect and clean but may require lifelong periodic care and can limit water exposure and hearing-aid use. Simpler cortical mastoidectomy (Schwartze operation) removes infected air cells without disturbing the middle ear contents.

Risks

Because of the structures around the mastoid, risks include hearing loss, dizziness from injury to the inner ear, facial nerve injury, infection, and recurrence of disease requiring further surgery.
