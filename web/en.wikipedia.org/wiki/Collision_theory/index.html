Collision theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collision theory
FieldChemical kinetics
Proposed byMax Trautz (1916), William Lewis (1918)
Requirements for reactionCollision, sufficient energy, proper orientation

Collision theory is a principle of chemistry used to predict the rates of chemical reactions. It states that when suitable particles of the reactants hit each other with the correct orientation, only a certain fraction of the collisions cause a noticeable chemical change; these successful changes are called successful collisions. To react, colliding particles must possess a total kinetic energy at least equal to the activation energy of the reaction.

Rate equation

For a bimolecular gas-phase reaction, collision theory gives a rate proportional to the collision frequency Z, the steric (orientation) factor ρ, and the fraction of collisions with energy above the activation energy, e−Ea/RT. This form resembles the empirical Arrhenius equation, with the pre-exponential factor A interpreted as ρZ. Raising the temperature increases both the frequency of collisions and, much more strongly, the fraction energetic enough to react.

Limitations

The theory works well for simple gas-phase reactions but often overestimates rates for complex molecules, whose orientation requirements are handled only crudely by the steric factor. More refined treatments, such as transition state theory, model the activated complex explicitly. In solution, diffusion and solvent cage effects further modify collision frequencies.
