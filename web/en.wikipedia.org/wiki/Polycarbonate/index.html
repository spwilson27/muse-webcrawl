Polycarbonate

Type: Thermoplastic polymer

Key properties: Transparent, impact-resistant, heat-resistant

Major trade names: Lexan, Makrolon

Polycarbonates (PC) are a group of thermoplastic polymers containing carbonate groups in their chemical structures. The most common, produced from bisphenol A and phosgene (or a carbonate ester), is a strong, tough, optically clear material used in a wide range of engineering applications.

Properties

Polycarbonate is notable for its very high impact resistance — far greater than glass or acrylic — combined with transparency comparable to glass and the ability to withstand temperatures from about −20 to 140 °C. It can be readily moulded, extruded and thermoformed. Its weaknesses include susceptibility to scratching (often mitigated by hard coatings) and to degradation by some solvents and alkaline cleaners; it also yellows slowly under prolonged ultraviolet exposure unless stabilised.

Production

Commercial production began in the 1950s, developed nearly simultaneously by Bayer in Germany (Makrolon) and General Electric in the United States (Lexan). The traditional interfacial process reacts bisphenol A with phosgene; a greener melt-transesterification route uses diphenyl carbonate instead.

Applications

Applications include compact discs and DVDs, eyeglass and sunglass lenses, safety glazing and bullet-resistant panels, riot shields, aircraft canopies, greenhouses, water bottles, electronic housings and automotive headlamp lenses. Because most polycarbonate is made with bisphenol A, its use in food-contact products — particularly baby bottles — has been restricted in several jurisdictions amid concerns over BPA leaching, and BPA-free alternatives are marketed for that segment.
