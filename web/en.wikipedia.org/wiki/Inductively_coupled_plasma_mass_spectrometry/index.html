Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) is a type of mass spectrometry that uses an inductively coupled plasma to ionise a sample. It atomises the sample and creates atomic and small polyatomic ions, which are then detected. It is known for its ability to detect metals and several non-metals at concentrations as low as one part per trillion on non-interfered low-background isotopes, making it one of the most sensitive techniques in analytical chemistry.

How it works

A liquid sample is nebulised into an aerosol and carried by argon gas into a plasma sustained by a radio-frequency induction coil, where temperatures reach roughly 6,000–10,000 K. The plasma dries, vaporises, atomises, and ionises the sample. Ions are extracted through sampler and skimmer cones into a high-vacuum mass analyser — typically a quadrupole — which separates them by mass-to-charge ratio before they strike a detector. The technique was commercialised in 1983, building on work by Alan Gray and by Robert Houk and colleagues.

Applications and limitations

ICP-MS is used for trace-element analysis in environmental testing, geochemistry, semiconductor quality control, clinical and forensic toxicology (for example, lead in blood), food safety, and isotope-ratio measurements; coupled with laser ablation it can analyse solids directly, and with liquid chromatography it can perform elemental speciation. Limitations include spectral interferences from polyatomic species (such as 40Ar35Cl+ on arsenic), matrix effects, and the cost of high-purity argon; collision/reaction cells and high-resolution sector instruments mitigate many interferences.
