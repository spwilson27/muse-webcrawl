Cinema of South Korea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The cinema of South Korea encompasses the film industry of the Republic of Korea, from its beginnings under Japanese colonial rule to its emergence, since the late 1990s, as one of the most dynamic and internationally acclaimed national cinemas. South Korean films command a large share of their domestic market — unusually high by world standards — and have won the highest honours at Cannes, Venice, and the Academy Awards.

Early history

Motion pictures were first screened in Korea in the early 1900s; the first Korean-produced film, Fight for Justice (1919), combined film with stage drama. Under Japanese occupation, filmmakers such as Na Woon-gyu (Arirang, 1926) worked under censorship. After liberation and the Korean War, a postwar golden age in the 1950s and 1960s produced directors like Shin Sang-ok and landmarks such as Kim Ki-young's The Housemaid (1960). The authoritarian decades that followed brought heavy censorship and decline, mitigated by the Korean New Wave of the late 1980s (Park Kwang-su, Jang Sun-woo).

Renaissance (1998–present)

Following democratisation and the Asian financial crisis, a generation of directors transformed the industry: Kang Je-gyu's blockbuster Shiri (1999) outgrossed Hollywood imports; Park Chan-wook's Oldboy (2003) won the Grand Prix at Cannes; Kim Ki-duk, Hong Sang-soo, Lee Chang-dong, and Bong Joon-ho gained worldwide followings. Bong's Parasite (2019) won the Palme d'Or and became the first non-English-language film to win the Academy Award for Best Picture. Korean genre cinema — thrillers, horror, melodrama — and a protected screen quota long supported domestic production.

Industry

The industry is centred in Seoul, supported by the Korean Film Council (KOFIC) and the Busan International Film Festival (founded 1996), Asia's largest. Major companies include CJ ENM and Lotte Entertainment. Korean cinema's popularity forms part of the broader Korean Wave (Hallyu).
