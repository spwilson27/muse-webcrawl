Surface mining

Surface mining, including strip mining, open-pit mining, and mountaintop removal, is a broad category of mining in which soil and rock overlying a mineral deposit (the overburden) are removed, rather than extracting the ore from underground workings. It is used when deposits lie close to the surface and is generally cheaper and safer than underground mining, though it disturbs much larger areas of land.

Forms

Open-pit mining excavates a stepped pit, with benches cut into the rock so that ore can be removed from successively deeper levels; many of the world's largest mines, such as the Bingham Canyon copper mine in Utah and the Super Pit gold mine in Australia, are open pits. Strip mining removes long strips of overburden to expose seams of coal or other bedded deposits, and includes area stripping on flat land and contour stripping on hillsides. Quarrying produces aggregate, limestone, and dimension stone. Mountaintop removal, practised chiefly in the Appalachian United States, uses explosives to remove hundreds of feet of summit rock to reach coal seams, with excess spoil placed in adjacent valleys. Placer mining recovers minerals such as gold from river gravels, sometimes by hydraulic methods or dredging.

Process

Vegetation and topsoil are cleared, overburden is drilled, blasted, and hauled away by some of the largest land machines ever built — dragline excavators, bucket-wheel excavators, and haul trucks carrying hundreds of tonnes. The exposed ore is then extracted, crushed, and processed. The ratio of waste to ore (the stripping ratio) largely determines whether surface mining is economic.

Environmental effects and reclamation

Surface mining destroys habitats, alters drainage, and can generate acid mine drainage when sulphide minerals are exposed to air and water. Spoil heaps and valley fills may bury streams. Modern regulation, such as the U.S. Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977, requires operators to post bonds and restore mined land approximately to its original contour and use. Reclamation ranges from regrading and revegetation to creating lakes, farmland, or industrial sites, though critics argue that original ecosystems, especially in mountaintop removal, cannot be fully restored.
