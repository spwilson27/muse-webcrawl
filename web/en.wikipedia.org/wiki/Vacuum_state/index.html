Vacuum state

Vacuum state
Field: Quantum field theory
Definition: Lowest-energy state of a quantum field
Notation: |0⟩
Related: Zero-point energy, Casimir effect, virtual particles

In quantum field theory, the vacuum state (also called the quantum vacuum or simply the vacuum) is the quantum state with the lowest possible energy. It contains no particles — the annihilation operators of every field reduce it to zero — yet it is far from "nothing": quantum fluctuations of the fields persist in it, and it carries the zero-point energy of every field mode.

Properties

By definition the vacuum is Lorentz invariant and has zero momentum, zero charge and zero values for all other quantum numbers that characterise particles. Nevertheless, expectation values of field products in the vacuum are non-zero, giving rise to observable vacuum fluctuations. The vacuum of quantum chromodynamics is particularly rich, containing condensates of quark and gluon fields, and in the Standard Model the Higgs field has a non-zero vacuum expectation value of about 246 GeV, which gives elementary particles their masses.

Observable consequences

  • The Casimir effect: measurable attraction between uncharged conducting plates caused by the exclusion of some vacuum modes between them.
  • The Lamb shift: a small shift in hydrogen's energy levels due to the electron's interaction with the quantised electromagnetic field.
  • Spontaneous emission of photons by excited atoms, stimulated by vacuum fluctuations.
  • Vacuum polarisation, which modifies the effective charge of particles at short distances.

False vacuum

A vacuum need not be the absolute ground state of a theory: a false vacuum is a metastable local minimum of energy that could in principle decay to a lower-energy "true" vacuum. Whether the electroweak vacuum of our universe is stable or metastable depends sensitively on the measured masses of the Higgs boson and top quark; current measurements suggest metastability with a lifetime vastly longer than the age of the universe.
