John Francis Welch Jr. (November 19, 1935 – March 1, 2020) was an American business executive and author. He served as chairman and chief executive officer of General Electric (GE) from 1981 to 2001, a period during which the company's market value rose from about $12 billion to roughly $410 billion, making him one of the most celebrated — and later, in some quarters, most criticized — managers of the late twentieth century.

Early life and education

Welch was born in Peabody, Massachusetts, to Grace and John Francis Welch Sr., a railroad conductor. He attended Salem High School and then the University of Massachusetts Amherst, graduating in 1957 with a degree in chemical engineering. He went on to earn a master's degree and a PhD in chemical engineering from the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign in 1960.

Career at GE

Welch joined GE in 1960 as a junior engineer in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, working in the plastics division. He rose rapidly through the company's chemicals and materials businesses, becoming a vice president in 1972 and vice chairman in 1979. On April 1, 1981, at age 45, he succeeded Reginald Jones as chairman and CEO, the youngest person to hold the posts.

Welch pursued an aggressive strategy of restructuring: GE would divest businesses that were not first or second in their markets, a doctrine often summarized as "fix it, sell it, or close it." He massively reduced the workforce — earning the nickname "Neutron Jack" — while expanding into financial services through GE Capital and media through the acquisition of RCA, which brought NBC into the fold.

Management style

Welch was known for the "vitality curve," a forced-ranking system that sorted employees into the top 20 percent, the vital 70 percent, and the bottom 10 percent, who were typically dismissed. He promoted candor, "work-out" sessions to reduce bureaucracy, and the Six Sigma quality program, adopted in 1995. His management philosophy was laid out in his bestselling memoir Jack: Straight from the Gut (2001) and in Winning (2005), co-authored with his wife, Suzy Welch.

Later life and legacy

Welch retired in 2001 and was succeeded by Jeffrey Immelt. His lavish retirement package, disclosed during his divorce proceedings, became a symbol of executive excess. In later years, as GE's stock declined and the company was broken up, assessments of Welch's legacy grew more ambivalent: critics argue his emphasis on short-term earnings and financial engineering left GE fragile. He died on March 1, 2020, of renal failure at his home in Manhattan.
