The River Bann (Irish: An Bhanna) is the longest river in Northern Ireland, flowing through the centre of the province. It is conventionally divided by Lough Neagh — the largest lake in the British Isles — into the Upper Bann, which flows from the Mourne Mountains into the lough, and the Lower Bann, which drains the lough northward to the Atlantic Ocean. The combined system is about 129 kilometres long, and its basin drains roughly a third of Northern Ireland.

Course

The Upper Bann rises in the Mourne Mountains in County Down, near the Silent Valley reservoir, and flows north-west through Banbridge and Portadown before entering Lough Neagh at its southern shore. The Lower Bann leaves the lough near Toome, passing through Lough Beg and past Kilrea and Coleraine, before reaching the sea between Portstewart and Portrush on the Atlantic coast. Only about a metre of fall over much of the Lower Bann's course makes it slow and navigable in stretches.

History and economy

The Bann valley is one of Ireland's oldest inhabited landscapes: the Mesolithic settlement at Mount Sandel, near Coleraine, dates to around 7000 BC and is among the earliest evidence of human habitation in Ireland. The river long supported famous salmon and eel fisheries; the eel fishery at Toome, managed by a local co-operative, was for centuries among the most productive in Europe. In the 19th century the Lower Bann was canalised with locks and weirs to aid navigation and to regulate Lough Neagh's level, and schemes to manage flooding of the lough shore remain a recurring political issue. The Bann also historically divided the counties of Antrim and Londonderry along part of its course and is a notable presence in Ulster's cultural geography — the traditional shorthand for communities "west of the Bann" reflects settlement patterns reaching back to the Plantation of Ulster.

Ecology

The system supports Atlantic salmon, brown trout, eels and coarse fish, and its wetlands around Lough Beg are protected bird habitats. Water quality pressures from agriculture in the catchment, including algal blooms in Lough Neagh, are a continuing management concern.
