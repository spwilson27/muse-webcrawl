Yupik peoples

The Yupik (also spelled Yup'ik) are a group of Indigenous peoples of western, southwestern and southcentral Alaska and the Russian Far East. They are related to the Inuit and Iñupiat peoples, and speak languages of the Yupik branch of the Eskimo–Aleut family. The name derives from yuk "person" plus -pik "real" — "real people".

Groups and languages

The principal Yupik groups are the Central Alaskan Yup'ik of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and Bristol Bay region; the Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) of the Alaska Peninsula, Kodiak Island and the Kenai coast; the Siberian Yupik of St. Lawrence Island and the Chukchi Peninsula in Russia; and the Naukan Yupik of the Bering Strait region. Central Alaskan Yup'ik is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in Alaska, with many children still acquiring it in villages of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, where immersion schools support its use.

Culture

Traditional Yupik life centred on subsistence hunting and fishing — salmon, seals, walrus, beluga whales and migratory birds — along with gathering of berries and greens. Men lived and worked for part of the year in the qasgiq, the communal men's house that was the centre of ceremony, teaching and winter festivals such as the Bladder Festival, which honoured the spirits (yua) of animals taken in the hunt. Storytelling, dancing with masks, and the winter "asking" festivals knitted villages together. Semi-subterranean sod houses provided shelter against the Arctic winter, and skin boats (umiaks) and kayaks (qayaq) were used for hunting and travel.

History

Yupik communities came into sustained contact with Russians in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and with American traders, whalers and missionaries after the 1867 Alaska Purchase. Epidemics, mission schooling and later boarding schools suppressed language and ceremony in many places, while the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 organised Yupik lands under regional and village corporations such as Calista and Bristol Bay Native Corporation. Today Yupik governments, schools and cultural centres work to sustain subsistence rights and revitalise language and dance traditions.
