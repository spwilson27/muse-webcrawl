Energy homeostasis

In biology, energy homeostasis, or the homeostatic control of energy balance, is a biological process that involves the coordinated homeostatic regulation of food intake (energy inflow) and energy expenditure (energy outflow). The human brain, particularly the hypothalamus, plays a central role in regulating energy homeostasis and generating the sense of hunger by integrating a number of biochemical signals that transmit information about energy balance.

Mechanism

Fifty percent of the energy from glucose metabolism is immediately converted to heat. Energy homeostasis involves both short-term signals, such as the stomach hormone ghrelin, which stimulates hunger before meals, and long-term signals such as leptin, secreted by adipose tissue in proportion to body fat stores, and insulin. These signals act on circuits in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus containing two populations of neurons: orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) NPY/AgRP neurons and anorexigenic (appetite-suppressing) POMC neurons.

Imbalance

When energy homeostasis fails, the result can be obesity (chronic positive energy balance) or starvation and cachexia (chronic negative energy balance). Rare genetic defects in the leptin–melanocortin pathway cause severe early-onset obesity, illustrating the importance of the system in humans.
