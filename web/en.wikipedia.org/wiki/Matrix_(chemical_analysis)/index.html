Matrix (chemical analysis)

In chemical analysis, the matrix refers to all the components of a sample other than the analyte of interest. The matrix can have a considerable effect on the accuracy, precision, and detection limits of an analysis — a phenomenon known as the matrix effect — because matrix constituents may interfere with the measurement of the analyte or alter its signal, for example by suppressing or enhancing ionisation in mass spectrometry.

Matrix effects and mitigation

Matrix effects occur across analytical techniques. In inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, co-extracted salts, lipids, and other compounds compete with the analyte during ionisation, causing ion suppression or enhancement. In spectrophotometry, matrix components may absorb at the same wavelength as the analyte. Analysts mitigate these effects by sample clean-up (such as solid-phase extraction), dilution, matrix-matched calibration standards, the method of standard additions, and the use of internal standards or isotope-labelled analogues that behave like the analyte in the same matrix.

Related concepts

A matrix-matched blank, a certified reference material with a similar matrix to the samples, and spike recovery experiments are standard tools for assessing matrix effects in method validation, particularly in environmental, food-safety, clinical, and pharmaceutical analysis where samples such as blood, soil, or wastewater have complex matrices.
