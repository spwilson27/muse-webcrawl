Body language

Body language is a type of communication in which physical behaviours, as opposed to words, are used to express or convey information. Such behaviour includes facial expressions, body posture, gestures, eye movement, touch and the use of space. The scientific study of body movement is known as kinesics. Body language exists in both humans and animals, much of it processed at a subconscious level, and it can complement, contradict or substitute for spoken language.

Components

Researchers generally distinguish several channels: facial expressions, which research associated with Paul Ekman suggests include some panculturally recognised displays of emotion; gestures, ranging from symbolic "emblems" to illustrators that emphasise speech; posture, indicating openness, dominance or defensiveness; oculesics, the study of eye behaviour; haptics, communication through touch; and proxemics, the use of interpersonal distance, a concept developed by anthropologist Edward T. Hall.

Interpretation and caveats

Interpretations of body language vary across cultures — eye contact, personal space and gesture meanings differ widely — and single cues are easily misread: crossed arms may signal cold or comfort rather than defensiveness. Popular claims, such as the often-quoted assertion that 93% of communication is nonverbal, misrepresent Albert Mehrabian's narrow experiments on feelings expressed in inconsistent messages. Scientific consensus is that body language is best read in clusters, in context, and against a person's baseline behaviour.

Applications

Body language awareness is used in negotiation, teaching, clinical psychology, acting, law enforcement interviewing and public speaking training, though claims of reliable deception detection from nonverbal cues alone are weakly supported.
