Congregationalism (also Congregationalist churches or Congregational churches) is a Reformed (Calvinist) tradition of Protestant Christianity in which each local congregation is autonomous and self-governing, acknowledging no ecclesiastical authority above itself. Congregationalists believe that Christ is the sole head of the church and that each gathered congregation of believers, covenanted together, has full authority to order its worship, choose its officers and manage its affairs. The tradition's motto reflects this autonomy: churches are bound in fellowship but not in hierarchy.

Origins

Congregationalism emerged in late 16th-century England out of Puritan dissatisfaction with the Church of England. Robert Browne articulated separatist congregational principles in the 1580s, and persecuted "Separatist" congregations fled to the Netherlands, where John Robinson pastored the Leiden congregation from which the Pilgrims sailed for Plymouth Colony in 1620. Non-separating congregational Puritans founded Massachusetts Bay Colony; the Cambridge Platform of 1648 codified congregational polity in New England, where Congregational churches were tax-supported establishments in Massachusetts until 1833.

Beliefs and practice

Congregational churches historically subscribed to confessions such as the Savoy Declaration (1658), a modification of the Westminster Confession. They practise believer's or infant baptism (most paedobaptist) and the Lord's Supper, ordain ministers chosen by the congregation, and govern through church meetings of the members. In the 19th and 20th centuries, English and American Congregationalism became theologically liberal in many of its bodies, pioneered the ordination of women — England's Constance Coltman was ordained in 1917 — and Antoinette Brown Blackwell had been ordained in an American Congregational church in 1853.

Unions and modern bodies

Many Congregational churches entered church unions: in 1957 the Congregational Christian Churches in the United States merged with the Evangelical and Reformed Church to form the United Church of Christ; in England and Wales most Congregationalists joined the United Reformed Church in 1972, while others maintained the Congregational Federation or the Evangelical Fellowship of Congregational Churches. Congregational churches founded historic educational institutions including Harvard and Yale universities and Oberlin College, and were early promoters of foreign missions through the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions.
