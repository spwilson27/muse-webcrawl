Relationship between religion and science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The relationship between religion and science concerns the disputes and interplay between the religious and scientific communities and the institutions, doctrines and methods of each. Scholars have proposed several models of the relationship, most famously Ian Barbour's fourfold typology of conflict, independence, dialogue and integration.

Models

The conflict thesis, popularised by John William Draper and Andrew Dickson White in the nineteenth century, held that religion and science are locked in inevitable opposition; most historians of science now regard it as oversimplified. The independence model, expressed by Stephen Jay Gould as "non-overlapping magisteria" (NOMA), assigns science the empirical realm and religion questions of meaning and value. The dialogue and integration models, characteristic of much work in theology and science, seek areas of contact — for example in natural theology, process theology, or studies of religious experience.

Historical themes

Debates recur around the trial of Galileo, the reception of Darwin's theory of evolution, creationism and intelligent design, cosmology and the Big Bang, and bioethical questions raised by modern medicine. Attitudes vary across religious traditions and between countries: surveys repeatedly find the United States more religious and more divided on issues such as evolution than most of Europe, while scientific communities worldwide contain both religious believers and non-believers.
