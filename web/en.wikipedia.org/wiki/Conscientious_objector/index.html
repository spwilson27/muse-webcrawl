Conscientious objector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A conscientious objector is an individual who claims the right to refuse to perform military service on the grounds of freedom of conscience or religion. Historically rooted in the peace churches (Quakers, Mennonites, Brethren — whose members faced imprisonment and execution for refusal across centuries), conscientious objection was progressively legalised through the world wars, as states moved from shooting or imprisoning objectors to providing alternative civilian service or non-combatant roles — Desmond Doss, a Seventh-day Adventist medic who refused to carry a weapon and won the Medal of Honor at Okinawa, is the canonical non-combatant case.

Modern status

The right is recognised in the case law of the European Court of Human Rights (under Article 9, since Bayatyan v. Armenia, 2011) and the UN Human Rights Committee (under ICCPR Article 18), while practice varies: many states abolished conscription altogether; some retain it with recognised objection procedures (Germany's civilian service tradition until conscription's 2011 suspension; Finland; Greece; Israel, where refusal by refuseniks is politically charged and rarely recognised); South Korea recognised objection only in 2018 after generations of imprisoned objectors (mostly Jehovah's Witnesses, who globally number many of the world's objectors in prison in states that jail them, including Eritrea, Russia and Singapore). Selective objection (refusing a particular war, as in Muhammad Ali's Vietnam case, won on other grounds in 1971) remains unrecognised almost everywhere — the line between conscience the law honours and conscience it calls politics.
