The Île d'Oléron (French: Île d'Oléron) is an island off the Atlantic coast of western France, in the department of Charente-Maritime. At roughly 174 square kilometres it is the largest French island in the Atlantic and the second-largest French island after Corsica. It is connected to the mainland by a road bridge, the Pont de l'île d'Oléron, opened in 1966 and for a time the longest bridge in France.

History

Oléron gave its name to the Rolls of Oléron, one of the most influential early codes of maritime law, promulgated in the 12th century and traditionally associated with Eleanor of Aquitaine; the code was widely adopted in northern European ports. The island changed hands during the Wars of Religion and was fortified under Richelieu and later engineers. The citadel at Le Château-d'Oléron is a major surviving fortification. In April 1945, French forces liberated the island from German occupation in one of the last operations of the Second World War on French soil.

Economy

The island is famous for oyster farming: the Marennes-Oléron basin, where oysters are fattened in shallow claires (old salt pans) that give them a characteristic green tint, is among France's most celebrated oyster regions. Salt production, fishing — centred on the port of La Cotinière — pine forestry, vineyards, and tourism are the other mainstays of the local economy. The mild climate, beaches, and cycling paths draw large numbers of summer visitors.

Geography and communes

The island is about 30 km long and 8 km wide, low-lying, with dunes, marshes, and the great Forêt des Saumonards pine forest. Its eight communes include Saint-Pierre-d'Oléron (the administrative centre), Saint-Georges-d'Oléron, Dolus-d'Oléron, Le Château-d'Oléron, La Brée-les-Bains, Saint-Denis-d'Oléron, Le Grand-Village-Plage, and Saint-Trojan-les-Bains. The Chassiron lighthouse stands at the northern tip.
