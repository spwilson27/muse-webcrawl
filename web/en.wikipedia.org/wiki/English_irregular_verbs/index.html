English irregular verbs

The English language has many irregular verbs, approaching 200 in normal use — and significantly more if prefixed forms are counted. In most cases the irregularity concerns the past tense (also called preterite) or the past participle.

Most English verbs are regular: their past tense and past participle are formed by adding the suffix -ed (or -d) to the base form, as in walk – walked. Irregular verbs, by contrast, form their past forms in other ways. English irregular verbs are mainly survivals of the Germanic strong verb system inherited from Old English, in which the past was marked by a change of the stem vowel (ablaut): sing – sang – sung, drive – drove – driven. Others are "weak" verbs that nonetheless developed irregular forms through sound changes, such as vowel shortening in keep – kept and teach – taught (with consonant changes as well).

Classes of irregular verbs

  • Strong verbs with ablaut: begin – began – begun; swim – swam – swum; break – broke – broken.
  • Verbs with vowel shortening and sometimes final-consonant change: buy – bought; think – thought; say – said; bleed – bled.
  • Verbs ending in -t in all forms: cut – cut – cut; put – put – put; set – set – set, where the expected -ed ending has disappeared.
  • Suppletive verbs, which combine forms from historically different roots: be – was/were – been; go – went – gone.
  • Defective and anomalous verbs, including the modal verbs, which lack some forms altogether.

Historical development

Old English had several hundred strong verbs organised into seven ablaut classes. Over the Middle and Modern English periods, many strong verbs became weak, adopting the productive -ed pattern — help, for example, once had the past holp. The process continues today: some speakers regularise forms such as dreamed where others keep dreamt, while a few verbs have moved in the opposite direction (American English snuck for sneaked). The most frequently used verbs in English are disproportionately irregular, which helps preserve their irregular forms against regularisation.
