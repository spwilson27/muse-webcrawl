Carpal tunnel

The carpal tunnel is a narrow passageway on the palmar (anterior) side of the wrist that connects the forearm to the hand. It is bounded by the carpal bones, which form an arch, and by the flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament), a strong band of connective tissue that spans the arch to form the tunnel's roof.

Contents

Through the carpal tunnel pass the median nerve and nine flexor tendons: the four tendons of flexor digitorum superficialis, the four of flexor digitorum profundus, and the tendon of flexor pollicis longus. The median nerve lies just beneath the retinaculum and is the structure most at risk from crowding within the tunnel.

Clinical significance

Raised pressure in the carpal tunnel compresses the median nerve, causing carpal tunnel syndrome — pain, numbness and tingling in the thumb, index, middle and radial half of the ring finger, often worse at night, and in advanced cases weakness and wasting of the thenar muscles. Treatment ranges from splinting and corticosteroid injection to surgical division of the flexor retinaculum (carpal tunnel release).
