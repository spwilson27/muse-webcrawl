Chibok schoolgirls kidnapping

On the night of 14–15 April 2014, members of the Islamist militant group Boko Haram kidnapped 276 female students, mostly aged between 16 and 18, from the Government Girls Secondary School in the town of Chibok in Borno State, northeastern Nigeria. The mass abduction drew worldwide condemnation and sparked the global #BringBackOurGirls campaign.

The abduction

The school had been closed for security reasons but students had returned to sit final examinations. Gunmen overcame the guards, herded the girls into trucks and set the school buildings ablaze. In the following days 57 girls escaped by jumping from the vehicles or fleeing into the bush, leaving 219 in captivity. Boko Haram leader Abubakar Shekau claimed responsibility in a video, saying the girls would be treated as slaves and married off.

International response

The slow initial response of President Goodluck Jonathan's government was widely criticised. The hashtag #BringBackOurGirls, started by Nigerian activists, was amplified globally — including by U.S. First Lady Michelle Obama — and the United States, United Kingdom, France, China and Israel offered surveillance and intelligence support.

Releases and aftermath

In October 2016, after negotiations mediated by the International Committee of the Red Cross and the Swiss government, 21 girls were released; a further 82 were freed in May 2017 in exchange for detained Boko Haram suspects. Other captives escaped, were rescued by the military, or were found in subsequent years, often with children born in captivity. As of the mid-2020s, around 90 of the Chibok girls remained missing. The kidnapping became a defining episode of the Boko Haram insurgency and spurred the Safe Schools Initiative for education in conflict zones.
