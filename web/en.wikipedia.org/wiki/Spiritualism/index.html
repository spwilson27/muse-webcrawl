Spiritualism


A séance of the kind popularised by the Spiritualist movement.

Spiritualism is a religious movement based on the belief that the spirits of the dead exist and have both the ability and the inclination to communicate with the living. It holds that the afterlife is an evolving place rather than a static state, and its adherents — Spiritualists — typically meet in churches or private homes for services featuring mediumship, healing, and messages from the departed.

History

The movement is conventionally dated to 1848, when the Fox sisters of Hydesville, New York, claimed to communicate with a spirit through mysterious rapping sounds. The phenomenon spread rapidly through the United States and Europe, attracting millions of adherents by the late nineteenth century, among them figures such as Arthur Conan Doyle. Interest surged again after the First World War, as bereaved families sought contact with the fallen.

Beliefs and practice

Spiritualism teaches the continuity of life after death, personal responsibility for one's actions, and the possibility of progress in the spirit world. Central practices include the séance, in which a medium attempts to relay messages from spirits, and spiritual healing. In Britain, the Spiritualists' National Union summarises the faith in Seven Principles received through the medium Emma Hardinge Britten.

Criticism

From its earliest years Spiritualism attracted accusations of fraud; professional magicians such as Harry Houdini campaigned against fraudulent mediums, and scientific investigations repeatedly failed to confirm mediumistic phenomena under controlled conditions.
