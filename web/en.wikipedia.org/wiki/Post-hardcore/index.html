Post-hardcore

Post-hardcore is a punk rock genre that maintains the aggression and intensity of hardcore punk but emphasizes greater levels of expression and experimentation — complex song structures, dynamic contrasts between quiet and loud passages, and emotive, often sung-and-screamed vocals. The genre emerged in the 1980s with bands such as Hüsker Dü and Fugazi, and evolved through the 1990s and 2000s into a broad umbrella covering emo, screamo, and commercially successful acts like At the Drive-In and My Chemical Romance.

First wave (1980s)

As hardcore's early-80s formula hardened, bands like Hüsker Dü, Big Black, Naked Raygun, and Washington, D.C.'s Rites of Spring and Embrace (with Ian MacKaye and Guy Picciotto) pushed toward melody and personal lyrics. Fugazi, formed in 1987, became the archetype: angular guitars, dub-influenced rhythm, dynamic restraint, and a strict DIY ethic centered on Dischord Records.

1990s development

The 1990s produced Helmet, Quicksand, Drive Like Jehu, Unwound, and the San Diego "graveyard sound," alongside the abrasive innovations of The Jesus Lizard. Sweden's Refused fused hardcore with politics and electronics on The Shape of Punk to Come (1998).

Mainstream era (2000s)

At the Drive-In's Relationship of Command (2000) broke the genre to a wider audience. A commercially dominant second wave — Thursday, Thrice, Underoath, Alexisonfire, Dance Gavin Dance — blended post-hardcore with emo and metalcore and thrived on the Warped Tour circuit, while the term's meaning broadened considerably in critical usage.
