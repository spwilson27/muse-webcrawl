Adonais: An Elegy on the Death of John Keats, Author of Endymion, Hyperion, etc. is a pastoral elegy written by Percy Bysshe Shelley for John Keats in 1821, after Keats's death in Rome that February at the age of twenty-five. It is widely regarded as one of Shelley's finest works and one of the greatest elegies in English literature. It was composed from late spring 1821 and published on 11 July 1821 in Pisa.

Background

Shelley had met Keats in England in 1816 and the two maintained a correspondence. Shelley believed, wrongly, that a savage review of Endymion in the Quarterly Review had hastened Keats's death from tuberculosis, and the poem's preface attacks the anonymous reviewer (in fact John Wilson Croker) in bitter terms. In his last letter to Keats, Shelley had invited him to stay in Pisa for his health; Keats died before the plan could be realized.

Form and content

The poem consists of 55 Spenserian stanzas — the form Edmund Spenser used in The Faerie Queene and Keats himself used in The Eve of St. Agnes. It follows the classical pastoral elegy tradition of Bion's Lament for Adonis (from which the title derives) and Milton's Lycidas: mourners including Byron, Thomas Moore, and Shelley himself — depicted as "a pardlike Spirit beautiful and swift" — grieve for the dead poet, before the poem turns to consolation, declaring that Adonais has become one with Nature and the eternal: "He is made one with Nature: there is heard / His voice in all her music". Shelley drowned the following year, aged twenty-nine.

Reception

Though little read at first, Adonais came to be ranked among the masterpieces of English elegy, admired for the grandeur of its closing stanzas, in which the poet imagines his own spirit drawn toward the soul of Adonais.
