Imagism

Imagism
Active: c. 1912–1917
Key figures: Ezra Pound, H.D., Amy Lowell
Manifesto: F. S. Flint, 'Imagisme' (1913)

Imagism was a movement in early-20th-century Anglo-American poetry that favoured precision of imagery and clear, sharp language. A forerunner of modernism, it reacted against Victorian and Georgian verbosity and abstraction.

Principles

F. S. Flint's 1913 statement, published with Ezra Pound's 'A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste' in Poetry magazine, laid down three rules: direct treatment of the 'thing', whether subjective or objective; absolutely no word that does not contribute to the presentation; and composition in the sequence of the musical phrase, not the metronome. Pound defined the Image as 'that which presents an intellectual and emotional complex in an instant of time'.

Poets and anthologies

Core figures included Ezra Pound, H.D. (Hilda Doolittle), Richard Aldington, F. S. Flint and, in its American phase under Amy Lowell, John Gould Fletcher and others. Pound's anthology Des Imagistes (1914) was followed by Lowell's Some Imagist Poets volumes (1915–1917). Signature miniature works include Pound's 'In a Station of the Metro' and H.D.'s 'Oread'.

Legacy

Imagism sharpened modernist verse, influenced the Objectivists and William Carlos Williams, and drew on haiku, Chinese poetry (via Pound's Fenollosa notebooks) and French Symbolism. See Modernist poetry.
