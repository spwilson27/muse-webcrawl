Christine Marion Korsgaard (born April 9, 1952) is an American philosopher and the Arthur Kingsley Porter University Professor Emerita at Harvard University. She is a leading figure in contemporary moral philosophy, best known for her work on Immanuel Kant, the sources of normativity, personal identity, and the philosophy of action and agency.

Education and career

Korsgaard received her B.A. from the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign in 1974 and her Ph.D. from Harvard University in 1981, where she studied with John Rawls. She taught at Yale University, the University of California, Santa Barbara, and the University of Chicago before joining the Harvard faculty in 1991. She was elected a Fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 2001 and a Corresponding Fellow of the British Academy in 2015.

Philosophy

The sources of normativity

In her 1992 Tanner Lectures, published as The Sources of Normativity (1996), Korsgaard addresses the "normative question": why we ought to do what morality requires. She defends a Kantian constructivist view on which moral obligations arise from the reflective structure of human consciousness — from our need to endorse our impulses under a practical identity that we can will to be a law for ourselves. The obligatory stands in contrast with competing accounts she surveys, including voluntarism, realism, and reflective endorsement.

Self-constitution and agency

In Self-Constitution: Agency, Identity, and Integrity (2009), Korsgaard argues that action is self-constitution: when we act, we unify ourselves into agents, and the principles of practical reason — Kant's categorical and hypothetical imperatives — are constitutive standards of action itself. Defective action is a failure to constitute oneself as a unified agent.

Fellow creatures

In Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals (2018), Korsgaard develops a Kantian defence of the moral standing of non-human animals, arguing that valuing our own animal nature commits us to valuing animals as ends in themselves.
