Michel Aflaq (Arabic: ميشيل عفلق‎; 9 January 1910 – 23 June 1989) was a Syrian philosopher, sociologist and Arab nationalist whose ideas gave rise to Ba'athism, the ideology of Arab nationalism and Arab socialism that shaped the politics of Syria and Iraq in the second half of the 20th century. With Salah al-Din al-Bitar and Zaki al-Arsuzi, he was a principal founder of the Arab Ba'ath Party.

Early life and education

Aflaq was born in Damascus to a middle-class Greek Orthodox Christian family. He studied philosophy at the Sorbonne in Paris from 1928, where he and his fellow Syrian student al-Bitar encountered Marxist and nationalist ideas that they fused into a new doctrine. Returning to Damascus, they taught at the Tajhiz secondary school and began organising the Arab Ba'ath ("Resurrection") movement, formally founded as a party in 1947.

Ba'athist doctrine

Aflaq's writings, above all the essay collection Fi Sabil al-Ba'th ("On the Road of Resurrection"), held that the Arab nation is one eternal nation with a single mission, divided artificially by colonial borders. Ba'athism's slogan — "Unity, Freedom, Socialism" — promised the unification of all Arabs in one state, liberation from foreign domination, and a socialist transformation. Aflaq, though a Christian, regarded Islam as the supreme cultural and spiritual achievement of the Arab nation, a view that helped Ba'athism appeal across religious lines.

Later life

After the Ba'ath seized power in Syria in 1963, Aflaq served as the party's secretary-general but was sidelined and then purged in the 1966 coup by radical officers. He left Syria and eventually settled in Iraq, where the rival Iraqi Ba'ath — which took power in 1968 — honoured him as the movement's founder, a role Damascus bitterly disputed. He died in Paris in 1989 and was buried in Baghdad with great ceremony.
