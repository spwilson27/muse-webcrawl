Boycott

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A boycott is an act of nonviolent, voluntary abstention from using, buying or dealing with a person, organization or country as an expression of protest, usually for moral, social, political or environmental reasons — organised withdrawal of economic and social relations intended to impose costs until behaviour changes. The word dates to 1880 and Captain Charles Boycott, the Irish land agent ostracised by his community in the Land War (the community's organiser, in a famous instruction, urged shunning rather than violence).

Forms and record

Boycotts run from consumer campaigns (the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955–56, sustained 381 days to a Supreme Court victory; farm-worker grape boycotts; anti-apartheid boycott and divestment, which business and universities joined and which historians count among the pressures ending the system) to state-level measures shading into sanctions, and cultural and academic boycotts — most controversially the BDS movement concerning Israel (from 2005), which has generated anti-boycott legislation in many US states and allied countries, itself litigated as compelled speech's mirror. Effectiveness research finds boycotts work less by direct sales damage than by reputation and elite signalling; legality varies (secondary boycotts are restricted in US labour law; boycotts as expression won constitutional protection in NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware, 1982). The etymology's moral — social death as nonviolent weapon — remains the tactic's logic and its critics' objection.
