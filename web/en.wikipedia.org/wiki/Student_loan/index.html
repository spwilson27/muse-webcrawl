Student loan

A student loan is a type of loan designed to help students pay for post-secondary education and the associated fees, such as tuition, books and supplies, and living expenses. It may differ from other types of loans in the fact that the interest rate may be substantially lower and the repayment schedule may be deferred while the student is still in school. Student-loan systems and their scale vary greatly between countries.

United States

In the United States, most student loans are issued under the federal student aid system (Title IV of the Higher Education Act), principally Direct Loans administered by the Department of Education, alongside a smaller market of private loans from banks and other lenders. Total outstanding U.S. student debt exceeds US$1.6 trillion, owed by more than 40 million borrowers, making it a major political and economic issue. Federal loans offer benefits such as income-driven repayment plans and, historically, limited forgiveness programmes; unlike most debts, student loans are generally not dischargeable in bankruptcy absent a showing of "undue hardship".

Other countries

The United Kingdom issues income-contingent loans through the Student Loans Company, repaid through the tax system once earnings exceed a threshold. Australia's HECS-HELP scheme similarly defers repayment through taxation. Some countries, such as Germany and the Nordic states, charge little or no tuition and thus have minimal student lending, while others rely on private lending at market rates.

Debate

Critics argue that heavy student debt delays home ownership and family formation and burdens borrowers who do not complete degrees; defenders of lending argue it expands access to education whose lifetime earnings premium exceeds the debt incurred. Proposals for large-scale debt cancellation in the U.S. have been repeatedly debated in Congress and the courts.
