Unconditional positive regard

Unconditional positive regard, a concept developed by the humanistic psychologist Stanley Standal in 1954 and elaborated by Carl Rogers, is the basic acceptance and support of a person regardless of what the person says or does, especially in a therapeutic context. Rogers regarded it as one of the necessary and sufficient conditions for therapeutic personality change.

In Rogerian therapy

In person-centered therapy, the therapist offers unconditional positive regard by valuing the client as a person without imposing conditions of worth — without judging the client's feelings, thoughts, or behaviour as good or bad. Rogers believed that when clients experience this acceptance, together with the therapist's empathy and congruence (genuineness), they can resolve incongruence between their self-concept and their experience and move toward psychological growth.

Critique and development

Rogers himself distinguished between unconditional regard for the person and approval of every behaviour; therapists can accept a client fully while not endorsing harmful actions. Critics from behavioural and psychodynamic traditions have questioned whether the Rogerian conditions are truly necessary or sufficient, and research on the therapeutic alliance suggests the quality of the client–therapist relationship is a strong predictor of outcome across schools of therapy. The concept has influenced education, parenting advice, and counselling practice well beyond humanistic psychology, and remains central to training in person-centred and experiential therapies.
