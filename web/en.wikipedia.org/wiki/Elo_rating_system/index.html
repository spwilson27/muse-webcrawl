Elo rating system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Elo rating system is a method for calculating the relative skill levels of players in zero-sum games such as chess. It is named after its creator, Arpad Elo, a Hungarian-American physics professor and chess master.

History

Elo devised the system as an improvement on the earlier Harkness rating system. The United States Chess Federation adopted it in 1960, and FIDE, the international chess federation, followed in 1970. The system has since been adapted to many other sports and games, including association football, American football, table tennis, board games such as Go and Scrabble, and esports.

Method

Each player has a rating, a number that changes after every game. Before a game, the expected score of a player is computed from the rating difference between the two players using a logistic (or, in the original formulation, normal) curve: a player rated 100 points higher than an opponent is expected to score about 0.64. After the game, the player's actual score (1 for a win, 0.5 for a draw, 0 for a loss) is compared with the expected score, and the rating is adjusted by a fraction of the difference, scaled by a development coefficient known as the K-factor.

Because the adjustment is proportional to the surprise of the result, a player gains many points by defeating a stronger opponent and few by defeating a weaker one, and ratings are zero-sum in aggregate.

Interpretation

A difference of 200 rating points implies the stronger player has roughly a 76% expected score. FIDE ratings classify players from novices below 1000 to elite grandmasters above 2700; the highest rating ever achieved is 2882, by Magnus Carlsen in 2014.

Issues and successors

Ratings can suffer from inflation or deflation over time and across rating pools, and the system does not account for uncertainty in a player's strength. Variants such as the Glicko and Glicko-2 systems add a rating deviation measure, and TrueSkill extends the idea to multiplayer games.
