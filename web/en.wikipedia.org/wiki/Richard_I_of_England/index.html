Richard I of England

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Effigy of Richard I from his tomb at Fontevraud Abbey]

Richard I (8 September 1157 – 6 April 1199), known as Richard the Lionheart, was King of England from 1189 until his death. Son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, he was a renowned military commander — the outstanding Christian leader of the Third Crusade — but spent barely six months of his ten-year reign in England, which he treated largely as a source of revenue for crusading and for war against Philip II of France. Captured on his way home from crusade and ransomed for an enormous sum, he died of a crossbow wound at Châlus in 1199 and was succeeded by his brother John.

The Third Crusade

Crowned in 1189 amid anti-Jewish riots he tried to punish, Richard raised unprecedented sums, took Messina and Cyprus en route (conquering the latter from its Byzantine ruler), and landed at Acre in 1191. He retook the coastal cities, defeated Saladin at Arsuf, and twice advanced within sight of Jerusalem, judging it untenable to hold. The Treaty of Jaffa (1192) secured the coast and pilgrim access; Richard sailed home in disguise, was arrested near Vienna by Duke Leopold of Austria, and held by the Emperor Henry VI until England paid 100,000 marks in ransom (1194).

Reputation

Contemporaries admired Richard's generalship and personal courage — even Saladin's court respected him — while chroniclers in England noted the crushing taxation of his reign. The Robin Hood legends later attached themselves to his absence. Modern historians debate his kingship: a gifted soldier and negligent ruler of England, though his government by able ministers (notably Hubert Walter) functioned well without him.
