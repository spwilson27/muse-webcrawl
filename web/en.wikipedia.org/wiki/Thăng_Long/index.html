Thăng Long

Thăng Long (literally "ascending dragon") is the former name of Hanoi, the capital of Vietnam, used for much of the period from the eleventh to the nineteenth century. The name is famously associated with the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long, whose central sector was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2010.

History

In 1010, King Lý Thái Tổ, founder of the Lý dynasty, issued the Edict on the Transfer of the Capital, moving the capital from Hoa Lư to the citadel of Đại La on the Red River. According to tradition, as the king arrived he saw a golden dragon ascending from the river, and he renamed the city Thăng Long, "ascending dragon". The city remained the political centre of Vietnam under the Lý, Trần, and later Lê dynasties, though the capital was interrupted when the Hồ dynasty moved it to Tây Đô at the end of the fourteenth century and during the period of the Tây Sơn.

Under the Nguyễn dynasty the capital was moved to Huế in 1802, and in 1831 Emperor Minh Mạng renamed the city Hà Nội ("city inside the river bend"). The citadel continued to serve as a military centre; during the French colonial period parts were demolished, and the site later housed the headquarters of the People's Army of Vietnam.

Imperial Citadel

Excavations beginning in 2002 at 18 Hoàng Diệu Street revealed extensive foundations of palaces from successive dynasties, layered one atop another. The central sector of the citadel, covering about 18 hectares, preserves remains including the Đoan Môn gate, the flag tower of Hanoi, and the foundations of the Kính Thiên Palace. In 2010, during the millennial anniversary of Hanoi, the central sector was recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site for its long cultural history and its testimony to Vietnamese independence.
