Jelling stones

The Jelling stones (Danish: Jellingstenene) are massive carved runestones from the 10th century, found at the town of Jelling in Denmark. Between the two stones stand Jelling Church and two great burial mounds. The stones and site, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1994, are considered among the most important monuments of the Viking Age and of Denmark's transition to Christianity.

The smaller stone

The older, smaller stone was raised by King Gorm the Old in memory of his wife Thyra. Its inscription reads: "King Gorm made this monument in memory of Thyra, his wife, Denmark's adornment."

The larger stone

The larger stone was raised by Gorm's son, Harald Bluetooth, in memory of his parents and in celebration of his own achievements. Its inscription proclaims: "King Harald ordered this monument made in memory of Gorm, his father, and in memory of Thyra, his mother; that Harald who won for himself all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian."

The stone, sometimes called "Denmark's birth certificate", bears on its faces a runic inscription, an interlaced animal ornament, and one of the oldest Scandinavian depictions of Christ. Together with the burial mounds and the church it marks the royal seat from which Denmark's first kings ruled, and symbolises the country's shift from Norse paganism to Christianity around 965.
