Lentil

The lentil (Lens culinaris or Vicia lens) is a bushy annual plant of the legume family, grown for its lens-shaped edible seeds. It is one of the oldest domesticated crops, cultivated since the Neolithic period in the Near East, and remains a staple food in South Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean. Lentils are about 25 percent protein and are a rich source of iron, folate, and dietary fibre.

Description and cultivation

The plant grows 15 to 45 cm tall with pinnate leaves and small white to pale violet flowers. The pods contain one or two seeds, 2–7 mm across, which may be green, brown, red, yellow, or black depending on variety. Lentils are typically grown in cool-season rotations on well-drained soils and, like other legumes, fix atmospheric nitrogen through root-nodule bacteria, improving soil fertility. The largest producers include Canada, India, Australia, and Turkey; Canada's prairie provinces supply much of the world export market.

Culinary use

Lentils are used in soups, stews, salads, and curries. In Indian cuisine they are a principal form of dal, split and cooked with spices. Red lentils cook quickly and disintegrate into a puree, while green and brown lentils hold their shape. The French Puy lentil (from Le Puy-en-Velay) holds protected geographical status. Unlike many dried legumes, lentils require no pre-soaking and cook in 15–30 minutes.

History

Archaeological remains from sites such as Franchthi Cave in Greece and Tell Ramad in Syria show lentils were eaten by around 11,000 years ago and domesticated among the founding crops of Neolithic agriculture in the Fertile Crescent. In the Hebrew Bible, Esau sells his birthright for a "mess of pottage" traditionally identified as lentil stew.
