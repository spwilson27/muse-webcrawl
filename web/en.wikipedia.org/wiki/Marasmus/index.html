Marasmus

SpecialtyPaediatrics, nutrition
SymptomsSevere weight loss, muscle wasting, growth failure, loss of subcutaneous fat
CausesSevere deficiency of calories (and protein) in the diet
Risk factorsPoverty, famine, abrupt weaning, infection

Marasmus is a form of severe malnutrition characterised by energy deficiency. It can occur in anyone with severe malnutrition but usually occurs in children. Body weight is reduced to less than 62% of the expected body weight for the age. Marasmus occurrence increases prior to age 1, whereas kwashiorkor — the other major form of severe protein–energy malnutrition — occurs after 18 months. The prognosis is better than it is for kwashiorkor, which is marked by oedema; marasmus is sometimes called the "non-oedematous" form of severe malnutrition.

Signs and symptoms

A child with marasmus looks emaciated: body weight may be less than 60% of the expected weight for age, growth is stunted, and subcutaneous fat and muscle are wasted, giving prominent ribs, loose folds of skin, and an alert but miserable, "old man" face. Unlike in kwashiorkor, the appetite may be preserved and hair changes are less pronounced; there is no oedema and no fatty liver enlargement. Chronic diarrhoea, respiratory infections, and dehydration commonly accompany the condition, and the child may be hypothermic and bradycardic as the body conserves energy.

Causes

Marasmus is caused by an overall deficiency of food energy — from inadequate intake of protein, carbohydrates, and fats alike — in contrast to the relatively protein-deficient diet implicated in kwashiorkor. It typically arises where infants are weaned early onto dilute, starchy foods, in famine and extreme poverty, or where malabsorption or chronic disease prevents adequate nutrition. The body's adaptation includes breakdown of fat stores and skeletal muscle, slowing of growth, and suppression of metabolic rate.

Treatment

Treatment follows the staged approach used for severe acute malnutrition (such as the WHO ten-step protocol): first stabilisation — correcting hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbances and treating infection — with cautious, low-energy, low-protein therapeutic feeds (e.g. F-75), because rapid feeding can precipitate potentially fatal refeeding syndrome. Once stabilised, the child moves to rehabilitation with energy-dense feeds (e.g. F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food) to achieve catch-up growth, along with micronutrient supplementation, especially vitamin A, zinc, and iron (iron withheld until the rehabilitation phase).

Epidemiology

Marasmus and other forms of severe acute malnutrition remain major causes of death in children under five in low-income regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, often precipitated by the combination of food insecurity and infectious disease.
