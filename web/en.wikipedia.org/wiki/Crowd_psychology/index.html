Crowd psychology

Crowd psychology (also mob psychology) is a branch of social psychology concerned with how the psychology of a crowd differs from, and influences, the psychology of the individuals within it. It studies collective behaviour in gatherings ranging from peaceful assemblies, audiences and queues to protests, riots, panics and lynch mobs, and asks why crowds sometimes act with unusual generosity or solidarity and at other times with destructiveness that few members would display alone.

Early theories

The field's classic text is Gustave Le Bon's The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind (1895), written amid elite anxiety about mass democracy and revolutionary mobs. Le Bon argued that in a crowd individuals undergo a psychological transformation: anonymity, contagion and suggestibility submerge personal identity in a "collective mind" that is impulsive, irrational and easily led. Gabriel Tarde emphasised imitation, and Sigmund Freud's Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921) recast crowd cohesion as libidinal identification with a leader. These theories treated crowds largely as pathological.

Deindividuation and its critics

Mid-twentieth-century social psychology formalised the idea as deindividuation: experiments by Leon Festinger, Philip Zimbardo and Ed Diener suggested that anonymity and arousal in groups reduce self-awareness and restraint, releasing antisocial impulses. Later researchers questioned both the experiments and the theory, pointing out that crowd members typically act in terms of shared social identities and norms rather than mindlessly. The elaborated social identity model of crowd behaviour (ESIM), developed by Stephen Reicher, Clifford Stott and John Drury, argues that crowd action is meaningful and norm-governed: people act according to the norms of the identity salient in the situation, and conflict with police often escalates when authorities treat a heterogeneous crowd as uniformly dangerous, uniting it against them — a finding that has reshaped public-order policing, for example in low-profile "dialogue" approaches to football crowds.

Emergent norm theory and collective behaviour

Ralph Turner and Lewis Killian's emergent norm theory holds that in ambiguous situations crowds generate new norms on the spot, guided by conspicuous "keynoters". Sociologists of collective behaviour have applied crowd theory to rumours, panics, fads, financial manias and disaster evacuations, where research generally shows that orderly, cooperative behaviour is more common than the "mass panic" of popular imagination. Contemporary work extends to online crowds, flash mobs and social-media cascades.
