Black Elk Speaks: Being the Life Story of a Holy Man of the Oglala Sioux is a 1932 book by poet and writer John G. Neihardt, based on a series of interviews with Black Elk (Heȟáka Sápa, 1863–1950), an Oglala Lakota holy man (wičháša wakȟáŋ). It recounts Black Elk's great vision, received at the age of nine, and his life through the Lakota wars, the Battle of the Little Bighorn, the Ghost Dance movement, and the Wounded Knee massacre of 1890.

Content

The narrative opens with Black Elk's childhood on the northern Plains and his vision, in which he was taken to the center of the world and shown the sacred hoop of his people. Black Elk participated as a young man in the events of the Great Sioux War, witnessed the killing of Crazy Horse, traveled with Buffalo Bill's Wild West show to England, and returned to Pine Ridge in time for the Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee. The book closes with Black Elk's prayer on Harney Peak, lamenting that "the nation's hoop is broken and scattered."

Publication and reception

Published during the Great Depression, the book sold modestly at first but was rediscovered in the 1960s and 1970s, becoming one of the best-known works of Native American literature and a key text in the study of Indigenous spirituality. Scholars have debated the degree to which Neihardt shaped or altered Black Elk's words; Raymond J. DeMallie's The Sixth Grandfather (1984) published the original interview transcripts, allowing comparison between the stenographic record and Neihardt's literary rendering.

Legacy

The book influenced writers, theologians, and the American Indian Movement, and has been translated into many languages. Black Elk's cause for canonization in the Roman Catholic Church—he later became a Catholic catechist—was opened in 2017.
