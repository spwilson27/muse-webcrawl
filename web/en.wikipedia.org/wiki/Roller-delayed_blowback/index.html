Roller-delayed blowback

Roller-delayed blowback is a firearms operating mechanism in which the bolt is held closed at the moment of firing by rollers that engage recesses in the receiver. The rollers do not fully lock the bolt; instead they impose a mechanical disadvantage so that the bolt head must force the rollers inward against the mass of a heavier bolt carrier, delaying opening until chamber pressure has dropped to a safe level.

History

The system was developed in Germany during the Second World War, derived from the roller-locked MG 42 and refined in the StG 45(M) prototype by engineer Ludwig Vorgrimler at Mauser. After the war Vorgrimler continued the work in France and then in Spain, where it produced the CETME rifle. The design was licensed to Heckler & Koch, becoming the basis of the G3 battle rifle and a large family of weapons including the MP5 submachine gun, HK21 machine gun and PSG1 marksman rifle.

Operation

On firing, pressure drives the bolt head rearward. The rollers, seated in recesses, must be cammed inward; the geometry is arranged so the bolt carrier must move several times farther than the bolt head, effectively multiplying the carrier's inertia. Once the rollers clear the recesses the action opens freely. The system suits rifle-calibre cartridges without requiring a gas system, though it is known for violent extraction and fluted chambers in many implementations to aid case extraction.
