Gilbert Keith Chesterton (29 May 1874 – 14 June 1936) was an English author, philosopher, Christian apologist, and literary and art critic. Known as the "prince of paradox", he created the fictional priest-detective Father Brown, and wrote influential works of apologetics and social criticism including Orthodoxy (1908), The Everlasting Man (1925), and the novel The Man Who Was Thursday (1908). He wrote around 80 books, several hundred poems, some 200 short stories, 4,000 essays, and several plays.

Life and career

Born in Kensington, London, Chesterton studied art at the Slade School before turning to journalism. He became one of the most prolific and flamboyant literary figures of his day, famous for his girth, his wit, and his friendly public debates with figures such as George Bernard Shaw, H. G. Wells, and Bertrand Russell; he was a close friend and sparring partner of Hilaire Belloc, with whom he promoted the economic philosophy of distributism. After a long spiritual journey from agnosticism through Anglo-Catholicism, he was received into the Roman Catholic Church in 1922.

Works and ideas

The Father Brown stories (beginning with "The Blue Cross", 1910) made the unassuming priest one of detective fiction's enduring figures and anticipated psychological crime writing. The Everlasting Man, a reply to Wells's Outline of History, influenced C. S. Lewis's conversion. Chesterton's aphorisms — such as "Chesterton's fence", the principle that reforms should not remove a rule or institution until its purpose is understood — remain widely quoted.

Legacy

Chesterton died at his home in Beaconsfield in 1936. His influence extends through writers from Lewis and Tolkien's circle to Neil Gaiman, and a cause for his canonisation has attracted periodic support within the Catholic Church.
