History of agriculture

The history of agriculture records the domestication of plants and animals and the development of farming techniques over some 12,000 years. Agriculture arose independently in several regions — the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, New Guinea and others — transforming human societies, enabling cities, states and population growth.

Agriculture history
Neolithic Revolution: c. 10,000 BC
First domesticates: Wheat, barley, sheep, goats
Modern revolutions: British Agricultural Revolution, Green Revolution

Origins

In the Fertile Crescent, Natufian hunter-gatherers harvested wild cereals before farming emmer wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats by c. 8500 BC. Rice and millet were domesticated in China, squash, maize and beans in Mesoamerica, potatoes in the Andes, and taro and bananas in New Guinea. Farming spread by migration and adoption, displacing foraging.

Ancient and medieval farming

Irrigation civilisations in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus and China built canals, ploughs, and granaries. Romans and Chinese wrote agronomic treatises. Medieval Europe adopted the heavy plough, horse collar, three-field rotation and watermills, raising yields.

Columbian exchange and agricultural revolution

After 1492, crops such as maize, potatoes, tomatoes and tobacco spread to the Old World, and wheat, sugar and livestock to the Americas, with plantation slavery central to production. In Britain (c. 1700–1850), enclosure, four-field rotation (Turnip Townshend), selective breeding and seed drills boosted output.

Modern agriculture

Mechanisation, synthetic fertilisers (Haber–Bosch), pesticides and hybrid seeds culminated in the Green Revolution of the 1960s–70s (Borlaug's dwarf wheat), averting famine but bringing environmental costs — soil loss, water depletion, biodiversity decline. Recent trends include GM crops, precision farming, organic agriculture and debates over sustainability and climate adaptation.
