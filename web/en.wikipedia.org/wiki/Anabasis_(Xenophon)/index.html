The Anabasis (Ancient Greek: Ἀνάβασις, "a going up" or "expedition inland") is the best-known work of the Athenian soldier and writer Xenophon, composed around 370 BC. In seven books it narrates the expedition of a Greek mercenary army — the Ten Thousand — hired by the Persian prince Cyrus the Younger in his bid to seize the throne from his brother Artaxerxes II, and the army's extraordinary fighting retreat from Mesopotamia to the Black Sea after Cyrus was killed.

Summary

In 401 BC Cyrus recruited some 10,000 Greek hoplites and light troops, ostensibly to fight rebels in Pisidia, but in fact to march on Babylon. At the Battle of Cunaxa, north of Babylon, the Greeks routed the troops facing them, but Cyrus was killed, leaving the mercenaries stranded deep inside the Persian Empire. Their senior generals, including Clearchus, were treacherously seized and executed by the satrap Tissaphernes at a parley. The army then elected new leaders — among them Xenophon, who recounts a dream and makes a celebrated speech rallying the troops — and fought its way north through the mountains of Kurdistan (the land of the Carduchians) and Armenia in winter.

The narrative's most famous moment comes when the vanguard, reaching Mount Theches, catches sight of the Black Sea and raises the cry "Thálatta! Thálatta!" ("The sea! The sea!"). The army reached the Greek colony of Trapezus (Trabzon) in 400 BC; the remainder of the work describes its journey along the coast and its fractious service with the Spartans in Asia Minor.

Authorship and style

Xenophon published the work under the pseudonym "Themistogenes of Syracuse", a device he uses to praise his own deeds in the third person. Written in clear, direct Attic prose, the Anabasis became a standard text for learners of Greek — traditionally the first authentic prose work read by students, much as Caesar's Gallic War is for Latin.

Influence

The march of the Ten Thousand demonstrated the vulnerability of the Persian Empire to a disciplined Greek force and is said to have inspired Philip II and Alexander the Great. The work has inspired numerous modern retellings, including Harold Coyle's novel and, indirectly, the 1979 film The Warriors (via Sol Yurick's novel), as well as Michael Curtis Ford's The Ten Thousand.
