Overview

São Tomé and Príncipe, officially the Democratic Republic of São Tomé and Príncipe, is an island country in the Gulf of Guinea, off the western equatorial coast of Central Africa. The two main islands — São Tomé and Príncipe, part of an extinct volcanic range — together with smaller islets make it Africa's second-smallest country by area (after Seychelles). Uninhabited before Portuguese arrival around 1470, the islands became a sugar and then cocoa colony worked first by enslaved Africans and later by contract labourers (serviçais) from Angola, Mozambique and Cape Verde, whose conditions provoked international scandal (the Cadbury cocoa boycott of 1909) and the Batepá massacre of 1953.

Modern state

Independence came in 1975 under the MLSTP and Manuel Pinto da Costa's one-party socialist state; multiparty democracy followed in 1991, and the country is now regularly cited among Africa's most stable democracies. Cocoa remains the chief export; hopes of offshore oil (the Nigeria joint development zone) have yet to transform the economy, while eco-tourism trades on the islands' extraordinary endemism (the Gulf of Guinea forests are a global biodiversity hotspot — Príncipe is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve). The equator crosses the islet of Ilhéu das Rolas, marked by a monument.
