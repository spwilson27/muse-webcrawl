Growth-share matrix

The growth-share matrix (also known as the BCG matrix, after the Boston Consulting Group) is a portfolio management framework that helps corporations decide how to allocate resources among their business units. It was developed by Bruce Doolin Henderson for the Boston Consulting Group in 1970.

The matrix classifies business units into four categories along two dimensions: market growth rate (vertical axis) and relative market share (horizontal axis). Units with high growth and high share are Stars, which typically generate and consume large amounts of cash. Units with low growth but high share are Cash Cows, reliable generators of cash. Units with high growth but low share are Question Marks (or problem children), which require investment to grow but may become stars. Units with low growth and low share are Dogs, often candidates for divestment.

Although widely taught in business schools, the matrix has been criticised for oversimplifying complex strategic decisions, neglecting synergies between units, and relying on market share and growth as the sole measures of competitive strength and industry attractiveness.
