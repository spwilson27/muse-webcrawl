Wu wei (Chinese: 無為; pinyin: wúwéi) is an ancient Chinese concept literally meaning "inexertion", "inaction", or "effortless action". It is a central idea of Taoism, appearing throughout the Tao Te Ching attributed to Laozi and the Zhuangzi. Wu wei describes a state of personal harmony, free-flowing spontaneity and, in government, a policy of governing by minimal interference — action through non-action, in which things are accomplished without forceful striving against the natural order of things.

Interpretations

Scholars debate whether wu wei prescribes literal non-intervention or a subtler ideal of unforced, responsive action aligned with circumstances. In the Tao Te Ching, the sage ruler "does nothing, and there is nothing that is not done"; water — soft and yielding yet wearing away stone — is a recurring metaphor. Zhuangzi illustrates the ideal through skilled practitioners such as Cook Ding, whose knife moves effortlessly through an ox, acting from cultivated attunement rather than deliberate calculation.

Political use

During the early Han dynasty, Huang–Lao thought applied wu wei to statecraft, advocating light taxation and minimal regulation after the austerity of the Qin. Confucian and Legalist writers also discussed the ruler's non-action, though with different emphases: the sovereign's stillness ordering the state through ritual example or through impersonal laws and techniques.
