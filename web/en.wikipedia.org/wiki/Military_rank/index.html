Military rank

Military rank
CategoriesCommissioned officers; warrant officers; non-commissioned officers; enlisted personnel
StandardisationNATO rank codes (STANAG 2116): OF-1 to OF-10, OR-1 to OR-9

Military ranks are a system of hierarchical relationships within armed forces, police, intelligence agencies and other institutions organised along military lines. The term rank describes the pay grade and position of an individual, and is usually indicated by insignia worn on the uniform. Rank is not solely a mark of pay but of responsibility: the higher the rank, the greater the number of personnel commanded and the weight of decisions entrusted.

Categories

Modern rank systems divide personnel into broad categories:

  • Commissioned officers hold a commission from the head of state and command units, progressing typically from junior ranks (lieutenant / ensign) through field grades (major, colonel) to general officers (brigadier, major general, lieutenant general, general) and, in some services, field marshal or general of the army.
  • Warrant officers are specialists appointed by warrant, ranking between enlisted personnel and commissioned officers (chiefly in Commonwealth and US practice).
  • Non-commissioned officers (NCOs) — corporals and sergeants of various grades — are experienced enlisted leaders who supervise small units and training.
  • Enlisted personnel / other ranks form the bulk of the force, beginning with privates (army), seamen (navy) and airmen (air force).

Naval and air ranks

Naval officer ranks parallel army ranks with distinct titles — ensign/sub-lieutenant, lieutenant, commander, captain, commodore and the admiral grades (rear admiral, vice admiral, admiral, admiral of the fleet). Air forces mostly adopt army-style ranks, though the Royal Air Force and Commonwealth air forces use their own sequence (pilot officer to marshal of the air force). Within NATO, STANAG 2116 assigns common codes (OF-1…OF-10 for officers, OR-1…OR-9 for other ranks) so equivalent ranks across nations can be compared.

History

Formal rank structures grew out of early modern European armies: the titles colonel, captain and lieutenant derive from the organisation of mercenary companies in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, while sergeant and corporal descend from medieval household and feudal service. Standardised insignia (epaulettes, chevrons, sleeve lace) spread in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and the twentieth century added ranks for technical specialists and senior enlisted advisors.
