The incense trade route was a network of ancient overland and maritime trade routes linking the frankincense- and myrrh-producing kingdoms of southern Arabia (modern Yemen and Oman) with the Mediterranean world. For centuries it channelled some of the ancient world's most valuable commodities, worth more by weight than gold in temple and funeral rituals across Egypt, Greece and Rome.

The route

Caravans assembled at Shabwah in the Hadhramaut and travelled north through Mecca and Medina to Petra, the Nabataean capital, and on to the port of Gaza or to Damascus — a journey of some 2,000 kilometres taking about two months, with dozens of way stations and wells spaced a day's march apart. The Nabataeans grew rich controlling the final desert legs through the Negev, where their incense-route cities — Avdat, Mamshit, Haluza and Shivta — are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Decline

The trade flourished from roughly the 3rd century BC but declined as Roman seafarers mastered direct monsoon sailing to India and southern Arabia, bypassing the overland middlemen; the conversion of the Roman world to Christianity later reduced demand for incense in pagan ritual, though it remained in demand for church use.
