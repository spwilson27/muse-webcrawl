In the First Folio of 1623, the plays of William Shakespeare were divided into three categories: comedies, histories and tragedies. A Shakespearean comedy is characterised not simply by humour but by its movement from confusion and conflict towards resolution, typically ending in marriage and social harmony. Modern scholars usually count about fifteen comedies, including the late romances and the so-called "problem plays".

Characteristics

Although there is no single formula, Shakespeare's comedies tend to share recurring features:

  • A struggle of young lovers to overcome difficulty, often imposed by older figures
  • Mistaken identity, frequently involving cross-dressing and disguise
  • Multiple, interwoven plot lines
  • Frequent use of puns, wordplay and comic servants or fools
  • A journey from the ordered court or city to a "green world" — a forest or pastoral space where normal rules are suspended, as in the Forest of Arden in As You Like It
  • An ending in marriage, dance and reconciliation

Subgenres

Romantic comedies

The festive romantic comedies of the 1590s — A Midsummer Night's Dream, Much Ado About Nothing, As You Like It and Twelfth Night — combine witty lovers, clowns and magical or pastoral settings, and end in multiple marriages.

Problem plays

The term "problem play", coined by critic F. S. Boas in 1896, is applied to works such as Measure for Measure, All's Well That Ends Well and Troilus and Cressida, whose treatment of sex, power and morality is darker and whose resolutions are morally ambiguous.

Late romances

Shakespeare's final plays — Pericles, Cymbeline, The Winter's Tale and The Tempest — mingle comedy with loss, separation across many years, apparent death and miraculous reunion, often of parents and children.

Sources and influences

Shakespeare drew on classical Roman comedy (Plautus and Terence), Italian novelle, medieval romance and pastoral literature. The mistaken-identity machinery of The Comedy of Errors derives directly from Plautus's Menaechmi, while the green-world structure identified by Northrop Frye became central to later criticism.

List of comedies

The First Folio comedies are: The Tempest, The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Merry Wives of Windsor, Measure for Measure, The Comedy of Errors, Much Ado About Nothing, Love's Labour's Lost, A Midsummer Night's Dream, The Merchant of Venice, As You Like It, The Taming of the Shrew, All's Well That Ends Well, Twelfth Night, The Winter's Tale and Pericles, Prince of Tyre (added in the Third Folio).
