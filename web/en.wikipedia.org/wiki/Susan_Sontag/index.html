Susan Sontag

Susan Sontag (January 16, 1933 – December 28, 2004) was an American writer, critic, and filmmaker, regarded as one of the most influential intellectuals of her generation. Her essays — notably "Notes on 'Camp'" (1964) and the books Against Interpretation (1966), On Photography (1977), and Illness as Metaphor (1978) — reshaped discussions of art, photography, illness, and culture.

Life

Born Susan Lee Rosenblatt in New York, she was raised largely in Arizona and California, a precociously intellectual child who entered the University of California, Berkeley at 15 before transferring to the University of Chicago. She married sociologist Philip Rieff at 17; they had a son, David Rieff, and divorced in 1959. She studied philosophy and literature at Harvard and Oxford and taught at several universities before supporting herself by writing. From 1987 her partner was the photographer Annie Leibovitz. Sontag survived breast cancer in the 1970s and a later uterine sarcoma, but died of myelodysplastic syndrome in 2004.

Work

"Against interpretation" argued for an "erotics of art" over hermeneutic decoding; "Notes on 'Camp'" popularised the concept of camp sensibility. On Photography examined how photographs shape — and desensitise — our relation to reality, themes she revisited in Regarding the Pain of Others (2003). Illness as Metaphor attacked the punitive metaphors attached to tuberculosis and cancer, extended in AIDS and Its Metaphors (1989). Her fiction includes The Benefactor (1963), Death Kit (1967), the bestselling historical novel The Volcano Lover (1992), and In America (1999), which won the National Book Award. She also directed films and staged Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot in besieged Sarajevo in 1993.

Activism and controversies

Sontag was an outspoken political commentator: she opposed the Vietnam War, controversially praised aspects of North Vietnam in 1968, later denounced communism in a famous 1982 Town Hall speech, supported Bosnian resistance during the Yugoslav wars, and criticised the U.S. response to the September 11 attacks. Her awards include a MacArthur "genius" grant (1990) and the Jerusalem Prize (2001).
