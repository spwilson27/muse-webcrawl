Intercontinental ballistic missile

An intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) is a ballistic missile with a range greater than 5,500 kilometres (3,400 miles), designed primarily to deliver nuclear weapons. ICBMs follow a ballistic trajectory: they are boosted out of the atmosphere by rocket stages, coast through space in a sub-orbital arc, and release one or more re-entry vehicles that fall to their targets at hypersonic speed. Alongside submarine-launched ballistic missiles and strategic bombers, they form part of the nuclear triad of the major nuclear powers.

History

The technology descends from the German V-2 rocket of the Second World War. The Soviet Union tested the first ICBM, the R-7 Semyorka, in August 1957 — the same design that launched Sputnik 1 — and the United States followed with the Atlas missile in 1959. Early liquid-fuelled missiles required lengthy preparation and were vulnerable to attack, prompting hardened underground silos, solid-fuel designs such as Minuteman and Polaris (for submarines), and later road- and rail-mobile missiles. Improvements included multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs), decoys and penetration aids, and greatly improved accuracy.

Operation

A typical flight lasts about 30 minutes and has three phases: the boost phase (3–5 minutes), in which the missile reaches altitudes of roughly 150–400 km at burnout; the midcourse phase (about 20 minutes), during which warheads and decoys coast along a ballistic arc reaching apogees around 1,200 km; and the terminal phase, in which re-entry vehicles re-enter the atmosphere at speeds up to about Mach 23 and strike within a few hundred metres of their aim points in modern systems. Guidance is inertial, sometimes updated by stellar or satellite navigation.

Operators and arms control

Land-based ICBMs are operated by the United States (Minuteman III, to be replaced by the Sentinel), Russia (including the RS-28 Sarmat and mobile Yars), China (DF-5, DF-31, DF-41), India (Agni-V and successors approaching ICBM range), and North Korea (Hwasong series). ICBM numbers and warheads have been limited by arms control treaties between the US and the USSR/Russia, including SALT, START I and New START, reflecting their central role in strategic deterrence and the doctrine of mutually assured destruction.
