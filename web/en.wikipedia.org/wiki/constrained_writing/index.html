Constrained writing

Constrained writing is a literary technique in which the writer is bound by some condition that forbids certain things or imposes a pattern. Constraints are very common in poetry, where the writer often works within strict metrical, stanzaic, or rhyme schemes, but they also appear in prose, where they are associated above all with the French literary group Oulipo (Ouvroir de littérature potentielle), founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais.

Forms of constraint

Famous constraints include the lipogram, which omits a particular letter — Ernest Vincent Wright's novel Gadsby (1939) avoids the letter "e", as does Georges Perec's La Disparition (1969), translated into English by Gilbert Adair as A Void. A univocalic text uses only one vowel. Other devices include palindromes, acrostics, the "N+7" method (replacing each noun with the seventh following noun in a dictionary), the snowball (each word one letter longer than the last), and restrictive arithmetic or geometrical structures such as Perec's Life: A User's Manual, organized by a knight's tour of a Paris apartment block.

History and motivation

Constrained texts are ancient: Greek poets composed lipogrammatic versions of the Odyssey, and pattern poetry dates to antiquity. The Oulipo writers argued, paradoxically, that constraints liberate the imagination by forcing invention — Queneau described Oulipians as "rats who build the labyrinth from which they plan to escape". Christian Bök's Eunoia (2001), built from univocalic chapters, won the Griffin Poetry Prize.

Other traditions

Beyond Oulipo, constraints structure haiku and sonnets, the "Six-Word Memoirs" popularized in the 2000s, National Novel Writing Month's word-count goals, and internet forms such as Twitterature. In children's literature, Dr. Seuss wrote Green Eggs and Ham (1960) using only 50 distinct words, on a bet with his publisher.
