Vapour-compression refrigeration is the most widely used method of artificial cooling, employed in household refrigerators, air-conditioning systems, and industrial cold storage. A circulating refrigerant undergoes a repeating thermodynamic cycle in which it absorbs heat at low temperature and pressure and rejects it at higher temperature and pressure, driven by mechanical work supplied to a compressor.

The cycle

The standard cycle has four stages:

  1. Compression. The refrigerant enters the compressor as a low-pressure superheated vapour and is compressed to a high pressure, which also raises its temperature.
  2. Condensation. The hot, high-pressure vapour passes through a condenser, where it rejects heat to the surroundings and condenses into a high-pressure liquid.
  3. Expansion. The liquid passes through an expansion valve or capillary tube, undergoing an abrupt pressure drop (throttling). Its temperature falls sharply and a fraction flash-evaporates.
  4. Evaporation. The cold liquid–vapour mixture flows through the evaporator inside the refrigerated space, absorbing heat and boiling completely before returning to the compressor.

Performance

Efficiency is measured by the coefficient of performance (COP), the ratio of heat removed from the cold space to the work input. Practical systems achieve COPs of roughly 2–5 depending on the temperature lift. The cycle is a realization of the reverse Carnot cycle, modified because throttling replaces a turbine and compression of wet vapour is impractical.

Refrigerants

Early systems used ether, ammonia, and sulphur dioxide. Chlorofluorocarbons (Freon) dominated from the 1930s until the Montreal Protocol phased them out for damaging the ozone layer; hydrofluorocarbons followed, and modern systems increasingly use low-global-warming refrigerants such as R-32, hydrofluoroolefins, propane (R-290), and carbon dioxide (R-744).

History

Jacob Perkins patented the first practical vapour-compression machine in 1834, using ether. James Harrison built commercial machines in Australia in the 1850s, and the technology enabled the refrigerated shipping trade — the first successful shipment of frozen meat sailed from Australia to London in 1880. Domestic electric refrigerators became common in the 1920s and 1930s.
