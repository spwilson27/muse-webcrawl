Adaptive market hypothesis

Adaptive market hypothesis
Proposed byAndrew Lo (2004)
FieldFinancial economics, behavioral finance
Key ideasMarket efficiency varies over time; adaptation and natural selection among market participants

The adaptive market hypothesis (AMH), proposed by MIT economist Andrew W. Lo in 2004, is an attempt to reconcile the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) with behavioral finance by applying the principles of evolution — competition, adaptation, and natural selection — to financial markets. Under the AMH, the degree of market efficiency is not an all-or-nothing state but changes over time and across markets, depending on the population of investors and the market environment.

Overview

Lo argued that investors are not the fully rational agents assumed by traditional theory, nor are they the hopelessly irrational actors sometimes portrayed in behavioral finance. Instead, they use heuristics that usually work well but occasionally fail spectacularly, and they adapt those heuristics as conditions change. Profit opportunities therefore arise from time to time and are exploited until competition eliminates them; new anomalies emerge as the environment changes.

Implications

  • Risk and return are not related in a stable, mechanical fashion; the relationship varies with the composition of market participants.
  • Investment strategies that once worked can stop working as they become crowded, and strategies that failed can become profitable again.
  • Survival, rather than utility maximization alone, is presented as the ultimate driver of behavior.

Evidence and reception

Empirical studies have found evidence of time-varying predictability in stock returns consistent with the AMH, including changing autocorrelation patterns over decades. The hypothesis became the organizing idea of Lo's popular 2017 book Adaptive Markets: Financial Evolution at the Speed of Thought, which won several awards. Critics note that the framework is broad and can be difficult to falsify, while supporters credit it with bridging two previously hostile schools of financial economics.
