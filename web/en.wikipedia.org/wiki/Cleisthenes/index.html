Cleisthenes

Cleisthenes (Greek: Κλεισθένης; c. 570 – c. 508 BC) was an ancient Athenian nobleman and reformer credited with reforming the constitution of ancient Athens and setting it on a democratic footing in 508/507 BC. A member of the Alcmaeonid family, he is often called the "father of Athenian democracy". His grandfather, also Cleisthenes, was the tyrant of Sicyon.

Reforms

After the tyranny of the Peisistratids ended (510 BC) and his rival Isagoras, backed by Sparta, seized power, Cleisthenes — in Herodotus's phrase — "took the people into his faction" and enacted sweeping reforms. He reorganised the citizens of Attica into ten new tribes (phylai), each composed of demes drawn from three different regions (city, coast, and inland), deliberately breaking the old clan and regional loyalties. The demes became the basic units of citizenship and local self-government. He expanded the Council (Boule) to 500 members, fifty from each tribe chosen by lot, to prepare business for the Assembly (Ekklesia), and is associated by later tradition with the institution of ostracism. When Sparta intervened to undo the reforms in 508 BC, the Athenian people besieged the Spartan king Cleomenes on the Acropolis and forced his withdrawal — a founding moment of democratic self-assertion.

Legacy

Cleisthenes' system of isonomia (equality before the law) laid the institutional foundations on which fifth-century Athenian democracy was built, succeeding the earlier reforms of Solon (594 BC) and preceding those of Ephialtes and Pericles.
