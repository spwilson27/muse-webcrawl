Spread spectrum

Spread spectrum is a technique in telecommunications in which a signal generated within a particular bandwidth is deliberately spread in the frequency domain, resulting in a signal with a much wider bandwidth. Spread-spectrum signals are resistant to interference and jamming, are hard to intercept because they can resemble background noise, and allow many users to share the same band.

Methods

The two classical methods are frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS), in which the carrier hops among many frequencies in a pseudorandom pattern known to the receiver, and direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS), in which the data are multiplied by a fast pseudorandom "chipping" code. Variants and hybrids include chirp spread spectrum, used in LoRa, and the time-hopping used in ultra-wideband systems.

History

Frequency hopping was famously described in a 1942 U.S. patent by actress Hedy Lamarr and composer George Antheil, intended for radio-guided torpedoes. Spread-spectrum methods were developed for military communications during and after the Second World War, and later commercialised in CDMA mobile-phone standards (IS-95 and CDMA2000), GPS, Bluetooth (FHSS), and the DSSS physical layer of early Wi-Fi (802.11b).
