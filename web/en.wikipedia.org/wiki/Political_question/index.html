Political question

In United States constitutional law, the political question doctrine holds that some questions are reserved by the Constitution to the political branches — Congress and the President — and are therefore nonjusticiable: federal courts will not decide them even when a litigant claims a constitutional violation. Rooted in the separation of powers and in Chief Justice Marshall's dictum in Marbury v. Madison (1803) that questions "in their nature political" can never be decided by the courts, the doctrine has been applied to such matters as foreign relations, impeachment procedure, and — historically — partisan gerrymandering.

Political questions are left to Congress and the President rather than the courts.

The Baker factors

The doctrine's canonical formulation comes from Baker v. Carr (1962), where Justice Brennan listed six indicia of a political question:

  1. a textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department;
  2. a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving it;
  3. the impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination of a kind clearly for nonjudicial discretion;
  4. the impossibility of a court's undertaking independent resolution without expressing lack of respect for coordinate branches;
  5. an unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made;
  6. the potentiality of embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements by various departments on one question.

Ironically, Baker itself held legislative apportionment justiciable under the Equal Protection Clause, opening the door to the "one person, one vote" cases.

Applications

The Supreme Court has found political questions in challenges to the Senate's impeachment procedures (Nixon v. United States, 1993), in aspects of foreign affairs and treaty termination (Goldwater v. Carter, 1979), and in the Guarantee Clause challenges to state government forms (Luther v. Borden, 1849). In Zivotofsky v. Clinton (2012) the Court narrowed the doctrine, holding that determining the constitutionality of a statute is a "familiar judicial exercise" even when it touches foreign policy. Most recently, Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts for want of manageable standards — a decision that, like the long debate over the 2001 AUMF, illustrates how courts police the boundary between law and politics.

Criticism

Scholars criticise the doctrine as incoherent in scope, manipulable through the open-ended Baker factors, and in tension with judicial supremacy in constitutional interpretation. Others defend it as an essential safety valve preserving democratic accountability for the most contestable exercises of public power. Comparable ideas exist in other systems, such as the French acte de gouvernement and the British courts' treatment of non-justiciable prerogative acts.
