Critical point (thermodynamics)

On a phase diagram, the critical point terminates the liquid–gas boundary

In thermodynamics, a critical point (or critical state) is the end point of a phase equilibrium curve. The most prominent example is the liquid–vapour critical point, the temperature and pressure beyond which a liquid and its vapour can no longer be distinguished: the two phases merge into a single supercritical fluid. The conditions at this point — the critical temperature (Tc), critical pressure (pc), and critical volume — are characteristic properties of each pure substance.

Helium-4Tc = 5.2 K, pc = 0.23 MPa

Behaviour near the critical point

Below the critical temperature, a substance can exist as distinct liquid and gas phases separated by a meniscus. As the critical point is approached, the densities of the two phases converge, the latent heat of vaporisation falls to zero, and at the critical point the meniscus disappears. Near-critical fluids show striking effects, including critical opalescence — strong scattering of light by large density fluctuations — and a divergent heat capacity and compressibility.

History and theory

The phenomenon was observed by Charles Cagniard de la Tour in 1822, and Thomas Andrews's experiments on carbon dioxide (1869) established the concept, coining the term "critical point". The van der Waals equation of state (1873) was the first theory to predict a critical point, giving the relations Tc = 8a/27Rb and pc = a/27b². The law of corresponding states — that fluids behave similarly when compared at the same reduced temperature and pressure — follows from this picture, although real critical behaviour belongs to the broader theory of critical phenomena and differs quantitatively from mean-field predictions.

Applications

Supercritical fluids, notably supercritical carbon dioxide, are used as solvents for extraction (e.g. decaffeination), chromatography, dry cleaning, and as working fluids in advanced power cycles; supercritical water is used in some reactor and oxidation processes. Other phase boundaries can also end in critical points, as in liquid–liquid miscibility (consolute points) and the ferromagnetic Curie point.
