Exodus (ship)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Exodus 1947 was a ship that carried Jewish emigrants, most of them Holocaust survivors, from France to Mandatory Palestine in July 1947. The vessel began life as the SS President Warfield, a Chesapeake Bay excursion steamer built in 1927, which served in the Second World War as a transport in both the Royal Navy and the US Navy. Purchased by the Haganah's clandestine immigration organisation (the Mossad LeAliyah Bet), she was renamed after the biblical Exodus for the voyage.

The voyage

The ship sailed from Sète, France, on July 11, 1947, carrying about 4,500 passengers — far beyond her licensed capacity — in an attempt to break the British blockade restricting Jewish immigration to Palestine. Royal Navy warships shadowed her and, on July 18, boarded her in international waters near Gaza. The passengers resisted fiercely; three people were killed and dozens wounded before the British took control and towed the ship into Haifa.

Deportation and aftermath

Instead of detaining the passengers in Cyprus as usual, the British government decided to return them to their port of embarkation aboard three prison ships. On arrival in France the passengers refused to disembark and held out for weeks in stifling conditions, attracting worldwide press attention. The British ultimately carried them to Hamburg, in the British zone of occupied Germany, where they were forcibly removed and interned — a spectacle of Holocaust survivors behind wire in Germany that severely damaged Britain's international standing. Most of the passengers eventually reached Israel after its independence in 1948. The affair is widely credited with hardening international sympathy for a Jewish state in the months before the UN voted on partition in November 1947. The episode also inspired Leon Uris's 1958 novel Exodus and its 1960 film adaptation, in which the ship and its story appear in fictionalised form. The damaged vessel remained rusting in Haifa harbour until it burned and sank in 1952.
