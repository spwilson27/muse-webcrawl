Vestal Virgin

In ancient Rome, the Vestal Virgins or Vestals (Latin: Vestālēs) were priestesses of Vesta, goddess of the hearth. The College of the Vestals was regarded as fundamental to the continuance and security of Rome. The Vestals cultivated the sacred fire that was not allowed to go out, and were freed of the usual social obligations to marry and bear children, taking instead a 30-year vow of chastity.

Duties

The six Vestals tended Vesta's flame in her round temple in the Forum, prepared ritual salt cake (mola salsa), guarded sacred objects and the wills of prominent Romans, and took part in festivals such as the Vestalia and Fordicidia. Their persons were sacrosanct: harming one was a capital offence, and they could free condemned prisoners they met by chance.

Privileges and punishments

Unlike most Roman women, Vestals could own property, make wills and vote in effect through influence; they had reserved seats at games and travelled in carriages. A Vestal who let the fire die was scourged; one who broke her vow of chastity was buried alive in the Campus Sceleratus, and her lover beaten to death.

History

Tradition credited Numa Pompilius with founding the order. It endured for over a thousand years until the Christian emperor Theodosius I extinguished the fire and disbanded the college in AD 394.
