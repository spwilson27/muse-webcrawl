Universal jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Universal jurisdiction is a principle of international law whereby states claim criminal jurisdiction over persons whose alleged crimes were committed outside the state's territory, without regard to the nationality of perpetrator or victims or any other connection — on the basis that the crimes (piracy classically; today grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions, torture, genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity) offend the international community as a whole.

Practice

Landmarks include Israel's trial of Adolf Eichmann (1961), the House of Lords' Pinochet decision (1998–99, denying former-head-of-state immunity for torture under the Torture Convention), Belgium's and Spain's broad universal-jurisdiction statutes (later narrowed under diplomatic pressure), and Germany's trials of Syrian officials for state torture (Koblenz, 2021–22) under its Code of Crimes against International Law. Universal jurisdiction complements the ICC by decentralising enforcement; friction arises over immunities of sitting officials (the ICJ's Arrest Warrant judgment, 2002, upheld incumbent foreign ministers' immunity before foreign courts), selective application, and the resource demands of atrocity trials far from the evidence.
