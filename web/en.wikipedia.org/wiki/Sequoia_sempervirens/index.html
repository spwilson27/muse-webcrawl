Sequoia sempervirens, commonly known as the coast redwood or California redwood, is an evergreen, long-lived, monoecious tree living 1,200–2,200 years or more. This species includes the tallest living trees on Earth, reaching up to 115.9 m (380.3 ft) in height and up to 8.9 m (29 ft) in trunk diameter. It is the sole living species of the genus Sequoia.

Distribution

Coast redwoods occupy a narrow strip of land approximately 750 km (470 mi) long and 8–56 km (5–35 mi) wide along the Pacific coast of North America, from southwestern Oregon to central California, mostly within the summer fog belt. They favour deep, moist soils in sheltered valleys at elevations of 30–750 m.

Description

The bark is thick—up to 30 cm—fibrous, and reddish brown, giving the tree its name; it protects against fire and insects. Leaves are variable: scale-like on upper and older growth, flat needles on shaded lower branches. Coast redwoods reproduce both by seed and by sprouting from the root crown, forming "fairy rings" of genetically identical trunks. The tallest known living specimen, Hyperion, was measured at 115.92 m in 2006 in Redwood National Park; its exact location is kept secret.

Ecology and conservation

Redwood forests support a rich canopy ecosystem of ferns, lichens, and salamanders high above the ground, and provide habitat for the marbled murrelet and northern spotted owl. Logging in the 19th and 20th centuries removed most old-growth; about 5% remains, protected in Redwood National and State Parks and other reserves, while second-growth is managed for timber—the wood is prized for its resistance to decay. Because of habitat loss and climate stress the species is listed as Endangered by the IUCN.
