Mean

A mean is a quantity representing the "center" or typical value of a set of numbers. In statistics and mathematics there are several kinds of mean, of which the most common is the arithmetic mean—the sum of the values divided by the number of values. Other kinds include the geometric mean, the harmonic mean, and various weighted and generalized means. Together with the median and the mode, the mean is one of the principal measures of central tendency used to summarize a data set with a single representative number. The term derives from the Old French meien and Latin medianus, "middle", a root it shares with "median".

Arithmetic mean

The arithmetic mean of a set of n numbers x1, …, xn is defined as the sum of the numbers divided by n:

x̄ = (x1 + x2 + ⋯ + xn) / n

The symbol x̄ ("x-bar") is the standard notation for the mean of a sample. The arithmetic mean is the most widely used average: it is the value that minimizes the sum of squared deviations from the data, it uses information from every observation, and it has convenient algebraic properties. Its principal weakness is sensitivity to outliers: a single extremely large or small value can pull the mean far from the bulk of the data, in which case the median may be more representative. In probability theory, the analogue of the arithmetic mean for a random variable is its expected value, and the law of large numbers guarantees that the sample mean converges to the expected value as the sample size grows.

Geometric mean

The geometric mean of n positive numbers is the n-th root of their product. It is the natural average for quantities that grow multiplicatively, such as investment returns, population growth, and ratios. For example, growth of 10% followed by a decline of 10% does not average to zero change arithmetically in effect: the geometric mean of the growth factors 1.10 and 0.90 is √(1.10 × 0.90) ≈ 0.995, a decline of about 0.5% per period. The geometric mean is never greater than the arithmetic mean for the same data, a consequence of the AM–GM inequality.

Harmonic mean

The harmonic mean is the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of the values. It is appropriate for averaging rates and ratios when the denominators differ in a particular way—for example, the average speed of a journey made at different speeds over equal distances. For positive data, the harmonic mean is less than or equal to the geometric mean, which is in turn less than or equal to the arithmetic mean (the H ≤ G ≤ A chain of inequalities).

Other means

The weighted mean multiplies each value by a weight reflecting its importance or frequency before averaging; course grades computed from assignments of different credit values are a common example. The quadratic mean, or root mean square, is the square root of the arithmetic mean of the squares, used extensively in physics and engineering (for example, RMS voltage in alternating-current circuits). These are all special cases of the generalized mean (power mean), which raises the data to a power p, averages, and takes the p-th root; as p tends to zero the generalized mean approaches the geometric mean, and as p tends to ±∞ it approaches the maximum or minimum value. Still other "means" include the midrange (average of maximum and minimum), trimmed means that discard extreme values, and the various Pythagorean means of ancient Greek mathematics.

Mean of a probability distribution

For a discrete random variable, the mean (expected value) is the probability-weighted sum of its possible values; for a continuous variable it is the integral of x times its probability density. Not every distribution has a finite mean: the Cauchy distribution is a standard example with no defined mean, so sample means of Cauchy data do not converge. The population mean is conventionally denoted by the Greek letter μ (mu), distinguished from the sample mean x̄.

History

The arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic means were studied in ancient Greek mathematics and associated with the Pythagoreans; they were used in music theory, geometry, and astronomy. The modern statistical use of the mean as a summary of observations developed from the 17th to the 19th centuries alongside probability theory and the method of least squares, in which the arithmetic mean emerges as the best estimate of a quantity measured repeatedly with symmetric errors.
