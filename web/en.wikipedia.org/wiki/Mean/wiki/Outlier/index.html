Outlier

In statistics, an outlier is a data point that differs significantly from other observations. An outlier may be due to a variability in the measurement, an indication of novel data, or it may be the result of experimental error; the latter are sometimes excluded from the data set. An outlier can be an indication of an experimental error or a heavy-tailed distribution in the data.

Causes

  • Measurement or recording error (faulty equipment, transcription mistakes, data-entry errors).
  • Data drawn from a different population than the rest of the sample.
  • A genuinely rare but real extreme value from a heavy-tailed distribution (for example, exceptional incomes, floods, or earthquakes).

Identifying outliers

Common methods include:

  • Tukey's fences: points lying more than 1.5 interquartile ranges (IQR) below the first quartile or above the third quartile, as displayed in a box plot.
  • Z-scores: points several standard deviations from the mean.
  • Grubbs's test, Dixon's Q test, and the Chauvenet criterion: formal hypothesis tests for a single outlier.
  • Robust methods: using the median and median absolute deviation, which are little affected by outliers themselves.

Working with outliers

Outliers should not be deleted reflexively: they may carry the most important information in the data (for example, in fraud detection or the discovery of anomalous astronomical events). Statisticians may instead use robust estimators, winsorisation (capping extreme values), transformations, or explicit modelling of the extreme-value tail. Retention or deletion decisions should be documented, since arbitrarily removing outliers can bias results and understate uncertainty.
