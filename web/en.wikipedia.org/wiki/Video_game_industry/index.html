The video game industry is the economic sector involved in the development, marketing and monetisation of video games. Encompassing thousands of studios and hundreds of thousands of jobs worldwide, it generates annual revenues rivalling or exceeding the film and recorded-music industries combined, with more than three billion people playing games.

History

The industry began commercially in the 1970s with arcade hits such as Pong and Space Invaders and home consoles from Atari. A glut of poor-quality games and competition from home computers caused the North American video game crash of 1983, after which Nintendo revived the console market with the NES and strict licensing. The 1990s brought CD-ROMs, 3D graphics and Sony's PlayStation; the 2000s added online play, the Wii's mass-market motion controls and digital distribution through platforms such as Steam. Smartphones after 2008 made mobile the largest segment, while free-to-play and live-service models, in-game purchases and esports reshaped the business in the 2010s. The 2020s have seen consolidation, including Microsoft's $69 billion acquisition of Activision Blizzard (2023), alongside recurring criticism of working conditions such as "crunch" and industry-wide layoff waves.

Structure

Games are created by developers and financed and marketed by publishers; platform holders (Nintendo, Sony, Microsoft, Apple, Google) take a share of sales on their consoles and stores. Development budgets for AAA titles can exceed hundreds of millions of dollars, and a single global hit can gross over a billion dollars. Asia-Pacific is the largest regional market, driven by China, Japan and South Korea, and games now feed a wider tourism and media economy of conventions, streaming and merchandise.
