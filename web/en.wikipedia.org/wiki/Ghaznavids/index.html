Ghaznavids

The Ghaznavid Empire (Persian: غزنویان Ghaznaviyān) was a Persianate Muslim dynasty of Turkic mamluk origin, ruling from 977 to 1186 over large parts of present-day Iran, Afghanistan, Transoxiana, Pakistan and northern India. Its capital at Ghazni (in present-day Afghanistan) became a brilliant centre of Persian culture and learning. The dynasty's most famous ruler, Mahmud of Ghazni (r. 998–1030), led some seventeen expeditions into India and made the empire one of the great powers of the Islamic world.

Origins

The dynasty began with Alp Tigin, a Turkic slave-soldier (ghulam) of the Samanid Empire who seized Ghazni in 962. His son-in-law and successor Sabuktigin (r. 977–997) is generally counted the true founder: he consolidated rule over the region, expanded into the Punjab, and won recognition from the Abbasid caliph. His son Mahmud inherited a compact, wealthy state and transformed it into an empire.

Mahmud of Ghazni

Mahmud (r. 998–1030), the first ruler in the Islamic world to bear the title Sultan, campaigned westward against the Buyids, annexing Khurasan and Ray, and eastward in celebrated raids on India, including the sack of the temple of Somnath in 1025–26, which brought immense plunder. His Indian campaigns permanently annexed the Punjab, with Lahore becoming a second capital and a gateway for Persianate culture into India. Mahmud patronised scholars and poets — al-Biruni wrote his great study of India at the Ghaznavid court, and Ferdowsi presented the Shahnameh to Mahmud (though the poet's reward was famously disappointing).

Decline

Mahmud's son Mas'ud I lost the western empire to the Seljuk Turks at the Battle of Dandanqan in 1040, after which the Ghaznavids shrank to Afghanistan and the Punjab. Later sultans maintained a Persianised court at Ghazni and then Lahore, surviving the Seljuks but falling first to the Ghurids, who burnt Ghazni in 1151, and finally losing Lahore to Muhammad of Ghor in 1186, ending the dynasty.

Culture

Despite its Turkic military origins, the Ghaznavid state was thoroughly Persianate: Persian was the language of administration and high culture, and its court became a model for later Turko-Persian empires. Ghazni's palaces, minarets and libraries symbolised the dynasty's role in transmitting Persian culture eastward.
