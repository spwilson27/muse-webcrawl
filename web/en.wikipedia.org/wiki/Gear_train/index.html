Gear train

Gear train
TypeMechanical transmission system
ComponentsTwo or more meshing gears, idlers, shafts
Key quantityGear ratio (ratio of output to input speed)
ApplicationsGearboxes, clocks, machine tools, differentials

A gear train (also called a gear drive or transmission) is a mechanical system formed by mounting two or more gears so their teeth mesh, transmitting rotation and torque from a driving shaft to a driven shaft. The ratio of the output speed to the input speed — the gear ratio — is fixed by the numbers of teeth on the gears.

Gear ratio

For a pair of meshing gears, the gear ratio is the ratio of the number of teeth on the driven gear to the number on the driver: a 40-tooth gear driven by a 10-tooth pinion turns at one quarter of the input speed but with roughly four times the torque (minus friction losses). When intermediate gears, called idlers, are inserted, they change the direction of rotation but not the overall ratio of a simple train.

Types

Simple gear train

Each shaft carries one gear. Idlers reverse direction so that input and output shafts can rotate the same way and be spaced apart.

Compound gear train

Two gears fixed on the same shaft rotate together, allowing large ratios in a compact space. A compound train with a ratio beyond about 10:1 per stage is typical in machine tools and clockwork.

Reverted gear train

The input and output shafts are coaxial, as in the back-gearing of a lathe or a clock's hands shaft.

Planetary (epicyclic) gear train

One or more planet gears orbit a central sun gear inside a ring gear. Planetary trains give several ratios from one set of gears and are the basis of automatic transmissions and hub gears.

Applications

Gear trains appear in vehicle gearboxes and differentials, wind turbines, watches and clocks, industrial machinery and robotics, wherever speed must be reduced (or increased) and torque multiplied with a fixed, reliable ratio.
