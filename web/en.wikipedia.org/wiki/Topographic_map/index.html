Topographic map

ShowsTerrain relief, elevation, hydrography, land cover, built features
Key conventionContour lines at a fixed vertical interval
Typical scales1:24,000 (USGS 7.5-minute quadrangles); 1:25,000 / 1:50,000 (many national surveys)

In modern mapping, a topographic map or topographic sheet is a type of map characterised by large-scale detail and quantitative representation of relief features, usually using contour lines — lines joining points of equal elevation — but historically using a variety of methods. A topographic survey and its published maps are typically produced by national mapping agencies and underpin land management, engineering, outdoor recreation, geology and military planning.

Conventions

Contour lines are usually printed in brown, with every fourth or fifth line ("index contour") bolded and labelled with its elevation; their spacing shows gradient — close lines mark steep slopes, widely spaced lines gentle ones. Standard colour schemes show water in blue, vegetation in green, urban areas in grey or pink, and roads in red or black, with extensive standardised symbology for buildings, boundaries, benchmarks and spot heights. Maps are drawn to a stated scale on a defined projection and geodetic datum, and carry marginal information (the "collar") about the survey, adjoining sheets and magnetic declination.

History and producers

The first systematically surveyed national series include France's Cassini map (18th century) and Britain's Ordnance Survey (from 1791). In the United States, the U.S. Geological Survey's 7.5-minute quadrangle series at 1:24,000 ultimately covered the whole country with some 55,000 printed sheets; the programme has since moved to digitally produced "US Topo" maps built from The National Map's GIS data. Modern topographic data increasingly derive from aerial photogrammetry, lidar and satellite imagery rather than field plane-tabling.
