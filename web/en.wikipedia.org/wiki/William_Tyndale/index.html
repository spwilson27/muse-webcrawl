William Tyndale

William Tyndale (c. 1494 – October 1536) was an English Biblical scholar and linguist who became a leading figure in the Protestant Reformation in the years leading up to his execution. He is well known as a translator of the Bible into English, influenced by the works of Erasmus of Rotterdam and Martin Luther. His translation was the first English Bible drawn directly from Hebrew and Greek texts, and the first to be printed using the printing press.

Early life

Tyndale was born in Gloucestershire and educated at Magdalen Hall (now Hertford College), Oxford, where he studied languages. He became a chaplain and tutor at Little Sodbury in Gloucestershire, where, according to Foxe's Book of Martyrs, he told a visiting cleric: "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou doest."

Translation work

Denied permission by Bishop Cuthbert Tunstall to translate the Bible in London, Tyndale travelled to Germany in 1524. An attempted printing of his New Testament at Cologne was raided in 1525, but a complete edition was printed at Worms in 1526 and smuggled into England, where copies were publicly burned. Tyndale also wrote The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), which influenced Henry VIII's break with Rome, though the king opposed his biblical work. He revised his New Testament in 1534 and translated the Pentateuch and other Old Testament books, work published posthumously in the Matthew Bible.

Arrest and death

Living among the English merchants at Antwerp, Tyndale was betrayed by Henry Phillips and arrested by imperial authorities in 1535. After imprisonment in the castle of Vilvoorde, he was convicted of heresy, strangled, and burned at the stake in October 1536. His dying prayer, as reported by Foxe, was "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."

Legacy

Tyndale's phrasing — including "let there be light," "the salt of the earth," "a law unto themselves," and "filthy lucre" — passed through the King James Version, whose New Testament follows his wording to a remarkable degree. Within a few years of his death, English Bibles drawing heavily on his work were officially licensed in England.
