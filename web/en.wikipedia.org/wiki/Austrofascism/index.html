Austrofascism is the term used to describe the authoritarian system of government in Austria between 1933/34 and 1938, established by Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss and continued after his assassination by Kurt Schuschnigg. Unlike Italian Fascism and German National Socialism, from which it borrowed organisational forms, Austrofascism rested ideologically on conservative Catholic corporatism and Austrian patriotism, and defined itself in opposition both to Marxist social democracy and to Nazi Germany's ambitions to annex Austria.

Rise to power

In March 1933, Dollfuss exploited a parliamentary procedural crisis to suspend the National Council and rule by emergency decree, invoking a wartime emergency powers law of 1917. In 1933 he founded the Fatherland Front (Vaterländische Front) as a monopolistic political movement uniting his Christian Social Party, the agrarian Landbund and the paramilitary Heimwehr. In February 1934, government forces and the Heimwehr crushed the Socialist Schutzbund in the brief Austrian Civil War, shelling the Karl Marx-Hof housing complex in Vienna; the Social Democratic Workers' Party was banned and its leaders fled or were imprisoned.

The corporatist state

On 1 May 1934 Austria received a new authoritarian constitution, opening with the words "In the name of God, the Almighty, from whom all law emanates", which formally created the Federal State of Austria (Bundesstaat Österreich). Society was to be organised into corporatist estates (Stände) on the model of the papal encyclicals Rerum novarum and Quadragesimo anno. Political pluralism, free trade unions and opposition parties were eliminated.

Assassination and the road to Anschluss

Dollfuss was assassinated by Austrian Nazi conspirators during the failed July Putsch of 1934. His successor Schuschnigg maintained the regime while coming under increasing pressure from Adolf Hitler's Germany, signing the July Agreement of 1936 that conceded amnesty and influence to Austrian Nazis. In March 1938, under direct German threat, Schuschnigg resigned, and German troops marched into Austria; the Anschluss incorporated Austria into the Third Reich, ending the Austrofascist experiment.

Assessment

Historians debate the label "fascist": the regime shared fascism's anti-democratic and anti-Marxist character, its cult of leadership and its mass organisation, but lacked expansionist racial ideology and pursued a conservative, clerical restoration rather than a revolutionary new order. The term "Austrofascism" remains standard in Austrian historiography, where the Dollfuss-Schuschnigg era is also called the Ständestaat ("corporate state").
