Midlife crisis

Midlife crisis
FieldPsychology

A midlife crisis is a transition of identity and self-confidence that can occur in middle-aged individuals, typically 45 to 64 years old. The phenomenon is described as a psychological crisis brought about by events that highlight a person's growing age, inevitable mortality, and possibly lack of accomplishments in life. This may produce feelings of intense depression, remorse, and high levels of anxiety, or the desire to achieve youthfulness or make drastic changes to their current lifestyle, or feel the need to change past decisions and events.

History of the concept

The term was introduced by the Canadian psychoanalyst Elliott Jaques in a 1965 paper, "Death and the Midlife Crisis", in which he observed that creative figures often underwent a marked change of style and productivity in their late thirties, confronting the realisation of their own mortality. The concept was popularised by Daniel Levinson's The Seasons of a Man's Life (1978) and Gail Sheehy's bestseller Passages (1976).

Research

Studies suggest that a true crisis in midlife is less universal than popular culture implies. Large surveys (such as the MIDUS studies in the United States) find that only a minority of middle-aged adults — roughly 10–26%, depending on definition — report a personal crisis, and such crises are often tied to major life events (divorce, job loss, illness, bereavement) rather than to age itself. Research on subjective well-being has described a "U-bend" of happiness, in which life satisfaction dips in the late forties before rising again in later life, though the size and universality of the effect are debated.

Symptoms and behaviours

Commonly reported features include questioning long-held values and life choices, restlessness or boredom, dissatisfaction with career or marriage, feelings of being trapped, and preoccupation with ageing, appearance and death. In popular stereotype, the midlife crisis is associated with impulsive purchases (such as a sports car), affairs, or abrupt career changes, though clinicians note that the underlying distress more often reflects ordinary midlife stressors and adjustment.
