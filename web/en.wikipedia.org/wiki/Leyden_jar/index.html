A Leyden jar (or Leiden jar) is an early device for storing static electric charge — in effect, the first capacitor. It consists of a glass jar lined inside and out with conductive foil (or partly filled with water), with a metal rod passing through an insulating stopper to contact the inner conductor. It was invented independently around 1745–46 by Ewald Georg von Kleist in Pomerania and by Pieter van Musschenbroek at Leiden (Leyden) in the Netherlands, from which it takes its name.

Operation and use

Charged from an electrostatic generator, the jar stores equal and opposite charges on its two coatings, separated by the glass dielectric. A typical jar had a capacitance of roughly one nanofarad and could deliver a startling shock or spark; banks of jars ("batteries") were connected together for stronger effects. Leyden jars made controlled electrical experiments possible for the first time, enabling the work of Benjamin Franklin — who coined the term "battery" for a linked set — and they were used throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries in demonstrations, early medical electricity and experiments on electric discharge. They remained in use as high-voltage capacitors into the early radio era.
