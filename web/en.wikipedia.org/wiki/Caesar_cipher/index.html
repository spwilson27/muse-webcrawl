In cryptography, a Caesar cipher, also known as Caesar's cipher, the shift cipher, Caesar's code, or Caesar shift, is one of the simplest and most widely known encryption techniques. It is a type of substitution cipher in which each letter in the plaintext is replaced by a letter some fixed number of positions down the alphabet. For example, with a left shift of 3, D would be replaced by A, E by B, and so on. The method is named after Julius Caesar, who used it in his private correspondence.

History

Suetonius records that Caesar used a shift of three to protect messages of military significance, and his nephew Augustus used a shift of one, wrapping from Z back to a doubled "AA". Caesar ciphers remained in occasional military use for centuries — the US Army's field ciphers in the First World War and Russian troops in 1915 used simple shift systems — but they are trivially broken: with only 25 possible keys, an attacker can simply try every shift, and even without the key, frequency analysis reveals the plaintext at once. The cipher is today used mainly in puzzles, children's secret codes, and as the special case ROT13, which was used on Usenet to obscure spoilers.

Example

Plaintext THE QUICK BROWN FOX with a right shift of 3 becomes WKH TXLFN EURZQ IRA. Decryption reverses the shift. Mathematically, encryption of a letter x by a shift n is (x + n) mod 26.
