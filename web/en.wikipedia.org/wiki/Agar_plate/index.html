An agar plate is a Petri dish containing a growth medium solidified with agar, used to culture microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. Because agar melts at about 95 °C but sets at around 40–45 °C and is indigestible to most microbes, it provides a stable, transparent solid surface on which individual cells grow into visible colonies.

History

Robert Koch's laboratory initially used gelatin, which melted at incubation temperatures and was digested by many bacteria. Fanny Angelina Hesse, wife of Koch's associate Walther Hesse, suggested agar — used in her household for jams — in the early 1880s; Koch adopted it in 1882. Julius Richard Petri introduced the shallow covered dish that bears his name in 1887.

Types and use

Plates may contain general-purpose media (nutrient agar), enriched media (blood agar), selective media that suppress unwanted organisms (MacConkey agar), or differential media whose colour changes distinguish species. Microbiologists inoculate plates by streaking to dilute the sample into isolated colonies, each derived from a single cell, enabling pure cultures, colony counts and antibiotic-susceptibility tests such as the disk diffusion method.
