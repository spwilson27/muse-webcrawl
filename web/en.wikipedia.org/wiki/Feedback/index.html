Feedback

Feedback occurs when outputs of a system are routed back as inputs, forming a circuit or loop. The system can then be said to "feed back" into itself. Feedback is fundamental to control theory, electronics, biology, climate science, economics and many other fields; the concept was generalised by Norbert Wiener in his founding work on cybernetics (1948).

Types

Negative feedback acts to counteract change, stabilising a system around a set point: a thermostat switching a heater on and off, the regulation of blood glucose by insulin, and the negative-feedback amplifier (invented by Harold Stephen Black in 1927) are classic examples. Negative feedback reduces gain but increases stability, bandwidth and linearity.

Positive feedback amplifies change, driving a system away from equilibrium: the squeal when a microphone is placed near its loudspeaker (audio feedback), runaway processes in electronics such as the Schmitt trigger, blood clotting, and self-reinforcing climate effects such as the ice–albedo feedback. Positive feedback can produce rapid switching, oscillation or runaway growth.

Control systems

In control engineering, feedback controllers measure the output of a process, compare it with the desired value (set point), and adjust the input to minimise the error. The PID controller (proportional–integral–derivative) is the most common industrial form. Feedback loops introduce concerns of stability — excessive gain or delay can cause oscillation, studied through tools such as the Nyquist stability criterion and Bode plots.

Applications

Feedback mechanisms pervade biology (homeostasis, gene regulatory networks), electronics (oscillators, amplifiers), acoustics, economics (market feedback and reflexivity), and social systems, where "feedback" has also come to mean evaluative information given to a person or organisation about their performance.
