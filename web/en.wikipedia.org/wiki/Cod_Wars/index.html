Cod Wars

The Cod Wars (Icelandic: Þorskastríðin) were a series of confrontations between the United Kingdom and Iceland over fishing rights in the North Atlantic between 1958 and 1976. Each begins with Iceland unilaterally extending its exclusive fishing zone, and each ends with Iceland achieving its objectives, despite its tiny population and navy — a result often attributed to Iceland's strategic importance to NATO during the Cold War.

The three wars

In the First Cod War (1958–1961), Iceland extended its limits from 4 to 12 nautical miles; Britain initially resisted but accepted the extension. In the Second Cod War (1972–1973) Iceland extended to 50 nautical miles, and its coast guard cut the warps (trawl wires) of British trawlers. In the Third Cod War (1975–1976) Iceland claimed 200 nautical miles. The Royal Navy deployed more than thirty frigates to protect the fishing fleet; there were collisions and ramming incidents, and one Icelandic engineer died repairing a vessel after a collision — the only fatality of the conflicts. Iceland broke off diplomatic relations with Britain, the first such rupture between NATO allies.

Resolution

Iceland's leverage was its NATO membership and the Keflavík air base, vital for monitoring Soviet submarines passing through the GIUK gap. Under American and NATO pressure, Britain conceded in June 1976, withdrawing its trawlers and accepting Iceland's 200-mile zone. The outcome helped establish the 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone as the international standard, later codified in the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea.
