Transonic

Transonic flow is air flowing around an object at speeds where both subsonic and local supersonic regions exist. The transonic regime is usually taken to span Mach numbers from about Mach 0.8 to Mach 1.2. Most modern jet airliners cruise in the lower transonic range, around Mach 0.78–0.85.

Flow behaviour

As an aircraft accelerates toward the speed of sound, air accelerating over the wing's curved upper surface reaches Mach 1 before the aircraft itself does—at the critical Mach number. A shock wave forms where the supersonic pocket terminates, thickening the boundary layer and sharply increasing drag (the drag-divergence Mach number). Shock-induced separation can cause buffeting, while rearward movement of the centre of pressure may produce the nose-down pitching tendency called Mach tuck.

Historical development

Transonic effects became a practical barrier in the 1940s, when fighters in dives encountered severe compressibility problems. Research with swept wings (first exploited in Germany), thin airfoils, and Richard Whitcomb's area rule (1952) and later supercritical airfoil (1960s–70s) made efficient near-sonic flight routine. The Bell X-1's 1947 level flight through Mach 1 demonstrated that the regime could be traversed safely with purpose-built designs.

Applications

Airliners, business jets and most military jets operate transonically; fan blade tips, propellers at high speed, and re-entering spacecraft also encounter transonic flow.
