Jefferson–Hemings controversy

The Jefferson–Hemings controversy concerns the long-debated question of whether US President Thomas Jefferson fathered children by Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman he owned at Monticello. Hemings was also the half-sister of Jefferson's late wife, Martha Wayles Skelton, sharing the same father, John Wayles.

History of the claims

Allegations appeared publicly in 1802, when journalist James T. Callender published claims that Jefferson had taken Hemings as a "concubine" and fathered children by her. Jefferson never publicly responded. For much of the 19th and 20th centuries, mainstream historians generally rejected the claims, often attributing the paternity instead to Jefferson's nephews Peter and Samuel Carr, as Jefferson's descendants had suggested.

Evidence and DNA study

In 1998, a DNA study published in Nature compared Y-chromosome samples from descendants of Jefferson's paternal uncle (the Jefferson male line), descendants of Eston Hemings (Sally Hemings's youngest son), and descendants of Thomas Woodson, whose family claimed descent from Jefferson and Hemings. The results showed a match between the Jefferson line and Eston Hemings's descendants, and no match for the Woodson line; the Carr nephews' line did not match Eston Hemings. Combined with documentary evidence that Jefferson was at Monticello during the conception windows of all of Hemings's children, the study shifted scholarly consensus.

Consensus

In 2000 the Thomas Jefferson Foundation issued a report concluding that Jefferson most likely fathered Eston Hemings and probably all six of Sally Hemings's children who lived at Monticello. Most historians now accept this conclusion, though a minority of scholars continue to dispute it. Monticello's interpretation of the site now presents Hemings and her family centrally in its account of Jefferson's household.
