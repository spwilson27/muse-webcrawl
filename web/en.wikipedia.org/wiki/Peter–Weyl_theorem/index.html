Peter–Weyl theorem

In mathematics, the Peter–Weyl theorem is a basic result in the theory of harmonic analysis, applying to topological groups that are compact, but not necessarily abelian. It was initially proved by Hermann Weyl, with his student Fritz Peter, in the setting of compact Lie groups in 1927. It is the principal tool in the representation theory of compact groups and underlies the classification of their irreducible representations.

Statement

The theorem has three main parts. Let G be a compact group, and let L2(G) denote the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on G with respect to normalized Haar measure.

  1. The matrix coefficients of the (isomorphism classes of) irreducible unitary representations of G, suitably normalized, form an orthonormal basis of L2(G). That is, finite linear combinations of matrix coefficients are dense in L2(G), and matrix coefficients of inequivalent representations are orthogonal (by Schur orthogonality).
  2. Every unitary representation of G decomposes completely into a direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible representations.
  3. Every irreducible unitary representation of G is finite-dimensional.

Equivalently, the regular representation of G on L2(G) decomposes as a direct sum over all irreducible representations V, each occurring with multiplicity equal to its dimension dim V — the Hilbert space analogue of the fact that the group algebra of a finite group decomposes as a direct sum of matrix algebras.

Proof idea

The classical proof uses the spectral theory of compact self-adjoint operators: convolution with a suitable symmetric kernel defines a compact operator on L2(G) commuting with translations; its finite-dimensional eigenspaces are translation-invariant, yielding finite-dimensional representations whose matrix coefficients are dense.

Consequences

The Peter–Weyl theorem implies that compact groups are linear in the sense that they admit faithful finite-dimensional unitary representations, enabling a proof that every compact Lie group has the structure used in their classification. It also reduces the harmonic analysis on compact groups — including Fourier series on the circle and spherical harmonics on the sphere — to representation-theoretic form.
