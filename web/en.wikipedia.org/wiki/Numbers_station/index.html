Numbers station

A numbers station is a shortwave radio station characterised by broadcasts of formatted numbers, which are believed to be addressed to intelligence officers operating abroad. The numbers typically consist of sequences of digits read by a (often synthesized or recorded) voice, sometimes in groups of five, and are presumed to be messages encrypted with a one-time pad. Numbers stations have been reported since the First World War and were widespread during the Cold War.

Characteristics

Broadcasts usually follow strict schedules on known frequencies, beginning with an identifier such as a phrase, musical interval signal, or a count. Well-known examples include the English-language "Lincolnshire Poacher" (named for its folk-tune interval signal) and "Cherry Ripe", both attributed to Britain's MI6; the Russian "UVB-76" is a related buzzer station rather than a true numbers station. Since the 1990s, some stations have used digital modes instead of voices.

Evidence and cases

Governments have rarely acknowledged the stations, but espionage trials have confirmed their use: the Cuban "¡Atención!" station featured in the case of the Cuban Five, and the United States charged several spies who received instructions via numbers broadcasts decoded with software or one-time pads distributed by handlers.

In popular culture

The eerie, disembodied broadcasts have inspired musicians and artists; the Conet Project (1997) released a landmark multi-disc collection of shortwave recordings, which has been sampled in numerous works of music and film.
