Gin Lane

Gin Lane is a print by the English artist William Hogarth, published on 1 February 1751 together with its companion piece Beer Street. The two works were issued in support of the campaign for the Gin Act of 1751, part of the British government's long effort to suppress the "Gin Craze" — the epidemic of cheap gin consumption in London in the first half of the 18th century.

Description

Gin Lane depicts a scene of devastation in the slum district of St Giles, London. In the foreground a drunken woman, her legs ulcerated, lets her baby slip from her arms to fall over a stair rail; around her, a carpenter pawns his tools, a man gnaws a bone with a dog, corpses are carried off, and a distiller's sign and pawnbroker's shop — the only flourishing businesses — anchor the composition, along with a gin cellar advertising "Drunk for a penny / Dead drunk for twopence / Clean straw for nothing". A hanged man is visible in a ruined house in the background. The verse beneath, by James Townley, ends by blaming the "fatal dram" for the ruin.

Companion piece

Beer Street, by contrast, shows prosperous, well-fed Londoners drinking native beer outside the Black Heart inn, with industry thriving and only the pawnbroker's house dilapidated. The pair contrasts gin — foreign, cheap, distilled — with wholesome English beer, and was sold cheaply (one shilling) to reach a wide audience as propaganda for reform.

Legacy

Together the prints became the most famous visual documents of the Gin Craze and remain standard illustrations in discussions of addiction, public health and moral panics. The original copper plates are held by the British Museum and other public collections.
