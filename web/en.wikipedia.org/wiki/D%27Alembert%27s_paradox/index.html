D'Alembert's paradox

D'Alembert's paradox, stated by Jean le Rond d'Alembert in 1752, is the observation that potential-flow theory for steady, incompressible, inviscid flow predicts zero drag on a body moving through a fluid — flatly contradicting the everyday experience of resistance.

Explanation

In ideal potential flow the pressure distribution around a symmetric body is symmetric fore and aft, so the net force vanishes. Real fluids have viscosity, however small, which produces a boundary layer, possible flow separation and a wake — the mechanisms behind actual drag.

Significance

The paradox exposed the limits of inviscid theory and drove nineteenth- and twentieth-century work on viscous flow, culminating in Ludwig Prandtl's boundary-layer theory of 1904, which reconciled theory with experiment.
