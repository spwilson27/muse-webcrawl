Carpe diem

Horace's Odes, source of the phrase

Carpe diem
LanguageLatin
Literal meaning"Pluck the day"
SourceHorace, Odes 1.11

Carpe diem is a Latin aphorism, usually translated "seize the day," taken from book 1 of the Roman poet Horace's Odes (23 BC). The full line reads carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero—"pluck the day, trusting as little as possible in the next one." The verb carpere properly means to pluck or harvest, as of ripe fruit or flowers, so the phrase urges the enjoyment of the present moment at its ripeness, in awareness of life's brevity.

The poem

Ode 1.11 is addressed to Leuconoe, whom Horace advises against consulting Babylonian astrologers about the future. Life is short, the winters are numbered, and the present moment should be used—pour the wine, he says—rather than spent worrying about what is to come. The sentiment is related to the Epicurean theme of mortality, though Horace's tone is characteristically wry and moderate rather than hedonistic.

In literature and culture

The carpe diem theme became a staple of European lyric poetry: it underlies the idea of tempus fugit ("time flies"), the medieval Gaudeamus igitur, Herrick's "To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time" ("Gather ye rosebuds while ye may"), Andrew Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress," and the tradition of the "carpe diem poem" urging a beloved to love now rather than later. In modern culture the phrase was famously re-popularized by the 1989 film Dead Poets Society, in which Robin Williams's teacher John Keating urges his students to "seize the day" and "make your lives extraordinary." It remains one of the most quoted Latin phrases in English, often used—somewhat loosely—as a motto for spontaneous enjoyment of life.
