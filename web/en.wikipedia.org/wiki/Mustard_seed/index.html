Mustard seed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A heap of yellow and brown mustard seeds with a few spilling onto a wooden surface]
A heap of yellow and brown mustard seeds with a few spilling onto a wooden surface

Mustard seeds are the small round seeds of the mustard plants — white/yellow mustard (Sinapis alba), brown mustard (Brassica juncea) and black mustard (Brassica nigra) — used whole as a spice and ground as the base of prepared mustard. Among the world's oldest spices, they are central to Indian cooking (tempered in hot oil), European mustards and pickling, and their pungency — released only when the seed is crushed and wetted — arises from the enzyme myrosinase meeting glucosinolates to form fiery isothiocyanates.

Chemistry of heat

Intact seeds are mild; crushing ruptures cells, letting myrosinase convert sinigrin (in black and brown mustard) into Allyl isothiocyanate — the volatile heat of mustard, horseradish and wasabi — while white mustard's sinalbin yields a gentler, lingering pungency. Acid (vinegar, verjuice) tames and fixes the heat, which is why prepared mustards are balanced to recipe and why mustard added early to cooking mellows.

Use and production

India uses brown seeds whole — popped in hot oil or ghee as the opening note of countless dishes — and presses them for mustard oil. Europe grinds them into Dijon, English, German and American mustards; the seed oils and cakes feed and manure farms. Canada is the leading exporter of seed, with Saskatchewan the heartland; the seed's biblical fame ('faith as a grain of mustard seed', the parable of the mustard seed) made it a byword for small things with great growth.
