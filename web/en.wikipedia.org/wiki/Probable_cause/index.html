Probable cause

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Probable cause is a requirement in the United States, derived from the Fourth Amendment, that law-enforcement action — arrests, searches, the issuance of warrants — be justified by facts sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that a crime has been committed and that evidence will be found in the place to be searched. It is a practical, non-technical standard, more than bare suspicion but well below proof beyond a reasonable doubt (often glossed as a "fair probability").

Doctrine

The Supreme Court's modern test is the totality of the circumstances (Illinois v. Gates, 1983), replacing a rigid two-pronged test for informant tips. Probable cause is assessed by magistrates for warrants, by officers for warrantless arrests (with prompt judicial review required, Gerstein hearings), and by grand juries for indictment; flaws are remedied chiefly through the exclusionary rule. Lower thresholds (reasonable suspicion for stops and frisks, Terry v. Ohio) and higher ones stack around it in a calibrated ladder of justification. The concept has analogues elsewhere (reasonable grounds for suspicion in UK law under PACE; hinreichender Tatverdacht in Germany) with different calibrations reflecting different balances between security and liberty.
