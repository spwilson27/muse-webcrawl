Boyar Duma

[Image: The Kremlin in Moscow, seat of the Tsar's council]

The Boyar Duma (Russian: Боя́рская ду́ма, romanized: Boyárskaya dúma) was the highest advisory and legislative council in the Tsardom of Russia from the 10th to the early 18th century. Composed of boyars — the highest rank of the Russian feudal aristocracy — together with okolnichy, duma nobles, and duma clerks, the Duma advised the tsar on matters of state, participated in drafting laws, and functioned at times as the effective government during minorities or interregna.

Composition

Membership was determined by the system of mestnichestvo, the precedence ranking of noble families, so seats were effectively hereditary among a small circle of aristocratic clans. In the 17th century the Duma typically numbered several dozen members, divided into four ranks: boyars, okolnichy, dumnye dvoryane (duma nobles), and dumnye dyaki (duma clerks) — the last a non-aristocratic grade of senior administrators.

Functions

The Duma deliberated on legislation, foreign policy, appointments, and judicial appeals. Laws were conventionally issued with the formula "the tsar has decreed and the boyars have resolved" (царь указал и бояре приговорили). It worked alongside the zemsky sobor (assembly of the land) and the prikazy (administrative departments). Its influence rose and fell with the strength of individual tsars; it played a central role in the 1613 election of Michael Romanov.

Abolition

Peter the Great, seeking to replace hereditary aristocratic counsel with bureaucratic institutions on Western models, ceased convening the Duma and in 1711 established the Governing Senate as Russia's supreme administrative body, formally ending the Boyar Duma's centuries-long role.
