Cytochrome P450

Cytochromes P450 (CYPs) are a superfamily of enzymes containing heme as a cofactor that mostly function as monooxygenases. The name comes from the characteristic absorption peak at 450 nm when the reduced enzyme is bound to carbon monoxide ("P" for pigment). In mammals they are found mainly in the liver and are central to the metabolism of drugs and other xenobiotics, as well as the synthesis of steroid hormones, cholesterol and other lipids.

Function and drug metabolism

P450 enzymes insert one atom of oxygen into a substrate, typically making lipophilic compounds more water-soluble so they can be excreted (phase I metabolism). A handful of human isoforms metabolise most clinically used drugs: CYP3A4 alone handles roughly half. Genetic polymorphisms — for example in CYP2D6, whose poor, intermediate, extensive and ultra-rapid metaboliser phenotypes affect drugs such as codeine and many antidepressants — cause major individual variation in drug response. Enzyme inhibition (e.g. by grapefruit juice or ketoconazole) and induction (e.g. by rifampicin or St John's wort) are major causes of drug interactions.

Nomenclature and distribution

Genes are grouped into families (≥40% sequence identity, e.g. CYP2) and subfamilies (≥55%, e.g. CYP2D), with individual genes numbered (CYP2D6). P450s occur in virtually all life forms — animals, plants, fungi, bacteria and even some viruses — and number in the hundreds of thousands of known sequences; plants use them to make defensive compounds and pigments, and insects' P450s underlie much insecticide resistance.
