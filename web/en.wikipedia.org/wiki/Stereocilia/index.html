Stereocilia

Stereocilia are mechanosensing organelles found on the apical surface of certain epithelial cells, most famously the hair cells of the inner ear. Despite their name, stereocilia are not true cilia: they are actin-filled protrusions more closely related to microvilli, lacking the microtubule-based axoneme and the ability to beat.

In the inner ear

On cochlear and vestibular hair cells, stereocilia are arranged in bundles of graduated height, like a staircase. Fine filamentous links, called tip links, connect the tip of each shorter stereocilium to the side of its taller neighbour. When sound vibrations or head movements deflect the bundle toward its tallest edge, the tip links tug open mechanosensitive ion channels, allowing potassium ions to flow in and depolarise the cell; deflection in the opposite direction closes the channels. This mechanoelectrical transduction is the first step in hearing and balance.

Structure

Each stereocilium contains a core of tightly packed, parallel actin filaments cross-linked by proteins such as espin and fimbrin. The shaft tapers at the base, where the actin core narrows into a rootlet anchored in the cuticular plate, allowing the stereocilium to pivot as a stiff rod. Mutations in the genes encoding stereocilia proteins cause several forms of hereditary deafness, including some types of Usher syndrome.

Elsewhere in the body

Unusually long stereocilia also line parts of the male reproductive tract, notably the epididymis and the vas deferens, where they absorb fluid and are involved in the maturation of sperm. These non-sensory stereocilia can branch and interconnect, unlike the stiff, unbranched stereocilia of the ear.
