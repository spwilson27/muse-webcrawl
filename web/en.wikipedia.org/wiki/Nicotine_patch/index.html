Nicotine patch
TypeTransdermal patch; nicotine replacement therapy
Typical strengths7 mg, 14 mg, 21 mg per 24 hours
PurposeSmoking cessation aid
AvailabilityOver the counter in many countries

A nicotine patch is a transdermal patch that releases nicotine into the body through the skin. It is used as an aid in smoking cessation, as the most widely used form of nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), supplying a steady low dose of nicotine to reduce withdrawal symptoms and cravings while avoiding the toxic substances in tobacco smoke.

History

The patch was developed in the 1980s, building on research by Murray Jarvik and others into transdermal nicotine absorption, and on Jed Rose's work demonstrating that nicotine could be delivered through the skin to suppress craving. The first commercial patches (such as Nicoderm and Habitrol) were approved in the United States in 1991–92, initially by prescription and later over the counter.

Use

A typical course uses a step-down schedule over 8–10 weeks: smokers of more than ten cigarettes a day usually begin with a 21 mg/24 h patch for about six weeks, then 14 mg for two weeks, then 7 mg for two weeks. Patches are applied once daily to clean, dry, hairless skin, rotating sites. Meta-analyses find that NRT increases the rate of successful quitting by roughly 50–60% compared with placebo, and combining a patch with a faster-acting form (gum or lozenge) improves outcomes further.

Side effects

  • Skin irritation at the application site
  • Vivid dreams or sleep disturbance (especially with 24-hour wear)
  • Nausea, headache or dizziness if the dose is too high
