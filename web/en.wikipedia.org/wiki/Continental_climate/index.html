Continental climate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Continental climate
Köppen typesDfa, Dfb, Dwa, Dwb (humid continental); Dfc, Dfd, Dwc, Dwd (subarctic)
DistributionInteriors of North America and Eurasia, mostly 40–60° latitude
Defining traitLarge seasonal temperature range; coldest month below freezing

A continental climate is a climate characterised by significant annual variation in temperature due to the lack of significant bodies of water nearby. Such climates occur in the interiors of continents, mainly in the Northern Hemisphere, where landmasses are large and prevailing winds blow from land rather than sea. In the Köppen climate classification, continental climates are the "D" group: the coldest month averages below 0 °C (or −3 °C in the original scheme) and at least one month averages above 10 °C.

Humid continental climate

The humid continental subtype (Dfa/Dfb, and the monsoon-influenced Dwa/Dwb of East Asia) has hot or warm summers and cold winters, with precipitation in all seasons. It covers the northeastern and midwestern United States and adjacent Canada (Chicago, Toronto, Montreal), much of Eastern Europe and Russia (Moscow, Kyiv), and northern China and Korea (Seoul, Beijing). Four distinct seasons, deciduous and mixed forests, and fertile soils make these regions major agricultural and population centres.

Subarctic climate

Farther north lies the subarctic or boreal subtype (Dfc and relatives): long, bitterly cold winters, brief cool summers, and the vast coniferous taiga forest. It dominates Siberia, Alaska and northern Canada. The Siberian town of Verkhoyansk and the village of Oymyakon — among the coldest inhabited places on Earth — record the most extreme continental temperature range on the planet, exceeding 100 °C between summer and winter extremes.

Causes

Because land heats and cools much faster than water, interior locations far from oceanic moderation experience extremes: they overheat in summer under long days and freeze in winter as cold continental air masses stagnate over them. The effect is strongest in Eurasia, the largest landmass, and weakens toward the coasts, where climates grade into oceanic types.
