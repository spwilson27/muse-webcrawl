Annie Scott Dill Maunder (née Russell; 14 April 1868 – 15 September 1947) was a British astronomer from Northern Ireland, one of the first women scientists at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, and a pioneer of solar astronomy. With her husband Edward Walter Maunder she documented the sunspot record whose latitude distribution is shown in the famous "butterfly diagram", and she studied the historical period of low solar activity now called the Maunder Minimum.

Career

Educated at Girton College, Cambridge — where she passed the mathematics tripos with honours but, as a woman, could not receive a degree — she was hired in 1891 as a "lady computer" in the solar department at Greenwich under E. Walter Maunder, whom she married in 1895. Rules then required her resignation from the observatory, but she continued research as a volunteer, leading eclipse expeditions and developing a small wide-field camera for photographing the Milky Way and the solar corona.

In 1898 she photographed a coronal streamer during an eclipse in India extending millions of miles from the Sun, an exceptional result for the time. She was among the first women elected Fellows of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1916, when the ban on women fellows was lifted. The lunar crater Maunder is named jointly for Annie and Walter Maunder.
