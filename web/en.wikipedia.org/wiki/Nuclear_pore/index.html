Nuclear pore

A nuclear pore is a protein-lined channel in the nuclear envelope that regulates the transport of molecules between the nucleus and the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. Each pore is occupied by a large macromolecular assembly called the nuclear pore complex (NPC), composed of multiple copies of some 30 different proteins known as nucleoporins. A typical mammalian nucleus contains several thousand pores.

Structure

The NPC has a mass of roughly 110–125 megadaltons in vertebrates and exhibits eightfold rotational symmetry. It consists of a central scaffold ring embedded in the nuclear envelope, a central transport channel filled with intrinsically disordered FG-repeat nucleoporins, cytoplasmic filaments, and a nuclear "basket" structure. The FG-repeat meshwork forms a selective barrier: small molecules (below roughly 40 kDa) can diffuse through freely, while larger cargo requires transport factors.

Transport

Selective import and export are mediated by karyopherins (importins and exportins) that recognize nuclear localization and export signals on cargo. Directionality is provided by the Ran GTPase system: RanGTP is concentrated in the nucleus and RanGDP in the cytoplasm. Transport through the NPC is extremely rapid, with an estimated throughput of hundreds to a thousand translocation events per pore per second.

Functions

Nuclear pores export messenger RNA and ribosomal subunits to the cytoplasm and import proteins needed in the nucleus, such as transcription factors, histones, and DNA polymerases. Defects in nucleoporins are linked to several human diseases, and many viruses interact with the NPC during infection.
