Tibial nerve

FromSciatic nerve (ventral divisions L4–S3)
ToMedial and lateral plantar nerves
InnervatesPosterior compartment of leg; intrinsic muscles of the sole of the foot
SensorySole of foot, heel, posterolateral leg

Course of the tibial nerve through the popliteal fossa and calf.

The tibial nerve is the larger of the two terminal branches of the sciatic nerve, arising from the ventral divisions of the spinal nerves L4 to S3. It supplies the muscles of the posterior (flexor) compartment of the leg and, through its terminal branches in the foot, most of the intrinsic muscles of the sole, together with sensation from the sole and heel. It is sometimes called the medial popliteal nerve in its course through the knee region.

Course

The tibial nerve separates from the common fibular (peroneal) nerve at the apex of the popliteal fossa, although the two run together within the sciatic sheath in the thigh. It descends vertically through the popliteal fossa — the most superficial of the fossa's main contents — crosses the popliteal vessels, then passes beneath the tendinous soleal arch into the deep posterior compartment of the leg, running with the posterior tibial vessels on tibialis posterior. At the ankle it passes behind the medial malleolus within the tarsal tunnel, deep to the flexor retinaculum, and divides into the medial plantar nerve and lateral plantar nerve, the nerves of the sole of the foot, functionally analogous to the median and ulnar nerves of the hand.

Branches and supply

In the leg it gives muscular branches to gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris and popliteus in the fossa, and to soleus, tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus in the deep compartment; the medial sural cutaneous branch contributes to the sural nerve, and the interosseous nerve of the leg runs on the interosseous membrane. Its calcaneal branches supply the skin of the heel. Through the plantar nerves it supplies the muscles of the sole (abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, the lumbricals, interossei and others) and the skin of the sole and the plantar surfaces and nail beds of the toes.

Clinical significance

Injury to the tibial nerve is comparatively uncommon because of its deep, protected course, but damage disables plantar flexion and inversion, so the patient cannot stand on tiptoe, and abolishes sensation on the sole, predisposing to painless plantar ulcers. Entrapment in the tarsal tunnel (tarsal tunnel syndrome) produces burning pain and paraesthesia in the sole, mimicking carpal tunnel syndrome of the wrist. The ankle jerk reflex tests the S1–S2 segments carried in part by the tibial nerve.
