Minbar

A minbar (Arabic: منبر) is a pulpit in a mosque where the imam (prayer leader) stands to deliver sermons (khutbah). It is typically shaped as a small tower with a pointed canopy roof and a staircase leading up to it, located to the right of the mihrab, the niche indicating the direction of prayer (qibla).

History

According to tradition, the Prophet Muhammad initially leaned against a palm trunk while preaching in Medina; a simple raised seat of a few steps was made for him, and later tradition holds he used a three-step seat. Under the Umayyad caliphs the minbar grew in height and ceremonial importance, becoming a symbol of religious and political authority; governors as well as prayer leaders addressed congregations from it.

Design

Minbars range from modest wooden staircases to monumental works of art. Famous historical examples include the minbar of the Kutubiyya Mosque in Marrakesh (12th century, made in Córdoba with intricate marquetry), the Minbar of Saladin in al-Aqsa Mosque, Jerusalem (commissioned by Nur al-Din in Aleppo and installed by Saladin; destroyed by arson in 1969 and later reconstructed), and Mamluk stone minbars of Cairo. Traditional forms feature a doorway at the base, often with muqarnas decoration, a flight of steps, a seat, and a conical or domed canopy, sometimes crowned with a finial.

Symbolism and use

The imam ascends only partway and sits before delivering the khutbah, following the practice attributed to the Prophet. The minbar's seat is left symbolically open — in some traditions the seat itself is not used, out of reverence. In Shi'a practice a similar elevated seat is used by preachers during Muharram commemorations.
