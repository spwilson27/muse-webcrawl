Exposure triangle

The exposure triangle is a pedagogical model in photography that groups the three camera settings that determine the exposure of a photograph: aperture, shutter speed, and ISO sensitivity. The model emphasises that the three settings are interdependent: changing one while holding the desired exposure constant requires a compensating change in at least one of the others, and each setting also carries a distinct creative side effect.

The three settings

Aperture

Aperture is the diameter of the lens opening, expressed in f-numbers (f/1.8, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, and so on). A wider aperture (smaller f-number) admits more light and produces a shallower depth of field, isolating a subject against a blurred background; a narrower aperture admits less light and increases the depth of field, keeping more of the scene sharp.

Shutter speed

Shutter speed is the length of time the shutter remains open, expressed in seconds or fractions of a second. A faster shutter admits less light and freezes motion; a slower shutter admits more light and blurs moving subjects, and increases the risk of camera shake unless the camera is stabilised.

ISO

ISO is the sensitivity of the film or digital sensor to light. Raising the ISO brightens the recorded image for a given amount of light, enabling faster shutters or narrower apertures in dim conditions, but at the cost of increased grain in film or digital noise, with reduced dynamic range and colour fidelity.

Reciprocity and equivalence

The three settings are measured in stops, each stop doubling or halving the light. Because of this common scale, a one-stop change in one setting can be exactly offset by an opposite one-stop change in another, yielding the same exposure while altering the creative character of the image — a wide aperture at a fast shutter speed and a narrow aperture at a slow shutter speed may produce equally bright photographs that look entirely different. This interchangeability underlies the concept of equivalent exposures.

The pedagogical model has been criticised in the digital era for treating ISO as a component of exposure itself: strictly, exposure is the light reaching the sensor per unit area, which depends only on aperture and shutter time, while ISO is a gain applied to the signal afterwards. Nevertheless, the triangle remains the standard teaching device for beginners because it correctly describes how the final brightness and appearance of the photograph respond to the three controls in combination.
