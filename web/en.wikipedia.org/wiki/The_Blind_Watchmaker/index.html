The Blind Watchmaker


Richard Dawkins's defence of evolution by natural selection

The Blind Watchmaker: Why the Evidence of Evolution Reveals a Universe without Design is a 1986 book by the British evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins, in which he presents an evidence-based case for evolution by cumulative natural selection and argues against the argument from design.

Argument

The title refers to William Paley's famous watchmaker analogy (1802), which held that the complexity of living organisms implies a designer, as a watch implies a watchmaker. Dawkins replies that the apparent design in nature arises from a blind, unconscious process: natural selection acting over immense time on small heritable variations. The key to the argument is cumulative selection — the non-random survival of successive slight improvements — which, unlike single-step random assembly, can generate complex adaptations such as the eye.

Biomorphs

To make cumulative selection intuitive, Dawkins introduces a computer programme that generates branching tree-like forms he calls "biomorphs", evolving under user-guided selection from simple rules. The programme became famous in its own right and illustrated how elaborate forms can emerge from simple developmental rules.

Reception

The book won the Royal Society of Literature Award and the Los Angeles Times Literary Prize in 1987 and established Dawkins as one of the foremost popular expositors of Darwinism. It remains a standard text in debates over evolution and creationism, and the phrase "blind watchmaker" has entered common usage as a metaphor for natural selection.
