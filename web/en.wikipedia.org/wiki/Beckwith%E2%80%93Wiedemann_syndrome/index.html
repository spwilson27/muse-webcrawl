Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome

SpecialtyMedical genetics
CauseImprinting defects at chromosome 11p15.5
Frequencyabout 1 in 13,700 newborns

Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) is a congenital overgrowth disorder characterised by macrosomia (large body size), macroglossia (enlarged tongue), abdominal wall defects such as umbilical hernia and omphalocele, ear creases and pits, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and an increased risk of embryonal tumours in childhood. It is named after J. Bruce Beckwith and Hans-Rudolf Wiedemann, who described it independently in the 1960s.

Cause

BWS is an imprinting disorder arising from disrupted expression of growth-regulating genes in the 11p15.5 chromosomal region, involving genes such as IGF2, H19, CDKN1C and KCNQ1. Mechanisms include loss or gain of methylation at two imprinting centres, paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 11, and, in a minority of cases, inherited mutations of CDKN1C.

Management

Management includes monitoring and treatment of neonatal hypoglycaemia, feeding and airway support for macroglossia (sometimes with tongue reduction surgery), repair of abdominal wall defects, and screening for tumours — especially Wilms tumour and hepatoblastoma — with regular abdominal ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein measurement during early childhood.
