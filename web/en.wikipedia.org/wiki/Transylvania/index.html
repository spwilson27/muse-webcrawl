Transylvania (Romanian: Transilvania or Ardeal; Hungarian: Erdély; German: Siebenbürgen) is a historical and cultural region in Central Europe, today the north-western and central part of Romania. It is bounded by the Carpathian Mountains to the east and south and by the Apuseni Mountains to the west, and its name derives from the Latin trans silvam — "beyond the forest".

History

In antiquity the region formed the heartland of the Dacian kingdom, conquered by the Roman emperor Trajan in 101–106 AD. After the Roman withdrawal in 271, the area passed through the hands of various peoples during the Migration Period. From the late 9th century it was gradually incorporated into the Kingdom of Hungary; Hungarian kings invited German ("Saxon") settlers in the 12th and 13th centuries, and the region's medieval estates were the Hungarian nobility, the Saxon towns and the Székely frontier guards.

After the Ottoman victory at Mohács in 1526, Transylvania became an autonomous principality under Ottoman suzerainty, notable for its relative religious tolerance — the Edict of Torda (1568) was one of Europe's earliest declarations of religious freedom. It passed to Habsburg rule at the end of the 17th century, and in 1867 was fully reintegrated into Hungary within Austria-Hungary. Following the First World War, the Treaty of Trianon (1920) assigned Transylvania to Romania, a settlement briefly and partially reversed by the Second Vienna Award (1940) before being restored in 1947.

Geography and cities

Transylvania is a plateau ringed by mountains, drained by rivers such as the Mureș, Someș and Olt. Its principal cities include Cluj-Napoca, Brașov, Sibiu, Târgu Mureș, Timișoara (in the broader definition), Oradea and Alba Iulia. The region is known for its medieval fortified churches — several of them UNESCO World Heritage Sites — and for castles such as Bran and Corvin.

Culture

Transylvania has a mixed Romanian, Hungarian and German heritage reflected in its architecture, cuisine and festivals. In world popular culture it is inseparably associated with Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula, whose Count is loosely inspired by the 15th-century Wallachian prince Vlad III ("the Impaler"), and the region's castles and forests draw substantial tourism on that basis.
