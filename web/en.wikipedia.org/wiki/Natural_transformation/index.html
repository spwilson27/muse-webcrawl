In category theory, a branch of mathematics, a natural transformation provides a way of transforming one functor into another while respecting the internal structure (i.e., the composition of morphisms) of the categories involved. Hence, a natural transformation can be considered to be a "morphism of functors". Informally, the notion of a transformation occurring "naturally" is what gave rise to category theory itself: Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane introduced categories and functors in their 1945 paper largely in order to give a precise definition of "natural".

Definition

Let F and G be functors between categories C and D. A natural transformation η from F to G is a family of morphisms ηX : F(X) → G(X), one for each object X in C, such that for every morphism f : X → Y in C, the following equation holds (the naturality condition): ηY ∘ F(f) = G(f) ∘ ηX.

Naturality condition

The equation above is conventionally expressed by the commutative diagram in which the images under F and G of the objects and morphism form the sides of a square, and the components of η complete it. The condition says that applying the transformation before or after the functors' action on a morphism gives the same result — the transformation "commutes with" the structure of the categories.

Examples

A classic example is the determinant: for each vector space, the determinant gives a morphism from the general linear group to the multiplicative group of the field, and these maps assemble into a natural transformation between functors from fields (or commutative rings) to groups. Another example is the canonical isomorphism from a finite-dimensional vector space to its double dual, which is natural, in contrast to an isomorphism with the single dual, which requires choosing a basis. In group theory, the abelianisation of groups is a natural transformation from the identity functor to the abelianisation functor.

Natural isomorphism

A natural transformation whose components are all isomorphisms is called a natural isomorphism. Naturally isomorphic functors are regarded as essentially the same for most categorical purposes. Two categories are equivalent precisely when there exist functors between them whose composites are naturally isomorphic to the respective identity functors.

Functor categories and the Yoneda lemma

Natural transformations are the morphisms of functor categories, whose objects are functors. Composition of natural transformations can be performed "vertically" (componentwise) and "horizontally" (between functor categories), satisfying an interchange law. The Yoneda lemma — among the most important results in category theory — states that natural transformations from a representable functor Hom(A, –) to any functor F correspond bijectively to elements of F(A), making natural transformations central to representability and universal properties.
