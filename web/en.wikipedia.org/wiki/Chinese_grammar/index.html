Chinese grammar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The grammar of Standard Chinese (Mandarin) is characterised by an isolating, analytic structure: words are largely invariable, with no inflection for person, number, tense or case. Grammatical relations are expressed through word order, particles and context. Much of this grammar is shared by the other Sinitic languages, though details differ, especially in the south.

Word order

Chinese is basically a subject–verb–object language, but it is strongly topic-prominent: a topic is often placed first and commented on by the rest of the sentence. Modifiers generally precede the word they modify — adjectives, relative clauses and possessives all come before the noun, relative clauses ending with the particle de (的).

Nouns and measure words

Nouns do not change for number, and countable nouns require a classifier (measure word) when combined with a numeral or demonstrative: e.g. 三本书 sān běn shū, "three books", where 本 běn is the classifier for books. The suffix 们 -men marks plurality mainly for pronouns and human nouns.

Verbs and aspect

Verbs do not conjugate. Instead, aspect is marked by particles: 了 le for completion, 过 guo for experience, and 着 zhe for duration; ongoing actions may use 在 zài. Modality is expressed by auxiliary verbs such as 会 huì "will/can", 能 néng "be able" and 要 yào "want/must". Special constructions include the 把 bǎ construction, which fronts the object, and the 被 bèi passive.

Questions and negation

Yes–no questions are formed with the sentence-final particle 吗 ma, or by the A-not-A pattern (e.g. 是不是 shì bu shì). The main negators are 不 bù and 没(有) méi(yǒu), whose distribution depends on aspect and the type of predicate.
