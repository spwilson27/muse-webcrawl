The Great Western Railway (GWR) was a British railway company that linked London with the south-west and west of England, the West Midlands and most of Wales. Founded in 1835, it was engineered by Isambard Kingdom Brunel, whose vision of a smooth, fast route from London to Bristol — and onwards by steamship to New York — gave the line the character it retained for over a century. It was the only one of the "Big Four" companies of 1923 to retain its name and identity, and survived until nationalisation in 1948. The GWR was affectionately known as "God's Wonderful Railway", and unkindly, by detractors, as the "Great Way Round".

Origins and Brunel's broad gauge

Bristol merchants promoted the company to defend their port's trade against Liverpool. Brunel surveyed a route from Paddington through Reading, Swindon and Bath to Bristol, engineered with gentle gradients and great works including Box Tunnel and the Maidenhead Railway Bridge. The line opened in stages from 1838 and throughout to Bristol in 1841.

Brunel chose a track gauge of 7 ft ¼ in — the broad gauge — for stability and speed, in contrast to the 4 ft 8½ in standard gauge elsewhere. The "gauge wars" followed: Parliament's Gauge Act of 1846 settled on standard gauge for new lines, and the GWR gradually converted, completing the change in a celebrated operation in May 1892.

Expansion

The company absorbed or built lines to Exeter and Plymouth (via the South Devon and Cornwall railways, including Brunel's Royal Albert Bridge at Saltash, 1859), to Oxford, Birmingham and Birkenhead, and extensively into South Wales for coal traffic. Cross-channel and Atlantic ambitions produced Brunel's steamships Great Western, Great Britain and Great Eastern. Its locomotive and carriage works at Swindon, established 1843, became one of the largest industrial complexes in the world.

The twentieth century

Under chief mechanical engineers George Churchward and Charles Collett, the GWR standardised locomotive design with classes such as the Stars, Castles and Kings; the Castle class Caerphilly Castle and later the streamlined publicity of the 1930s fostered a reputation for speed and elegance — the "Cheltenham Flyer" was billed as the world's fastest scheduled train. The company pioneered diesel railcars and promoted tourism with posters and the slogan of the "Holiday Line". In 1948 the GWR became the Western Region of British Railways.

Legacy

Many GWR structures and locomotives survive in preservation, including at STEAM — Museum of the Great Western Railway in Swindon and Didcot Railway Centre. In 2015 the First Great Western franchise was rebranded as Great Western Railway, reviving the historic name and green livery.
