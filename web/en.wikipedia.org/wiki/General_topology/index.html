General topology

General topology studies topological spaces and the structures defined on them.

In mathematics, general topology (or point set topology) is the branch of topology that deals with the basic set-theoretic definitions and constructions used in topology. It is the foundation of most other branches of topology, including differential topology, geometric topology, and algebraic topology.

History

General topology grew out of several areas: analysis, in particular the study of real numbers and function spaces, and geometry. Key developments came from the work of Georg Cantor on sets, Maurice Fréchet's introduction of metric spaces, Felix Hausdorff's definition of a topological space in 1914, and Kazimierz Kuratowski's closure axioms. By the mid-20th century the subject had been systematised in influential texts such as John L. Kelley's General Topology (1955).

Basic concepts

A topological space is a set equipped with a collection of subsets, called open sets, satisfying axioms generalising the notion of nearness without reference to distance. From this basis one defines closed sets, neighbourhoods, continuity of functions, limits of sequences and nets, and connectedness. A function between topological spaces is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open.

Main topics

  • Separation axioms — properties such as Hausdorff (T2), regular, and normal spaces, classifying how points and sets can be separated by neighbourhoods.
  • Compactness — the property that every open cover has a finite subcover, generalising the Heine–Borel theorem.
  • Connectedness — spaces that cannot be split into disjoint non-empty open parts, and related notions such as path-connectedness.
  • Convergence — sequences, nets, and filters, and their role in describing topology.
  • Metrization theorems — conditions under which a topological space arises from a metric.
  • Product and quotient topologies — constructions of new spaces from old, including Tychonoff's theorem on products of compact spaces.
