High-tech architecture

High-tech architecture, also known as structural expressionism, is a type of late modern architectural style that emerged in the 1970s, incorporating elements of high-tech industry and technology into building design. High-tech buildings make a feature of their structure and services — steel trusses, ducts, lifts, and pipes are placed on the exterior and expressed as ornament — and use prefabricated components, glass walls, and exposed steel frames.

The Centre Pompidou, Paris — the landmark of high-tech architecture

Origins

The movement grew out of the British post-war avant-garde, especially the Archigram group's visionary projects of the 1960s and the work of Team 4. Its acknowledged landmark is the Centre Pompidou in Paris (1971–77), designed by Renzo Piano, Richard Rogers, and Gianfranco Franchini, which turned the building inside out by placing all its structural and service elements — colour-coded by function — on the façade, freeing the interior as a single flexible space.

Characteristics

High-tech buildings celebrate technology and engineering rather than concealing them: skeleton frames are displayed, mechanical systems are visible, and interiors are designed for flexibility and change. Materials are industrial — steel, aluminium, glass, and plastic — and finishes often use bright, primary colours. Leading practitioners include Rogers, Piano, Norman Foster (whose HSBC headquarters in Hong Kong and Sainsbury Centre for Visual Arts in Norwich are key works), Nicholas Grimshaw, and Michael and Patty Hopkins.

Later development

In the 1980s high-tech became the favoured style of corporate and institutional clients, exemplified by the Lloyd's building in London (Rogers, 1978–86). By the 1990s several of its practitioners had turned toward ecological concerns, producing an "eco-tech" architecture that combined exposed technology with energy-conscious design, as in Foster's Reichstag dome in Berlin and Commerzbank Tower in Frankfurt.
