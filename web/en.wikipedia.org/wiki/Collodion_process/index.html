The collodion process was one of the principal photographic processes of the nineteenth century. Introduced by the English sculptor Frederick Scott Archer in 1851, it produced a negative on a glass plate that could be used to print many sharp positive copies, combining the fine detail of the daguerreotype with the reproducibility of the paper-negative calotype.

Method

The photographer coated a sheet of glass with collodion — a syrupy solution of nitrocellulose in ether and alcohol — containing potassium iodide, then immersed the still-tacky plate in a bath of silver nitrate to make it light-sensitive. The plate had to be exposed in the camera and developed (typically with an iron-based developer) while still wet, all within roughly ten to fifteen minutes, before being fixed with sodium thiosulfate or potassium cyanide and washed. Because the entire sequence had to happen on the spot, landscape and battlefield photographers travelled with portable darkrooms, often converted wagons.

Variants and use

The underexposed collodion negative, backed with black material, appeared as a positive and was sold cheaply as the ambrotype; on a thin sheet of iron it became the tintype (ferrotype), the inexpensive portrait format of the American Civil War era. Wet collodion was the medium of the great documentary photographs of the 1850s–1870s: Roger Fenton's Crimean War images, the American Civil War photographs of Mathew Brady's operators and Alexander Gardner, and early expedition and architectural photography worldwide.

Decline and revival

Factory-made gelatin dry plates, commercially available from the late 1870s, freed photographers from the darkroom wagon and displaced wet collodion in general use by the 1880s, though the process survived in photoengraving and photomechanical printing into the twentieth century. Since the 1990s the wet-plate process has enjoyed an artistic revival, valued for its tonal range, imperfections and historic character.
