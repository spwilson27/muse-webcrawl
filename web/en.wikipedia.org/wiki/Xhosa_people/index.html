Xhosa people

The Xhosa people (Xhosa: amaXhosa) are a Nguni ethnic group and nation native to Southern Africa, primarily inhabiting the Eastern Cape and Western Cape provinces of South Africa. They are the second-largest ethnic group in South Africa after the Zulu people, numbering roughly 8–9 million, and their language, isiXhosa, is one of the country's official languages and is notable for its click consonants.

History

The Xhosa are part of the Nguni peoples who migrated southward along Africa's east coast over many centuries. By the 18th century, Xhosa chiefdoms occupied the territory between the Great Fish River and the lands further east. A series of nine frontier wars (1779–1879) between Xhosa kingdoms and European settlers, later the British Cape Colony, gradually brought Xhosa lands under colonial control. The cattle-killing movement of 1856–57, prompted by the prophetess Nongqawuse, devastated Xhosa society and accelerated incorporation into the colonial economy.

During the apartheid era, the Xhosa homelands of Transkei and Ciskei were created by the South African government. Many prominent anti-apartheid leaders were Xhosa, including Nelson Mandela, Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, and Desmond Tutu.

Culture

Traditional Xhosa society is organized into patrilineal clans (iziduko) under chiefs. Important customs include ulwaluko, the initiation rite marking the transition to manhood, and elaborate beadwork whose colors and patterns carry social meaning. Cattle hold central economic and ritual importance. Oral tradition, including praise poetry (izibongo), remains a valued art form.

Language

IsiXhosa is a Bantu language of the Nguni group, closely related to isiZulu. About 15 percent of its vocabulary derives from Khoisan languages, and it uses three click consonant types represented by the letters c, q, and x.
