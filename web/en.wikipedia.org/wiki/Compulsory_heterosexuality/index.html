Compulsory heterosexuality

Compulsory heterosexuality is the theory that heterosexuality is assumed and enforced by a patriarchal society, rather than being simply a natural or freely chosen orientation. The term was popularised by the poet and essayist Adrienne Rich in her influential 1980 essay "Compulsory Heterosexuality and Lesbian Existence", published in the journal Signs.

Rich's argument

Rich argued that heterosexuality should be studied as a political institution, not merely a private preference, and that a range of social forces — economic dependence of women on men, the denial and stigmatisation of lesbian existence in scholarship and culture, laws, religious teaching, media imagery, and violence — channel women into heterosexual relationships and marriage. She criticised feminist writing that treated lesbianism as marginal or merely sexual, and proposed the concept of a "lesbian continuum": a range of woman-identified experience, including friendship, solidarity, and community, through which women resist male power. The essay is a landmark of second-wave and lesbian feminism and was widely reprinted, including in Rich's collection Blood, Bread, and Poetry (1986).

Development and critique

The concept influenced later theory: Judith Butler's account of the "heterosexual matrix" in Gender Trouble (1990) developed related ideas about how norms of sex, gender, and desire reinforce one another, and the later shorthand "comphet" circulated the idea widely in online LGBTQ communities. Critics have questioned aspects of Rich's essay: its treatment of women of colour and of cultures outside the West, its relative neglect of women who experience heterosexual desire as authentic, and the breadth of the "lesbian continuum", which some lesbian scholars argued diluted lesbian identity. Rich herself revised parts of the argument in later forewords. Historians also note precursors in earlier feminist and gay-liberation writing, including the Radicalesbians' 1970 manifesto "The Woman-Identified Woman".
