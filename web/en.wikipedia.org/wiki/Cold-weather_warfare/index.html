Cold-weather warfare

Ski troops in winter camouflage; mobility on skis and snowshoes is central to cold-weather operations.

Cold-weather warfare, also known as arctic warfare or winter warfare, comprises military operations carried out in extremely cold weather and snow-covered terrain, mainly in subarctic and arctic regions and in high mountains during winter. Cold, snow and ice affect soldiers, weapons, vehicles, logistics and medical care in ways that require specialised training, clothing and equipment.

Effects of cold

Extreme cold causes frostbite and hypothermia, increases caloric needs, freezes lubricants and batteries, makes metals brittle, and complicates the treatment and evacuation of the wounded. Snow can impede wheeled movement yet support mobility on skis, snowshoes and low-ground-pressure vehicles; white camouflage and heated shelters become essential.

History

Famous winter campaigns include Napoleon's 1812 invasion of Russia, the Finnish defence in the Winter War of 1939–40 (whose ski troops and motti tactics became textbook examples), the Eastern Front winters of the Second World War, the Battle of Chosin Reservoir in Korea (1950), and the fighting around the Siachen Glacier between India and Pakistan.

Modern forces

States with arctic territory—such as Norway, Finland, Sweden, Canada, Russia and the United States (notably in Alaska)—maintain dedicated training centres and doctrines for cold-weather operations, an area of growing strategic interest as Arctic sea routes open.
