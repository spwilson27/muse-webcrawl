Abstract algebra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Abstract algebra is the study of algebraic structures — sets equipped with operations satisfying stated axioms — including groups, rings, fields, modules, vector spaces and lattices. Rather than solving particular equations, it classifies and compares the structures themselves, discovering results that apply simultaneously to numbers, symmetries, matrices, permutations and many other systems. It is a central pillar of modern mathematics, and it grew out of 19th-century work on polynomial equations and number systems.

History

The subject crystallised when Évariste Galois, in the 1830s, used groups of permutations to show why no general formula exists for solving quintic equations (extending the work of Abel) — the birth of group theory and of Galois theory. Cayley gave the first abstract definition of a group (1854); Dedekind and others developed ideals in rings; and in the 1920s–30s Emmy Noether's axiomatic approach unified the field, earning her the retrospective title "mother of modern abstract algebra". The landmark textbook Moderne Algebra (van der Waerden, 1930), based on lectures by Noether and Emil Artin, taught the structural viewpoint to generations.

Main structures

A group is a set with one associative operation, an identity and inverses — the algebra of symmetry, appearing from crystal structures to particle physics. A ring adds a second operation (like addition and multiplication in the integers); a field is a ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse (the rationals, reals and complex numbers). Vector spaces generalise arrows and coordinates. Applications include error-correcting codes, cryptography (elliptic-curve methods rest on group theory), and the classification of particles by symmetry groups — the same mathematics that underlies the group theory used across physics.
