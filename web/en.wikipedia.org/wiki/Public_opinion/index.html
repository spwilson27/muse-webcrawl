Public opinion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public opinion, or popular opinion, is the aggregate of individual attitudes or beliefs held by the population of a given society — for example, the views of an electorate on a political issue, a government, or a public figure. Political scientists and sociologists study how public opinion forms, how it changes, and how it constrains or influences democratic politics.

Formation

Public opinion is shaped by family, education, peer groups, and the mass media. Concepts such as agenda-setting (the media's power to determine which issues the public thinks about), framing, and priming describe media influence. Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann's "spiral of silence" theory holds that people conceal views they believe to be in the minority, making majority views appear stronger still. Political scientist John Zaller modelled opinion formation as a process in which citizens sample from the elite messages they receive, depending on their political awareness and predispositions.

Measurement

The standard instrument for measuring public opinion is the opinion poll, developed scientifically by George Gallup and others in the 1930s using representative sampling. Famous failures — such as the Literary Digest straw poll of 1936 and the polling errors surrounding the 1948 U.S. presidential election — drove the adoption of quota and probability sampling. Modern polling increasingly uses online panels and modelling techniques such as multilevel regression and post-stratification. Questions of wording, question order, nonresponse, and "shy" respondents remain persistent sources of error.

Public opinion and politics

In democratic theory, responsiveness to public opinion is both a norm and an empirical question: studies find that policy often tracks majority preferences, especially on salient issues, though critics argue that organised interests and elites have disproportionate influence. Leaders also attempt to lead and shape opinion ("going public"), and measured opinion itself, once published, can influence behaviour through bandwagon and expectation effects.
