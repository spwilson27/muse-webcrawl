HighScope

The HighScope early childhood education approach, developed by psychologist David Weikart and colleagues in Ypsilanti, Michigan, beginning in the 1960s, emphasises "active participatory learning" in which children construct their own knowledge through direct experiences with people, materials and ideas. It is best known as the curriculum of the Perry Preschool Project (1962–1967).

The Perry Preschool study

The Perry Preschool Project randomly assigned 123 low-income African American children to a high-quality preschool programme using the HighScope approach or to no preschool. Followed longitudinally into middle age, the programme group showed higher educational attainment, earnings and rates of home ownership, and lower rates of arrest — findings that made the study one of the most cited cost–benefit analyses in early education (economist James Heckman estimated a return of 7–10% per annum on the investment).

The approach

Central elements include the plan–do–review sequence, in which children plan an activity, carry it out, and recall and reflect on it; a consistent daily routine; learning centres with labelled, accessible materials; content organised into "key developmental indicators"; and teachers who act as partners in children's play, using conflict-resolution steps to solve social problems. The HighScope Educational Research Foundation continues to publish curriculum materials, assessments (such as the COR Advantage) and training.
