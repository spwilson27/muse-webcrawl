The cinema of the United States, consisting mainly of major film studios (also known metonymously as Hollywood) along with some independent films, has had a large effect on the global film industry since the early 20th century. The dominant style of American cinema is classical Hollywood cinema, which developed from 1910 to 1962 and remains typical of most films made there.

History

The industry began on the East Coast around Thomas Edison's motion-picture patents before producers relocated to the Los Angeles district of Hollywood for its light, varied scenery, and distance from Edison's lawyers. By the 1920s the vertically integrated studio system dominated world cinema. Sound (from 1927), Technicolor, and the star system defined the Golden Age of the 1930s–40s, which produced Gone with the Wind and Casablanca.

The 1948 Paramount Decrees, television, and suburbanisation weakened the majors; the ratings system (1968) replaced the Production Code. The "New Hollywood" of the late 1960s–70s (Coppola, Scorsese, Altman) gave way to the blockbuster era launched by Jaws (1975) and Star Wars (1977). Conglomeration, franchise filmmaking, digital production, and the rise of streaming services in the 2010s–2020s reshaped the business, while American films continued to dominate global box office.

Industry

The "Big Five" major studios today are Walt Disney Pictures, Warner Bros. Pictures, Universal Pictures, Paramount Pictures, and Columbia Pictures (Sony). Independent and arthouse sectors operate alongside through companies such as A24 and Neon. The industry's awards centre on the Academy Awards, held at the Dolby Theatre.
