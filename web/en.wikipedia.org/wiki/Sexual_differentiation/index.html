Sexual differentiation

Sexual differentiation is the process by which an initially undifferentiated embryo develops the anatomical and physiological characteristics of a male or a female. In mammals it unfolds in stages: chromosomal sex is set at fertilisation, gonadal sex develops from the bipotential gonad, and phenotypic sex — the internal ducts and external genitalia — is driven mainly by hormones secreted by the gonads.

Gonadal differentiation

In humans, the presence of the SRY gene on the Y chromosome around week 6–7 of gestation triggers the genital ridge to form testes, via up-regulation of SOX9 and differentiation of Sertoli and Leydig cells. In the absence of SRY, ovarian pathways (involving WNT4, RSPO1, and FOXL2) predominate and the gonad becomes an ovary.

Phenotypic differentiation

Testicular hormones direct the male phenotype: anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) from Sertoli cells causes the Müllerian ducts to regress, while testosterone from Leydig cells maintains the Wolffian ducts (forming epididymis, vas deferens, and seminal vesicles) and, converted to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), masculinises the external genitalia. Without these hormones, the Wolffian ducts regress, the Müllerian ducts form the uterus, fallopian tubes, and upper vagina, and the genitalia develop along female lines — experiments by Alfred Jost in 1947 famously showed that removing the gonads from rabbit embryos of either chromosomal sex yields female development.

Brain and behaviour

In many animals, perinatal hormones organise sexually dimorphic brain circuits; the extent and mechanisms of such effects in humans remain an active research area.

Variations

Variations at any stage can produce differences (disorders) of sex development (DSD), such as androgen insensitivity syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and Turner and Klinefelter syndromes, some of which fall under the umbrella of intersex variations.
