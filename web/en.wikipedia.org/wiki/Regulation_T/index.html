Regulation T

Regulation T is a regulation of the United States Federal Reserve Board (12 CFR Part 220) governing the extension of credit by securities brokers and dealers. It implements Section 7 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 by setting the initial margin requirement for purchases of securities on credit in margin accounts. Since 1974 the initial margin requirement has been 50 per cent: an investor may borrow up to half the purchase price of a margin security from their broker, depositing the rest in cash or securities.

Scope and mechanics

Regulation T applies to credit extended by broker-dealers to customers for buying or carrying securities, in margin accounts and in the special cash, arbitrage and other accounts it defines. It specifies which securities are "margin securities" (exchange-listed and certain OTC securities, and securities convertible into them), sets payment periods for cash-account purchases (with "freeriding" prohibitions when a security bought in a cash account is sold before it is paid for), and governs how much credit may be extended against collateral, including rules for portfolio-margining arrangements added in the 2000s for eligible accounts. The Board has not changed the 50% initial margin since January 1974, preferring other macroprudential tools; maintenance margin levels are instead set by self-regulatory organisations such as FINRA (25% minimum for long stock positions) and by firms' house rules.

History

Margin lending fuelled speculation before the 1929 Crash, and Congress gave the Federal Reserve authority over securities credit in the 1934 Act. The Board first set margin requirements in 1934 (via a formula), moving to fixed percentages from 1936; the requirement varied between 40% and 100% in the mid-twentieth century as an instrument of monetary and anti-speculation policy — reaching 100% in 1946 — before settling at 50% in 1974. Regulation U (banks), Regulation G (repealed and folded into U in 1998) and Regulation X (borrowers obtaining credit abroad) complete the statutory scheme.
