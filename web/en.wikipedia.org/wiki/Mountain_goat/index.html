The mountain goat (Oreamnos americanus), also known as the Rocky Mountain goat, is a cloven-footed mammal endemic to the remote and rugged mountainous areas of western North America. Despite its vernacular name, it is not a true goat of the genus Capra, but belongs to the goat-antelope tribe (Rupicaprini), and is the only surviving species of its genus.

Description

Both sexes have shaggy white double coats — a dense wool undercoat beneath long guard hairs — that protect them in temperatures as low as −46 °C (−51 °F), along with black horns up to about 30 cm long, beards, and short black tails. Males (billies) weigh 70–145 kg; females (nannies) are somewhat smaller. Their cloven hooves have rough, pliable pads and sharp dewclaws, giving extraordinary grip on steep rock, and their muscular shoulders help them climb cliffs where few predators can follow.

Behaviour and habitat

Mountain goats inhabit alpine and subalpine terrain in the Rockies, Cascades, and coastal ranges from Alaska and British Columbia to Idaho and Montana, and have been introduced to several other ranges. They graze grasses, herbs, lichens, and mosses, migrating seasonally between high summer pastures and lower, sheltered wintering areas, and visiting natural salt licks. Nannies live with kids in nursery groups; billies are largely solitary outside the November–December rut, during which males spar and sometimes fight on cliff edges. Gestation is about six months, and a single kid is usual.

Conservation

The species is listed as Least Concern; regulated hunting and careful management follow the die-off caused by market hunting in the early 20th century. Introduced populations, notably in Olympic National Park, were removed (2018–2020) because the non-native goats damaged fragile alpine vegetation and had become dangerously habituated to people.
