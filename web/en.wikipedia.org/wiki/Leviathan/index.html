Leviathan

Leviathan (Hebrew: לִוְיָתָן, Līvyāṯān) is a sea serpent noted in theology and mythology. It is referenced in several books of the Hebrew Bible, including Psalms, the Book of Job, the Book of Isaiah, the Book of Amos, and, according to some translations, in the Book of Jonah; it is also mentioned in the Book of Enoch. The Leviathan is often an embodiment of chaos and threatens to eat the damned after their life ends. In the end, it is annihilated. Leviathan is sometimes used as a synonym for huge sea monsters in general.

Biblical references

Psalm 74 declares that God "broke the heads of Leviathan in pieces, and gave him to be food to the creatures inhabiting the wilderness", while Psalm 104 presents it as a creature God formed to play in the sea. In Job 41, Leviathan is described in elaborate detail — armored with scales no weapon can pierce, breathing fire and smoke — as a demonstration to Job of God's power over creation. Isaiah 27 prophesies that God will punish "leviathan the piercing serpent... and he shall slay the dragon that is in the sea."

Origins

Scholars see Leviathan as related to older Near Eastern combat myths, particularly the storm god Baal's defeat of the seven-headed serpent Lotan in Ugaritic texts, and in turn to Babylonian Tiamat, the salt-water goddess slain by Marduk. The Hebrew name derives from a root meaning "to twist, to coil" — the "coiled one".

Jewish and Christian tradition

In Talmudic legend, God created a male and female Leviathan but destroyed the female lest their offspring overwhelm the world; at the end of days, the righteous will feast on the flesh of Leviathan, Behemoth, and Ziz beneath a canopy made from its skin. Medieval Christian writers identified Leviathan with Satan and with the mouth of Hell. Thomas Hobbes borrowed the name for his 1651 treatise Leviathan, casting the sovereign commonwealth as a "mortal god", and the word entered general usage for anything of immense size or power — from great whales and warships to the modern state itself.
