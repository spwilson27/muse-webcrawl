Eugène Scribe

Augustin Eugène Scribe (24 December 1791 – 20 February 1861) was a French dramatist and librettist. Immensely prolific and commercially successful, he perfected the "well-made play" (pièce bien faite) — a tightly engineered drama of secrets, coincidences and reversals whose techniques dominated the nineteenth-century stage and still underpin popular drama and screenwriting.

Career

Born in Paris, the son of a silk merchant, Scribe abandoned the law for the theatre. After early failures he found success with vaudevilles written with collaborators, developing a veritable workshop system in which assistants drafted scenes he revised. His comedies of manners, such as Le Verre d'eau (1840, "The Glass of Water"), dramatise the idea that great historical events turn on trifling causes. He was elected to the Académie française in 1834.

Opera librettos

Scribe was the defining librettist of French grand opera, writing the texts for Auber's La muette de Portici (1828) and Fra Diavolo, Meyerbeer's Robert le diable, Les Huguenots, Le prophète and L'Africaine, Halévy's La Juive, and Donizetti's La favorite; he also supplied the libretto adapted for Verdi's Les vêpres siciliennes and Un ballo in maschera. In all he is credited with several hundred stage works.

Influence

The well-made play's machinery — exposition withheld, the obligatory scene (scène à faire), climaxes at each curtain — was taken up by Henrik Ibsen, who learned from it even while deepening its psychology, and passed through Sardou and the boulevard theatre into cinema. Later critics mocked Scribe's clockwork plotting, but his structural craft remains a foundation of dramatic writing.
