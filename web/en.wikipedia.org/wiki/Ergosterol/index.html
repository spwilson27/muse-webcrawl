Ergosterol

Ergosterol, the principal sterol of fungal cell membranes.

Ergosterol (ergosta-5,7,10(19),22-tetraen-3β-ol) is a mycosterol found in cell membranes of fungi and protozoa, serving many of the same functions that cholesterol serves in animal cells. Because many fungi and protozoa cannot survive without ergosterol, the enzymes that create it have become important targets for drug discovery. In human nutrition, ergosterol is a biological precursor to vitamin D2; the chemical composition of ergosterol is converted to viosterol by exposure to ultraviolet light, and then to vitamin D2.

Role in fungi

Ergosterol is the primary sterol in fungal membranes, where it regulates membrane fluidity, permeability, and the activity of membrane-bound enzymes. Its absence from animal cell membranes makes the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway a selective target for antifungal drugs.

Antifungal drug target

Amphotericin B, an antifungal drug, causes pores to form in fungal membranes by binding to ergosterol; the affected cells leak potassium ions and other cellular contents, causing cell death. Azole antifungals such as fluconazole and miconazole work by inhibiting the enzyme 14α-demethylase, which is needed in the synthesis of ergosterol, depleting the membrane of the sterol and impairing fungal growth.

Precursor to vitamin D2

Ergosterol is a provitamin form of vitamin D2. Exposure of ergosterol-rich foods such as mushrooms to ultraviolet light converts ergosterol into vitamin D2, a process exploited commercially to produce vitamin D supplements.
