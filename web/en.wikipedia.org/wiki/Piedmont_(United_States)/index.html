The Piedmont is a plateau region located in the eastern United States. It sits between the Atlantic coastal plain and the main Appalachian Mountains, stretching from New York in the north to central Alabama in the south. The word Piedmont comes from the Italian Piemonte, meaning "foot of the mountains", a reference to the region of Italy at the foot of the Alps.

Geology and geography

The Piedmont is underlain by ancient, deeply eroded metamorphic and igneous rocks — the roots of mountains raised in the Palaeozoic. Its gently rolling surface is cut by river valleys, and isolated hills (monadnocks) such as Stone Mountain in Georgia rise above the general level. Soils are typically red clays formed by long weathering.

The fall line

The eastern edge of the Piedmont is the fall line, where hard crystalline rock meets the soft sediments of the coastal plain. Rivers descending it form rapids and waterfalls that historically powered mills and marked the head of navigation; many major cities — including Trenton, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington, D.C., Richmond, Columbia and Augusta — grew up at fall-line sites.

History and settlement

Farmed first by Native American peoples, the Piedmont was settled by European colonists moving inland from the tidewater. It became a major agricultural region (tobacco, then cotton) and later a centre of textile manufacturing, furniture and tobacco processing in the Carolinas and Virginia. The Piedmont Triad and Piedmont Crescent regions of North Carolina remain important urban corridors.

Major cities

Cities in or at the edge of the Piedmont include Atlanta, Charlotte, Greensboro, Raleigh–Durham, Richmond, Greenville–Spartanburg, and the Washington–Baltimore area at its margin.
