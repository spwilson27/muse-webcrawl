Clavicle

Clavicle
Common name: Collarbone
Articulations: Sternum, acromion of scapula
Classification: Long bone
First bone to ossify in the fetus

The clavicle, or collarbone, is a slender, S-shaped long bone approximately 15 cm (6 in) long that serves as a strut between the shoulder blade and the sternum (breastbone). There are two clavicles, one on the left and one on the right. The clavicle is the only long bone in the body that lies horizontally.

Structure

The clavicle is a doubly curved bone forming part of the shoulder girdle (pectoral girdle). Medially it articulates with the manubrium of the sternum at the sternoclavicular joint; laterally it articulates with the acromion of the scapula at the acromioclavicular joint. Its shaft has a medial two-thirds that is convex forward and a lateral third that is flattened and concave forward.

Function

The clavicle serves several functions: it acts as a strut holding the scapula in place so the arm can hang freely; it transmits physical impacts from the upper limb to the axial skeleton; and it protects the underlying neurovascular structures of the neck and shoulder.

Development

The clavicle is the first bone to begin ossification during fetal development, around the fifth and sixth weeks of gestation, and one of the last to complete it, at around 21–25 years of age. Unusually, it ossifies partly by intramembranous and partly by endochondral ossification.

Clinical significance

The clavicle is the most commonly fractured bone in the human body, typically from a fall onto an outstretched hand or a direct blow to the shoulder. Most fractures occur in the middle third of the shaft and heal well with conservative treatment, though displaced fractures may require surgical fixation.
