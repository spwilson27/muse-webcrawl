Embalming is the art and science of preserving human remains by treating them, in their modern form principally with chemicals, in order to forestall decomposition. The intention is to keep the remains suitable for public display at a funeral, for religious reasons, or for transport and anatomical study. A successful embalming temporarily preserves the body, disinfects it and restores a lifelike appearance.

History

Deliberate preservation of the dead is very ancient: the Chinchorro culture of coastal Chile and Peru mummified its dead from around 5000 BC, and ancient Egypt perfected an elaborate, natron-based mummification process tied to beliefs about the afterlife. Modern chemical embalming developed in the nineteenth century — Jean Gannal published a foundational manual in 1838 — and spread widely during the American Civil War, when embalmers such as Thomas Holmes prepared fallen soldiers for the journey home. Abraham Lincoln's embalmed body, carried by train to Illinois in 1865, showcased the technique to the American public and helped entrench open-casket funerals in the United States.

Modern process

In contemporary practice the embalmer washes and disinfects the body, sets the features, then injects preservative fluid — typically a solution based on formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde and methanol — into an artery (commonly the common carotid or femoral) while draining blood from a corresponding vein. The body cavities are aspirated and treated with concentrated fluid, and cosmetics and restoration complete the presentation. Embalming is routine in the United States and Canada, less common in much of Europe, and restricted or rare in some religions and jurisdictions; environmental concerns about formaldehyde have spurred alternatives such as refrigeration and "green" burial.
