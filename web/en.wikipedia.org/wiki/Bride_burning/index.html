Bride burning

Bride burning is a form of domestic violence and murder practised in parts of South Asia, particularly in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal. It occurs when a woman is killed by her husband or his family for her family's failure to meet demands for additional dowry. The victim is typically doused with a flammable liquid such as kerosene and set alight, and the death is often disguised as a kitchen accident or suicide.

Background

Dowry is the payment of money or goods by a bride's family to the groom and his family at marriage. Although dowry was prohibited in India by the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961, the practice remains widespread. Disputes over dowry may lead to harassment, violence and, in the most extreme cases, murder by burning.

Legal response

In India, Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code (introduced in 1983) criminalises cruelty by a husband or his relatives, and Section 304B (introduced in 1986) created the offence of "dowry death," covering deaths of women within seven years of marriage under suspicious circumstances connected with dowry demands. Under the Indian Evidence Act, courts may presume that a dowry death was caused by the persons who subjected the woman to cruelty shortly before her death.

Incidence

Official statistics in India have recorded several thousand dowry deaths annually, with several thousand more cases registered for cruelty by husbands or relatives. Activists and researchers note that many cases go unreported or are recorded as accidents, so the true incidence is believed to be higher.

Related practices

Bride burning is one form of dowry-related violence; others include acid attacks and other forms of dowry harassment. It is distinct from sati, the historical practice of a widow immolating herself on her husband's funeral pyre.
