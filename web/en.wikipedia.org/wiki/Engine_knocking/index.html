Engine knocking

Engine knocking (also called knock, detonation, spark knock, pinking or pinging) is an undesirable combustion phenomenon in spark-ignition internal combustion engines. It occurs when part of the air–fuel mixture in the cylinder ignites spontaneously — auto-ignites — before the flame front from the spark plug reaches it, rather than burning smoothly. The near-instantaneous combustion of the remaining charge generates extremely rapid pressure spikes and a characteristic metallic pinging or knocking sound, and sustained heavy knock can damage pistons, rings and cylinder heads.

In knock, the end-gas auto-ignites ahead of the propagating flame front.

Mechanism

During normal combustion the spark initiates a flame front that travels smoothly across the chamber, compressing and heating the unburned "end-gas" ahead of it. If the end-gas remains at high temperature and pressure long enough, its auto-ignition delay elapses and it combusts almost instantaneously, creating pressure waves that resonate within the cylinder bore — typically in the 5–25 kHz range depending on bore size. Knock is distinct from pre-ignition, in which the charge is ignited before the spark by a hot surface; pre-ignition can in turn provoke destructive "super-knock" in modern turbocharged direct-injection engines, a phenomenon known as LSPI (low-speed pre-ignition).

Causes and effects

Knock tendency increases with compression ratio, intake temperature and pressure (boost), lean mixtures at high load, excessive spark advance, and fuels of low octane rating. It is the principal factor limiting the compression ratio — and hence the thermal efficiency — of petrol engines. Light knock wastes fuel and increases emissions; heavy knock erodes piston crowns and ring lands and can destroy an engine in minutes.

Control

Fuel resistance to knock is measured by the octane rating (RON and MON). Historically, tetraethyl lead was added to petrol as an anti-knock agent from the 1920s until its phase-out on health grounds (completed globally in 2021). Modern engines use piezoelectric knock sensors bolted to the block; the engine control unit retards ignition timing (and may reduce boost or enrich the mixture) when knock is detected, allowing engines to run close to the knock limit for efficiency. Design measures include compact combustion chambers, central spark plugs, direct injection (whose charge-cooling suppresses knock) and water injection.
