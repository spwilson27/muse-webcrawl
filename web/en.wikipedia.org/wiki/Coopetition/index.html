Coopetition

Coopetition: rivals cooperate to grow the market while competing to divide it.

Coopetition (also spelled co-opetition) is a neologism coined to describe cooperative competition: a business strategy in which competing firms cooperate in some areas while competing in others. The term was popularised by Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff in their 1996 book Co-opetition, which applied game theory to business strategy.

Concept

In traditional strategy, business is often framed as a war in which one company's gain is another's loss. Coopetition rejects the purely zero-sum view: competitors may cooperate to create value — for example by developing shared technical standards, funding joint research, or building common infrastructure — while continuing to compete over customers, prices and market share. Brandenburger and Nalebuff formalised this with the "value net", which maps a firm's customers, suppliers, competitors and complementors.

Examples

  • Rival car manufacturers jointly developing platforms, batteries or charging standards while competing on branding and sales.
  • Airlines cooperating within alliances on routes and loyalty programmes while competing on price and service.
  • Technology companies contributing to shared open-source projects that they also compete to commercialise.

Criticism and risks

Coopetition carries risks: cooperation can drift into collusion that harms consumers and breaches competition law, and sharing knowledge with a rival can erode a firm's competitive advantage. Managing the tension requires clear boundaries about which activities are cooperative and which remain competitive.
