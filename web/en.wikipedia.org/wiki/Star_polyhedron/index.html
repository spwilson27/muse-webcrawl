Star polyhedron

In geometry, a star polyhedron is a polyhedron which has some repetitive quality of nonconvexity giving it a star-like, visual quality. There are two general kinds of star polyhedra: self-intersecting polyhedra that repeat in a regular fashion, and self-intersecting polyhedra of particular types. The study of star polyhedra is usually concerned with regular, or uniform, examples.

Regular star polyhedra

The four regular star polyhedra are known as the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra: the small stellated dodecahedron, great dodecahedron, great stellated dodecahedron, and great icosahedron. Johannes Kepler described the two stellated forms in his Harmonices Mundi (1619), and Louis Poinsot rediscovered all four in 1809. Their faces or vertex figures are pentagrams ({5/2} polygons), and their Schläfli symbols involve fractional entries such as {5/2, 5} and {5/2, 3}.

Uniform star polyhedra

Beyond the regular forms there are 57 non-prismatic uniform nonconvex polyhedra, together with infinite families of nonconvex prisms and antiprisms. These were enumerated through the work of Coxeter, Longuet-Higgins, Miller, and Skilling in the twentieth century; Skilling's figure (1975) completed the list of 75 uniform polyhedra.

Stellation

Many star polyhedra arise by stellation — extending the faces of a convex polyhedron until they meet to form new closed solids. Famous stellations include the stellated octahedron (stella octangula, two interpenetrating tetrahedra depicted by Kepler) and the 58 stellations of the icosahedron catalogued in The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra (1938).
