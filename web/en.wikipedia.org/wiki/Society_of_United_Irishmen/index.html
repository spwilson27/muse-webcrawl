Society of United Irishmen


Founded: 1791
Dissolved: 1804
Headquarters: Belfast / Dublin
Ideology: Irish republicanism, liberalism, Catholic emancipation

The Society of United Irishmen was a sworn society in the Kingdom of Ireland formed in the wake of the French Revolution to secure "an equal representation of all the people" in a national government. It sought parliamentary reform and, from 1795, worked toward an independent Irish republic. Its leadership included Theobald Wolfe Tone, Thomas Russell, Henry Joy McCracken, and William Drennan.

Origins

The Society was founded in Belfast in October 1791 by a group of Presbyterian radicals inspired by the American and French revolutions and by Thomas Paine's Rights of Man. A Dublin society followed in November. Its original aims were constitutional reform, Catholic emancipation, and the union of Irishmen of all religions — reflected in its resolve to substitute "the common name of Irishman in place of the denominations of Protestant, Catholic and Dissenter".

Radicalisation and rebellion

Britain's war with revolutionary France from 1793 drove the Society underground; it was suppressed in 1794 and reorganised as a secret, oath-bound revolutionary movement allied with France. In May 1798 it launched the Irish Rebellion of 1798, which, despite French assistance, was defeated with great loss of life. Wolfe Tone died in prison in November 1798 after capture at sea.

Legacy

The United Irishmen are regarded as founders of Irish republicanism. Their movement influenced later organisations from the Young Irelanders to the Irish Republican Brotherhood, and Tone is commemorated annually at Bodenstown.
