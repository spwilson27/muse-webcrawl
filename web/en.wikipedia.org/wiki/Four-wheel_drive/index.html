A four-wheel drive, also called 4×4 ("four by four") or 4WD, is a two-axle vehicle drivetrain capable of providing torque to all of its wheels simultaneously. It may be full-time or on-demand, and is typically linked via a transfer case providing an additional output drive shaft along with additional gear ranges.

Terminology

In North American usage, "four-wheel drive" usually denotes part-time systems for off-road use, with a selectable low-range gear set, whereas all-wheel drive (AWD) refers to full-time systems, often in cars and crossovers, that cannot be switched off. Conventions vary by manufacturer and market, and marketing usage blurs the distinction.

History

Early four-wheel-drive vehicles included the Dutch Spyker of 1903 and the American Four Wheel Drive Auto Company's trucks. The system became famous through the wartime Jeep and the post-war Land Rover. Ferguson Formula four-wheel drive influenced later road cars; the Jensen FF of the 1960s was an early performance application, and Audi's quattro system (1980) transformed rallying and spread full-time AWD through the passenger-car market. Subaru built its brand around symmetrical all-wheel drive.

Operation

Power is split between axles by a centre differential, viscous coupling, multi-plate clutch, or locking transfer case. Because the front and rear wheels travel different distances in a turn, a rigidly locked part-time system causes driveline "wind-up" on high-grip surfaces and is meant for loose or slippery ground. Modern systems use electronic control and individual wheel braking to distribute torque where grip is available.
