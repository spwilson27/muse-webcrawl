History of mathematics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE) defined mathematical proof for two millennia.

The history of mathematics traces the development of number, structure, space and change from tally marks and Babylonian and Egyptian computation, through the Greek invention of deductive proof, the achievements of Chinese, Indian and Islamic scholars, the European calculus revolution, and the abstraction and rigour of the 19th and 20th centuries.

Ancient mathematics

Babylonian scribes used a sexagesimal place-value system and solved quadratic problems by 1800 BCE; Egyptian sources such as the Rhind papyrus show unit-fraction arithmetic. Greek mathematics transformed the field: Thales and Pythagoras are credited with early theorems, and Euclid's Elements organised geometry axiomatically. Archimedes anticipated integration; Apollonius studied conics; Diophantus pioneered algebraic notation. Chinese mathematicians produced the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art and a remainder theorem; Indian scholars developed the decimal place-value system with zero, transmitted to Europe via Islamic scholars such as al-Khwarizmi, whose name gives us "algorithm".

Early modern mathematics

Renaissance algebra (Cardano, Tartaglia), Viète's symbolism and Descartes's analytic geometry (1637) prepared the calculus, invented independently by Newton and Leibniz in the 1670s–80s and developed by the Bernoullis and Euler, the most prolific mathematician of the 18th century. Probability emerged from the Pascal–Fermat correspondence of 1654 — see the history of probability.

Modern mathematics

The 19th century brought rigour (Cauchy, Weierstrass), non-Euclidean geometry (Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann), group theory (Galois), set theory (Cantor) and mathematical logic. The 20th century added Hilbert's problems (1900), Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931), Bourbaki's structuralism, computer-assisted proof, and the classification of finite simple groups, while applied mathematics fused with statistics and computing.
