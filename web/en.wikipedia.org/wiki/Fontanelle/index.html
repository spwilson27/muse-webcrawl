Fontanelle

A fontanelle (or fontanel) is an anatomical feature of the infant human skull comprising soft membranous gaps (sutures) between the cranial bones that make up the calvaria of a fetus or infant. Fontanelles allow for stretching and deformation of the neurocranium during birth and in early life, easing passage through the birth canal and accommodating rapid brain growth.

Types

A newborn has six fontanelles. The largest and best known is the anterior fontanelle, a diamond-shaped opening at the junction of the frontal and parietal bones, commonly called the "soft spot". The smaller triangular posterior fontanelle lies at the junction of the parietal and occipital bones. Two sphenoidal and two mastoid fontanelles are found on the sides of the skull.

Closure

The posterior fontanelle usually closes by about two months of age, while the anterior fontanelle typically closes between 12 and 18 months, with a normal range extending to about two years. As the cranial bones ossify and the sutures knit, the fontanelles disappear.

Clinical significance

Examination of the fontanelles is a routine part of infant assessment. A sunken anterior fontanelle can indicate dehydration, while a bulging fontanelle may signal raised intracranial pressure, as in meningitis or hydrocephalus. Persistently large fontanelles or delayed closure can be associated with conditions such as hypothyroidism, Down syndrome, or rickets, while premature closure relates to craniosynostosis.
