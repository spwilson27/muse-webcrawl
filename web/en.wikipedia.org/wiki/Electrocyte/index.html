An electrocyte is a specialised cell, derived from muscle tissue, used by electric fish to generate electric fields for navigation, communication, defence and stunning prey. Also called electroplaques, electrocytes are flattened, disc-like cells stacked in long columns within the animal's electric organ. Each individual cell contributes only about a tenth of a volt, but thousands arranged in series (and many columns in parallel) can sum to the several hundred volts produced by a large electric eel.

Mechanism

Electrocytes are innervated on one face only. At rest, pumps maintain ion gradients so both faces sit at a similar potential. When a nerve signal arrives, the innervated face depolarises — sodium channels open and its membrane potential reverses — while the opposite face stays polarised, creating a small net voltage across the cell. Because the cells are oriented the same way in a stack, their voltages add like batteries in series. Synchronous firing, coordinated by a pacemaker nucleus in the brain, produces a brief, powerful discharge.

Evolution and research

Strongly and weakly electric organs evolved independently in at least six lineages of fish, in both freshwater South American and African groups and in marine rays and stargazers. Electric fish inspired early studies of electricity — Volta modelled his battery on the torpedo ray's organ — and electrocytes remain a model system in neuroscience, historically important for purifying the acetylcholine receptor. Engineers have mimicked their stacking principle in experimental biological batteries and soft power sources.
