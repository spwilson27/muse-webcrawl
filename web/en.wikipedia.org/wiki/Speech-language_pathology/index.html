Speech-language pathology

Speech-language pathology (also known as speech and language therapy or logopedics in parts of Europe) is a healthcare and education field addressing communication and swallowing disorders. Practitioners — speech-language pathologists (SLPs), or speech therapists — assess, diagnose and treat speech sound disorders, language disorders, stuttering, voice disorders, aphasia, cognitive-communication disorders, and dysphagia across the lifespan.

An SLP working with a child using picture cards.

Scope of practice

SLPs work in schools, hospitals, rehabilitation centres, private practice, and academia. Their caseloads include toddlers with developmental language delay, children with speech sound disorders and autism, adults recovering language after stroke (aphasia therapy), people with Parkinson's disease (e.g., LSVT LOUD voice treatment), patients with head and neck cancer, and infants and adults with unsafe swallowing. Services also cover fluency (stuttering), resonance, social communication, and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Assessment and treatment

Assessment combines standardised tests, language samples, oral-mechanism examination, and instrumental studies such as videofluoroscopy for swallowing. Interventions are evidence-based and goal-directed, ranging from articulation drills and language intervention activities to melodic intonation therapy for aphasia, neuromuscular electrical stimulation in some dysphagia protocols, and counselling for people who stutter.

Training and regulation

In the United States, SLPs require a master's degree, supervised clinical fellowship, and state licensure; the professional body is ASHA (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association), which awards the Certificate of Clinical Competence. In the UK, speech and language therapists train via approved degrees and register with the HCPC, overseen professionally by the Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists.

History

The discipline grew from elocution and speech-correction movements of the 19th and early 20th centuries and was professionalised after the world wars, when rehabilitation of brain-injured servicemen demonstrated demand for aphasia services. Lee Silverman's and Lionel Logue's work with stuttering (Logue with King George VI) are celebrated episodes in its public history.
