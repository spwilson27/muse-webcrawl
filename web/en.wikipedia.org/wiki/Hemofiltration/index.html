Hemofiltration

Hemofiltration (American spelling), also spelled haemofiltration, is a renal replacement therapy used in intensive care medicine for patients with acute kidney injury. Blood is passed through a highly permeable filter; a pressure gradient drives plasma water and dissolved solutes across the membrane by convection, and the removed fluid is replaced with a sterile substitution fluid. Unlike hemodialysis, which relies mainly on diffusion, hemofiltration removes larger ("middle") molecules more effectively because solutes are dragged across the membrane with the water.

Modes

Hemofiltration is usually delivered continuously over 24 hours as part of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Variants include continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF), which combines convection with diffusion, and slow continuous ultrafiltration for fluid removal alone. Continuous therapy is generally better tolerated by haemodynamically unstable patients than intermittent dialysis because fluid and solute shifts are gentler.

Principles and practice

Blood is withdrawn through a large-bore central venous catheter, pumped through the hemofilter and returned to the patient. Anticoagulation — historically heparin, increasingly regional citrate — prevents the circuit from clotting. Ultrafiltrate volumes may reach 20–35 mL/kg per hour; the "dose" of therapy and its effect on survival have been studied in large trials, which found no benefit to very high-intensity treatment. Complications include electrolyte disturbances (notably phosphate and potassium depletion), nutrient and drug losses, hypothermia and line infection.

History

Arteriovenous hemofiltration was pioneered in the late 1970s by Peter Kramer and others as a simple bedside method of fluid control in critically ill patients; pumped venovenous systems developed in the 1980s and 1990s made controlled continuous therapy routine in intensive care units.
