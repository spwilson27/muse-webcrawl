Invoice discounting

An invoice used as security for short-term finance

Invoice discounting is a form of short-term working capital finance in which a business sells or pledges its accounts receivable — the unpaid invoices owed to it by customers — to a finance company (the discounter) in exchange for an immediate advance of most of the invoice value, typically 80–90 per cent. The balance, less the discounter's charges, is paid to the business once the customer settles the invoice.

Unlike factoring, invoice discounting is usually confidential: the business retains control of its own sales ledger and credit control, and its customers are not normally aware that a third party is involved. It is one of the principal forms of asset-based lending.

How it works

Under a typical whole-turnover invoice discounting facility, the client assigns its trade debts to the discounter. As invoices are raised, copies are uploaded to the discounter, which advances an agreed percentage (the prepayment percentage) within 24 hours. When the customer pays, the payment clears the advance and the discounter releases the remaining balance after deducting:

  • A service charge, a percentage of turnover covering administration and, sometimes, bad-debt protection.
  • A discount charge, an interest-like fee calculated on the amount advanced for the period until the invoice is paid, usually priced as a margin over a reference rate such as base rate or SONIA.

Facilities may be with recourse, meaning the client must repurchase debts that remain unpaid after an agreed period (commonly 90 days), or without recourse (non-recourse), where the discounter bears the risk of non-payment for approved debtors, effectively bundling credit insurance into the arrangement.

Differences from factoring

Invoice discounting and factoring both accelerate cash flow against receivables, but differ in administration and disclosure. In factoring, the factor typically takes over the sales ledger, issues statements and chases payment, and the arrangement is usually disclosed to customers. In invoice discounting the client keeps its ledger and customer relationships, and the facility can remain confidential. Because the discounter does less work and relies on the client's own credit control, discounting is generally offered to larger, more established businesses with robust accounting systems, while factoring is more common for smaller firms.

Advantages and disadvantages

The principal advantage is improved cash flow: a business need not wait 30–90 days for payment before funding wages, materials or growth. Because the facility grows automatically with sales (more invoices generate more availability), it is sometimes described as a self-liquidating form of finance. Against this, the cost is generally higher than a conventional overdraft or term loan, and the business remains exposed to customer default under recourse arrangements. Concentration limits may also restrict the proportion of the ledger attributable to a single large debtor that can be funded.

History

Financing trade debts against invoices has roots in the factoring houses of the textile trade in the 19th century, but confidential invoice discounting developed mainly in the United Kingdom from the 1960s onwards, offered by the clearing banks' specialist subsidiaries and independent finance houses. The industry is represented in the UK by UK Finance (successor to the Asset Based Finance Association), whose members report advances against invoices forming the largest part of asset-based lending to UK businesses.
