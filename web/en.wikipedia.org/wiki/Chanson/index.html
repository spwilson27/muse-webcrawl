Chanson

A chanson (French for "song") is, in its broadest sense, any lyric-driven French song. The term is used in at least three senses: the polyphonic song of the late Middle Ages and Renaissance; the solo song tradition of the French court and salon; and the modern chanson française — the cabaret and popular song tradition epitomised by Édith Piaf, Jacques Brel and Charles Aznavour, in which the lyrical text takes precedence over musical style.

Édith Piaf, the most famous exponent of the modern chanson réaliste.

Medieval and Renaissance chanson

The earliest chansons were the epic poems of the chansons de geste and the courtly songs of the trouvères. From the 14th century the polyphonic chanson flourished in the formes fixes — ballade, rondeau and virelai — in the works of Guillaume de Machaut, Guillaume Dufay and Josquin des Prez. The Parisian chanson of the 16th century, lighter and more chordal, was popularised by Clément Janequin, whose pieces imitated battle sounds, birdsong and street cries.

Chanson réaliste and the modern tradition

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, singers of the Parisian cabarets and music halls developed the chanson réaliste, songs of the poor and outcast districts, sung by artists such as Aristide Bruant and later Piaf. After the Second World War the singer-songwriter (auteur-compositeur-interprète) became the dominant figure: Georges Brassens, Jacques Brel, Léo Ferré, Charles Aznavour, Barbara and Serge Gainsbourg wrote literary, often poetic chansons that remain central to French cultural identity. The tradition persists in artists who prioritise text and storytelling over genre conventions.
