Government bond

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A government bond or sovereign bond is a debt security issued by a national government to support government spending. Government bonds are generally considered low-risk investments, since the issuing government backs them with its taxing power; bonds issued by stable governments in their own currency are often treated as close to risk-free, though they still carry interest-rate, inflation, and — for foreign-currency or less stable sovereigns — default risk.

Issuance

Governments typically sell bonds at auction through their treasury or debt management office, in maturities ranging from a few months (treasury bills, often classified separately) to 30 years or more. Bonds usually pay periodic interest (coupons) and return the principal (face value) at maturity. Examples include US Treasury bonds, British gilts, German Bunds, and Japanese government bonds (JGBs).

Risks

Investors face currency risk when bonds are denominated in a foreign currency, inflation risk that erodes real returns, and credit risk — the possibility of sovereign default, as has occurred historically in various countries. Credit rating agencies grade sovereign bonds; those below investment grade are colloquially called "junk" or high-yield bonds.

Role in monetary policy

Central banks buy and sell government bonds in open market operations to implement monetary policy; large-scale purchases are known as quantitative easing. Government bond yields form the benchmark "risk-free" curve against which other borrowing costs are priced.
