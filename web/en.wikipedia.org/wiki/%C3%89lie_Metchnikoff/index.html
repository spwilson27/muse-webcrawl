Élie Metchnikoff

Élie Metchnikoff (Ilya Ilyich Mechnikov; 15 May 1845 – 15 July 1916) was a Russian and French zoologist and immunologist, best known for his discovery of phagocytosis and his pioneering work on cellular immunity. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1908 with Paul Ehrlich. He also coined the term gerontology and promoted the consumption of fermented milk products for longevity.

Early life and education

Metchnikoff was born in the village of Ivanovka near Kharkov, the son of an Imperial Guard officer and a mother of Jewish descent. He studied natural sciences at the University of Kharkov and completed his doctoral work in Germany, specialising in embryology and comparative zoology.

Discovery of phagocytosis

Working in Messina, Sicily, in 1882, Metchnikoff observed mobile cells in transparent starfish larvae surrounding foreign particles he had introduced, and realised these cells constituted a defence mechanism: the process he named phagocytosis. The discovery, extended to white blood cells in animals and humans, founded the cellular theory of immunity and redirected the science of immunology.

Pasteur Institute and later work

In 1888 Louis Pasteur invited Metchnikoff to Paris, where he spent the rest of his career at the Pasteur Institute, becoming its deputy director. His later research addressed ageing and longevity; in The Prolongation of Life (1907) he argued that lactic-acid bacteria in soured milk could counter harmful gut microbes, helping popularise yogurt in Europe.
