History of Hispanic and Latino Americans

The history of Hispanic and Latino Americans in the United States reaches back to the early 16th century, when Spanish explorers and colonists established settlements that predate the founding of the United States by more than two centuries. Hispanic and Latino Americans — people of Spanish-speaking or Latin American origin — today constitute the largest ethnic minority group in the country, numbering over 60 million.

Spanish colonial period

Juan Ponce de León reached Florida in 1513, and Spanish expeditions explored the Southeast, the Southwest and California. St. Augustine, Florida, founded in 1565, is the oldest continuously inhabited European-established city in the continental United States. Spain governed Florida, Puerto Rico and the vast territories of New Spain's northern frontier — including Santa Fe (1610), San Antonio (1718) and the California missions (from 1769) — building towns, presidios and missions whose architecture and place names still mark the map.

Annexation and the Mexican-American experience

Following the Mexican–American War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) transferred Mexico's northern territories to the United States, and some 100,000 Spanish-speaking Mexican citizens found themselves in the new country, with promises (often broken) that their property and rights would be respected. The Gadsden Purchase (1853) and the annexation of Texas completed the continental Southwest. Puerto Rico and other territories were ceded by Spain after the Spanish–American War of 1898.

Immigration and labour

The Mexican Revolution (from 1910) and labour demand drove large-scale Mexican immigration, interrupted by the mass repatriations of the Great Depression. The Bracero Program (1942–1964) brought millions of Mexican guest workers to US farms. After the Cuban Revolution (1959), successive waves of Cuban exiles transformed Miami, while Puerto Ricans — US citizens since 1917 — migrated in large numbers to New York and other northern cities. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened further migration from across Latin America.

Civil rights and contemporary era

The Chicano Movement of the 1960s–70s, the farmworker organising of César Chávez and Dolores Huerta, landmark cases such as Mendez v. Westminster (1947) and Hernandez v. Texas (1954), and struggles over bilingual education placed Latino Americans at the centre of US civil rights history. Since 1970 the Hispanic population has grown rapidly through immigration and natural increase, reshaping American politics, culture, language and cuisine; Spanish is the second most spoken language in the country.
