Trepanging

Trepanging is the harvesting of sea cucumbers (trepang, from the Malay word teripang) for the food trade, an industry of great historical importance in the maritime commerce of Southeast Asia, northern Australia, and the Pacific. The dried product, known in Malay as teripang and in Chinese as hǎishēn (海參, "sea ginseng"), was a prized delicacy and medicinal ingredient in China, and the demand for it drove long-distance voyages and intercultural contact across the region for centuries.

The trade

Sea cucumbers were collected from shallow tropical waters by divers or at low tide, then processed by boiling, gutting, drying in the sun, and smoking, producing a hard, dark, shrivelled product that kept well and could be transported long distances. From at least the eighteenth century, fleets from Makassar in Sulawesi sailed annually with the monsoon to the northern coast of Australia — a region the Macassans called Marege' — to harvest trepang along the coasts of Arnhem Land and the Kimberley. This Macassan trepang industry is notable as a major pre-European point of contact between Southeast Asia and Aboriginal Australians, leaving loanwords, tamarind trees, and artistic depictions in its wake; it was ended by Australian government restrictions in the early twentieth century.

Processing and use

Fresh sea cucumbers spoil rapidly, so processing began immediately after collection. The animals were eviscerated, boiled in seawater, dried, and often smoked over mangrove wood. In Chinese cuisine the rehydrated product is valued less for flavour — it is nearly tasteless — than for its gelatinous texture and reputed health benefits, and it appears in banquet dishes and soups. Grades vary enormously by species, with spiky species such as prickly redfish commanding high prices.

Sustainability

Because sea cucumbers are slow-growing and easily over-collected, trepanging has caused serial depletion of stocks throughout the Indo-Pacific, with fisheries moving to ever more remote grounds. Many countries now regulate the harvest through seasons, size limits, and export controls, and several high-value species have been listed under CITES.
