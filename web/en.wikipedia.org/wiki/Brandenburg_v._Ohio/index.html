Brandenburg v. Ohio

Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444 (1969), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States establishing the modern standard for when speech advocating illegal conduct may be restricted under the First Amendment. The Court held that inflammatory speech is protected unless it is "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action" — a standard known as the Brandenburg test or the imminent lawless action test.

Background

Clarence Brandenburg, a leader of the Ku Klux Klan in Ohio, invited a television reporter to film a Klan rally at a farm in Hamilton County. The film showed hooded figures, some carrying firearms, burning a cross and making racist and anti-government speeches, including remarks about possible "revengeance" against the federal government. Brandenburg was convicted under Ohio's criminal syndicalism statute, which prohibited advocating "crime, sabotage, violence, or unlawful methods of terrorism as a means of accomplishing industrial or political reform," and fined $1,000 and sentenced to one to ten years in prison.

Decision

The Court reversed the conviction in a short per curiam opinion. It held that earlier decisions such as Whitney v. California (1927), which had upheld convictions for abstract advocacy of violence, had been effectively undermined, and it articulated the two-part test: speech may be forbidden only when it is (1) directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and (2) likely to incite or produce such action. Because Ohio's statute punished mere advocacy without distinguishing it from incitement to imminent lawlessness, it violated the First and Fourteenth Amendments. Justices Hugo Black and William O. Douglas wrote separate concurrences arguing that the "clear and present danger" doctrine had no place in First Amendment law.

Legacy

Brandenburg replaced the older "clear and present danger" test derived from Schenck v. United States (1919) and remains the governing standard for incitement in American law. Its demanding requirements — intent, imminence, and likelihood — make prosecutions for advocacy of violence rare and difficult, and the decision is frequently cited in debates over extremist speech, protest, and online content.
