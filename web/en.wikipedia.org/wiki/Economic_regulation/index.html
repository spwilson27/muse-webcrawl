Economic regulation

Economic regulation refers to rules and laws designed to control the behaviour of firms and individuals in the private sector, particularly in markets where competition is limited or absent. It commonly includes controls over prices, quantities, and entry into and exit from industries, and is usually administered by specialised government agencies or independent regulators.

Forms

Economic regulation takes several forms. Rate-of-return regulation allows a regulated firm, typically a utility, to charge prices that cover its costs plus an allowed return on capital. Price-cap regulation instead sets a maximum price path that rises by inflation minus an expected productivity factor. Entry regulation includes licensing requirements and certificates of public convenience and necessity, while antitrust or competition law addresses monopolies and cartels in otherwise competitive markets.

Rationale

The classic justification for economic regulation is market failure: natural monopolies such as water networks and electricity grids can supply a market at lower cost than multiple competing firms but may abuse their position without oversight. Regulation may also pursue universal service, safety and stability in sectors such as transport, energy, telecommunications and finance.

Criticism and reform

Economists have criticised economic regulation for distorting incentives, protecting incumbents and being subject to regulatory capture, in which regulated firms influence the regulator. These concerns drove waves of deregulation and privatisation in airlines, trucking, railways, telecommunications and energy in the United States, the United Kingdom and elsewhere from the late 1970s onward. Modern regulatory practice often combines competition where feasible with incentive-based price controls in the remaining monopoly segments, and uses cost-benefit analysis to appraise new rules.
