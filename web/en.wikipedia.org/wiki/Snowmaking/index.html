Snowmaking

Snowmaking is the production of snow by forcing water and pressurised air through a "snow gun", "snow cannon" or "snow maker". Snowmaking is employed mainly at ski resorts to supplement natural snow, extend the season, and guarantee open terrain for scheduled events.

How it works

Atomised water droplets must freeze before hitting the ground. Efficiency depends on the wet-bulb temperature, generally requiring conditions around −2.5 °C (27.5 °F) or colder, though nucleating agents — historically proteins from the bacterium Pseudomonas syringae (marketed as Snomax) — allow freezing at marginally warmer temperatures. The two main technologies are air-water guns, which use compressed air to atomise water, and fan guns, which use an electric fan to blow droplets through the air.

History

Artificial snow was first made accidentally in a Canadian laboratory in the 1940s; the first commercial snow guns were developed in the United States in the 1950s. The 1980 Winter Olympics at Lake Placid were the first to rely heavily on machine-made snow.

Environmental concerns

Snowmaking consumes large volumes of water and energy, often drawn from reservoirs built for the purpose. The meltwater returns to local watersheds in spring, generally later than natural melt, which can alter local hydrology. As winters warm, resorts invest increasingly heavily in snowmaking infrastructure.
