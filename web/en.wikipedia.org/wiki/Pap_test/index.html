Pap test

PurposeScreening for cervical cancer and precancerous changes
Named afterGeorgios Papanikolaou
Other namesPap smear, cervical smear

The Pap test (also called a Pap smear) is a screening procedure for cervical cancer. Cells are gently scraped from the cervix and examined under a microscope for precancerous or cancerous changes. The test was developed by Greek physician Georgios Papanikolaou in the 1920s and became widespread from the 1950s onward; regular screening programs have dramatically reduced cervical cancer mortality in countries where they are implemented.

Procedure

During a pelvic examination, a speculum is used to hold the vaginal walls apart so the cervix can be seen. A small brush or spatula collects cells from the cervix's transformation zone. In the conventional method the sample is smeared directly onto a glass slide; in liquid-based cytology, now more common, the cells are rinsed into a vial of preservative fluid and processed in a laboratory.

Results

Results are commonly reported using the Bethesda System, ranging from negative (no abnormal cells) through atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US) to low-grade and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL, HSIL) and carcinoma. Most abnormal results reflect HPV infection or mild changes that resolve spontaneously; abnormal findings may prompt repeat testing, HPV testing, colposcopy, or biopsy.

Guidelines

Recommendations vary by country, but screening typically begins at age 21 to 25 and continues to about age 65, at intervals of three years for cytology alone or five years when combined with HPV co-testing. Because most cervical cancers are caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), HPV-based primary screening is increasingly adopted.
