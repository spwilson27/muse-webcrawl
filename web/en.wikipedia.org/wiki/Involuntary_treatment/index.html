Involuntary treatment

Involuntary treatment is medical treatment administered without the patient's consent.

Involuntary treatment refers to medical treatment undertaken without the consent of the person being treated. It is most commonly associated with psychiatric care, where a person with a mental disorder may be detained in hospital and treated against their will under mental health law, a process sometimes called involuntary commitment or sectioning.

Legal grounds

Most jurisdictions permit involuntary treatment only when a person is judged to pose a danger to themselves or others, or to be gravely disabled and unable to care for themselves, and usually require assessment by medical professionals and review by a tribunal or court. Laws vary widely between countries and, in the United States, between states.

Forms

Treatment may include involuntary hospitalisation, the administration of psychiatric medication without consent, electroconvulsive therapy under strict conditions, and in some systems outpatient commitment (assisted outpatient treatment), under which a person living in the community is required to comply with a treatment plan.

Debate

Involuntary treatment is deeply controversial. Supporters argue it protects vulnerable people and the public when illness impairs insight. Critics, including some service users and human-rights bodies, argue that it infringes autonomy and bodily integrity, and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities has been interpreted as calling for its abolition.
