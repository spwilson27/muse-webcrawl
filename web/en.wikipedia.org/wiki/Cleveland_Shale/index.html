The Cleveland Shale, also called the Cleveland Member, is the uppermost member of the Ohio Shale formation, a sequence of organic-rich black shales deposited during the Late Devonian period, roughly 360 million years ago, in a shallow sea covering much of what is now the American Midwest.

Geology

The Cleveland Shale consists of dark grey to black, fissile, bituminous shale interbedded with thin siltstone layers. It is famous among palaeontologists for its carbonate concretions, which preserve fossils in exceptional three-dimensional detail.

Palaeontology

The unit is one of the world's most important sources of Devonian fish fossils. Its concretions have yielded arthrodire placoderms including the giant apex predator Dunkleosteus, the bizarre Stethacanthus and other early sharks, coelacanths, and the remains of early tetrapodomorph fishes. Much of the classic material is held by the Cleveland Museum of Natural History, and classic collecting localities line the river valleys and road cuts around Cleveland, Ohio.
