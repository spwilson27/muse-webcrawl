Manidae is the only extant family in the order Pholidota, comprising the pangolins. The family includes three living genera — Manis, Phataginus, and Smutsia — containing eight living species, four in Asia and four in Africa. Pangolins are the only mammals wholly covered in scales, which are made of keratin, the same material as human hair and nails.

Characteristics

Members of Manidae are specialised myrmecophages: they feed almost exclusively on ants and termites, which they locate by smell and lap up with extremely long, sticky tongues. They have no teeth. Their overlapping scales act as armour, and when threatened many species roll into a tight ball. Their powerful claws are adapted for breaking into insect nests and, in the ground-dwelling species, for digging burrows.

Classification

The living genera are:

  • Manis — the Asian pangolins, including the Indian, Chinese, Sunda, and Philippine pangolins.
  • Phataginus — the small African tree pangolins, including the white-bellied and black-bellied pangolins.
  • Smutsia — the large African ground pangolins, including the giant pangolin and Temminck's pangolin.

The family also includes several extinct genera known from Eocene and later deposits in Europe, Asia, and Africa, such as Eomanis and Patriomanis.

Conservation

All eight living species are threatened, ranging from Vulnerable to Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Pangolins are heavily poached for their scales, used in traditional medicine, and for their meat, and they are considered among the most trafficked mammals in the world. Since 2017, international commercial trade in all pangolin species has been prohibited under CITES Appendix I.
