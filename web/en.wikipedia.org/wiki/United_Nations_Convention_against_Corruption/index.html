United Nations Convention against Corruption

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) is the only legally binding universal anti-corruption instrument. Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 31 October 2003 and in force from 14 December 2005, it has near-universal adherence (about 190 parties). UNCAC requires states to criminalise core corruption offences (bribery of public officials, embezzlement, trading in influence and abuse of functions in qualified terms, private-sector bribery and embezzlement as “shall consider” offences), to adopt preventive measures (merit-based civil services, procurement and asset-declaration systems, anti-money-laundering controls), and to cooperate internationally — its Chapter V made asset recovery a fundamental principle of the treaty, the first such commitment in international law, aimed at restoring kleptocrats' stolen wealth.

Implementation is reviewed by peer review (the Implementation Review Mechanism, from 2010) with country visits and executive summaries published, full reports by choice. Measured against its ambitions the Convention's record is mixed — criminalisation is broad, enforcement highly uneven, and asset returns remain small beside estimated thefts — but UNCAC supplies the global vocabulary and framework within which national anti-corruption law (and the State capture debate) operates.
