Contempt of court

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Contempt of court, often referred to simply as contempt, is the offence of being disobedient to or disrespectful toward a court of law and its officers in the form of behaviour that opposes or defies the authority, justice and dignity of the court. Courts punish it summarily (often by the judge offended, an anomaly tolerated for necessity) by fine or imprisonment, to vindicate authority and — in civil contempt — to coerce compliance.

Varieties

Two species dominate: criminal contempt punishes past disobedience or disruption (courtroom misconduct, violating orders, publications prejudicing live proceedings — England's strict-liability rule under the Contempt of Court Act 1981, balanced uneasily against free press), while civil contempt is coercive and remedial — the contemnor "carries the keys of his prison in his own pocket", jailed until they comply (testify, produce documents, pay, desist). Famous deployments include journalists jailed for protecting sources, officials refusing orders, and legislatures' analogous inherent-contempt powers. The doctrine's friction with free expression and due process (punishment without jury trial for serious penalties having been curtailed in US law — Bloom v. Illinois, 1968) makes contempt the site where judicial power most nakedly defends itself.
