Seed bank

Notable seed banks

PurposeEx situ conservation of plant genetic diversity

A seed bank (also called a seedsaver or gene bank, in this sense) stores seeds as a source for planting in case seed reserves elsewhere are destroyed. Stored seeds are dried to a low moisture content and frozen, under which conditions many "orthodox" seeds can remain viable for decades or even centuries. Seed banks preserve the genetic diversity of crops and their wild relatives against loss from war, disaster, disease, and the erosion of traditional varieties.

The term also has an ecological meaning: the soil seed bank is the natural store of dormant seeds present in the soil of an ecosystem, which allows plant populations to recover after disturbance.

Major facilities

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008 on the Norwegian archipelago of Svalbard, stores backup samples from gene banks around the world deep inside a mountain in permafrost. The Millennium Seed Bank at Wakehurst in England, run by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, aims to bank seed from the world's wild plant species. National collections such as the United States National Plant Germplasm System preserve crop germplasm for breeders and researchers.

Challenges

Not all seeds tolerate drying and freezing; species with "recalcitrant" seeds, including many tropical trees, must be conserved by other means such as tissue culture or living collections. Stored accessions must be periodically tested and regenerated by growing them out, a costly process that risks genetic drift.
