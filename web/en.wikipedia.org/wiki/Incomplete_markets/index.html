Incomplete markets

In economics, incomplete markets are markets in which the set of available assets is not sufficient for agents to insure themselves fully against all future risks, or equivalently, in which there are fewer tradable securities than there are possible future states of the world. The concept is central to general equilibrium theory and financial economics, where it is contrasted with the idealised case of complete markets, in which a security exists paying off in every conceivable state.

When some future states cannot be insured, markets are incomplete.

Background

In the Arrow–Debreu model of general equilibrium, complete markets guarantee that competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient. Real markets fall short of this ideal: individuals cannot buy insurance against many personal risks — such as future unemployment, illness, or the failure of their own business — because of moral hazard, adverse selection, transaction costs, and the impossibility of writing enforceable contracts on every contingency.

Consequences

With incomplete markets, several benchmark results fail. Equilibria are generally not Pareto efficient, even in the constrained sense once asset prices are taken into account (results associated with Oliver Hart and with John Geanakoplos and Herakles Polemarchakis). Households that cannot insure resort to self-insurance through precautionary saving — holding buffer stocks of safe assets — which alters interest rates and consumption patterns. In asset pricing, incompleteness means that some risks carry no unique market price, so options and other derivatives cannot always be replicated or priced by arbitrage alone.

Examples

Labour income risk is the classic uninsurable risk: no market sells a contract paying out if one's career falters. Human capital cannot be sold or fully pledged as collateral, housing is an illiquid and undiversifiable asset for most households, and private businesses face similar limits. Much of applied macroeconomics — including models of wealth inequality and business cycles — therefore assumes incomplete markets with borrowing constraints.
