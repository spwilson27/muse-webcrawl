The Pueblo Revival style is an architectural style of the Southwestern United States that draws its inspiration from the adobe architecture of the Pueblo people and from Spanish colonial missions. It flourished from the 1910s through the 1930s and remains influential in New Mexico, where the closely related Santa Fe style became the dominant regional idiom.

Characteristics

Pueblo Revival buildings imitate the appearance of traditional multi-storey adobe pueblos such as Taos Pueblo. Hallmarks include heavy, battered walls of adobe or stuccoed masonry with softened, rounded corners; flat or nearly flat roofs set behind stepped parapets; rows of projecting roof beams called vigas whose ends punctuate the façades; small, deeply set windows; wooden lintels; and sometimes ladders reaching upper roofs, corner fireplaces (kiva fireplaces), and enclosed courtyards. Interiors often feature viga-and-latilla ceilings and hand-adzed timber.

History

The style emerged as part of the broader regionalist movement in American architecture and was promoted by architects and institutions in Santa Fe. Early landmark examples include the New Mexico Museum of Art (1917) and the La Fonda hotel; architect John Gaw Meem (1894–1983) became its leading practitioner, designing the Los Alamos Ranch School buildings and much of the University of New Mexico campus in Albuquerque. In 1957 Santa Fe adopted a historic-styles ordinance requiring Pueblo or Spanish Revival forms in its historic districts, institutionalising the look. Variants combined with Spanish colonial elements are called Territorial Revival, after the later territorial period's use of brick coping and milled lumber.
