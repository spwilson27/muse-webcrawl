The Berlin Circle (German: Berliner Kreis) was a group of philosophers and scientists associated with Hans Reichenbach in Berlin in the late 1920s and 1930s. Together with the Vienna Circle, it formed the heart of the logical positivist (logical empiricist) movement. Its institutional base was the Society for Empirical Philosophy (Gesellschaft für empirische Philosophie), founded in Berlin in 1928.

Members and ideas

Around Reichenbach gathered figures including Carl Gustav Hempel, David Hilbert's collaborator in logic circles, Richard von Mises, Kurt Grelling, and Walter Dubislav. Compared with Vienna, the Berlin group placed particular emphasis on probability and inductive logic — Reichenbach developed a frequentist theory of probability and a probabilistic account of induction — as well as on the philosophy of physics, including the analysis of space, time, and relativity.

Activities

The Berlin and Vienna groups cooperated closely: jointly they organised the 1929 Prague conference on the epistemology of the exact sciences and founded the journal Erkenntnis (1930), edited by Reichenbach and Rudolf Carnap, which became the principal organ of logical empiricism. A shared manifesto and congresses followed.

Dissolution

The Nazi seizure of power in 1933 scattered the group: Reichenbach emigrated to Istanbul and then to UCLA; Hempel and others moved to the United States, where members of the Berlin Circle helped shape post-war analytic philosophy of science.
