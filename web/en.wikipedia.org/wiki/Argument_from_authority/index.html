Argument from authority

Argument from authority
LatinArgumentum ad verecundiam
TypeInformal argument; fallacy when misused

An argument from authority (Latin: argumentum ad verecundiam) is a form of argument in which the opinion of an authority on a topic is used as evidence to support a claim. When the authority is not a genuine expert, is cited outside their field, or when the appeal substitutes for evidence, the argument is often classified as an informal fallacy. However, appeals to legitimate expertise are a normal and often rational part of reasoning, since individuals cannot verify everything themselves.

An appeal to authority in public debate

Structure

The argument typically follows the form: "Expert A says that P; A is an authority on the subject; therefore P is (probably) true." Its strength depends on the authority's actual expertise, the existence of expert consensus, the absence of bias, and whether the claim is quoted accurately and in context.

Fallacious uses

The appeal becomes fallacious when the cited figure lacks relevant expertise (for example, a celebrity endorsing a medical treatment), when authorities are anonymous or unverifiable ("scientists say"), when experts disagree but only one side is presented, or when the authority's word is treated as conclusive proof rather than defeasible evidence. Historical discussions, from John Locke — who coined the term argumentum ad verecundiam — onward, treat the argument as at best presumptive.

Legitimate uses

Philosophers such as Douglas Walton have analyzed the argument from authority as a defeasible argumentation scheme subject to critical questions: How credible is the source? Is the source an expert in the relevant field? Is there agreement among experts? Properly used, deference to experts underlies much of science, law, and everyday decision-making.
