Field Marshal Garnet Joseph Wolseley, 1st Viscount Wolseley (4 June 1833 – 25 March 1913) was an Anglo-Irish officer in the British Army. He became one of the most influential and admired British generals of the Victorian era, serving in Burma, the Crimean War, the Indian Mutiny, China, Canada and widely across Africa.

Career

Wolseley built his reputation through a series of successful colonial campaigns. He led the Red River Expedition in Canada (1870), the Ashanti campaign in West Africa (1873–74) that captured Kumasi, and the expedition to Egypt (1882) that culminated in victory at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir. In 1884–85 he commanded the Nile Expedition sent to relieve General Gordon at Khartoum, arriving two days after the city fell.

His efficient planning made "all Sir Garnet" a slang phrase for everything being in order, and he is widely thought to be a model for the "very model of a modern Major-General" in Gilbert and Sullivan's operetta The Pirates of Penzance.

Reforms and later life

As adjutant-general and later Commander-in-Chief of the Forces (1895–1900), Wolseley championed army reform, including short-service enlistment, linked battalions and improved conditions for ordinary soldiers. He was raised to the peerage as Baron Wolseley in 1882 and Viscount Wolseley in 1885, and promoted field marshal in 1894. He wrote extensive memoirs and works on soldiering, including The Soldier's Pocket-book.
