The Gall–Peters projection is a rectangular map projection that maps all areas on Earth such that areas of equal size on the globe are equally sized on the map — an equal-area cylindrical projection with standard parallels at 45° north and south. It was described by the Scottish clergyman and cartographer James Gall in 1855 and reintroduced in 1973 by the German filmmaker and historian Arno Peters, who presented it as a corrective to the distortions of the widely used Mercator projection.

Properties

Because it preserves relative area, the Gall–Peters map shows continents and countries in their true proportional sizes: Africa, for example, appears much larger relative to Europe and North America than on a Mercator map. The cost is considerable distortion of shapes, especially stretching near the equator and compression toward the poles.

The Peters controversy

Peters promoted the projection as a politically fairer world map, arguing that the Mercator projection over-represents the sizes of wealthy northern countries. His claims of originality and of the projection's unique fairness were disputed by cartographers, who noted that many equal-area projections exist and that Gall had published the same construction more than a century earlier. Nevertheless, the map was adopted by some schools, aid agencies and publishers, and it remains a popular symbol in debates about representation in cartography.
