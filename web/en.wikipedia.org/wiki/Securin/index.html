Securin

Securin binds and inhibits separase until the cell is ready for anaphase

Gene (human)PTTG1
FunctionSeparase inhibitor; sister-chromatid cohesion control

Securin is a protein that regulates the separation of sister chromatids during cell division. It binds to and inhibits the protease separase, preventing separase from cleaving the cohesin complexes that hold sister chromatids together until the appropriate moment in mitosis. In humans, securin is encoded by the PTTG1 gene (pituitary tumor-transforming gene 1).

Mechanism

During metaphase, securin keeps separase inactive while chromosomes align on the spindle. When all kinetochores are correctly attached, the spindle assembly checkpoint is satisfied and the anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C), activated by Cdc20, ubiquitinates securin, targeting it for destruction by the proteasome. The liberated separase then cleaves the Scc1/Rad21 subunit of cohesin, allowing sister chromatids to separate and anaphase to begin. APC/C also degrades mitotic cyclins at the same transition.

Discovery

Securin was first characterised in budding and fission yeasts as the products of the PDS1 and Cut2 genes respectively, whose mutants showed premature sister separation or failure to separate chromosomes. The vertebrate homologue was identified independently as a transforming gene isolated from pituitary tumours, hence its name PTTG1.

Clinical significance

Overexpression of securin/PTTG1 is observed in a variety of tumours, including pituitary, thyroid, breast and colorectal cancers, and is associated with aneuploidy and genomic instability. Abnormal securin levels can both delay and prematurely trigger chromatid separation, contributing to the chromosome mis-segregation characteristic of cancer cells.
