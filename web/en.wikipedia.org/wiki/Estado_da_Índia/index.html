The Estado da Índia (Portuguese for "State of India") was the Portuguese colonial state in the Indian Ocean world, established in 1505 under the first viceroy, Francisco de Almeida, six years after Vasco da Gama's sea voyage to India. It comprised Portugal's colonies, fortresses and trading posts (feitorias) across Asia and, at times, East Africa — at its 16th-century height a network stretching from Mozambique and Hormuz to Malacca, Macau and Nagasaki.

History

Under Almeida and his successor Afonso de Albuquerque, the Portuguese seized key chokepoints of the spice trade — Goa (1510), Malacca (1511) and Hormuz (1515) — and enforced their dominance with naval power and the cartaz pass system. The capital moved from Cochin to Goa in 1530. From the 17th century, Dutch and English competition stripped the Estado of many possessions, and it shrank to its Indian enclaves and later Timor and Macau.

The final centuries

By the 20th century the Estado da Índia consisted of Goa, Daman and Diu (with Dadra and Nagar Haveli). After Indian independence in 1947, Portugal under Salazar refused to negotiate their transfer, and in December 1961 India annexed the territories in Operation Vijay, ending 456 years of the Estado. Portugal recognised Indian sovereignty only in 1974, after the Carnation Revolution.
