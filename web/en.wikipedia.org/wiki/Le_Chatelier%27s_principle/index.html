Le Chatelier's principle

Le Chatelier's principle, named after the French chemist Henry Louis Le Chatelier, is a principle of chemistry used to predict the effect of a change in conditions on a chemical system in dynamic equilibrium. It states that if a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to counteract the change and re-establish an equilibrium.

Effect of concentration

If the concentration of a reactant is increased, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that consumes that reactant, producing more products. Conversely, removing a product drives the reaction forward, a fact exploited industrially, for example in the Haber process for ammonia synthesis.

Effect of temperature

Increasing the temperature favours the endothermic direction of a reaction, while decreasing it favours the exothermic direction. Temperature is the only common disturbance that also changes the value of the equilibrium constant itself.

Effect of pressure

For reactions involving gases, increasing the pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Changes in pressure have negligible effect on condensed phases. Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not shift the equilibrium, since the partial pressures of the reacting gases are unchanged.

Relation to thermodynamics

The principle can be derived from thermodynamic arguments: at equilibrium the Gibbs free energy is minimised, and a disturbed system evolves toward a new minimum. Although qualitative, the principle remains a powerful rule of thumb for predicting equilibrium shifts.
