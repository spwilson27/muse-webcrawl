Slavery in Brazil

Slavery in Brazil began long before the first Portuguese settlement and lasted until the promulgation of the Lei Áurea (Golden Law) on 13 May 1888, making Brazil the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. Over the course of the Atlantic slave trade, Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other destination — an estimated 4.9 million people, roughly 40% of all Africans transported to the Americas.

Early slavery

The Portuguese first enslaved Indigenous Brazilians, who were put to work in the brazilwood trade and then on the sugar plantations (engenhos) of the northeast from the 1530s. Indigenous resistance, flight, catastrophic mortality from Old World diseases and legal restrictions (partial bans from 1570 onward) led planters to turn increasingly to African labour. Bandeirantes from São Paulo raided deep into the interior for Indigenous captives, notably attacking the Jesuit missions of Guayrá in the 1620s–30s.

The African trade

From the late 16th century, enslaved Africans became the mainstay of the plantation economy. The sugar cycle of Pernambuco and Bahia, then the gold and diamond rushes of Minas Gerais in the 18th century, and finally the coffee boom of the Paraíba Valley and São Paulo in the 19th century each generated enormous demand. Enslaved people came largely from West-Central Africa (Angola and the Congo) and from the Bight of Benin and Mozambique. Ports such as Rio de Janeiro, Salvador and Recife became major centres of the trade; Rio's Valongo Wharf alone received around a million Africans.

Resistance

Enslaved people resisted through flight, work slowdowns, cultural preservation and revolt. Runaway communities called quilombos formed throughout Brazil; the largest, Palmares in Alagoas, survived for most of the 17th century under leaders including Zumbi, who was killed in 1695 and is now commemorated on Black Consciousness Day (20 November). In 1835, Muslim West African slaves and freedmen launched the Malê Revolt in Salvador, one of the most significant urban slave uprisings in the Americas.

Abolition

Under British pressure, the transatlantic trade was effectively suppressed after the Eusébio de Queirós Law of 1850. Thereafter abolition came in stages: the Law of the Free Womb (1871) freed children born to enslaved women thereafter; the Sexagenarian Law (1885) freed the elderly; mass flight and the refusal of the army to pursue runaways collapsed the system in the late 1880s. Finally, Princess Isabel, as regent, signed the Lei Áurea on 13 May 1888, abolishing slavery outright and without compensation.

Legacy

Abolition came without land reform, reparations or integration programs, and Afro-Brazilians — today a majority of the population — continue to face stark inequalities in income, education and exposure to violence. The legacies of slavery shape Brazilian culture profoundly, from samba, capoeira and Candomblé to cuisine and language. Brazil officially recognised the Valongo Wharf as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017 as a memorial of the trade.
