Basement membrane

Basement membrane
ComponentsBasal lamina, reticular lamina
Main proteinsType IV collagen, laminin, nidogen, perlecan
Found inBeneath epithelia and endothelia; around muscle, fat and nerve cells

The basement membrane, also known as the basal lamina in its epithelial part, is a thin, fibrous sheet of extracellular matrix that separates epithelial and endothelial cell layers from the underlying connective tissue, and also surrounds individual muscle cells, fat cells and Schwann cells.

Structure

Under the electron microscope the basement membrane shows two layers: the basal lamina, secreted by the overlying cells and itself divided into the lamina lucida and lamina densa, and the reticular lamina, produced by connective-tissue cells. Its principal constituents are type IV collagen, laminins, nidogen/entactin and the heparan sulfate proteoglycan perlecan.

Function

Basement membranes anchor cells, act as selective filtration barriers (notably in the kidney glomerulus), influence cell polarity, differentiation and migration, and serve as a reservoir for growth factors. Defects in their components cause diseases such as Alport syndrome (type IV collagen) and the blistering disorder epidermolysis bullosa; in cancer, breaching the basement membrane is a defining step of invasive carcinoma.
