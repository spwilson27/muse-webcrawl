Telegraphy is the long-distance transmission of messages in coded form without the physical transport of letters. The word (from Greek tele, "far", and graphein, "to write") was first applied to optical semaphore networks, notably Claude Chappe's system in revolutionary France, whose lines of articulated-arm towers relayed signals across the country.

Electrical telegraph

In the 1830s and 1840s, inventors including William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in Britain and Samuel Morse in the United States developed practical electrical telegraphs. Morse's single-wire system and code were demonstrated on the Washington–Baltimore line in 1844 with the message "What hath God wrought". Networks spread rapidly along railway lines; submarine cables linked Britain and France (1850) and, after failures in 1858, durably crossed the Atlantic in 1866.

Impact

The telegraph separated communication from transportation for the first time, transforming journalism, finance, railway operation, diplomacy and warfare. Standard time zones were adopted largely because telegraph-linked railways required them. Operating companies such as Western Union built vast networks; messages were charged by the word, producing the terse "telegraphic" style.

Decline

Printing telegraphs, the telex network and eventually the telephone displaced Morse operation during the twentieth century. Western Union sent its last telegram in 2006, and India — the last major operator — closed its service in 2013. Morse code survives in amateur radio and as an accessibility aid.
