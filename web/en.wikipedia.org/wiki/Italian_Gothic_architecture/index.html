Italian Gothic architecture

Italian Gothic architecture is the regional form of Gothic architecture that developed in Italy from the late 12th century into the 15th century. Unlike the skeletal, glass-filled Gothic of northern France, Italian Gothic retained massive wall surfaces, polychrome marble, and wide, frescoed interiors suited to the Mediterranean climate and local traditions.

Characteristics

Italian Gothic churches often use round or only slightly pointed arches, short towers, striped marble façades, and large wall areas for fresco cycles. The Cistercian order introduced early Gothic forms, followed by the great Franciscan and Dominican churches such as the Basilica of Saint Francis of Assisi and Santa Maria Novella in Florence.

Major examples

Notable buildings include Milan Cathedral (begun 1386, the most northern-European in style), Siena Cathedral, Orvieto Cathedral with its mosaic-covered façade, the Doge's Palace in Venice with its distinctive Venetian Gothic arcades, and Florence Cathedral, whose Gothic nave was later crowned by Brunelleschi's Renaissance dome.

Civic architecture

Gothic forms also shaped town halls and palaces, such as the Palazzo Vecchio in Florence and the Palazzo Pubblico in Siena, symbols of the Italian communes.
