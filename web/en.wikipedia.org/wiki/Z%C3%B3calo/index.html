Zócalo

The Zócalo is the common name of the main square in central Mexico City, officially the Plaza de la Constitución (Constitution Square). Prior to the colonial period it was the ceremonial centre of the Aztec city of Tenochtitlan, adjacent to the Templo Mayor. In other Mexican towns and cities, zócalo is used generically to mean the main square or plaza.

History

The plaza occupies the site of the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan. After the Spanish conquest, the square was laid out as the centre of the new colonial city, flanked by the cathedral, the viceregal palace (now the National Palace), and merchant portals. The name Zócalo ("plinth" or "base") derives from a large pedestal built in the 1840s for a planned independence monument that was never completed; the empty base remained for years, and its name passed to the square itself.

Description

Measuring roughly 240 by 240 metres, the Zócalo is one of the largest city squares in the world. It is bounded by Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral to the north, the National Palace to the east, the city's government buildings to the south, and the Old Portal de Mercaderes to the west. In 1987 the square and the historic centre were inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage list. A monumental Mexican flag in the centre of the square is raised and lowered daily with military ceremony.

Use

The Zócalo is a focal point for civic life: it hosts concerts, festivals such as Day of the Dead celebrations, political demonstrations, and the annual Grito de Dolores on the eve of Independence Day. Excavations beside the cathedral have uncovered the remains of the Aztec Templo Mayor, now a museum.
