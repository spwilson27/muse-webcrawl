Yemelyan Pugachev

Yemelyan Ivanovich Pugachev (1742 – 21 January 1775) was a Don Cossack who led the largest peasant and Cossack revolt in Russian history — Pugachev's Rebellion (1773–1775) — during the reign of Catherine the Great. Claiming to be the murdered Emperor Peter III, miraculously survived, he raised the Yaik Cossacks, Bashkirs, factory serfs of the Urals and Volga peasants in a rising that for a year made the imperial government fear for its throne, before he was captured, transported to Moscow in an iron cage, and executed in Bolotnaya Square.

The rebellion

Pugachev, a veteran of the Seven Years' War and the Turkish war who had deserted and wandered as a fugitive, appeared among the Yaik (Ural) Cossacks in 1773 declaring himself Peter III and promising land, freedom from serfdom and taxes, and the Old Believer faith. His army took forts along the Yaik, besieged Orenburg, burned Kazan in July 1774, and issued manifestos abolishing serfdom as his host swelled with serfs, miners, Bashkirs and Tatars. Landowners were hanged across the Volga country; the terror and the looming Turkish peace concentrated the government, and regular troops under Michelson defeated Pugachev repeatedly. Betrayed by his own Cossack elders, he was handed over in September 1774; Alexander Suvorov escorted the prisoner east. After interrogation in Moscow, Pugachev was executed in January 1775.

Aftermath and memory

Catherine renamed the Yaik the Ural and its Cossacks likewise, to erase the memory of the rising; the repression and the extension of serfdom marked her reign's conservative turn. Pushkin researched the events for both a history of the rebellion and his novel The Captain's Daughter (1836), which fixed Pugachev's image — brutal, charismatic, doomed — in Russian literature.
