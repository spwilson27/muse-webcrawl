Dissociative disorders

Dissociative disorders (DDs) are a group of mental disorders characterised by disruptions or discontinuities in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control, and behaviour. They are frequently associated with psychological trauma, especially trauma in childhood.

Types

The DSM-5 lists the following dissociative disorders:

  • Dissociative identity disorder (DID) — formerly multiple personality disorder; the presence of two or more distinct personality states with recurrent gaps in memory.
  • Dissociative amnesia — inability to recall important autobiographical information, sometimes with dissociative fugue.
  • Depersonalization-derealization disorder — persistent experiences of detachment from oneself or of unreality of surroundings.
  • Other specified and unspecified dissociative disorders.

Causes and treatment

Dissociation is often understood as a coping response to overwhelming trauma, though the aetiology and diagnosis of some dissociative disorders, particularly DID, have been debated. Treatment typically involves trauma-informed psychotherapy, sometimes integrated with treatment for comorbid post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and anxiety.
