Physical geography

Physical geography (also known as physiography) is one of the two major branches of geography, the other being human geography. It is the natural-science discipline concerned with the processes and patterns of the natural environment — the atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere and geosphere — and with the spatial distribution of landforms, climates, soils, waters and organisms on Earth's surface.

Sub-disciplines

Its principal fields include geomorphology (the study of landforms and the processes that shape them, such as plate tectonics, erosion and glaciation), climatology (long-term weather patterns and climate systems), hydrology and oceanography (water in rivers, lakes, groundwater and the oceans), biogeography (the distribution of species and ecosystems), soil geography (pedology), and glaciology. Closely related are Quaternary science, landscape ecology and palaeogeography, which reconstruct past environments.

Approach and methods

Physical geographers increasingly study Earth as an integrated system, examining exchanges of energy and matter among atmosphere, oceans, land and life, and the impacts of human activity — most prominently climate change — on natural systems. Methods combine field measurement, remote sensing from satellites, geographic information systems (GIS) and numerical modelling. Foundational figures include Alexander von Humboldt, whose work linked vegetation, climate and altitude, and William Morris Davis, who proposed a cycle of erosion for landscapes.
