The Big Bad Wolf is a fictional wolf appearing in several cautionary tales that include some of Aesop's Fables (c. 5th century BCE) and Grimms' Fairy Tales. Versions of the character appear in "The Boy Who Cried Wolf," "The Three Little Pigs," "Little Red Riding Hood," "Peter and the Wolf," and "The Wolf and the Seven Young Goats."

Traditional tales

In "The Three Little Pigs" (1843, popularised by Joseph Jacobs), the wolf destroys the houses of straw and sticks by "huffing and puffing" and eats their owners, but is defeated by the third pig's brick house. In Charles Perrault's and the Grimms' "Little Red Riding Hood," the wolf devours the grandmother and the girl; in the Grimm version a huntsman cuts them free alive. The wolf commonly embodies the danger of the wilderness and of predatory strangers.

Interpretations

Folklorists read the wolf as a stock villain representing hunger, greed and threat in agrarian societies where wolves genuinely preyed on livestock. Psychoanalytic and feminist readings have treated the Red Riding Hood wolf as a figure of seduction and male predation; revisionist versions often soften or redeem the character.

In popular culture

Walt Disney's Silly Symphony cartoon Three Little Pigs (1933) named him the Big Bad Wolf and popularised the song "Who's Afraid of the Big Bad Wolf?" — an anthem of the Great Depression. Tex Avery's Red Riding Hood cartoons turned him into a lecherous comic figure; in modern works from The True Story of the 3 Little Pigs! to Fables and Shrek, the wolf is frequently recast as misunderstood or heroic.
