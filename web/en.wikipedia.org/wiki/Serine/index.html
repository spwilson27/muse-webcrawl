Serine

NamesSerine
AbbreviationsSer, S
Chemical formulaC3H7NO3
Molar mass105.09 g·mol−1
AppearanceWhite crystals or powder
CodonsUCU, UCC, UCA, UCG, AGU, AGC

Serine (symbol Ser or S) is an α-amino acid that is used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It contains an α-amino group, an α-carboxylic acid group, and a side chain consisting of a hydroxymethyl group, classifying it as a polar amino acid. It can be synthesized in the human body under normal physiological circumstances, making it a non-essential amino acid.

Serine was first obtained from silk protein, a particularly rich source, in 1865 by Emil Cramer, who named it after the Latin sericum, "silk." Its structure was established in 1902.

Biosynthesis

Serine is synthesized from 3-phosphoglycerate, an intermediate of glycolysis, in a three-step pathway involving the enzymes phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase, phosphoserine aminotransferase, and phosphoserine phosphatase. Serine in turn serves as a precursor for the amino acids glycine and cysteine, and for sphingolipids and phospholipids.

Function

Serine residues in proteins are frequent sites of phosphorylation by serine kinases, a key mechanism of cellular signal transduction, and of O-linked glycosylation. Serine forms part of the catalytic triad of serine proteases such as trypsin and chymotrypsin. D-serine, produced in the brain, acts as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors and functions as a neurotransmitter.

Clinical significance

Rare inherited defects in serine biosynthesis cause neurological disorders that can be treated with serine supplementation. Reduced serine levels have also been associated with various metabolic conditions, and serine metabolism is a subject of research in cancer biology because many tumours depend on the serine synthesis pathway.
