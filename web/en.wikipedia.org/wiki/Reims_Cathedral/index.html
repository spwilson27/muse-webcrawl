Reims Cathedral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reims Cathedral (French: Notre-Dame de Reims) is a Roman Catholic cathedral in Reims, France, built in the High Gothic style. It was the traditional site of the coronation of the kings of France: twenty-five kings were crowned there between 1223 and 1825, most famously Charles VII in 1429 in the presence of Joan of Arc. The cathedral, together with the adjacent Palace of Tau and the Abbey of Saint-Rémi, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991.

History

The present cathedral replaced an older church destroyed by fire in 1210; construction began in 1211 under architect Jean d'Orbais and the main structure was largely complete by 1275. Remarkable for the unity of its design, the building is celebrated for its sculpture, including the famous Smiling Angel, and for its stained glass, which ranges from 13th-century windows to a set by Marc Chagall installed in 1974.

World War I and restoration

The cathedral was heavily shelled during the First World War, and images of the burning, damaged building became a powerful symbol in French and Allied propaganda. Restoration, funded in part by the Rockefeller family, continued through the interwar years and beyond; in 1962 the cathedral hosted a Mass of reconciliation attended by Charles de Gaulle and Konrad Adenauer.
