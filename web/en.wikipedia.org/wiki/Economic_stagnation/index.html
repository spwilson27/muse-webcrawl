Economic stagnation
DefinitionA prolonged period of little or no economic growth
IndicatorsNear-zero real GDP growth, stagnant real wages, weak investment and productivity
Notable episodesJapan's "Lost Decades" (from 1991); 1970s stagflation; interwar Britain
Related conceptSecular stagnation

Economic stagnation is a prolonged period of slow, negligible, or no economic growth in an economy, typically accompanied by high or persistent unemployment, flat real wages, and weak investment. Unlike a recession — a shorter, sharper contraction — stagnation describes a long-lasting condition in which an economy fails to advance at rates its population has come to expect, often with significant political consequences.

Causes

Economists have attributed stagnation to many causes: demographic decline and ageing populations; slowing technological progress and productivity growth; excessive private or public debt and the aftermath of financial crises; chronic deficiency of aggregate demand; inequality that concentrates income among those with a low propensity to spend; and structural mismatches after shocks. The related theory of secular stagnation, proposed by Alvin Hansen in the 1930s and revived by Lawrence Summers after the 2008 financial crisis, holds that persistent excess of desired saving over investment drives the natural interest rate below zero, making full-employment output hard to sustain with conventional monetary policy.

Examples

The canonical modern case is Japan: after its asset-price bubble collapsed around 1991, Japan experienced its "Lost Decades" of near-zero growth, deflation, and rising public debt despite very low interest rates. The 1970s in the United States and Britain combined stagnation with inflation ("stagflation"), challenging the postwar Keynesian consensus. Parts of the euro area after 2010, and several middle-income economies caught in a "middle-income trap," have also been described as stagnating.

Policy responses

Proposed remedies include expansionary fiscal policy and public investment, structural reforms (competition, education, planning), monetary innovations such as quantitative easing, and measures to raise labour-force participation. The political economy of stagnation — slower income growth fuelling discontent and populism — is itself an active research topic.
