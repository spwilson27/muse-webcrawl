Free-ranging dog

A free-ranging dog is a dog that is not confined to a yard or house. The category includes stray dogs, village dogs, street dogs, and feral dogs, whether owned or ownerless; free-ranging dogs make up the great majority of the world's dog population, often estimated at roughly 75–85% of a global total of several hundred million dogs.

Categories

Free-ranging dogs may be owned but allowed to roam (common in rural and village settings worldwide), unowned but tolerated and often fed by communities ("community dogs"), lost or abandoned strays, or truly feral dogs that survive independently of people. Village dogs in many regions form ancient, genetically distinct populations adapted to scavenging around human settlements.

Ecology and human relations

Free-ranging dogs subsist largely on human refuse and handouts and play a role as scavengers, but they also prey on wildlife, compete with native carnivores, and hybridize with wild canids. They are the principal reservoir and vector of rabies in many countries, and dog bites and road accidents are significant public-health concerns. Management strategies range from culling (increasingly criticized as ineffective) to capture–neuter–vaccinate–release (CNVR/ABC) programmes promoted by animal-welfare and health organizations.
