Dolby noise-reduction system

The Dolby noise-reduction system (Dolby NR) is a series of noise reduction systems developed by Dolby Laboratories for use in analogue audio tape recording. It works by companding: low-level high-frequency signals are boosted during recording and correspondingly attenuated during playback, reducing the audibility of tape hiss.

Versions

Dolby A (1966) was a professional four-band system used in recording studios and cinema. Dolby B (1968) was a simpler single-band system for consumer cassette recorders; it became nearly universal on prerecorded cassettes and home decks. Dolby C (1980) roughly doubled the noise reduction of B by cascading two stages. Dolby S (1989), derived from the professional Dolby SR, arrived late in the cassette era and saw limited adoption. Dolby HX Pro was a related headroom-extension system that was not itself noise reduction.

Operation

Because the system is level-dependent, correct playback requires accurate alignment of the reference level (Dolby level); mistracking causes frequency-response errors sometimes called "pumping" or "breathing". Dolby-encoded tapes played without decoding sound unnaturally bright, and unencoded tapes played with decoding sound dull.
