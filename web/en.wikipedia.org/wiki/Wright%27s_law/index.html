Wright's law, also known as the experience curve effect, is a learning-curve model stating that for each cumulative doubling of units produced, the cost per unit falls by a constant percentage. It was described in 1936 by Theodore Paul Wright, an aeronautical engineer at the Curtiss-Wright Corporation, who observed that the labour required to build aircraft declined predictably as cumulative production increased.

Formulation

The model expresses unit cost as a function of cumulative output: cost falls as a power law of cumulative production, with the slope (or "learning rate") varying by industry — often 10–25 per cent per doubling. Wright's law is closely related to Moore's law but is framed in terms of production volume rather than time, and it has been used extensively in studies of the declining costs of technologies such as solar photovoltaics, batteries and aircraft.

Applications

The effect is used in manufacturing strategy, cost forecasting and technology policy. The Boston Consulting Group popularised a broader version as the "experience curve" in the 1960s, holding that all costs, not just labour, decline predictably with cumulative experience.
