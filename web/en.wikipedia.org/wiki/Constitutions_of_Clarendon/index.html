Constitutions of Clarendon

The Constitutions of Clarendon were a set of legislative procedures passed by King Henry II of England in 1164 at Clarendon Palace in Wiltshire. Presented as a record of the customs of his grandfather Henry I's reign, the sixteen clauses sought to restrict ecclesiastical privileges and the power of church courts and the papacy in England.

Contents

Key clauses provided that: clerks accused of crimes ("criminous clerks") could be tried in royal courts after church proceedings; bishops could not leave the realm without royal permission; excommunications of royal tenants required the king's consent; appeals to Rome were restricted, ending at the archbishop and king; and disputes over land tenure (advowsons) belonged to royal courts.

Conflict with Becket

Archbishop Thomas Becket initially assented, then repudiated the Constitutions, sparking his six-year conflict and exile. After Becket's murder by Henry's knights in Canterbury Cathedral in 1170 and Henry's public penance, the king was forced in the Compromise of Avranches (1172) to abandon the most offensive customs, particularly on criminous clerks, though royal jurisdiction continued to expand in practice.

Significance

The Constitutions are a landmark in the struggle between secular and ecclesiastical jurisdiction in medieval Europe and in the development of English common law under Henry II, alongside his assizes and itinerant justices.
