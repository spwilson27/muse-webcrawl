Shoaling and schooling

Shoaling and schooling
FieldIchthyology, behavioural ecology
ShoalAny social group of fish
SchoolA synchronised, polarised group swimming in the same direction
PrevalenceRoughly a quarter of fish species shoal throughout life; about half shoal as juveniles

In ichthyology, a shoal is any group of fish that remain together for social reasons, while a school is a shoal in which the fish are synchronised and polarised — swimming in the same direction in a coordinated manner. The terms are often used loosely, but the distinction is standard in the scientific literature: all schools are shoals, but not all shoals are schools. Herring, anchovies, sardines and many other forage fish form the most spectacular schools, sometimes numbering in the millions.

Benefits

  • Predator avoidance: grouping dilutes each individual's risk, provides many eyes for vigilance, and allows defensive manoeuvres such as the "fountain effect", flash expansion, and the formation of a hollow bait ball around which confusion effect makes it hard for a predator to target one fish.
  • Foraging success: shoals locate food patches more effectively and can overwhelm the territorial defences of other animals.
  • Hydrodynamic efficiency: fish in coordinated formations may save energy by exploiting vortices shed by neighbours, as has also been proposed for birds in formation flight.
  • Reproduction: aggregation increases the chance of finding mates and synchronises spawning.

Costs

Shoaling increases competition for food, can spread parasites and disease, and makes the group conspicuous to predators and to fishing fleets. An "oddity effect" means individuals that look different from the group are disproportionately targeted, which helps explain the uniformity of size and appearance within schools.

How schools are coordinated

Fish maintain position using vision and the lateral line system, which senses pressure changes from nearby swimmers. Schooling can be modelled as self-organised behaviour emerging from simple local rules — attraction to distant neighbours, alignment with close neighbours, and repulsion at very short range — without any leader. About a quarter of the world's fish species shoal for their whole lives, and roughly half shoal at some stage, especially as juveniles.
