Extractive metallurgy

Tapping molten metal from a furnace, a central pyrometallurgical operation

Extractive metallurgy is the practice of removing valuable metals from an ore and refining the extracted raw metals into a purer form. It is a branch of metallurgical engineering concerned with the processes and technologies by which metals are won from their natural mineral deposits, and it is divided into mineral processing and the three main extraction routes: pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy and electrometallurgy.

Stages

The field begins with mineral processing (ore dressing), in which mined ore is crushed, ground and concentrated to separate valuable minerals from gangue, using gravity separation, froth flotation and magnetic separation. The concentrate then undergoes extraction. Pyrometallurgy uses high-temperature processes such as roasting, smelting and converting, as in blast-furnace ironmaking and copper smelting. Hydrometallurgy uses aqueous chemistry — leaching, solution purification, solvent extraction and precipitation — as in the cyanidation of gold ores and the Bayer process for alumina. Electrometallurgy applies electrolysis, either to aqueous solutions (electrowinning and electrorefining of copper and zinc) or to molten salts (aluminium production by the Hall–Héroult process).

Refining and significance

Crude metal from extraction is refined to the purity its application demands, by fire refining, distillation, or electrolytic refining. Extractive metallurgy underpins the supply of virtually all structural and technological metals, and its efficiency determines much of the cost and environmental footprint of metal production. Modern research emphasises recycling (secondary production), treatment of lower-grade and more complex ores, and reducing the energy and water demands of extraction.
