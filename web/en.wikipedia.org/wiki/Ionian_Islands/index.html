The Ionian Islands (Greek: Ιόνια νησιά) are a group of islands in the Ionian Sea, off the western coast of mainland Greece. Traditionally called the Heptanese ("Seven Islands"), the main group comprises Corfu (Kerkyra), Paxos, Lefkada, Ithaca, Cephalonia, Zakynthos, and the more southerly Kythira, along with numerous smaller islands and islets.

Geography

The islands are mountainous, green and comparatively well watered, with mild winters and hot summers. They form a distinct geographical and historical region separated from the rest of Greece by centuries under Venetian, French and British rule rather than Ottoman rule.

History

In antiquity the islands were colonised by Greek city-states; Corfu, a Corinthian colony, played a role in the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War. After Byzantine and Norman phases, Venice ruled most of the islands from the late Middle Ages until 1797. Following Napoleonic-era upheavals, the United States of the Ionian Islands became a British protectorate in 1815 under the Treaty of Paris. In 1864 Britain ceded the islands to Greece, where they became part of the new Greek kingdom — the first territorial expansion of the modern Greek state. The islands suffered severe earthquakes, notably the 1953 Ionian earthquake that devastated Cephalonia and Zakynthos.

Culture

Centuries of Venetian influence left the islands with distinctive architecture, cuisine, music (such as the kantades serenades) and a historically Catholic presence alongside the Orthodox majority. Corfu's Old Town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Ithaca is traditionally identified as the home of Odysseus in Homer's Odyssey.
