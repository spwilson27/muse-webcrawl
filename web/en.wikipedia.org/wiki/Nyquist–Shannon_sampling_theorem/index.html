Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem is a fundamental result of information theory and signal processing that links continuous-time (analog) signals to discrete-time (digital) samples. It states that a signal whose frequency content is entirely below some maximum frequency B (a bandlimited signal) can be perfectly reconstructed from samples taken at a rate of at least 2B samples per second.

fs > 2B

The threshold rate 2B is called the Nyquist rate, and half the sampling rate is the Nyquist frequency. The theorem is named for Harry Nyquist, whose 1928 work on telegraph transmission laid the groundwork, and Claude Shannon, who published the definitive formulation in 1949; closely related results were developed independently by Edmund Whittaker and Vladimir Kotelnikov.

Aliasing

If a signal is sampled below its Nyquist rate, high-frequency components masquerade as lower frequencies, a distortion called aliasing. Aliasing is visible as the wagon-wheel effect in film and as moiré patterns in images. Practical converters therefore filter out frequencies above half the sampling rate with an anti-aliasing filter before sampling.

Reconstruction

Shannon's proof reconstructs the continuous signal by summing sinc functions centred on each sample (the Whittaker–Shannon interpolation formula). Real digital-to-analog converters approximate this ideal.

Applications

The theorem underlies all digital audio, video, and communications. Compact discs, for example, sample audio at 44.1 kHz because human hearing extends to about 20 kHz, so a rate comfortably above 40 kHz is required to capture the audible band without aliasing.
