Piazza San Marco (Italian pronunciation: [ˈpjattsa sam ˈmarko]; Venetian: Piasa San Marco), often known in English as St Mark's Square, is the principal public square of Venice, Italy, where it is generally known just as la Piazza ("the Square"). All other urban spaces in the city (except the Piazzetta and the Piazzale Roma) are called campi ("fields"). The Piazzetta ("little Piazza") is an extension of the Piazza towards St Mark's Basin in its south-eastern part. The two spaces together form the social, religious and political centre of Venice and are commonly considered together.

Piazza San Marco, looking east towards St Mark's Basilica and the Campanile

A remark usually attributed to Napoleon calls the Piazza San Marco "the drawing room of Europe". It is one of the great urban squares in Europe, and the only large urban space in Venice.

History

The square was constructed in the ninth century as a small area in front of the original St Mark's Basilica. It was enlarged to its present size and shape in 1177, when the Rio Batario, which had bounded it to the west, and a dock, which had isolated the Doge's Palace from the square, were filled in. The rearrangement was undertaken for the meeting of Pope Alexander III and the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa.

The square has always been seen as the centre of Venice. It was the location of all the important offices of the Venetian state, and has been the seat of the archbishopric since the 19th century. It was also the focus for many of Venice's festivals. It is a greatly popular place in Italy even today.

Buildings

The Piazza is dominated at its eastern end by St Mark's Basilica. To the south-east, in the Piazzetta, stands the Doge's Palace. The west end is closed by the Ala Napoleonica, built under Napoleonic occupation. Along the north and south sides run the Procuratie, the long arcaded buildings that housed the offices and residences of the Procurators of St Mark. The Campanile, Venice's tallest structure at nearly 99 metres, stands isolated in the square; it collapsed in 1902 and was rebuilt "as it was, where it was" by 1912.

Flooding

The Piazza San Marco is the lowest point in Venice, and as a result, during the acqua alta ("high water") — the exceptional tide peaks in the Venetian Lagoon — it is one of the first parts of the city to flood. In November 2019 the square was submerged under more than a metre of water in one of the worst floods in its history.
