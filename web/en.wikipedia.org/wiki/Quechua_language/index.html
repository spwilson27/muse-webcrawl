Quechua language

Quechua (also called Runasimi, "people's language") is a family of Indigenous languages spoken by the Quechua peoples, primarily in the Andes of South America — in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and parts of Argentina, Chile, and Colombia. With roughly 7 to 10 million speakers, it is the most widely spoken Indigenous language family in the Americas. Quechua was the language of the Inca Empire and spread widely under both Inca and Spanish colonial administration.

Classification and varieties

Quechua is usually divided into two main branches: Quechua I (Central), spoken in the central Peruvian highlands, and Quechua II, which includes the Southern Quechua of Cusco and Bolivia, the varieties of Ecuador and Colombia (Kichwa), and the northern Peruvian varieties. Mutual intelligibility between distant varieties is limited, leading some linguists to describe Quechua as a family of closely related languages rather than a single language.

Features

Quechua languages are agglutinative, building words extensively with suffixes, and have subject–object–verb word order. They have a three-vowel system /i, a, u/ (with lowered allophones near the uvular consonant /q/), a series of plain, aspirated, and ejective stops in the southern varieties, evidential suffixes marking the source of information, and an inclusive/exclusive distinction in the first-person plural ("we" including or excluding the addressee).

Status

Quechua is an official language in Peru and Bolivia and is recognized in Ecuador. Despite its large number of speakers, many varieties are endangered as speakers shift to Spanish, particularly in urban areas. Efforts to standardize orthography — including debates over three-vowel versus five-vowel spelling — and bilingual intercultural education programmes aim to support the language.
