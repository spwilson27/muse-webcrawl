The mesopelagic zone, also known as the twilight zone or midwater zone, is the part of the pelagic zone of the ocean between depths of about 200 and 1,000 metres. It lies below the sunlit epipelagic zone and above the dark bathypelagic (midnight) zone. Sunlight penetrates to these depths only faintly — enough for vision but not for photosynthesis — and temperatures fall from around 10 °C at the top of the zone to about 4 °C at its base.

Physical conditions

The upper boundary of the mesopelagic is usually marked by the thermocline, the layer in which temperature drops rapidly with depth. Pressure increases by roughly one atmosphere every ten metres, reaching about 100 atmospheres at 1,000 m. Despite the dim conditions, the zone is far from lifeless: it contains the largest biomass of fish in the ocean.

Life in the twilight zone

Mesopelagic organisms display adaptations to low light and scarce food. Many fishes — including lanternfish (myctophids), the most abundant vertebrates on Earth — have large eyes, silvery or dark countershaded bodies, and rows of light-producing organs called photophores used for counter-illumination camouflage, communication and prey attraction. Bioluminescence is produced by an estimated three-quarters of the animals in this zone.

Other characteristic inhabitants include bristlemouths (among the most numerous fish genera), hatchetfish, viperfish, krill, copepods, squid and gelatinous animals such as siphonophores and ctenophores. Many mesopelagic animals undertake diel vertical migration: they swim up to feed in surface waters at night and return to the depths by day — the largest migration, by biomass, on the planet.

Ecological role

The mesopelagic zone is central to the ocean's biological pump. Migrant animals feed at the surface and release carbon-rich waste at depth, while sinking particles of "marine snow" carry carbon from the surface into the twilight zone, where some is sequestered for decades to centuries. Mesopelagic fish are also a key food source for commercially important predators such as tuna, billfish and marine mammals.

Human interest and threats

Estimates of mesopelagic fish biomass have ranged from about 1 to 10 billion tonnes — potentially dwarfing the global catch of conventional fisheries — which has prompted interest in harvesting mesopelagic fish for fishmeal and nutraceuticals. Scientists urge caution, because industrial fishing in the twilight zone could disrupt food webs and carbon sequestration. The zone also faces pressures from deoxygenation, warming and potential deep-sea mining.
