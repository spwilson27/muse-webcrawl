Perplexity

In information theory, perplexity is a measure of how well a probability distribution or probability model predicts a sample. It may be used to compare probability models: a low perplexity indicates the probability distribution is good at predicting the sample. Perplexity is closely related to entropy and to the cross entropy of the model with respect to the true distribution of the data.

Definition

For a probability distribution p, the perplexity is 2H(p), where H(p) is the entropy measured in bits. For a model q evaluated against test data drawn from p, the perplexity is the exponential of the cross entropy: 2H(p,q). Equivalently, perplexity can be expressed as the inverse of the geometric mean of the probabilities the model assigns to the test items, and can be interpreted as a weighted average branching factor — the effective number of equally likely choices the model is uncertain between at each step.

Perplexity of a language model

Perplexity is the standard intrinsic evaluation metric for language models. For a test sequence of N words, the model's perplexity is the inverse probability of the sequence, normalised by the number of words. Lower perplexity generally indicates a better model. Improvements in neural language modelling have driven perplexity on standard benchmarks down dramatically since the 2010s. Perplexity is, however, an imperfect proxy for downstream task performance, and models with identical perplexity may behave differently in generation.

Perplexity in topic models

In topic modelling, held-out perplexity is sometimes used to choose the number of topics, although studies have shown that human judgements of topic quality can correlate negatively with perplexity.
