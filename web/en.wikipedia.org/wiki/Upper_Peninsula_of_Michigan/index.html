Upper Peninsula of Michigan

The Upper Peninsula of Michigan – also known as Upper Michigan or colloquially the U.P. – is the northern and more elevated of the two major landmasses that make up the U.S. state of Michigan; it is separated from the Lower Peninsula by the Straits of Mackinac. It is bounded primarily by Lake Superior to the north, with other delimits to Lake Huron, Lake Michigan, and the state of Wisconsin.

Geography

The Upper Peninsula makes up about 29% of Michigan's land area but has only about 3% of its population. The largest city is Marquette. The peninsula contains about one-third of Michigan's landmass, heavily forested, with rugged terrain in the west including the Porcupine Mountains and the Keweenaw Peninsula, famous for copper mining. The Mackinac Bridge, completed in 1957, connects it to the Lower Peninsula.

History

Indigenous peoples, including the Ojibwe (Chippewa), inhabited the region for millennia. French explorers and missionaries arrived in the 17th century. The region passed between French, British, and American control before becoming part of Michigan. Iron and copper mining boomed in the 19th century, followed by logging. Michigan received the Upper Peninsula as compensation in the settlement of the Toledo War with Ohio.

Culture

Residents of the Upper Peninsula are known as "Yoopers", a term derived from "U.P.-ers". The region has a distinct culture influenced by Finnish, Cornish, and other immigrant communities, with foods such as the pasty. Its dialect and identity are frequently topics of regional pride, and periodic (mostly tongue-in-cheek) movements have proposed statehood as "Superior".
