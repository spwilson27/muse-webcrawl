Tholin

Tholins (from the Ancient Greek tholós, "hazy" or "muddy") are a class of complex organic molecules formed when mixtures of simple carbon- and nitrogen-bearing compounds, such as methane and nitrogen gas, are exposed to ultraviolet radiation or energetic electrons. They are not a single defined compound but a heterogeneous, tar-like mixture of organic polymers.

Formation and properties

The term was coined by astronomer Carl Sagan and his colleague Bishun Khare, who produced tholins in the laboratory by irradiating gas mixtures resembling the atmosphere of Saturn's moon Titan. Laboratory tholins are typically reddish-brown solids built from chains and rings of carbon and nitrogen, incorporating hydrogen cyanide units, amino acids precursors, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. When hydrolysed, some experimental tholins yield amino acids and other compounds of prebiotic interest.

Occurrence in the Solar System

Tholins are thought to be widespread on icy bodies of the outer Solar System. They are believed to give Titan's atmosphere and haze their orange colour, to colour regions of Pluto (such as the dark Cthulhu Macula) and its moon Charon's north pole, and to account for the reddish tint of many Kuiper belt objects, centaurs, and comets. Spacecraft including Cassini–Huygens at Titan and New Horizons at Pluto gathered data consistent with widespread tholin-like organic material.

Significance

Because tholins are rich in carbon and nitrogen and can deliver biologically relevant building blocks, they are of interest to astrobiology and studies of the origin of life. Similar chemistry may have operated on the early Earth, although the strongly reducing atmosphere required is debated.
