Carbon monoxide poisoning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carbon monoxide poisoning typically occurs from breathing in carbon monoxide (CO) at excessive levels. CO is a colourless, odourless gas produced by the incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels; because it cannot be detected by the human senses, it is sometimes called the "silent killer". Hundreds of people die each year from accidental CO poisoning in homes, typically from faulty furnaces, gas appliances, blocked flues, generators used indoors, or vehicles left running in attached garages.

Mechanism

Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin with an affinity roughly 200–250 times that of oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin and reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. It also binds to myoglobin and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase and triggers inflammatory processes, so tissue injury can continue after exposure ends. The brain and heart, with their high oxygen demands, are the organs most vulnerable.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms are non-specific — headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness and confusion — and are often mistaken for influenza, which lacks the fever... In fact flu-like illness that affects a whole household, improves away from home, or sickens pets as well is a classic clue. Higher exposures cause loss of consciousness, seizures, dangerously low blood pressure, cardiac arrhythmia and death. A delayed neuropsychiatric syndrome of cognitive and movement problems can appear days to weeks after apparent recovery. The traditional "cherry-red" skin colour is rare in life and is mostly a post-mortem finding.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is removal from exposure and high-flow 100% oxygen by mask, which accelerates CO elimination; hyperbaric oxygen is used in severe cases in some centres. Prevention centres on servicing fuel-burning appliances, never using generators or grills indoors, and fitting audible CO alarms: detectors are required by law in many jurisdictions.
