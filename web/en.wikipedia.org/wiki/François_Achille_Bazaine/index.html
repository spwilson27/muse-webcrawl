François Achille Bazaine (13 February 1811 – 23 September 1888) was a Marshal of France whose career ended in disgrace after he surrendered the fortress city of Metz, with some 180,000 men, to the Prussians during the Franco-Prussian War in 1870. Before that catastrophe he had built a distinguished record over four decades of service in Algeria, Spain, the Crimea, Italy, and Mexico.

Early career

Bazaine entered the French Foreign Legion as a private soldier in 1831 after failing to gain admission to the École Polytechnique, an unusual start for a future marshal. He rose through the ranks in Algeria and served as a lieutenant in the Carlist War in Spain. He distinguished himself in the Crimean War, becoming a general of division, and fought at the Battle of Solferino in 1859 during the Franco-Austrian War.

Mexico and 1870

During the French intervention in Mexico, Bazaine succeeded General Forey in command of the expeditionary corps in 1863 and was made Marshal of France in 1864. He conducted the campaign supporting Emperor Maximilian until Napoleon III ordered the French withdrawal in 1866–67.

In July 1870, at the outbreak of war with Prussia, Bazaine took command of the Army of the Rhine. After the frontier battles he fell back on Metz, fought the costly battles of Mars-la-Tour and Gravelotte, and allowed his army to be encircled in the fortress. Besieged and short of food, he surrendered on 27 October 1870 — the largest capitulation of the war and a national humiliation for France.

Trial and exile

After the war Bazaine was tried by a military court at the Grand Trianon in 1873 on charges of treason and failing in his duty. He was convicted and sentenced to death, commuted to twenty years' imprisonment. Imprisoned on the Île Sainte-Marguerite off Cannes, he escaped in August 1874 — allegedly by rope from the fortress — and fled to Spain, where he lived in obscurity in Madrid until his death in 1888. His name remained a byword in France for treasonous surrender.
