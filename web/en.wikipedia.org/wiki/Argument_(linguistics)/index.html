In linguistics, an argument is an expression that helps complete the meaning of a predicate, the most common of which is a verb. Arguments are the entities or propositions that a predicate relates or describes; typically, a verb's arguments are noun phrases such as the subject and object. Arguments are distinguished from adjuncts, which are optional modifiers.

A transitive verb with two arguments: subject and object

Arguments and adjuncts

The distinction between arguments and adjuncts is central to syntax. Arguments are required by the predicate's valency: the verb devour requires both a devourer (subject) and something devoured (object), while the verb sleep requires only a subject. Adjuncts, such as temporal or locative phrases ("on Tuesday", "in the park"), add information but can be freely omitted and iterated. Tests for argumenthood include obligatoriness, selectional restrictions imposed by the verb, and the "do so" substitution test.

Argument structure

Argument structure describes how a predicate's participants are organized and realized in syntax. In semantic terms, arguments bear thematic roles (theta roles) such as agent, patient, theme, experiencer, and goal. Theories of argument structure — including lexical-functional grammar, head-driven phrase structure grammar, and construction grammar — differ in how they link semantic participants to grammatical functions. Phenomena such as the dative alternation ("give Mary the book" / "give the book to Mary") and passivization show that the same arguments can be expressed in different syntactic positions.

Clause arguments

Arguments need not be noun phrases: verbs such as think and say can take whole clauses as arguments ("She said that the train was late"). Some predicates, such as weather verbs ("It rained"), appear with a dummy subject that is not a true semantic argument.
