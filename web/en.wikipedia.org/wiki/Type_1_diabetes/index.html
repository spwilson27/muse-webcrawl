Overview

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic condition in which autoimmune destruction of the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas leaves the body unable to produce sufficient insulin, causing high blood sugar (hyperglycaemia). It accounts for roughly 5–10% of diabetes cases and is distinct from type 2 diabetes, which is driven mainly by insulin resistance.

Presentation and management

Onset is often abrupt in children, with thirst, frequent urination, weight loss and fatigue; untreated, it progresses to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which can be fatal. Before insulin's discovery in Toronto in 1921 (Banting, Best, Macleod and Collip), T1D was a death sentence; since then it is managed with multiple daily insulin injections or pumps, increasingly paired with continuous glucose monitors and automated insulin-delivery ("hybrid closed-loop") systems. The immunotherapy teplizumab can delay onset in at-risk individuals — the first disease-modifying treatment, approved in the US in 2022.
