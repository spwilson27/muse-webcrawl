Contrafact

In jazz, a contrafact (plural contrafacts) is a musical composition consisting of a new melody overlaid on a familiar harmonic structure — usually the chord changes of a popular song or standard. Because chord progressions themselves generally cannot be copyrighted, musicians could compose and record new tunes over existing changes without paying royalties on the underlying harmony, while creating works that functioned like original compositions.

History

The practice became central to bebop in the 1940s. Charlie Parker's "Ornithology" is built on the changes of "How High the Moon"; "Ko-Ko" reworks "Cherokee"; and dozens of Parker, Dizzy Gillespie and Thelonious Monk compositions are based on the "rhythm changes" of George Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm" — including "Anthropology", "Moose the Mooche" and "Dexterity". Other famous contrafacts include "Groovin' High" (over "Whispering"), "Hot House" (over "What Is This Thing Called Love?") and "Donna Lee" (over "Indiana").

Characteristics

Contrafacts are not mere improvisations: the melody is a fixed, composed element, often rhythmically intricate and designed to showcase the harmonic possibilities of the progression. The technique also served a practical purpose in jam sessions and recording dates, allowing musicians who knew the standard changes to play new material without rehearsal of unfamiliar harmony.

The term is related to the older practice of contrafactum, in which a song's text is replaced with new lyrics, common in both sacred and secular music from the medieval period onward.
