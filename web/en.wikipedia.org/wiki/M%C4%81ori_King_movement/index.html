Māori King movement

Kīngitanga
Founded: 1858, Ngāruawāhia
First king: Pōtatau Te Wherowhero
Seat: Tūrongo House, Ngāruawāhia
Monarch (from 2024): Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō

The Māori King movement, called the Kīngitanga in Māori, is a movement among some Māori iwi (tribes) of New Zealand that arose in the 1850s to unite Māori under a single sovereign, resist the sale and alienation of Māori land, and stand as a counterpart to the British monarch.

Origins

In 1858, after years of discussion among central North Island chiefs, the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero was installed as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia. The movement's heartland, the King Country, resisted Crown authority after the government's invasion of Waikato in 1863 during the New Zealand Wars, and the second king, Tāwhiao, spent years in exile before returning in 1881.

Monarchs

The kingship is hereditary in the Te Wherowhero line though confirmed by tribal leaders. Twentieth-century monarchs included Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned for forty years (1966–2006) and became a widely respected national figure, and her son Tūheitia, who reigned from 2006 until his death in 2024, when his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō was chosen as queen.

Role today

The Kīngitanga does not claim political sovereignty over all Māori, many of whom are outside its allegiance, but it remains an influential institution in Māori and national life, hosting annual celebrations (poukai) and advocating for Māori interests.
