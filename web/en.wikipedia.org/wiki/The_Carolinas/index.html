The Carolinas are the U.S. states of North Carolina and South Carolina, considered together. They are bordered by Virginia to the north, Tennessee to the west, Georgia to the southwest, and the Atlantic Ocean to the east. The Carolinas were known as the Province of Carolina during America's early colonial period, from 1663 to 1710, when the province was separated into the colonies of North Carolina and South Carolina (formally divided in 1712, with the final administrative split in 1729).

History

In 1663, King Charles II of England granted a charter for the territory named "Carolina" — from Carolus, the Latin form of Charles — to eight Lords Proprietors. The colony's Fundamental Constitutions, associated with philosopher John Locke, were drafted in 1669. Charles Town (Charleston), founded in 1670, became the principal settlement in the south, while settlers spreading from Virginia colonized the Albemarle region in the north. Growing differences between the two regions led to the appointment of separate governors and, eventually, separate royal colonies.

Both Carolinas were centers of early resistance to British policy and of fierce fighting during the American Revolutionary War, including the battles of Kings Mountain and Cowpens. Both were slave states and members of the Confederacy during the American Civil War; South Carolina was the first state to secede in December 1860.

Geography and culture

The Carolinas extend from the Atlantic coastal plain through the rolling Piedmont to the Blue Ridge and Appalachian Mountains in the west, where Mount Mitchell, the highest point east of the Mississippi River, rises in North Carolina. Culturally, the region is known for its barbecue traditions — with distinct eastern and Lexington styles in North Carolina and mustard-based sauces in South Carolina — its college basketball rivalries, beach resorts such as Myrtle Beach and the Outer Banks, and the Gullah culture of the Lowcountry and Sea Islands.
