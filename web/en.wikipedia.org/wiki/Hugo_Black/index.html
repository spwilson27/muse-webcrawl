Hugo Black

Hugo Lafayette Black (27 February 1886 – 25 September 1971) was an American lawyer, politician and jurist who served as a United States Senator from Alabama from 1927 to 1937 and as an associate justice of the U.S. Supreme Court from 1937 to 1971. A member of the Democratic Party and a devoted New Dealer, he was one of the Court's great civil libertarians, famous for his textualist reading of the Bill of Rights and his theory that the Fourteenth Amendment incorporated all of its guarantees against the states.

Early life and Senate career

Black was born in rural Clay County, Alabama, studied law at the University of Alabama, and built a successful plaintiffs' practice in Birmingham, serving as a police court judge and county prosecutor. Elected to the Senate in 1926, he became a populist investigator of utility holding companies and a loyal supporter of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, backing the Fair Labor Standards Act and, controversially, Roosevelt's 1937 Court-packing plan.

In 1923 Black had joined the Ku Klux Klan, a common step for ambitious Alabama politicians of the era; he resigned in 1925. The membership became public only after his Supreme Court confirmation in 1937, causing a national controversy. Black addressed it in a radio broadcast, acknowledged the past membership, and said he had resigned long before; he later maintained he had joined to win votes and never shared its purposes.

Supreme Court

Black served 34 years, among the longest tenures in the Court's history. His jurisprudence centred on the belief that the Constitution's guarantees are absolute and specific: "Congress shall make no law" in the First Amendment meant, in his words, "no law means no law". He wrote the majority opinion in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), guaranteeing counsel to indigent criminal defendants; in Engel v. Vitale (1962), striking down state-sponsored school prayer; and in the Pentagon Papers case, New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), against prior restraint of the press. His opinion in Duncan v. Louisiana (1968), joined as author in related incorporation cases, advanced his longstanding view, first fully set out in dissent in Adamson v. California (1947), that the Fourteenth Amendment made the entire Bill of Rights binding on the states.

Black was not uniformly liberal: he wrote Korematsu v. United States (1944), upholding the wartime exclusion of Japanese Americans, a decision now widely repudiated, and he dissented from rulings, such as Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), that found unenumerated privacy rights not textually specified. He retired on 17 September 1971, in failing health, and died eight days later.
