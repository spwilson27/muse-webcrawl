Gladys Marie Smith (8 April 1892 – 29 May 1979), known professionally as Mary Pickford, was a Canadian actress resident in the United States, and a co-founder of the Pickford–Fairbanks Studios and United Artists, and one of the 36 founders of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences. Known as "America's Sweetheart", "the girl with the curls" and one of the Canadian pioneers in early Hollywood, she was a major influence in the development of film acting.

Pickford, "America's Sweetheart", around 1919.

Career

After a childhood on the stage, Pickford entered films in 1909 at D. W. Griffith's Biograph company, where audiences soon singled her out even before actors were credited by name. Moving between studios for rapidly rising salaries, she became arguably the first international movie star; by 1916 she was producing her own films and earning unprecedented sums. Her screen persona — spunky, curly-haired young girls and adolescents — was sustained in hits such as Rebecca of Sunnybrook Farm (1917), Pollyanna (1920) and Little Lord Fauntleroy (1921).

In 1919 she joined Charlie Chaplin, Douglas Fairbanks and D. W. Griffith to found United Artists, an early attempt by artists to control distribution of their own work. Her marriage to Fairbanks in 1920 made them Hollywood royalty; their home, Pickfair, became a social landmark. She won the Academy Award for Best Actress for her first sound film, Coquette (1929), but her popularity faded with the talkies, and she retired from acting in 1933, continuing as a producer.

Later life and legacy

Pickford received an Academy Honorary Award in 1976 for her contribution to American film. She died in 1979 from complications of a cerebral hemorrhage. The American Film Institute ranked her 24th among the greatest female stars of classic Hollywood cinema. Her extensive archive is held by the Academy Film Archive and the Library of Congress.
