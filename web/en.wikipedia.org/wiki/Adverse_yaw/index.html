Adverse yaw

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adverse yaw is the natural and undesirable tendency of an aircraft to yaw in the opposite direction of a roll. It is caused by the difference in lift, and hence in induced drag, produced by the upward- and downward-deflected ailerons when a roll is commanded. The effect couples roll and yaw and must be counteracted by the pilot or by design features if a coordinated turn is to be maintained.

Mechanism

When the pilot moves the control stick to bank the aircraft, one aileron rises and the other descends. The descending aileron increases the camber and lift of its wing, while the rising aileron reduces lift on the opposite wing, producing the rolling moment. However, induced drag increases with lift: the wing with the deflected-down aileron generates more induced drag than the wing with the raised aileron. This drag difference produces a yawing moment that swings the nose away from the direction of the turn — for example, in a right bank the nose initially yaws left.

Countermeasures

Pilots counteract adverse yaw by applying rudder in the direction of the turn ("stepping on the ball" to keep the turn coordinator balanced). Aircraft designers reduce the effect by several means:

  • Differential ailerons — rigged so the upward-travelling aileron moves through a larger angle than the downward one, increasing drag on the downgoing wing.
  • Frise ailerons — shaped so that the nose of the raised aileron protrudes into the airflow, adding drag on that side.
  • Aileron–rudder interconnects and yaw dampers — mechanical or electronic systems that automatically apply coordinating rudder.
  • Spoilers — roll control by spoilers reduces lift and increases drag on the same wing, largely eliminating the adverse moment.

Significance

Adverse yaw is most pronounced at low speeds and high angles of attack, where induced drag differences are greatest — for instance in gliders with long, high-aspect-ratio wings. Uncorrected, it degrades turn quality, increases drag, and, near the stall, can contribute to a wing dropping. The phenomenon was recognized by the Wright brothers, whose early gliders used wing-warping coupled with a movable rudder to cancel it.
