DNA microarray

A DNA microarray (also commonly known as a DNA chip or biochip) is a collection of microscopic DNA spots attached to a solid surface. Scientists use DNA microarrays to measure the expression levels of large numbers of genes simultaneously or to genotype multiple regions of a genome. Each DNA spot contains picomoles (10−12 moles) of a specific DNA sequence, known as a probe (or reporter or oligo). These can be a short section of a gene or other DNA element that is used to hybridize a cDNA or cRNA (also called antisense RNA) sample (called the target) under high-stringency conditions. Probe–target hybridization is usually detected and quantified by detection of fluorophore-, silver-, or chemiluminescence-labelled targets to determine relative abundance of nucleic acid sequences in the target.

Principle

The core principle behind microarrays is hybridization between two DNA strands, the property of complementary nucleic acid sequences to specifically pair with each other by forming hydrogen bonds between complementary nucleotide base pairs. A high number of complementary base pairs in a nucleotide sequence means tighter non-covalent bonding between the two strands. After washing off non-specific bonding sequences, only strongly paired strands will remain hybridized. Fluorescently labelled target sequences that bind to a probe sequence generate a signal that depends on the hybridization conditions (such as temperature) and on the number of paired bases. The total strength of the signal, from a spot, depends upon the amount of target sample binding to the probes present on that spot, allowing thousands of measurements to be made in a single experiment.

Manufacture

Microarrays can be manufactured in different ways, depending on the number of probes under examination, costs, customization requirements, and the type of scientific question being asked. Two broad families exist: spotted arrays, in which pre-synthesized probes are printed onto a surface by robotic pins, and in-situ synthesized arrays, in which oligonucleotides are built base by base directly on the chip. The best-known commercial example of the latter is the Affymetrix GeneChip, produced using photolithographic techniques adapted from the semiconductor industry.

Uses

Standard applications include gene expression profiling, in which the RNA transcripts produced by thousands of genes are compared between samples (for example, healthy versus diseased tissue); detection of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for genotyping and forensic analysis; comparative genomic hybridization, used to detect gains and losses of genetic material in cancers; and the identification of pathogens. In a classic two-colour experiment, samples labelled with different fluorophores (typically Cy3 and Cy5) are hybridized to the same array, and the ratio of the two signals reveals relative expression.

History

Microarray technology evolved from Southern blotting, where fragmented DNA is attached to a substrate and then probed with a known DNA sequence. The first miniaturized arrays were reported in the late 1980s and early 1990s; the use of collections of distinct DNA sequences in an array format for expression profiling was demonstrated in 1995 by the laboratory of Patrick Brown at Stanford University, and a complete eukaryotic genome on a microarray was published in 1997. Microarrays have since been partly displaced by high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), but remain widely used for genotyping and in clinical diagnostics.
