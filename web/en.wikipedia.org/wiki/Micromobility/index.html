Micromobility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Micromobility
Vehicle typesBicycles, e-bikes, electric scooters, skateboards, shared bikes
Typical speedUp to about 25 km/h (15.5 mph)
Typical weightUnder 500 kg (often far less)
Common usesShort urban trips; first- and last-mile connections to public transport

Micromobility refers to a range of small, lightweight vehicles operating at speeds typically below 25 km/h (15 mph) and driven by users personally, principally for short trips in urban areas. The category includes bicycles, electric bicycles, electric scooters, electric skateboards and shared-mobility devices such as docked and dockless bike-share and scooter-share systems.

Definition and scope

The term was popularised in the late 2010s by transport analyst Horace Dediu. Definitions vary: the Society of Automotive Engineers and other bodies generally class micromobility vehicles by low weight (under 500 kg), low speed and small footprint, in contrast to mopeds and motorcycles ("minimobility") and cars. Most micromobility trips replace journeys of under 8 km, and a large share substitute for walking, public transport or car trips.

History

Human-powered micromobility is as old as the bicycle itself, and public bike-share schemes emerged from the 1960s (Amsterdam's White Bikes) through the large third-generation systems of the 2000s such as Paris's Vélib' (2007). The modern boom began in 2017–2018 with venture-funded dockless bike-share in China and the rapid spread of shared e-scooter companies such as Bird and Lime in the United States and Europe, prompting cities to develop permit schemes, parking rules and safety regulations. Sales of privately owned e-bikes surged during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Impact and issues

Micromobility is promoted for reducing congestion, emissions and the space devoted to cars, and for solving the first- and last-mile problem of public transport. Concerns include rider and pedestrian safety, pavement clutter from dockless parking, the environmental cost of short-lived shared scooters and their collection logistics, accessibility, and the precarious economics of sharing operators, many of which have merged or withdrawn from markets.
