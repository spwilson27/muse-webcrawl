Progressive lenses, also called progressive addition lenses (PAL), progressive power lenses, graduated prescription lenses, and varifocal or multifocal lenses, are corrective lenses used in eyeglasses to correct presbyopia and other disorders of accommodation. They are characterised by a gradient of increasing lens power, added to the wearer's distance correction. The gradient starts at the top of the lens and reaches a maximum addition power at the bottom, providing clear vision at distance, intermediate, and near ranges without the visible line of bifocals or the image jump of trifocals.

History

The first patent for a progressive addition lens was filed in 1907 by Owen Aves, but it was not commercially successful. In 1959, Bernard Maitenaz of the French company Essel (later Essilor) marketed the Varilux lens, the first commercially successful progressive design. Subsequent designs improved the width of the intermediate corridor and reduced unwanted peripheral astigmatism; modern free-form manufacturing computes the lens surface individually for each prescription and frame.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include cosmetically line-free lenses and continuous vision at all distances. Disadvantages include peripheral distortion ("swim" sensation), a narrow intermediate zone, higher cost, and an adaptation period during which some wearers experience dizziness or difficulty judging distances on stairs. Because of Minkwitz's theorem, unwanted astigmatism cannot be eliminated entirely from a progressive surface. See also Presbyopia, Bifocals, and Corrective lens.
