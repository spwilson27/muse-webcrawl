Alfred Charles Kinsey (June 23, 1894 – August 25, 1956) was an American sexologist, biologist, and professor of entomology and zoology at Indiana University. He founded the Institute for Sex Research at Indiana University (now the Kinsey Institute) and authored the Kinsey Reports, Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948) and Sexual Behavior in the Human Female (1953), which challenged widely held beliefs about sexuality and remain landmark — and controversial — works in the field of sexology.

Early career

Kinsey trained as a biologist, earning his doctorate from Harvard in 1919. Joining Indiana University in 1920, he became a leading authority on gall wasps, amassing a collection of millions of specimens and publishing major taxonomic monographs. His study of variation within species shaped his later statistical approach to human behaviour.

The Kinsey Reports

Beginning in 1938, Kinsey and his team conducted thousands of structured interviews about sexual histories. The 1948 male volume reported higher levels of premarital, extramarital, and homosexual experience than the public assumed, and introduced the Kinsey scale, a 0-to-6 continuum from exclusively heterosexual to exclusively homosexual. The 1953 female volume provoked still greater public controversy. Critics attacked his sampling methods and some of his data sources, while supporters credited him with opening human sexuality to scientific study and helping pave the way for the sexual revolution and gay rights movements.

Death and legacy

Kinsey died of heart disease in 1956 while working on further volumes. His life was dramatized in the 2004 film Kinsey. The Kinsey Institute continues research on sexuality, gender, and reproduction.
