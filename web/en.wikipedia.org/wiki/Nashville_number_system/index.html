Nashville number system

TypeMusical shorthand notation
RegionNashville, Tennessee, United States
Era1950s–present
Notation unitsScale degrees 1–7 with diacritical symbols

The Nashville number system is a shorthand for writing chord progressions in which each chord is identified by the degree of the scale on which it is built, rather than by a fixed letter name. It is the standard working notation of Nashville's professional session musicians and underlies the city's famed ability to record songs quickly with little rehearsal.

Notation

A chart in the number system lays out the song bar by bar, using Arabic numerals for chords and a compact set of marks for rhythm and form: underlining groups beats within a bar, diamonds indicate whole-note chords, carets and accents mark rhythmic hits, and repeat signs and codas show the arrangement's structure. Alterations are written beside the numeral, so a major chord on a normally minor degree, a flatted seventh, or a split bar shared by two chords can all be specified. Singers can request a new key moments before a take, and because the chart is key-independent, nothing needs to be rewritten.

Origins

The system grew out of the practice of vocal-group singer Neal Matthews Jr. in the late 1950s, drawing on much older traditions of figured bass and numeric notation, and was carried throughout Music Row by musicians such as Charlie McCoy. Written accounts and instruction books codified the symbols over the following decades, and the method spread from country sessions into gospel, rock and contemporary worship bands across the United States.
