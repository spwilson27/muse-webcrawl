In genetics, anticipation is a phenomenon whereby the symptoms of a genetic disorder become apparent at an earlier age and with increasing severity as the condition is passed on to the next generation. It is a hallmark of the trinucleotide repeat disorders.

Mechanism

Anticipation is caused by the expansion of an unstable repeated DNA sequence — most famously a trinucleotide repeat — when a gene is transmitted. Longer repeats tend to be more unstable and to produce a more severely affected or earlier-onset phenotype, so the repeat length, and hence the disease severity, can grow across generations. In Huntington's disease, a CAG repeat in the HTT gene lengthens preferentially during spermatogenesis, so children inheriting the mutation from their father often develop the disease much earlier (juvenile Huntington's). In myotonic dystrophy type 1, by contrast, large expansions occur more through maternal transmission, producing the congenital form.

History

Anticipation was described clinically in the early 20th century, but Lionel Penrose argued in 1948 that reports of it were largely an artefact of ascertainment bias — mildly affected parents being identified only through severely affected children. The molecular discoveries of the early 1990s (fragile X syndrome in 1991, Huntington's disease in 1993) vindicated the phenomenon by identifying its repeat-expansion mechanism.

Associated conditions

Besides Huntington's disease, myotonic dystrophy and fragile X syndrome, anticipation occurs in several spinocerebellar ataxias, Friedreich's ataxia, and some other repeat expansion disorders; a non-repeat mechanism involving telomere shortening has been proposed in dyskeratosis congenita.
