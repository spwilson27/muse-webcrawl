Microbial mat

A microbial mat is a multi-layered sheet of microorganisms, mainly bacteria and archaea (and sometimes microalgae and fungi), held together by extracellular polymeric substances they secrete. Microbial mats grow at interfaces between different types of material, mostly on submerged or moist surfaces — hot springs, hypersaline lagoons, tidal flats, lake and ocean floors — and are among the oldest ecosystems on Earth, with a fossil record extending back more than 3.4 billion years.

Pigmented layers of a microbial mat around a hot spring, Yellowstone.

Structure

A typical mat is stratified by light and chemistry: an upper layer of oxygenic phototrophs such as cyanobacteria produces oxygen and organic matter; below lie purple sulfur bacteria and other anoxygenic phototrophs; deeper, anaerobic layers host sulfate-reducing bacteria and methanogens. Steep gradients of oxygen, sulfide, and pH change between day and night as photosynthesis starts and stops. By trapping sediment and precipitating minerals, mats build laminated rock structures called stromatolites.

Significance

Cyanobacterial mats oxygenated the atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago, transforming the planet's chemistry and making aerobic life possible. Modern mats in places like Shark Bay (Australia), the Bahamas, and Yellowstone National Park serve as living models for early Earth ecosystems and as analogues for possible life on Mars and icy moons.
