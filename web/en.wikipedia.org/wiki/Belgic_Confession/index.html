The Belgic Confession (Latin: Confessio Belgica; Dutch: Nederlandse Geloofsbelijdenis) is a doctrinal standard of the Reformed churches, written in 1561 by the Reformed preacher Guido de Brès in the southern Low Countries (present-day Belgium), then under Spanish Habsburg rule. It is one of the best-known confessions of faith of the Protestant Reformation and remains a confessional standard, together with the Heidelberg Catechism and the Canons of Dort, in many Reformed denominations around the world — the so-called Three Forms of Unity.

Origins

De Brès, influenced by John Calvin and modelling his work partly on the French (Gallican) Confession of 1559, composed the confession to show the authorities that the Reformed believers were not seditious revolutionaries but law-abiding Christians holding the historic faith. In November 1561 a copy, accompanied by a letter to King Philip II of Spain, was famously thrown over the castle wall at Tournai. Despite the confession's pleas for toleration, persecution continued; De Brès himself was hanged at Valenciennes in 1567.

Content

The confession consists of 37 articles. It treats the doctrine of God and the Trinity, creation and providence, the fall of man, election, the incarnation and two natures of Christ, justification by faith alone, the church and its marks and government, the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, and civil government, concluding with an article on the last judgment. It explicitly rejects Anabaptist teachings and Roman Catholic doctrines such as transubstantiation, while affirming the authority of Scripture.

Adoption and revisions

Successive synods of the Dutch Reformed churches revised and adopted the confession, at Antwerp (1566), Wesel (1568), Emden (1571) and Dordrecht (1574, 1578). The Synod of Dort (1618–19) confirmed it as a doctrinal standard and its text has remained essentially unchanged since, apart from a modification by the Reformed Churches in the Netherlands in 1905 to Article 36 concerning the role of the magistrate in religion.
