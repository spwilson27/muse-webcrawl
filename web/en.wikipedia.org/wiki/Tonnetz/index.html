Tonnetz

The Tonnetz (German: tonal network) is a conceptual lattice diagram introduced by Leonhard Euler in 1739 and later developed by Hugo Riemann and by neo-Riemannian theorists. It represents tonal space as a grid in which notes are arranged by perfect fifths along one axis and by major thirds along another, so that consonant triads appear as small triangles.

Structure

In the classical Tonnetz, moving one step to the right raises a pitch by a perfect fifth, moving diagonally up-right raises it by a major third, and moving vertically raises it by a minor third. Each major or minor triad occupies a triangle of three adjacent nodes. Triads that share two notes — such as a chord and its parallel or relative major/minor — are neighbours on the lattice, making voice-leading proximity visually obvious.

History

Euler proposed the speculum musicum in his Tentamen novae theoriae musicae (1739). In the 19th century Arthur von Oettingen and Riemann recast the lattice in harmonic dualist terms, treating the minor triad as generated downward from a root. The Tonnetz lapsed in the early 20th century but was revived from the 1990s by neo-Riemannian theory (Hyler, Cohn, Lewin), which uses the network to model parsimonious voice leading through the transformations P (parallel), L (leading-tone exchange) and R (relative).

Generalizations

Modern versions extend the lattice to seventh chords, to equal-tempered pitch classes (where the grid wraps into a torus), and to tuning systems beyond just intonation. Software visualisations let analysts trace harmonic progressions — for example the wandering, planing chord sequences of late-Romantic music — as paths across the surface.
