Shōchū

Shōchū (焼酎) is a Japanese distilled spirit, traditionally from Kyushu. It is typically distilled from rice, barley, sweet potatoes, buckwheat, or brown sugar, and is usually about 25% alcohol by volume — weaker than whisky or vodka but stronger than wine or sake. Shōchū should not be confused with sake, which is brewed rather than distilled.

Production

Shōchū production begins with a kōji fermentation: steamed grain or rice is inoculated with the mould Aspergillus (white, black or yellow kōji), which converts starches to sugar. A first mash (moromi) is fermented with yeast, then the main ingredient — for example steamed sweet potatoes for imo-jōchū — is added for a second fermentation. Premium honkaku shōchū is distilled once in a pot still, preserving the character of the base ingredient, and may be aged in earthenware jars, enamel tanks or oak casks. Mass-market kōrui shōchū is multiply distilled from molasses or grain and is nearly neutral in flavour.

Styles

The best-known regional styles include sweet-potato shōchū from Kagoshima (where it is called satsuma-jōchū), barley (mugi) shōchū from Ōita and Iki Island, rice (kome) shōchū from Kumamoto, and brown-sugar (kokutō) shōchū from the Amami Islands. Ryukyu awamori from Okinawa is a closely related but legally distinct spirit made from long-grain rice and black kōji.

Serving

Shōchū is drunk neat, on the rocks, diluted with cold or hot water (mizuwari and oyuwari), or mixed with soda and citrus in a chūhai. In Kyushu, mixing with hot water is especially popular in winter.
