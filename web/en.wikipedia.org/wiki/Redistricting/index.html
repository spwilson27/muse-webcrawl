Redistricting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Redistricting is the process of drawing electoral district boundaries, usually after each census to reflect population change. In the United States the term covers both congressional and state-legislative maps drawn by state legislatures (or, in a growing number of states, by independent or bipartisan commissions), under constitutional requirements of equal population (Wesberry v. Sanders, 1964; Reynolds v. Sims, 1964) and the Voting Rights Act's protections for minority voters.

Redistricting is politically explosive because map-drawers can practise gerrymandering — manipulating boundaries (by “packing” opponents into few districts and “cracking” them across many) to entrench a party's advantage. The US Supreme Court held in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) that partisan gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable political questions in federal court, leaving remedies to Congress, state courts and commissions; racial gerrymandering remains federally reviewable. Other countries entrust the task to independent boundary commissions (the UK model), removing most controversy at the price of some local-representation disputes.
