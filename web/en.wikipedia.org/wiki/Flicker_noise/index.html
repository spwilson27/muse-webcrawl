Flicker noise

Flicker noise is a type of electronic noise with a 1/f power spectral density. It is therefore often referred to as 1/f noise or pink noise, though these terms have wider definitions. It occurs in almost all electronic devices and can appear in a variety of other systems. It was first discovered in early vacuum tubes by John Bertrand Johnson and described by Walter H. Schottky in 1918 and 1925 analyses of flicker effects in thermionic emission.

Properties

Flicker noise has a spectral density proportional to 1/fα with α close to 1, meaning it dominates at low frequencies and produces a spectrum that looks "pink" — intermediate between white noise (flat spectrum) and red/Brownian noise (1/f2). Because the noise power is concentrated at low frequencies, flicker noise is a significant limit in DC and low-frequency measurements, in amplifiers, and in oscillators, where it is upconverted to phase noise close to the carrier (described empirically by Leeson's model). Chopper stabilisation and auto-zero techniques are widely used in precision analogue design to move signals away from the flicker-noise-dominated band.

Origins

The microscopic origin of flicker noise varies by device. In metal films it is associated with resistance fluctuations; in semiconductors it is commonly modelled by the Hooge empirical relation, linking the noise level to the total number of charge carriers, and by the McWhorter model, in which carriers tunnel into and out of traps near interfaces (as in MOSFETs) with a wide distribution of time constants, summing to a 1/f spectrum. Similar 1/f spectra are observed far beyond electronics — in music, in heartbeats, in astronomy and in geophysics — which has made 1/f noise a broad subject of study in statistical physics.
