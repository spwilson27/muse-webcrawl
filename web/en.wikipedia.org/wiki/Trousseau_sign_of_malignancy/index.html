Trousseau sign of malignancy

The Trousseau sign of malignancy, also called Trousseau's syndrome, is a medical sign involving episodes of migratory thrombophlebitis — inflammation of veins due to blood clots that appear in different locations over time — occurring as a paraneoplastic manifestation of an underlying cancer. It is classically associated with pancreatic adenocarcinoma, but also occurs with gastric, lung and other mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas.

PresentationMigratory superficial thrombophlebitis
Associated cancersPancreatic, gastric, lung adenocarcinomas
Named afterArmand Trousseau

History

The French physician Armand Trousseau described the association in the 1860s; in a famous twist, he later recognised the sign in himself and correctly predicted the gastric cancer that caused his death in 1867. The syndrome should not be confused with Trousseau's sign of latent tetany, a different sign by the same physician involving carpal spasm on inflating a blood-pressure cuff.

Mechanism

The hypercoagulable state is thought to arise from tumour-secreted mucins and tissue factor that activate platelets and the coagulation cascade. Patients may require anticoagulation, and low-molecular-weight heparin is often preferred over warfarin.
