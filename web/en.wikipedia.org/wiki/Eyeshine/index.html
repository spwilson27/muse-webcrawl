Eyeshine

Eyeshine is a visible effect of the tapetum lucidum. When light shines into the eye of an animal possessing a tapetum lucidum, the pupil appears to glow. Eyeshine can be seen in many animals, in nature and in flash photographs. The colour of the eyeshine varies with species and with the minerals and structure of the reflective layer: it is often greenish or yellow in cats and dogs, red in some primates and opossums, and white or blue in other species.

The tapetum lucidum is a layer of tissue behind the retina that reflects visible light back through the retina, increasing the light available to photoreceptors and improving night vision. The reflected light that escapes through the pupil is what observers see as eyeshine. Nocturnal and crepuscular animals — including many carnivores, ungulates, crocodilians, and some fish — commonly show strong eyeshine.

Uses

Wildlife observers and hunters use "spotlighting" or "lamping", scanning with a light near their own line of sight to locate animals by their eyeshine. Researchers use eyeshine characteristics to help identify species at night. In photography, the human "red-eye effect" is a weaker phenomenon caused by reflection from the blood-rich choroid, since humans lack a tapetum lucidum.
