Glossina

Tsetse fly (Glossina)
KingdomAnimalia
OrderDiptera
FamilyGlossinidae
GenusGlossina

Glossina is a genus of large flies, the tsetse flies, found only in Africa. They are the sole members of the family Glossinidae and are obligate parasites that live by feeding on the blood of vertebrate animals. Tsetse flies are the principal vectors of the trypanosome parasites that cause African sleeping sickness in humans and nagana (animal African trypanosomiasis) in livestock.

Biology

Tsetse are unusual among insects in their reproduction: the female fertilises one egg at a time, and the larva develops inside the uterus, nourished by secretions from a "milk gland", in a strategy called adenotrophic viviparity. She deposits a single fully grown larva, which pupates in the soil. Consequently tsetse reproduce slowly, with a female producing only a handful of offspring in her lifetime. Both sexes feed exclusively on blood, roughly every two to three days, and are active by day; their bite is painful. Species are conventionally divided into three groups by habitat: the riverine palpalis group, the savanna morsitans group, and the forest fusca group.

Distribution and disease

Tsetse inhabit a belt of roughly 10 million square kilometres across 38 sub-Saharan African countries, between the Sahara and the Kalahari. By transmitting trypanosomes, they have profoundly shaped African agriculture and settlement, restricting cattle rearing across much of the fertile belt. Control methods include insecticide-treated targets and traps (often blue and black cloth, which attracts the flies), bush clearing, aerial spraying, odour baits, and the sterile insect technique — used to eradicate Glossina austeni from Zanzibar in the 1990s.

Species

About 23 living species are recognised, including Glossina morsitans, G. palpalis, G. fuscipes, G. tachinoides, G. pallidipes, G. swynnertoni and G. austeni. Fossil tsetse are known from the Florissant Formation of Colorado, indicating the genus once occurred in North America.
