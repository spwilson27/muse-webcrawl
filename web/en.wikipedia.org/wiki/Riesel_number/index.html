Riesel number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a Riesel number is an odd natural number k for which the integers of the form k·2n − 1 are all composite for every natural number n. In other words, the set of numbers of the form

{ k·2n − 1 : n ∈ ℕ }

contains no prime numbers. The concept is named after Hans Riesel, who studied such numbers in the 1950s. Riesel numbers are the analogue, for the "minus" case, of Sierpiński numbers, which concern the sequence k·2n + 1.

Known Riesel numbers

In 1956, Hans Riesel showed that 509203 is a Riesel number by exhibiting a covering set of prime divisors {3, 5, 7, 13, 19, 37, 73}: for every n, at least one of these primes divides 509203·2n − 1. Multiples of 509203 are therefore also Riesel numbers. It is conjectured that 509203 is the smallest Riesel number.

The Riesel problem

The Riesel problem asks for the smallest Riesel number. Because 509203 is conjectured to be the smallest, the problem is to prove that every odd k below 509203 yields at least one prime of the form k·2n − 1 for some n. Distributed computing projects, notably PrimeGrid's Riesel Problem effort (building on earlier work by the Riesel Sieve project), have eliminated candidate values of k one by one by finding a prime for each; only a handful of candidates below 509203 remain uneliminated.

Covering sets

Most known Riesel numbers are proved using covering sets: a finite set of primes such that every term of the sequence is divisible by at least one of them, depending on n modulo the multiplicative orders involved. Numbers for which no covering set is known may still be Riesel numbers, but this cannot currently be proved by that method.
