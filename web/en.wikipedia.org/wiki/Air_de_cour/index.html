The air de cour (French, "court air") was the principal form of secular vocal music in France from the late 1570s until about the middle of the seventeenth century. Courtly, refined, and predominantly amorous in subject, airs de cour were composed for performance at the French royal court and in aristocratic salons, first in polyphonic settings and increasingly as solo songs with lute accompaniment.

Development

The term first appeared in Adrian Le Roy's Livre d'airs de cour miz sur le luth (1571), a collection of chansons arranged for solo voice and lute. Early airs were closely related to the voix de ville and to the measured music (musique mesurée à l'antique) promoted by Jean-Antoine de Baïf's Académie de Poésie et de Musique, which sought to align musical rhythm with classical poetic meter. During the reigns of Henri IV and Louis XIII, the genre became the dominant secular song form in France.

Composers and publications

The Parisian publisher Pierre Ballard issued numerous books of airs, and the most important composer of the mature genre was Michel Lambert, whose airs exemplify its elegance and expressive declamation. Other significant figures include Pierre Guédron, Antoine Boësset, Étienne Moulinié, Gabriel Bataille (who published large anthologies in lute tablature), and Denis Gaultier. Sets of airs appeared both as partbooks for several voices and, increasingly, as solo songs with lute or theorbo accompaniment.

Style

Airs de cour are typically strophic, syllabic, and homophonic, with graceful, dance-influenced melodies and texts about love, pastoral dalliance, or (in the air à boire) drinking. Their restrained clarity contrasted with the elaborate polyphony of the Franco-Flemish chanson, and their textures prepared the way for the French Baroque solo song and, indirectly, for the recitative style of French opera. The genre declined after about 1650 as the air sérieux and the court ballet and opera of Jean-Baptiste Lully reshaped French musical taste.
