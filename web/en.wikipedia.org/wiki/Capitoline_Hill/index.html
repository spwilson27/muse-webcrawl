The Capitoline Hill (Latin: Collis Capitolinus; Italian: Campidoglio) is one of the Seven Hills of Rome and was the citadel and religious centre of the ancient city. Though the smallest of the hills, it held the great Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus — the most important temple of the Roman state — and the fortress known as the Arx.

Ancient history

According to legend, the Sabine women intervened here to stop the battle between Romans and Sabines, and the Tarpeian Rock, from which traitors were thrown, overlooked the Forum below. The Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, dedicated in 509 BC in the first year of the Republic, housed the Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno and Minerva. Triumphal processions ended on the Capitoline, and the hill's name gave English the word "capitol".

In 390 BC the honking of the sacred geese of Juno is said to have alerted Marcus Manlius Capitolinus to a night attack by the Gauls, saving the citadel even as the city below was sacked.

The Campidoglio today

The present appearance of the hill owes much to Michelangelo, who in 1536 redesigned the summit as the Piazza del Campidoglio, with a distinctive oval paving pattern and an equestrian statue of Marcus Aurelius at its centre. The surrounding palaces house the Capitoline Museums, among the oldest public museums in the world, founded in 1471 when Pope Sixtus IV donated a group of bronze statues.
