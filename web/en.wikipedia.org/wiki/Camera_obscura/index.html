A camera obscura (Latin for "dark chamber") is an optical device that projects an image of its surroundings onto a screen or wall inside a darkened room or box. Light passing through a small hole — later, a lens — forms an inverted, reversed image of the external scene. The camera obscura was used for centuries to observe solar eclipses, as a drawing aid for artists, and as public entertainment, and it is the direct ancestor of the photographic camera.

History

The principle was described in the 5th century BC by the Chinese philosopher Mozi and was analysed by Aristotle, who observed the crescent of a partially eclipsed sun projected through gaps between leaves. The Arab physicist Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) gave the first clear experimental account around 1020 in his Book of Optics. Leonardo da Vinci described the device in his notebooks, and in 1568 Giambattista della Porta recommended it as an aid to drawing.

From the 17th century, portable box and tent forms fitted with lenses and mirrors made the camera obscura a standard artist's tool; scholars have debated its use by painters such as Vermeer and Canaletto. In the 18th and 19th centuries, large room-sized camera obscuras at seaside resorts — projecting live panoramic views onto a table — became popular attractions, several of which still operate. The name was ultimately applied to the light-tight box of the photographic camera once light-sensitive materials, developed in the 1820s–30s, made it possible to fix the projected image permanently.

Principle

Light travels in straight lines. Rays from each point of a bright scene passing through a small aperture cross over and arrive at corresponding points on the opposite surface, forming an image that is upside-down and left-right reversed. A lens in place of the pinhole gathers more light, producing a brighter, sharper image; a mirror can flip the projection for viewing on a horizontal table.
