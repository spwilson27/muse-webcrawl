Weird number

In number theory, a weird number is a natural number that is abundant but not semiperfect: the sum of its proper divisors exceeds the number itself, yet no subset of those divisors adds up to the number.

Examples

The smallest weird number is 70. Its proper divisors are 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 14, and 35, summing to 74 > 70; but no combination of them sums to 70. The first few weird numbers are 70, 836, 4030, 5830, 7192, 7912, 9272, 10430, and 10570.

Properties

Infinitely many weird numbers exist: if n is weird and p is a prime larger than the sum of the divisors of n, then np is also weird. All known weird numbers are even; whether any odd weird number exists is an open problem. Primitive weird numbers — weird numbers not a multiple of any other weird number — begin 70, 836, 4030, 5830, and so on.

Weird numbers were introduced by Stan Benkoski in 1972. They are related to the subset-sum problem: determining whether a number is semiperfect is computationally harder than determining whether it is abundant, and weird numbers sit at the intersection of the two notions.
