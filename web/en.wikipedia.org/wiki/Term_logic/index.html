Term logic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Term logic, also known as traditional logic, syllogistic logic or Aristotelian logic, is a name for the approach to logic founded by Aristotle and developed by his followers, the Peripatetics, and later by medieval scholastic logicians. It analyses propositions as composed of terms — a subject term and a predicate term joined by a copula — and studies the inferences, especially syllogisms, that can be drawn from such propositions.

Categorical propositions

Term logic recognises four standard forms of categorical proposition, traditionally labelled by the vowels A, E, I and O (from the Latin affirmo and nego):

  • A — universal affirmative: "All S are P"
  • E — universal negative: "No S are P"
  • I — particular affirmative: "Some S are P"
  • O — particular negative: "Some S are not P"

The logical relations among these forms — contradiction, contrariety, subcontrariety and subalternation — are displayed in the traditional square of opposition.

The syllogism

A syllogism is an argument of two premises and a conclusion containing exactly three terms, each occurring twice. Aristotle catalogued the valid moods and figures, such as the famous Barbara: "All men are mortal; all Greeks are men; therefore all Greeks are mortal." Medieval logicians developed elaborate mnemonic verses and rules (distribution of terms, quality and quantity of premises) for testing validity, and enriched the system with theories of supposition and consequences.

Comparison with modern logic

Term logic dominated Western logic until the late 19th century, when Frege's predicate logic (1879) superseded it in mathematical contexts. Predicate logic handles relations and multiple generality (nested quantifiers), which term logic cannot adequately express — for example, "every boy loves some girl" is ambiguous in ways term logic cannot disentangle. Term logic traditionally also assumed that universal propositions have existential import (that their subject terms refer to something), an assumption modern logic drops. Nevertheless, term logic remains equivalent in power to monadic predicate logic, and has seen revival in formalisations such as those deriving from Sommers' calculus of terms.
