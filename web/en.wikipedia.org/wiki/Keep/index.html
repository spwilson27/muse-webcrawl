A keep is a type of fortified tower built within castles during the Middle Ages by European nobility. The term derives from the Middle English kype and was applied from the 16th century to the largest, strongest tower of a castle. The keep served as the final refuge during a siege and often contained the lord's halls, chambers, a chapel, wells and stores.

Origins and development

Keeps evolved from the timber towers of early motte-and-bailey castles and from earlier great towers such as those of the Frankish world. Following the Norman Conquest of England, stone keeps proliferated: the White Tower at the Tower of London (begun c. 1078) and the great keep at Colchester are among the earliest and largest in England. In France, examples include the donjons of Loches and Vincennes; the word donjon is the French equivalent, from which "dungeon" ultimately derives.

Design

Keeps were typically square or rectangular in plan in the 11th and 12th centuries, with thick walls, few and small windows on lower floors, and an entrance raised above ground level reached by an external stair — a defensive measure against battering and mining. Later designs experimented with round, polygonal and shell keep forms; Henry II's polygonal keep at Orford and the cylindrical keep at Conisbrough are notable English examples. Interiors were divided by cross-walls, with a great hall, solar, chapel and garderobes arranged over several floors above vaulted basements used for storage.

Later use

As concentric castles with strong curtain walls and gatehouses developed in the 13th and 14th centuries, the keep's defensive role diminished, though many remained symbols of lordship and continued in residential or administrative use. In Japan, the analogous main tower of a castle is the tenshu, as at Himeji Castle.
