Homo economicus

Homo economicus, or economic man, is the portrayal of humans as agents who are consistently rational and narrowly self-interested, and who pursue their subjectively defined ends optimally. It is a word play on Homo sapiens, and is used in economic theory and in critiques of economics. The idealized agent compares the costs and benefits of each possible action with perfect or near-perfect information and chooses the option that maximizes their own utility.

History of the term

The concept developed from the work of classical economists such as Adam Smith and David Ricardo, and it was given an early explicit form in John Stuart Mill's 1836 essay "On the Definition of Political Economy," which described political economy as treating humankind "solely as a being who desires to possess wealth." The Latin phrase itself became common in the late nineteenth century, often used by critics of Mill's abstraction. In the twentieth century, the model was formalized within neoclassical economics through expected utility theory and rational choice theory.

Characteristics

In its standard form, the model assumes that the agent has complete and transitive preferences, can rank all possible outcomes, possesses full information, and calculates without error or cost. The agent's preferences are usually taken as stable, self-regarding, and measurable in terms of utility. These assumptions make behavior mathematically tractable and allow economists to derive predictions about markets, prices, and incentives.

Criticism

Behavioral economists such as Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky demonstrated systematic deviations from the model, including loss aversion, framing effects, and overconfidence. Critics also note that real people care about fairness, reciprocity, status, and moral commitments, not only personal gain. Sociologists and anthropologists add that the model is culturally specific, and experiments in small-scale societies have found widely varying patterns of bargaining and sharing. Some economists defend Homo economicus as a useful idealization rather than a literal description of human nature, arguing that markets can behave "as if" participants were rational even when individuals are not.
