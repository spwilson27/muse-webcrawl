Integer factorization

In number theory, integer factorization is the decomposition of a composite number into a product of smaller integers, ultimately into prime numbers, whose existence and uniqueness is guaranteed by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. When the factors are restricted to primes, the task is also called prime factorization. For large numbers, factorization is computationally very hard: no efficient classical algorithm is known for factoring large semiprimes, and this difficulty underlies the security of the widely used RSA cryptosystem.

Complexity

Naive trial division requires testing divisors up to the square root of the number, which is exponential in the number of digits. The best known classical algorithms, such as the general number field sieve, run in sub-exponential time and have factored numbers of more than 800 bits (RSA-250, a 250-digit number, was factored in 2020). By contrast, a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could factor in polynomial time, which motivates the development of post-quantum cryptography. The problem is believed to lie outside the complexity class P for classical computers, though it is not known to be NP-complete, and it belongs to NP ∩ co-NP.

Algorithms

Special-purpose algorithms exploit structure in the number: trial division and wheel factorization for small factors; Pollard's rho algorithm and the elliptic curve method for numbers with moderately small factors; and Fermat's method when the factors are close together. General-purpose methods — Dixon's algorithm, the quadratic sieve and the number field sieve — are used for large hard instances, which are typically products of two primes of similar size.

Applications

Beyond RSA encryption and digital signatures, whose security relies on the practical infeasibility of factoring large moduli, factorization problems appear in hash table probing, computational number theory research and benchmarking of supercomputers and distributed computing projects such as RSA Factoring Challenge efforts and the Cunningham project.
