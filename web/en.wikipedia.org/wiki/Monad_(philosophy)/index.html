Monad (philosophy)

The term monad (from Greek μονάς monas, "unit", from μόνος monos, "alone") is used in philosophy for a variety of interrelated ideas about unity and indivisible substance. The concept appears in Pythagoreanism, Neoplatonism, and the philosophies of Giordano Bruno and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose Monadology (1714) gives the best-known system of monads.

Historical overview

For the Pythagoreans, the monad was the first thing that came into existence, the source of the dyad and of all number. In Neoplatonism, "the One" is the ineffable source from which all being emanates. Giordano Bruno adopted monads as the physical and spiritual minima of the universe, living units that compose all things.

Leibniz's monadology

In the Monadology, written in 1714 and published posthumously, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz proposed that the universe is composed of an infinite number of simple, indivisible, non-material substances called monads. Key features of his system include:

  • Simplicity: monads have no parts and cannot be created or destroyed naturally; they are not extended and do not occupy space in the way bodies do.
  • Windowlessness: monads "have no windows" — nothing can enter or leave them; they do not causally interact with one another.
  • Perception and appetition: each monad internally represents ("perceives") the entire universe from its own point of view, and passes from one perception to the next by an internal tendency called appetition.
  • Pre-established harmony: the apparent coordination between monads — including between soul and body — results from God's having created each monad to unfold in perfect correspondence with all the others.
  • Hierarchy: monads range from bare monads with only unconscious petites perceptions, through animal souls with memory, up to rational human minds capable of apperception; God is the supreme monad, the "monad of monads".

What we call physical objects are, for Leibniz, aggregates or phenomena grounded in monads, and his metaphysics was designed in part to reconcile mechanism in physics with teleology and the existence of soul-like substances.

Later use

The term continues to be used in various senses in metaphysics, in the phenomenology of Edmund Husserl (for the concrete ego), and in the process philosophy influenced by Alfred North Whitehead. In mathematics and computer science, "monad" has acquired distinct technical meanings in category theory and functional programming.
