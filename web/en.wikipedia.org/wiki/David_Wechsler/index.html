David Wechsler (January 12, 1896 – May 2, 1981) was a Romanian-born American psychologist. He developed well-known intelligence scales, such as the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC), and defined intelligence as "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment."

Early life and career

Wechsler was born in Lespezi, Romania, to a Jewish family and emigrated to the United States as a child. He studied at the City College of New York and Columbia University, where he earned his doctorate in 1925. During World War I he worked with the U.S. Army testing recruits. He later became chief psychologist at Bellevue Psychiatric Hospital in New York, a post he held from 1932 to 1967, and a clinical professor at New York University Medical Center.

The Wechsler scales

Dissatisfied with the limitations of the Stanford–Binet test, particularly its reliance on a single score and its design for children, Wechsler published the Wechsler–Bellevue Intelligence Scale in 1939. It introduced separate verbal and performance scores and a deviation IQ based on comparison with one's age group rather than a ratio of mental to chronological age. The WAIS followed in 1955, and the WISC for children in 1949. The scales have been revised repeatedly and remain among the most widely used intelligence tests in clinical and educational psychology.

Views on intelligence

Wechsler argued that intelligence involves more than cognitive abilities measurable by traditional tests, emphasizing personality and motivational factors. His scales' structure influenced later theories of intelligence and the design of many subsequent test batteries.
