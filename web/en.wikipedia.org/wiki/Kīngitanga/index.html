The Kīngitanga, or Māori King Movement, is a movement that arose among some Māori iwi in the North Island of New Zealand in the 1850s to establish a monarchy that could unify Māori, halt the alienation of land to European settlers, and stand as a counterpart to the British Crown under the Treaty of Waitangi. Its first king, the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero, was crowned in 1858.

Origins and war

Supporters of a Māori king, including the tohunga and strategist Wiremu Tamihana of Ngāti Hauā, sought a figure of mana equal to Queen Victoria who could enforce laws and stop land sales. Pōtatau accepted the kingship reluctantly and died in 1860, succeeded by his son Tāwhiao. In 1863, colonial forces invaded the Waikato, beginning the New Zealand Wars' largest campaign; the Kīngitanga was defeated at Ōrākau in 1864 and withdrew into the rugged hill country thereafter called the King Country, where Tāwhiao remained in exile for some twenty years while confiscated Waikato lands (raupatu) passed to settlers.

The movement today

The Kīngitanga endured as a focus of Māori unity, culture and political advocacy rather than a separatist state. Its headquarters are at Tūrangawaewae Marae in Ngāruawāhia, where the annual Koroneihana (coronation) celebrations draw thousands. Monarchs have included Mahuta, Te Rata, Korokī, and Dame Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned for forty years from 1966 until her death in 2006 and was widely respected across New Zealand. Her son, Kīngi Tūheitia, reigned from 2006 until his death in 2024, when his daughter, Nga wai hono i te po, was chosen as the eighth Māori monarch. The Kīngitanga played an important role in the Waikato-Tainui raupatu settlement of 1995, in which the Crown apologised for the invasion and confiscations.
