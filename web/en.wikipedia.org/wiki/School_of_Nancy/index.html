School of Nancy

School: Psychotherapy, hypnosis

Location: Nancy, France

Key figures: Ambroise-Auguste Liébeault, Hippolyte Bernheim

Era: Late 19th century

The School of Nancy was a French school of psychotherapy founded in the second half of the nineteenth century in the city of Nancy, in northeastern France. It is best known for its theory and practice of hypnosis, which it explained through the psychological mechanism of suggestion, in opposition to the rival Salpêtrière school of Paris led by Jean-Martin Charcot, which regarded hypnosis as a pathological state associated with hysteria.

History

The school is generally traced to the country physician Ambroise-Auguste Liébeault (1823–1904), who settled in Nancy in 1864 and began treating patients, many of them poor, free of charge using hypnotic suggestion. In 1866 he published Du sommeil et des états analogues ("On Sleep and Analogous States"), which argued that hypnotic phenomena were produced by suggestion and concentrated attention rather than by any physical agent or disease. The book attracted little notice until the early 1880s, when it was taken up by Hippolyte Bernheim (1840–1919), a professor of medicine at the University of Nancy, whose endorsement transformed Liébeault's practice into an influential movement.

Bernheim's works, notably De la suggestion dans l'état hypnotique et dans l'état de veille (1884) and De la suggestion et de ses applications à la thérapeutique (1886), systematised the Nancy position. Other members of the circle included the physiologist Henri-Étienne Beaunis and the jurist Jules Liégeois.

Doctrine

The central claims of the Nancy school were:

  • Hypnosis is not a disease or a special neurological condition, but a state closely related to ordinary sleep that can be induced in most healthy people.
  • The phenomena of hypnosis — catalepsy, anaesthesia, hallucinations, post-hypnotic actions — are produced by suggestion: an idea accepted by the mind tends to realise itself in action, sensation or belief.
  • Suggestion also operates in the waking state, and therefore hypnotic suggestion could be used therapeutically for functional disorders, pain, insomnia and habits such as alcoholism.

Bernheim eventually went so far as to declare "there is no hypnosis, there is only suggestion," and developed psychotherapy as a general method based on waking suggestion, foreshadowing later talking cures.

Rivalry with the Salpêtrière school

Charcot, at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris, maintained that hypnosis was a genuine neurological state with fixed stages (lethargy, catalepsy, somnambulism) found essentially in hysterics. The Nancy physicians countered that Charcot's stages were artefacts produced by training and suggestion among his patients. The dispute was one of the famous controversies of nineteenth-century medicine; by the early 1890s the Nancy interpretation had largely prevailed, and Charcot's model collapsed soon after his death in 1893.

Influence

The school influenced the development of psychotherapy across Europe and America. Émile Coué, who studied with Liébeault, derived his method of conscious autosuggestion ("Every day, in every way, I am getting better and better") from Nancy principles. Sigmund Freud visited Nancy in 1889 to observe Bernheim and Liébeault at work, an experience that contributed to his abandonment of hypnosis in favour of free association. The school's emphasis on suggestion also fed into crowd psychology and the study of placebo effects.
