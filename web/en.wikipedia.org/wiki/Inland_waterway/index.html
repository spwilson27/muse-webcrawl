Inland waterway

An inland waterway is a navigable channel used to transport goods and passengers in the interior of a landmass, including rivers, lakes, canals and estuaries. Inland waterways have been used since antiquity and remain a major mode of freight transport where geography permits, because a single barge can carry the equivalent load of dozens of trucks or a full train, with correspondingly low fuel consumption and emissions per ton-kilometre.

Networks

The largest systems include the Mississippi–Ohio–Missouri river network in the United States, the Rhine–Danube corridor in Europe, the Yangtze and Pearl River systems in China, and the Ganges–Brahmaputra in India. Canals extend and connect these rivers: major examples are the Grand Canal of China, the Volga–Don Canal, the Rhine–Main–Danube Canal (opened 1992, linking the North Sea to the Black Sea) and the lock systems of the Saint Lawrence Seaway. Waterways are classified by the size of vessel they can carry; in Europe the CEMT classification grades channels from Class I to Class VII.

Infrastructure and operation

Locks overcome differences in water level; dams, weirs and dredging maintain navigable depth; and towboats push lashed-together barge fleets on the larger American rivers. Seasonal constraints — ice, floods and drought — together with the need for transshipment to other modes at each end, limit waterways to bulk and low-value, time-insensitive cargoes such as grain, coal, aggregates, petroleum and containers on scheduled routes.
