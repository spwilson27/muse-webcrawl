Definitive map

A definitive map is a map prepared by a surveying authority (usually a county council or unitary authority) in England and Wales recording public rights of way — public footpaths, bridleways, restricted byways and byways open to all traffic. The definitive map, together with its accompanying definitive statement, is the legal record of public rights of way and is conclusive evidence of the particulars it contains, as provided for under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.

History

The system originated in the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, which required county councils to survey and map rights of way in their areas. The Countryside Act 1968 and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 refined the process, including procedures for modifying the map where evidence shows a way exists, does not exist, or is recorded with the wrong particulars. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 set a cut-off date (originally 1 January 2026 in England) after which some historical rights of way not recorded on the map could be extinguished, though the government later announced the cut-off would be repealed in England.

Modification orders

Any person may apply for a definitive map modification order, supported by evidence — for example, 20 years' public use (as of right), or historical documents such as inclosure awards, tithe maps and early Ordnance Survey mapping. User groups such as the Ramblers and the British Horse Society research and claim unrecorded routes.
