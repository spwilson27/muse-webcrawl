Dentition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dentition pertains to the development of teeth and their arrangement in the mouth. In particular, it is the characteristic arrangement, kind, and number of teeth in a given species at a given age. That is, the number, type and morpho-physiology of the teeth of an animal.

The dentition of animals (with the exception of some groups such as birds and turtles) shows a repeated pattern: teeth are differentiated into incisors at the front, followed by canines, premolars and molars at the back. The number of each type of tooth is expressed in a dental formula for one side of the upper and lower jaws.

Human dentition

Humans are diphyodont: they develop two sets of teeth. The deciduous (primary, "milk") dentition comprises 20 teeth — in each quadrant two incisors, one canine and two molars. The permanent dentition comprises 32 teeth, with each quadrant having two incisors, one canine, two premolars and three molars, expressed by the dental formula 2.1.2.3 / 2.1.2.3. Eruption of the primary teeth begins around six months of age; permanent teeth replace them between roughly six and thirteen years, with third molars ("wisdom teeth") erupting in late adolescence or adulthood, if at all.

Dental formulae

Dental formulae are a shorthand for comparing species. For example, the domestic cat has a formula of 3.1.3.1 / 3.1.2.1 (30 teeth), while dogs have 3.1.4.2 / 3.1.4.3 (42 teeth). Marsupials and placental mammals differ characteristically, and formulae are used by zoologists and palaeontologists to identify and classify animals from jaw remains.

Dentition types

Animals may be homodont (all teeth alike, as in most toothed whales) or heterodont (teeth of different shapes and functions, as in most mammals). Monophyodont animals have one set of teeth for life; diphyodont animals have two; polyphyodont animals, such as sharks and crocodiles, continuously replace their teeth.
