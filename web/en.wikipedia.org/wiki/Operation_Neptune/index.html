Operation Neptune was the naval component of Operation Overlord, the Allied invasion of German-occupied Normandy in the Second World War. It covered the planning and execution of the cross-Channel movement, the naval bombardment, and the amphibious landings on D-Day, 6 June 1944, together with the subsequent build-up of men and supplies over the beaches. Neptune is often loosely used as a synonym for the Normandy landings themselves, although strictly the landings were the assault phase of Neptune within the wider Overlord plan.

Planning

The plan required transporting some 156,000 troops across the English Channel on the first day, landing on five beaches — Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword — spread along the Normandy coast. The naval forces, commanded by Admiral Sir Bertram Ramsay, totalled nearly 7,000 vessels, from battleships and cruisers providing gunfire support to over 4,000 landing craft. Minesweepers cleared swept channels through the Channel minefields, midget submarines marked the eastern beaches, and airborne divisions dropped inland during the night to secure the flanks.

D-Day

After a 24-hour postponement due to bad weather, the assault began in the early hours of 6 June 1944 with naval bombardment and airborne landings, followed by seaborne landings from 06:30. By nightfall the Allies had established beachheads on all five beaches, though at heavy cost on Omaha Beach. Artificial Mulberry harbours were towed across the Channel and assembled off Arromanches and Omaha to sustain the build-up, allowing the landing of hundreds of thousands of tons of supplies in the following weeks.

Outcome

Neptune succeeded in its central purpose: it delivered and sustained the Allied armies in Normandy, enabling the breakout and liberation of France that followed. It remains the largest seaborne invasion in history.

Not to be confused with Operation Neptune Spear, the 2011 raid that killed Osama bin Laden.
