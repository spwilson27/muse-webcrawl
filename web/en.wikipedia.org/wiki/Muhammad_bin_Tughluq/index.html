Muhammad bin Tughluq (c. 1300–1351) was the Sultan of Delhi from 1325 to 1351, the most famous ruler of the Tughlaq dynasty. A scholar-prince of formidable learning — in Arabic, Persian, mathematics, astronomy and philosophy — he inherited an empire stretching across most of the Indian subcontinent, and is remembered for bold administrative experiments that contemporaries such as the chronicler Barani and the traveller Ibn Battuta, who served as his judge in Delhi, judged visionary in conception but ruinous in execution.

Experiments and their failure

Three projects dominate his reputation. First, the transfer of the capital (c. 1327) from Delhi to Devagiri in the Deccan, renamed Daulatabad, intended to secure the south and govern the empire from its centre: the court and much of Delhi's population were ordered to migrate some 1,500 kilometres, causing great suffering, and within a few years the move was reversed. Second, a token currency (c. 1330) of brass and copper tankas issued at face value equal to silver, which invited mass counterfeiting and was withdrawn at heavy cost to the treasury. Third, an ambitious increase in taxation in the fertile Doab, imposed during drought, which drove peasants to revolt.

Grand expeditions — against Khurasan and against the Himalayan kingdom of Qarachil — ended in disaster, and the vast empire began to fragment: Bengal, Madurai, Warangal and the Deccan broke away, the last giving rise, in 1347, to the Bahmani Sultanate. Muhammad spent his last years campaigning against rebels, dying in Sindh in 1351 while pursuing the rebel Taghi. Though often portrayed as a brilliant failure — "the wisest fool in Christendom's" Indian counterpart, in one historian's framing — modern scholarship credits him with serious attempts at imperial integration ahead of his time.
