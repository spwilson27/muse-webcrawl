B vitamins are a class of water-soluble vitamins that play important roles in cell metabolism and the synthesis of red blood cells. Though these vitamins share similar names, they are chemically distinct compounds that often coexist in the same foods. In general, dietary supplements containing all eight are referred to as a vitamin B complex. Individual B vitamins are referred to by their specific number or name, while "vitamin B" refers to the class as a whole.

Discovery

The B vitamins were once thought to be a single vitamin, referred to as vitamin B. Research showed that it was several distinct compounds, each given a number. Numbers B4, B8, B10, and B11 were assigned to substances later found not to be true vitamins, so those numbers were dropped. The key discovery was made by Casimir Funk, who in 1912 isolated thiamine and coined the term "vitamine".

Function

B vitamins act largely as coenzymes or coenzyme precursors. For example, thiamine is converted to thiamine pyrophosphate, a coenzyme in carbohydrate metabolism; riboflavin forms FAD and FMN; niacin forms NAD and NADP; and folate derivatives carry one-carbon units in the synthesis of DNA. Vitamin B12 is notable for containing the metal cobalt and for being absent from plant foods, making it a nutrient of concern in vegan diets.

Sources

B vitamins are found in whole grains, meat, eggs, dairy products, legumes, seeds, nuts, and dark leafy vegetables. Many breakfast cereals and flour products are fortified with B vitamins, particularly folic acid (synthetic folate), a measure that significantly reduced neural tube defects in countries that adopted it.
