The Discourse on Voluntary Servitude (French: Discours de la servitude volontaire) is an essay by Étienne de La Boétie, written when he was about eighteen years old, around 1548. It asks a deceptively simple question: how can one man, a tyrant, rule over millions who vastly outnumber him? La Boétie's answer is that tyranny rests not on the tyrant's strength but on the consent of the governed — servitude is, in a sense, voluntary.

Argument

La Boétie argues that people are born free and equal, but habit, custom and upbringing make subjection seem natural. The tyrant's power is sustained by a pyramid of beneficiaries — a handful who share his spoils, who in turn are supported by hundreds, and so down through society. His remedy is correspondingly simple: withdraw support. "Resolve to serve no more, and you are at once freed. I do not ask that you place hands upon the tyrant to topple him over, but simply that you support him no longer; then you will behold him, like a great Colossus whose pedestal has been pulled away, fall of his own weight and break in pieces."

Publication and influence

The essay circulated in manuscript among humanist circles. After La Boétie's early death in 1563, his friend Michel de Montaigne planned to publish it, but it first appeared in print in Huguenot pamphlet literature (1576–1577), pressed into service against the French monarchy. Rediscovered repeatedly ever since, it has been hailed as a founding text of civil disobedience and anarchist thought, influencing writers from the French revolutionaries to Tolstoy and Gandhi's intellectual milieu — a classic illustration of how a discourse can outlive its author by centuries.
