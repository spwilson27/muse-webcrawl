Sundaland is a biogeographical region of Southeast Asia corresponding to a larger landmass that was exposed throughout the last 2.6 million years during periods when sea level was lower. It includes the Malay Peninsula and the large islands of Borneo, Java, and Sumatra, together with the surrounding shallow seas — the Java Sea, the southern South China Sea, and the Strait of Malacca — that overlie the Sunda Shelf.

Formation and flooding

At the Last Glacial Maximum, about 20,000 years ago, sea level stood roughly 120 metres below present, and Sundaland formed a contiguous subcontinent crossed by great river systems that extended the courses of modern rivers such as the Kapuas and the Chao Phraya drainage. As ice sheets melted between about 19,000 and 7,000 years ago, the shelf flooded rapidly, isolating the islands. Researchers including Stephen Oppenheimer have speculated, controversially, that this flooding influenced regional flood myths and population dispersal into the Pacific.

Biogeography

Sundaland's fauna is characteristically Asian — tigers, rhinoceroses, orangutans, elephants, and sun bears — in contrast to the Australasian fauna east of the Wallace Line, which runs through the deep-water straits between Borneo and Sulawesi and between Bali and Lombok that remained flooded even at glacial maxima. The region's equatorial rainforests are among the world's most biodiverse and are recognized as the Sundaland biodiversity hotspot, heavily threatened by deforestation, much of it for oil palm plantations.

Human prehistory

Modern humans reached Sundaland by at least 50,000–70,000 years ago, and sites such as Niah Cave in Sarawak preserve evidence of early occupation. Fossil Homo erectus remains from Java (Java Man) date to well over a million years. During low-sea-level periods, Sundaland served as a corridor for migrations toward Sahul, the joined Australia–New Guinea landmass, which still required sea crossings from Sundaland's eastern shore.
