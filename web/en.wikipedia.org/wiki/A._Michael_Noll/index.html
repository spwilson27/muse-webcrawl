A. Michael Noll (born 1939) is an American engineer and professor who was one of the pioneers of digital computer art, 3D animation, and tactile communication. While a researcher at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the early 1960s, he produced some of the first artworks generated by a digital computer.

Early life and education

Noll was born in Newark, New Jersey. He earned a B.S. in electrical engineering from the Newark College of Engineering (now part of the New Jersey Institute of Technology), an M.S. from New York University, and a Ph.D. in electrical engineering from the Polytechnic Institute of Brooklyn.

Career

At Bell Labs, Noll investigated the aesthetic perception of patterns and the use of computers in the visual arts. In 1962 he programmed an IBM 7090 to produce pseudo-random artistic compositions; one of these works, Gaussian-Quadratic, was exhibited in 1965 at the Howard Wise Gallery in New York in one of the earliest exhibitions of computer art in the United States, alongside work by Béla Julesz. Because U.S. copyright law at the time did not clearly cover computer-generated works, Noll's Computer Composition with Lines (1964) became a test case in discussions of authorship and the computer.

Noll also created early computer-animated films, stereoscopic 3D displays, and a tactile man-machine communication system. He later served as Director of Technology Research at AT&T Consumer Products and as a science advisor in the White House Office of Science and Technology during the Nixon administration.

He subsequently became a professor at the Annenberg School for Communication at the University of Southern California, where he researched telecommunications policy and the social effects of new media, and served as Associate Dean. He has written extensively on the history of Bell Labs and on the future of communications.
