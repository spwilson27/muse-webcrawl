Sanitary engineering


Aeration basins at a wastewater treatment plant

Sanitary engineering, also known as public health engineering or environmental health engineering, is the application of engineering methods to improve the sanitation of human communities, principally by providing systems for the removal and disposal of human waste and the supply of safe drinking water. It is traditionally a branch of civil engineering and overlaps with environmental engineering and public health.

Scope

The discipline covers the design, construction and operation of:

  • Water supply systems — sourcing, treatment, storage and distribution of potable water;
  • Sewerage — collection networks conveying sewage and storm water;
  • Wastewater treatment — physical, chemical and biological processes that remove contaminants before discharge or reuse;
  • Solid waste management — collection, recycling, composting and landfilling of municipal refuse;
  • Drainage and flood control, plumbing within buildings, and control of disease-carrying vectors such as mosquitoes.

History

Organised sanitation is ancient: the cities of the Indus Valley had covered drains and wells, and Rome built the Cloaca Maxima and aqueducts. The modern discipline emerged in nineteenth-century Britain during the Industrial Revolution, when overcrowded cities suffered repeated cholera and typhoid epidemics. Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population led to the Public Health Act 1848, and engineers such as Joseph Bazalgette designed the London sewerage system after the Great Stink of 1858. John Snow's epidemiological work on cholera in 1854 demonstrated the water-borne transmission of disease, grounding sanitary engineering in germ theory.

Landmark works include the first slow sand filters, the development of the activated sludge process (1914) by Ardern and Lockett in Manchester, and the introduction of chlorination of water supplies in the early twentieth century, which sharply reduced water-borne disease.

Practice

Sanitary engineers estimate water demand and sewage flows, model pipe networks hydraulically, select treatment processes to meet effluent standards, and manage sludge disposal. Modern practice increasingly addresses resource recovery from wastewater (biogas, nutrients, reclaimed water), antimicrobial resistance, microplastics, and sanitation for the large share of the world's population still lacking safely managed services — the subject of the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goal 6.
