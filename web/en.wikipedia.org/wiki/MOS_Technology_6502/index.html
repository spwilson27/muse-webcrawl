MOS Technology 6502

The MOS Technology 6502 (typically pronounced "sixty-five-oh-two") is an 8-bit microprocessor that was designed by a small team led by Chuck Peddle for MOS Technology. The design team had formerly worked at Motorola on the Motorola 6800 project; the 6502 is essentially a simplified, cheaper and faster version of that design.

When it was introduced in 1975, the 6502 was the least expensive microprocessor on the market by a considerable margin. It initially sold for less than one-sixth the cost of competing designs from larger companies, such as the 6800 or Intel 8080. Its introduction caused rapid decreases in pricing across the entire processor market. Along with the Zilog Z80, it sparked a series of projects that resulted in the home computer revolution of the early 1980s.

History and use

Home computers using the 6502 or its variants included the Commodore PET and VIC-20, the Apple II series, the Atari 8-bit computers, the BBC Micro and the Ohio Scientific Challenger. Derivatives powered other landmark systems: the Commodore 64 used the 6510 variant, the Nintendo Entertainment System used the Ricoh 2A03, and the Atari Lynx and many arcade machines used 65C02-family chips. The design remains in production in the 21st century as the WDC 65C02 and W65C816S, used in embedded systems.

Description

The 6502 is a little-endian 8-bit processor with a 16-bit address bus, giving access to 64 KB of memory. It has one general-purpose accumulator register (A), two index registers (X and Y), a program counter, a stack pointer and a status register. Its instruction set contains 56 instructions using 13 addressing modes, encoded in 151 of the 256 possible opcodes. Unused opcodes were later exploited as "illegal opcodes" by programmers.

A distinctive feature is the use of the first page of memory, the "zero page", as a set of fast pseudo-registers: zero-page addressing takes fewer bytes and cycles than absolute addressing, and compilers and programmers arranged frequently used variables there.
