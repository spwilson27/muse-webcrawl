Nicolas Poussin (June 1594 – 19 November 1665) was the leading painter of the classical French Baroque style, although he spent most of his working life in Rome. His work is characterised by clarity, logic and order, favouring line over colour. His paintings dominate French and Italian art history and influenced generations of artists, from Jacques-Louis David to Cézanne, who aspired to "redo Poussin after nature".

Life

Born in Normandy, Poussin trained in Rouen and Paris before arriving in Rome in 1624, where the patronage of Cardinal Barberini and the antiquarian Cassiano dal Pozzo shaped his career. In 1640 he was summoned to Paris as First Painter to Louis XIII, but court intrigues drove him back to Rome in 1642, where he remained for the rest of his life.

Work

Poussin's mature paintings treat biblical, mythological and historical subjects with a stoic, philosophical gravity: Et in Arcadia Ego, The Birth of Adonis, The Plague of Ashdod, the two series of the Seven Sacraments, and his late, serene landscapes such as Landscape with Orpheus and Eurydice and The Seasons. He prepared compositions with wax figurines arranged in a box stage, and wrote letters — notably to his patron Chantelou — that remain foundational texts of art theory, including his theory of the modes.

Legacy

The 17th-century French Academy's "Poussiniste" party championed drawing and classical order against the colouristic "Rubénistes". Poussin's reputation, eclipsed during the Rococo, was revived by Neoclassicism and remains central to the French national tradition of painting.
