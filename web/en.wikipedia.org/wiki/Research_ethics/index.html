Research ethics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Research ethics is a discipline concerned with the moral principles governing the conduct of research, including the treatment of human and animal subjects, honesty in the design, conduct and reporting of studies, and the responsibilities of researchers to society. Modern research ethics frameworks emerged in the twentieth century in response to abuses in medical experimentation, and are now embedded in law, funding conditions, and institutional review procedures across most fields of science and the social sciences.

Key principles

The 1979 Belmont Report in the United States articulated three core principles for research involving human subjects:

  • Respect for persons — individuals should be treated as autonomous agents, and those with diminished autonomy are entitled to protection; this underlies the requirement of informed consent.
  • Beneficence — researchers should maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms.
  • Justice — the benefits and burdens of research should be distributed fairly.

Additional norms include scientific integrity (avoiding fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism), appropriate attribution of authorship, management of conflicts of interest, confidentiality of participants' data, and humane treatment and minimization of suffering in animal research, often summarized by the "3Rs": replacement, reduction, and refinement.

Historical development

The Nuremberg Code (1947), arising from the trials of Nazi physicians who conducted experiments on concentration camp prisoners, set out ten principles including voluntary consent. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964, and repeatedly revised) became the foundational international statement of ethics for medical research. Revelations about studies such as the Tuskegee syphilis study in the United States — in which treatment was withheld from African American men for decades — led to stronger regulation and the creation of institutional review boards (IRBs) and research ethics committees.

Research misconduct

Research misconduct is commonly defined as fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism in proposing, performing, reviewing, or reporting research. Notable cases, from Piltdown Man to modern large-scale fraud, have prompted journals and funders to adopt screening, data-sharing, and retraction policies. Ethical debates continue over emerging areas including human germline editing, dual-use research of concern, artificial intelligence, big-data research using social media, and research in low-income settings sponsored by wealthy institutions.
