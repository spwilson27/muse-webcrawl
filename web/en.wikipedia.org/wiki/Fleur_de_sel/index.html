Fleur de sel

TypeSea salt
OriginCoastal France (Guérande, Camargue, Île de Ré)
UseFinishing salt

Fleur de sel ("flower of salt" in French) is a hand-harvested sea salt that forms as a thin, delicate crust on the surface of salt evaporation ponds. It consists of fine, moist, flaky crystals rich in trace minerals, and is prized as a finishing salt sprinkled over dishes just before serving, rather than as a cooking salt.

Production

On sunny, windy afternoons, when conditions in the shallow clay ponds (œillets) of a salt marsh are right, tiny crystals form on the brine's surface like a film of flowers. Salt workers (paludiers) skim the crust off with a wooden rake before it sinks overnight, a laborious process yielding only a small fraction of the pond's total harvest; the denser, greyer salt that sinks is sold as coarse sel gris. The most famous sources are Guérande in Brittany — whose Fleur de sel de Guérande holds EU Protected Geographical Indication status — the Camargue, and the Île de Ré. Similar salts are produced in Portugal (flor de sal), Spain, and elsewhere.

Characteristics and use

Fleur de sel retains more moisture and a more complex mineral flavour than refined table salt, and its fragile flakes dissolve quickly, giving a clean burst of salinity. Because of its cost — often more than ten times that of ordinary sea salt — it is reserved for finishing: over salads, grilled meats, buttered radishes, eggs, and salted caramels and chocolates.
