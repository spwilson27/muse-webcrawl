Remote sensing in archaeology

Remote sensing in archaeology is the application of remote-sensing techniques — aerial photography, satellite imagery, LiDAR, and geophysical survey — to the discovery and study of archaeological sites without excavation. Buried features affect surface vegetation, soil colour and microtopography, producing cropmarks, soilmarks and shadow marks visible from above.

Methods

Aerial archaeology began in earnest after the First World War with pioneers such as O. G. S. Crawford. Satellite imagery, including declassified Cold War spy-satellite photographs, allows survey of large regions, while airborne LiDAR can strip away forest canopy digitally — famously revealing extensive Maya settlement in Guatemala and the city of Mahendraparvata at Angkor. Ground-based geophysics (magnetometry, ground-penetrating radar, resistivity) maps subsurface features at site scale. These methods are central to modern landscape archaeology and heritage management.
