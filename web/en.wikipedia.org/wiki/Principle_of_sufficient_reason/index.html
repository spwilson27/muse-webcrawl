Principle of sufficient reason

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz made the principle of sufficient reason one of the two great principles of his philosophy.

The principle of sufficient reason states that everything must have a reason or a cause. The principle is usually attributed to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who formulated it as one of the two fundamental principles of reasoning, along with the principle of non-contradiction: "there can be found no fact that is true or existent, no proposition which is true, unless there is a sufficient reason why it should be so and not otherwise, although these reasons are most often impossible for us to know."

Formulations

The principle has been expressed in many forms: that every entity has an explanation of its existence either in the necessity of its own nature or in an external cause; that every true proposition has an explanation of why it is true; and, in Alexander Pruss's formulation, every contingent fact has an explanation. Versions of the idea appear earlier in Anaximander, Parmenides, Archimedes, Cicero, Avicenna, and Thomas Aquinas's "third way". Leibniz used the principle to argue that God, as a necessary being, is the ultimate reason for the contingent world, and that this is the best of all possible worlds because a perfectly rational agent would always act on sufficient reason.

Schopenhauer's fourfold root

Arthur Schopenhauer devoted his doctoral dissertation, On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason (1813), to the principle, distinguishing four forms: sufficient reason of becoming (causality), of knowing (grounds of judgements), of being (mathematical and logical relations), and of acting (motivation).

Criticism

David Hume argued that we can conceive of things beginning to exist without a cause, and Immanuel Kant treated causal principles as conditions of experience rather than truths about things in themselves. In modern physics, the apparent indeterminacy of quantum mechanics has been taken by some as a counterexample—though defenders reply that probabilistic laws still provide explanations. The principle also raises the problem of infinite regress or brute facts: if everything requires an explanation, the explanation of the whole must either stop at a necessary being, continue infinitely, or rest on a brute fact the principle itself forbids.
