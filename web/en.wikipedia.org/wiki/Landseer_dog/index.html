The Landseer is a dog breed named after the artist Sir Edwin Landseer (1802–1873), whose paintings famously depicted black-and-white Newfoundland-type dogs — most memorably in A Distinguished Member of the Humane Society (1838), showing a dog that had saved lives from drowning. Two populations carry the name: in Britain and North America the black-and-white colour variety of the Newfoundland dog is simply called the Landseer, while in continental Europe the FCI recognises a separate breed, the Landseer (European Continental Type), developed in Germany and Switzerland from Landseer-coloured Newfoundlands in the early 20th century.

Characteristics

The continental Landseer is a large, powerful water dog, noticeably leggier and lighter in head than the modern Newfoundland, standing 67–80 cm at the withers. The coat is long, straight and dense with a water-resistant undercoat; the ground colour is white with distinct black patches, ideally a black saddle and a white blaze, the head predominantly black with a white muzzle. Webbed feet and a strong swimming stroke suit it to water work and water rescue, in which the breed is trained in several countries. The temperament is calm, gentle and people-oriented, like the Newfoundland's, with the breed historically used by fishermen to haul nets and rescue the drowning. Health concerns typical of giant breeds include hip dysplasia, bloat and cystinuria inherited from its Newfoundland ancestry.

History

The original black-and-white dogs came from the island of Newfoundland, where dogs of both colours worked with fishermen; in 19th-century England the black-and-white ("Landseer") variety was widely popular and painted, sculpted and bred before solid black became fashionable in the show ring. Continental breeders, led by German and Swiss fanciers, maintained the marked variety as a distinct type, formalised under FCI standard No. 226 in 1960.
