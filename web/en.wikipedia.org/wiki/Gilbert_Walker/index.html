Gilbert Walker

Sir Gilbert Thomas Walker CSI FRS (14 June 1868 – 4 November 1958) was a British physicist and statistician best known for his discovery of the Southern Oscillation, the east–west atmospheric seesaw over the tropical Pacific and Indian Oceans that underlies the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). The zonal Pacific circulation pattern associated with it is named the Walker circulation in his honour.

Career

Walker was educated at Whitgift School and St John's College, Cambridge, where he was Senior Wrangler in 1889. Health problems led him to seek a drier climate, and in 1904 he became director-general of observatories in India. Confronted with the problem of predicting the Indian monsoon — whose failures could mean famine — Walker undertook a vast statistical search for correlations between weather patterns around the globe, using data from stations worldwide.

The Southern Oscillation

From these studies, published mainly in the 1920s, Walker identified coherent global pressure oscillations, including the Southern Oscillation: when pressure is high in the eastern South Pacific it tends to be low over the Indian Ocean and Indonesia, and vice versa. He developed regression formulae for seasonal forecasting of monsoon rainfall — pioneering work in statistical weather prediction, though the forecasts had limited skill in his lifetime. The physical mechanism linking his oscillation to El Niño sea-surface temperature anomalies was clarified decades later by Jacob Bjerknes, who named the associated circulation the Walker circulation in 1969.

Other work

Walker also made contributions to the physics of boomerangs — he wrote on their aerodynamics and is credited with work relevant to their design — and studied bird flight, gliders, and the harmonograph. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1904, knighted in 1924, and after retiring from India taught meteorology at Imperial College London.
