The Chi Rho (☧) is one of the earliest Christograms, formed by superimposing the Greek letters chi (Χ) and rho (Ρ), the first two letters of Christos (ΧΡΙΣΤΟΣ, "Christ"). It became a central symbol of early Christianity and of the Roman Empire after its adoption by Constantine the Great.

Constantine and the Labarum

According to Lactantius and Eusebius, before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 AD, Constantine had a vision or dream instructing him to mark his soldiers' shields with the Chi Rho. His military standard bearing the symbol, the Labarum, became an emblem of imperial Christianity.

Usage

The Chi Rho appears on coins, sarcophagi, mosaics, and in illuminated manuscripts — famously at the opening of the Gospel of Matthew in the Book of Kells, where the Chi Rho page is a masterpiece of Insular art. It is still used in the liturgy and iconography of many churches, often combined with the Alpha and Omega.
