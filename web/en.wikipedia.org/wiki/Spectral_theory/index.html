Spectral theory

In mathematics, spectral theory is an inclusive term for theories extending the eigenvector and eigenvalue theory of a single square matrix to a much broader theory of the structure of operators in a variety of mathematical spaces. It is a theory with compelling applications in many areas, including linear systems of equations, ordinary and partial differential equations, integral equations, quantum mechanics, and signal processing.

Background

The name spectral theory was introduced by David Hilbert in his formulation of Hilbert space theory, in analogy with the spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix and, further afield, with the spectral lines of atomic spectra. The finite-dimensional spectral theorem says that a normal matrix can be diagonalised by a unitary transformation: it is a sum of projections onto its eigenspaces weighted by its eigenvalues. Spectral theory generalises this picture to infinite-dimensional operators, where the spectrum may include continuous parts and eigenvectors may need to be replaced by spectral measures and projections.

The spectrum of an operator

For a bounded linear operator T on a Banach space, the spectrum σ(T) is the set of complex numbers λ for which T − λI fails to have a bounded inverse. The spectrum is traditionally decomposed into:

  • the point spectrum — eigenvalues, where T − λI is not injective;
  • the continuous spectrum — where the operator is injective with dense but non-surjective range;
  • the residual spectrum — where the range is not dense (empty for self-adjoint operators).

Spectral theorem

The central result is the spectral theorem for self-adjoint (and more generally normal) operators, which represents such an operator as an integral over its spectrum with respect to a projection-valued measure, and provides a functional calculus: functions of the operator can be defined by applying the function to its spectral values. For compact self-adjoint operators the spectrum consists of eigenvalues accumulating only at zero, and the operator is diagonalisable in an orthonormal eigenbasis — a direct generalisation of the finite-dimensional theory, originating with Fredholm's theory of integral equations.

Unbounded operators

Much of mathematical physics involves unbounded operators, such as the position, momentum, and Hamiltonian operators of quantum mechanics defined on dense domains in Hilbert space. Their spectral theory, developed by von Neumann and others, distinguishes discrete spectra (bound states) from continuous spectra (scattering states), and underpins the rigorous formulation of quantum theory.
