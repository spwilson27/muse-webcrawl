Priming (psychology)

Priming: exposure to a stimulus influences the response to a subsequent stimulus.

In psychology, priming is a phenomenon whereby exposure to one stimulus influences a response to a subsequent stimulus, without conscious guidance or intention. For example, a person shown the word nurse will subsequently recognise the word doctor faster than an unrelated word. Priming is considered evidence of the operation of implicit memory and of spreading activation in semantic networks.

FieldCognitive psychology
Related conceptsImplicit memory, spreading activation, semantic network
Classic studyMeyer & Schvaneveldt (1971), lexical decision task

Types

  • Semantic priming: a prime speeds processing of a semantically related target (bread → butter).
  • Repetition priming: earlier presentation of the same item improves later identification or production.
  • Conceptual priming: based on shared meaning rather than form.
  • Perceptual priming: based on shared sensory form.
  • Negative priming: slowed response to a stimulus previously ignored.
  • Affective priming: evaluation is biased by the valence of a preceding stimulus.
  • Masked priming: the prime is presented so briefly or masked that it is not consciously perceived.

Behavioural ("social") priming

Studies in social psychology reported that priming concepts such as old age, money, or rudeness could alter walking speed, helpfulness, or performance. Several of these high-profile results failed to replicate in the 2010s — notably the "elderly priming" walking-speed study — and the subfield became a focal point of the replication crisis. Semantic and repetition priming, by contrast, are among the most robust effects in cognitive psychology.

Clinical relevance

Priming tasks are used to study amnesia: patients with damage to explicit memory systems may show intact priming, demonstrating a dissociation between implicit and explicit memory.
