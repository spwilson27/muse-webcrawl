Modern Hebrew (עברית חדשה, Ivrit Hadaša), also called Israeli Hebrew, is the standard form of the Hebrew language spoken today. Developed and spoken in Israel, it is the country's official language and is spoken by more than nine million people, including most Israeli Jews as a first language and many Arab citizens of Israel and diaspora communities as an additional language. Modern Hebrew arose from the revival of Hebrew as a spoken vernacular in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — the only large-scale successful example of a language with no native speakers being restored to everyday use, though scholars debate whether it is a direct continuation of earlier Hebrew or a new, hybrid language shaped by its revivers' European mother tongues.

History

Hebrew ceased to be a daily spoken language around the 3rd–5th centuries CE but survived for nearly two millennia as a language of liturgy, scholarship, law, commerce and literature (in its Biblical, Mishnaic and medieval strata). The revival began in earnest in the 1880s–1890s in Ottoman Palestine, driven by the Zionist movement. Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, who arrived in Jerusalem in 1881, is popularly credited as the revival's central figure: he raised his son as the first native Hebrew speaker of modern times, compiled a monumental dictionary, and coined thousands of words for modern concepts. Hebrew schools, newspapers and eventually the Hebrew University (1925) entrenched the language; in 1922 the British Mandate recognised Hebrew as an official language of Palestine, and in 1948 it became an official language of the State of Israel. Arabic was an official language alongside it until the 2018 Nation-State Law gave Arabic a "special status" and affirmed Hebrew alone as the state language.

Classification debate

Linguists debate the genetic status of Modern Hebrew. The traditional view treats it as the continuation of ancient Hebrew. The linguist Paul Wexler argued it is a relexified Slavic language — essentially Yiddish with Hebrew vocabulary. Ghil'ad Zuckermann proposes that "Israeli" is a hybrid, Semito-European language, in which Hebrew is one major contributor alongside Yiddish and other languages of the revivers. Most scholars accept that substrate influence from Yiddish, Russian, Polish, German, Ladino and Arabic substantially shaped its phonology and syntax, whatever label is preferred.

Phonology and grammar

Modern Hebrew has five vowel phonemes and a consonant inventory that (in mainstream Israeli pronunciation) merges several Biblical distinctions; the pharyngeal consonants are retained mainly by Mizrahi speakers. Grammatically, the language retains the Semitic root-and-pattern morphology (e.g. the binyanim verb system) but has developed analytic features: word order is predominantly subject–verb–object (unlike the verb-initial order of Biblical Hebrew), possessive and tense systems were restructured, and the lexicon has absorbed many loanwords from Arabic, English, Russian and other languages. New vocabulary is officially coined by the Academy of the Hebrew Language (founded 1953, successor to Ben-Yehuda's Language Committee), though many coinages compete with popular borrowings.
