Electrolytic cell

Electrolytic cell
Process: electrolysis
Anode: positive (oxidation)
Cathode: negative (reduction)
Power: external DC source

An electrolytic cell is an electrochemical cell that uses electrical energy from an external source to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction, a process known as electrolysis. It consists of two electrodes immersed in an electrolyte, connected to a direct-current power supply.

Operation

The power supply forces electrons into the cathode, where reduction occurs, and draws them from the anode, where oxidation occurs. Unlike a galvanic cell, in an electrolytic cell the anode is positive and the cathode negative. The minimum voltage required is set by the decomposition potential of the electrolyte.

Applications

Electrolytic cells are used to extract reactive metals such as aluminium (Hall–Héroult process) and sodium, to produce chlorine and caustic soda from brine (chlor-alkali process), to electroplate metals, and to split water into hydrogen and oxygen.

Faraday's laws

The amount of substance produced at each electrode is proportional to the total electric charge passed through the cell, as stated by Faraday's laws of electrolysis.
