Close reading of the literary text itself is the hallmark of New Criticism.

New Criticism was a formalist movement in literary theory that dominated American literary criticism in the middle decades of the 20th century. It emphasized close reading, particularly of poetry, to discover how a work of literature functions as a self-contained, self-referential aesthetic object, independent of the author's intentions or the reader's emotional responses.

The movement derived its name from John Crowe Ransom's 1941 book The New Criticism. Its roots lay in the work of British critics I. A. Richards (Practical Criticism, 1929) and William Empson (Seven Types of Ambiguity, 1930), and in the essays of T. S. Eliot, who argued that a poem should be understood as an impersonal "verbal icon" rather than an expression of its author's personality.

Principles

New Critics treated the text as an autonomous object. Meaning was to be found in the work's internal tensions, ironies, paradoxes, and ambiguities, resolved through its formal unity. Cleanth Brooks, in The Well Wrought Urn (1947), argued that the meaning of a poem cannot be separated from its form, denouncing the reduction of a poem to a paraphrasable statement as the "heresy of paraphrase."

Two famous essays by W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley codified central doctrines: "The Intentional Fallacy" (1946) held that an author's intended meaning is neither available nor desirable as a standard for judging a work, and "The Affective Fallacy" (1949) held that a work's emotional effect on the reader is not a valid basis for interpretation.

Influence and decline

New Criticism transformed the teaching of literature in American universities, making close reading of short poems and stories the standard classroom practice through textbooks such as Brooks and Robert Penn Warren's Understanding Poetry (1938). From the 1960s onward it was challenged by reader-response criticism, structuralism, deconstruction, and historically oriented approaches such as New Historicism, which accused it of ignoring history, biography, and social context. Nevertheless, close reading remains a foundational skill in literary study.
