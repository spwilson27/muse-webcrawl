Tasting menu

A tasting menu is a meal format, offered by many fine-dining restaurants, in which the kitchen serves a fixed sequence of numerous small courses chosen by the chef rather than by the diner. Known in French as a menu dégustation, it is designed to display the kitchen's range, technique and seasonal ingredients in a single sitting, usually with an optional accompanying sequence of wines (a wine pairing or accord mets et vins).

Format

Tasting menus typically run from five to more than twenty courses, progressing from light snacks and raw preparations through fish and meat to cheese and desserts, often with signature extras such as an amuse-bouche, palate cleansers and petit fours. Portions are small but the meal commonly lasts two to four hours. Many restaurants require the menu to be taken by the whole table, and leading establishments — from traditional French houses to the modernist kitchens of Spain, Denmark and Japan — have made the format their only offering, abolishing the à la carte menu altogether.

History

The idea descends from the sequential service of classical French haute cuisine and from multi-course banquet traditions such as Japanese kaiseki. The modern tasting menu was popularised from the 1970s onward by nouvelle cuisine and by chefs such as Ferran Adrià at elBulli, whose menus of 30 or more small "snacks" and dishes in the 1990s and 2000s made the long tasting format a hallmark of avant-garde restaurants such as Noma, The Fat Duck and Alinea.

Variants and criticism

Variants include shorter "prix fixe" menus, chef's counter formats such as the Japanese omakase ("I leave it to you"), vegetarian tastings, and lunch menus offered as a more accessible introduction. Critics have at times attacked long tasting menus as expensive, controlling or exhausting rituals, prompting some prominent chefs to shorten or abandon them in favour of more flexible formats.
