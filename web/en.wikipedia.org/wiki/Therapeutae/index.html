The Therapeutae (singular Therapeutēs, feminine Therapeutris; from Greek θεραπευτής, "servant", "attendant", or "one who heals") were a Jewish sect or monastic-like community that flourished in the area around Alexandria in Egypt during the late Second Temple period. They are known almost entirely from the treatise De vita contemplativa ("On the Contemplative Life") by the Hellenistic Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria, written in the first half of the 1st century CE.

Philo describes the Therapeutae as philosophers who devoted themselves to contemplation of God, prayer, and the allegorical interpretation of scripture, abandoning their property and families to live in individual dwellings near Lake Mareotis. Their name, Philo suggests, derived either from their professed ability to heal the soul or from their service and worship of the divine.

Description in Philo

According to Philo, the community included both men and women, the latter mostly elderly virgins. Each member lived in a simple individual house containing a sacred room or sanctuary (semneion or monastērion) where they studied the Torah, the writings of the prophets, hymns, and psalms from dawn until dusk. They ate only after sunset, taking simple food of bread, salt, and hyssop, and drank only water. Some fasted for several days at a time.

On the seventh day the community gathered in a common sanctuary, separated by a partition between men and women, to hear an elder expound the scriptures, usually through allegorical interpretation. Every seven weeks they held an all-night festival: after a frugal banquet they sang and danced in two choirs, men's and women's, which later merged into a single choir — a re-enactment, Philo says, of the song of Moses and Miriam after the crossing of the Red Sea (Exodus 15).

Interpretations and debates

Scholars have long debated the relationship between the Therapeutae and the Essenes, another ascetic Jewish group described by Philo, Josephus, and the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Therapeutae are often seen as a contemplative counterpart to the more communally organised and agricultural Essenes, and some have proposed that the two groups were related branches of a wider movement. Unlike the Essenes, however, the Therapeutae are not mentioned by Josephus or Pliny, and no archaeological site has been securely identified with them.

In late antiquity, Christian writers such as Eusebius of Caesarea mistakenly (or tendentiously) identified the Therapeutae as early Christian monks, reading Philo's account as evidence of apostolic-era monasticism in Egypt. This identification influenced the later reception of the group, and the authenticity of De vita contemplativa itself was questioned by some modern critics, though most scholars today accept it as a genuine work of Philo describing a real community.
