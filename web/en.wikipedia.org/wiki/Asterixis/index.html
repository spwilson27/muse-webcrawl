Asterixis

Asterixis (also called the flapping tremor, or liver flap) is a tremor of the hand when the wrist is extended, sometimes said to resemble a bird flapping its wings. This motor disorder is characterized by an inability to maintain a position, which is demonstrated by jerking movements of the outstretched hands when bent upward at the wrist.

SpecialtyNeurology, hepatology
SymptomsBrief, arrhythmic lapses of sustained posture, especially of the dorsiflexed hand
CausesMetabolic encephalopathies, e.g. hepatic encephalopathy, hypercapnia, uraemia

Clinical features

Asterixis is not a true tremor but a form of negative myoclonus: a brief, involuntary loss of muscle contraction in tonically active muscles, followed by a corrective jerk as posture is regained. It is classically elicited by asking the patient to extend the arms and dorsiflex the wrists with the fingers spread; after a short latency the hands fall forwards in an irregular flapping motion. It can also be demonstrated at other sites, including the tongue, feet and eyelids.

Causes

Asterixis is a sign of encephalopathy due to disturbed metabolism. It is most famously associated with hepatic encephalopathy in decompensated liver disease, hence the term "liver flap", but it also occurs in respiratory failure with carbon dioxide retention (hypercapnia), kidney failure (uraemia), and with certain drugs such as anticonvulsants. Unilateral asterixis can occur with structural lesions of the brain, such as thalamic stroke.

History

The phenomenon was described by Raymond Adams and Joseph Foley in 1949 in patients with liver failure; the name derives from the Greek a- ("not") and sterixis ("fixed position").

Mechanism

The lapses of posture are thought to arise from dysfunction of the diencephalic motor centres that maintain tonic muscle activity, which are exquisitely sensitive to metabolic disturbance. Surface electromyography shows sudden silent periods in the contracting muscles corresponding to the flap.
