The Maxim gun was the first fully automatic machine gun, invented by the American-born British inventor Hiram Stevens Maxim in 1884. Instead of requiring an operator to work a crank or lever, the Maxim used the energy of the recoil from each shot to eject the spent cartridge, load the next round and fire again, sustaining fire for as long as the trigger was held and ammunition remained. It was adopted by armies around the world and became one of the defining weapons of the colonial era and the First World War.

Design

The Maxim fired rifle-calibre ammunition from a fabric belt, typically 250 rounds. Its barrel was surrounded by a water jacket holding roughly four litres of water to absorb the heat of sustained firing; after prolonged fire the water would boil and the steam was vented or condensed through a tube into a can. The gun was heavy — around 27 kg for the gun alone, plus tripod and water — and was served by a crew of four to six. Its mechanism, a recoil-operated toggle lock, became the basis for the German MG 08 and the British Vickers machine gun, both essentially Maxim variants.

History and use

Demonstrated to European royalty and general staffs in the 1880s, the Maxim was taken up by the British Army and colonial forces and proved decisive in numerous colonial engagements — most famously at the Battle of the Shangani (1893) and Omdurman (1898). Hilaire Belloc's line captured its reputation: "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not." Every major belligerent in the First World War fielded a Maxim derivative; the Russian Pulemyot Maksima (PM1910), with its wheeled mount and shield, served through the Second World War and beyond. In British service the improved Vickers version remained standard until 1968.

Legacy

The Maxim gun transformed infantry tactics: a single gun could deliver the firepower of a company of riflemen, defensive fire dominated the battlefields of 1914–18, and the word "Maxim" became generic for machine guns in several languages. Its derivatives remained in service in some countries well into the late 20th century.
