The nasal septum is the wall that separates the left and right airways of the nasal cavity, dividing the two nostrils. It is composed of bone posteriorly and cartilage anteriorly, and is covered on each side by a mucous membrane. The fleshy external end of the septum is called the columella.

Structure

The bony part of the septum consists of the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone above and the vomer below, with contributions from the nasal crests of the maxilla and palatine bones. The anterior portion is formed by the septal (quadrangular) cartilage. The septum receives blood from the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries, the sphenopalatine artery, the greater palatine artery, and the septal branch of the superior labial artery; these anastomose at Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior septum, the most common site of nosebleeds (epistaxis).

Clinical significance

A deviated septum is a displacement of the septal wall to one side, found in some degree in a large proportion of adults. Severe deviation can obstruct airflow and contribute to sinusitis, and may be corrected surgically by septoplasty. Septal perforation can result from trauma, surgery, infection, or chronic intranasal drug use. Septal haematoma following injury requires prompt drainage to prevent cartilage necrosis.

Development

The septum develops from the frontonasal prominence, growing downward to meet the palate; fusion defects of the surrounding structures are associated with cleft lip and palate.
