Douglas Hartree

Douglas Rayner Hartree (27 March 1897 – 12 February 1958) was an English mathematician and physicist most famous for the development of numerical analysis and its application to the equations of quantum mechanics, in particular the Hartree–Fock method for approximating the wave functions of many-electron atoms. The atomic unit of energy, the hartree, is named in his honour.

Life and work

Born in Cambridge, Hartree studied at St John's College, Cambridge, where his studies were interrupted by the First World War, during which he worked on anti-aircraft gunnery calculations. After the war he completed his degree and a PhD, and in the 1920s, following a visit from Niels Bohr, he began applying numerical methods to solve the Schrödinger equation for atoms, introducing the self-consistent field approach in 1928. Vladimir Fock later extended the method to include exchange effects, giving the Hartree–Fock equations.

Computing

Hartree was a pioneer of mechanical and electronic computation. He promoted the use of differential analysers, built a Meccano model of one that inspired the Manchester machine, and after the Second World War he championed the development of computers in Britain, advising on the use of early machines such as EDSAC for scientific problems. He held chairs at Manchester and Cambridge, becoming Plummer Professor of Mathematical Physics at Cambridge in 1946.
