Late veneer

Schematic of the late veneer delivered to Earth after core formation

In planetary science, the late veneer is a thin layer of material, rich in highly siderophile elements, that was accreted to Earth (and probably to the other terrestrial planets) after the planet's core had formed. Because siderophile ("iron-loving") elements partition strongly into metal, they should have been almost completely removed from Earth's silicate mantle during core formation; yet the mantle contains abundances of elements such as gold, platinum, and iridium that are too high to be explained that way. The late veneer hypothesis holds that these elements were delivered later by impacts of chondritic planetesimals after the core had closed.

Evidence

The relative abundances of highly siderophile elements in Earth's mantle occur in roughly chondritic proportions, suggesting a common late-accreted source resembling primitive meteorites. Estimates of the total mass of the late veneer are around 0.3–0.8 percent of Earth's mass, roughly consistent with the amount required to explain the observed abundances. Similar signatures have been reported for the Moon and Mars, although the Moon's much smaller veneer relative to its size has been debated.

Timing

The late veneer was delivered near the end of planetary accretion, broadly overlapping in time with the Late Heavy Bombardment around 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago. Material delivered by the late veneer may also have contributed volatiles, including water and organic compounds, to the early Earth, with implications for the origin of life.
