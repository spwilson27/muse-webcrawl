United States Federal Sentencing Guidelines

The United States Federal Sentencing Guidelines are policies issued by the United States Sentencing Commission that advise judges on sentences for individuals and organizations convicted of federal crimes. First effective in 1987, they aimed to reduce unwarranted sentencing disparities.

Sentencing Guidelines
Issued by: U.S. Sentencing Commission
Effective: November 1, 1987
Status: Advisory since United States v. Booker (2005)

How they work

A calculation combines an offense level (based on the crime and specific characteristics such as loss amount or drug quantity) with a criminal-history category, yielding a recommended range in months on the sentencing table. Enhancements, reductions for acceptance of responsibility, and departures can move the result.

History

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 created the Commission. In United States v. Booker (2005) the Supreme Court held the mandatory system violated the Sixth Amendment and rendered the Guidelines advisory; judges must still calculate and consider them.

Criticism

Critics cite severity for drug and fraud offenses, past disparities between crack and powder cocaine (reduced by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010), and complexity.
