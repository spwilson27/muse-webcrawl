Archaeological excavation
Field: Archaeology
Key concepts: Stratigraphy, context, Harris matrix
Purpose: Recovery and recording of archaeological remains

Excavation is the exposure, processing and recording of archaeological remains. An archaeological excavation is a destructive process: once a deposit has been dug, its original arrangement cannot be restored, so excavation places a heavy emphasis on meticulous recording. In modern practice, excavations range from small evaluation trenches to the total investigation of large sites threatened by development.

Principles

The fundamental principle of excavation is stratigraphy: deposits accumulate in layers, with earlier material generally lying below later material. Excavators remove deposits in reverse chronological order, one context at a time, recording each layer, cut and fill by written description, photography, scaled plans and sections. Relationships between contexts can be summarised in a Harris matrix, a diagram of the stratigraphic sequence.

Methods

Sites are commonly divided into a grid so that finds can be precisely located. Topsoil may be removed by machine under supervision, after which archaeologists work by hand with trowels, hand picks and brushes. Spoil is often sieved to recover small artefacts and environmental evidence such as seeds and animal bone; flotation is used to recover charred plant remains.

Types of excavation

Research excavations are undertaken to answer academic questions, while rescue or salvage archaeology, now the majority of fieldwork in many countries, investigates sites ahead of construction. Trial trenching and watching briefs assess the significance of remains before decisions about full excavation are taken.
