Virtual power plant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ComponentsDistributed energy resources: rooftop solar, batteries, EVs, flexible loads
FunctionsEnergy trading, frequency control, peak shaving, grid balancing
Related conceptsSmart grid, demand response, microgrid

A virtual power plant (VPP) is a system that integrates multiple, possibly heterogeneous, power sources to provide a reliable overall power supply, and to participate in energy markets as if it were a single conventional power station. The sources are often a collection of distributed energy resources (DERs)—rooftop solar photovoltaic systems, home and grid batteries, electric vehicles, heat pumps, and controllable loads—linked and dispatched by central control software. Because no single physical plant exists, the aggregation is "virtual."

Operation

A VPP's control platform forecasts generation and demand, monitors the state of charge and availability of each asset, and dispatches them in coordination: discharging batteries during peak prices or grid stress, curtailing or shifting flexible consumption, and aggregating small resources to meet minimum bid sizes in wholesale energy, capacity, and ancillary-services markets such as frequency regulation. Participants—households or businesses—typically retain override control of their devices and are paid for the grid services their assets provide.

Examples

Notable VPPs include the South Australian Virtual Power Plant, launched with Tesla from 2018, which networks Powerwall batteries in public and private housing and was credited with rapid frequency responses during grid events; Next Kraftwerke in Germany, one of Europe's largest VPP operators, aggregating thousands of renewable generators and flexible consumers; and utility programs in the United States run with platforms such as those of Enel X, Sunrun, and Tesla, which aggregate home batteries in states including California, Massachusetts, and Vermont (Green Mountain Power's pioneering battery program).

Benefits and challenges

Advocates argue VPPs defer investment in peaking plants and grid upgrades, integrate variable renewables, and improve resilience by distributing capacity. Challenges include interoperability standards, cybersecurity of networked devices, market rules that were written for large centralized generators, consumer trust and data privacy, and ensuring that participation benefits renters and low-income households as well as homeowners.
