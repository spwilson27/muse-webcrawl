Northern Black Polished Ware

Periodc. 700–200 BCE
RegionIndian subcontinent (Indo-Gangetic Plain and beyond)
Associated culturesMahajanapadas, Maurya Empire
Preceded byPainted Grey Ware

Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) is a fine, glossy black pottery of ancient India, produced roughly between 700 and 200 BCE. It is the characteristic luxury ceramic of the period of the "second urbanisation" of the Ganges plain, associated with the rise of the mahajanapada states and later the Maurya Empire. Archaeologists use it as a horizon marker: its presence dates a site to this broad period.

Characteristics

NBPW is a highly polished, thin-walled ware, most often in black but also occurring in deep blue, purple, pink, orange, brown and gold. It was made from very finely levigated clay and fired to produce a hard, lustrous surface, sometimes described as having a metallic sheen. Common shapes are bowls and dishes, evidently tableware for an elite. The firing technique remains debated; the gloss may result from a special slip, dressing with organic material, or high-temperature reducing firing.

Distribution

The ware originated in the middle Ganges plain and spread across northern India, from Taxila in the north-west to sites in Bengal, and south into the Deccan. Its period coincides with the spread of Buddhism and Jainism, the growth of cities such as Pataliputra, Varanasi and Kaushambi, and the consolidation of the Mauryan state under Ashoka.

Chronology

Scholars divide NBPW into two phases: an earlier phase (c. 700–400 BCE) with bichrome painting, and a later, monochrome luxury phase (c. 400–200 BCE) of the Mauryan period. It declines after about 200 BCE, succeeded by the red wares of the early historic period.
