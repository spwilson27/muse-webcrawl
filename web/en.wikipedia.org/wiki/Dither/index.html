Dither

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dither is an intentionally applied form of noise used to randomise quantization error, preventing large-scale patterns such as colour banding in images. Dither is routinely used in processing of both digital audio and video data, and is often one of the last stages of mastering audio to CD. The word comes from an old English verb meaning "to tremble"; early mechanical engineers and gunners noticed that machines and weapons worked more smoothly with a slight vibration present.

Digital audio

When an audio signal is converted to a lower bit depth (for example, from a 24-bit studio master to 16-bit CD audio), the resulting quantisation error is correlated with the signal and is heard as distortion. Adding a small amount of random noise — about the size of the least significant bit — before quantisation decorrelates the error, turning objectionable distortion into benign, steady hiss. Common noise-shaping filters, including triangular probability density function (TPDF) dither, push the noise energy into frequency ranges where the ear is less sensitive.

Images

In image processing, dithering approximates colours or grey levels that a limited palette cannot represent by interleaving pixels of available colours. Error-diffusion algorithms such as Floyd–Steinberg dithering spread each pixel's quantisation error to its neighbours, producing fine-grained patterns the eye integrates into smooth tones; ordered dithering uses a fixed threshold matrix (such as a Bayer matrix) and is faster and more parallelisable. Dithering was ubiquitous in the era of 256-colour displays and GIF images, and remains used in printing (halftoning), e-ink displays, and retro graphics.

Other uses

Analog-to-digital converters use dither to linearise conversion and resolve signals smaller than one least significant bit. In control systems and seismology, added vibration ("dither") reduces static friction and stiction. Stochastic resonance — in which noise improves detection of weak signals — is a related phenomenon.
