The Shiji (史記, "Records of the Grand Historian") is a monumental history of China and its surrounding peoples, spanning from the mythical Yellow Emperor to the reign of Emperor Wu of Han (c. 91 BC), written by the Han-dynasty historian Sima Qian. It is the first of the Twenty-Four Histories and established the form of Chinese dynastic history for the next two thousand years, as well as becoming a classic of Chinese literary prose.

Composition

The work was begun by Sima's father, Sima Tan, Grand Historian (Taishiling) at the Han court, and continued by Sima Qian after his father's death in 110 BC. Sima Qian travelled widely to gather traditions, interviewed witnesses, and drew on the palace archives. In 99 BC he offended Emperor Wu by defending the defeated general Li Ling; given the choice of execution or castration, he chose castration so that he could finish the history — explaining in his famous letter to Ren An that he endured the disgrace to complete a work that would "explore the boundary between Heaven and man, and master the changes of past and present."

Structure

The Shiji pioneered a composite, biographical format in five sections: 12 Basic Annals (the chronicles of rulers and dynasties), 10 Tables (chronological charts), 8 Treatises (on rites, music, the calendar, astronomy, economics and other institutions), 30 Hereditary Houses (the great feudal families and figures such as Confucius), and 70 Biographies of individuals and peoples — merchants, assassins, generals, scholars and foreign nations among them. Events are thus told from multiple angles, and the author's own judgements close many chapters with the formula "The Grand Historian remarks."

Legacy

Nearly every later Chinese dynasty produced an official history in the Shiji's mould, and its vivid biographies — of Li Si, Xiang Yu, Han Xin and many others — remain the principal source for early Chinese history and a benchmark of narrative art. Its account of episodes such as the burning of books under the Qin is both indispensable and, as Han-era testimony about a fallen dynasty, read critically by modern historians.
