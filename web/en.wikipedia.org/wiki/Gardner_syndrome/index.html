Gardner syndrome

Gardner syndrome is a subtype of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP). Gardner syndrome is an autosomal dominant form of polyposis characterized by the presence of multiple polyps in the colon together with tumors outside the colon. The extracolonic tumors may include osteomas of the skull, thyroid cancer, epidermoid cysts, fibromas, and desmoid tumors, as well as congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium.

Genetics

Gardner syndrome is caused by mutations in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene, a tumor suppressor gene located on chromosome 5q21. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, so each child of an affected person has a 50% chance of inheriting the mutation.

Signs and symptoms

  • Hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps of the colon, which almost invariably progress to colorectal cancer if untreated.
  • Osteomas (benign bone tumors), especially of the skull and jaw.
  • Soft tissue tumors, including epidermoid cysts, fibromas, lipomas, and desmoid tumors.
  • Dental abnormalities, including extra (supernumerary) teeth and odontomas.
  • Congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE).
  • Increased risk of thyroid cancer and other malignancies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on clinical findings and genetic testing for APC mutations. Management includes regular colonoscopic surveillance beginning in adolescence, and preventive colectomy is usually recommended once numerous polyps develop. Extracolonic manifestations are monitored and treated as they arise. The syndrome is named after geneticist Eldon J. Gardner, who first described it in 1951.
