Tonsil

The tonsils are collections of lymphoid tissue located in the pharynx that form part of the immune system. Together — the palatine tonsils, adenoids (pharyngeal tonsil), tubal tonsils and lingual tonsils — they constitute Waldeyer's tonsillar ring, stationed at the entrance to the digestive and respiratory tracts where they sample inhaled and ingested antigens.

Types

The palatine tonsils, usually meant by "the tonsils", sit in the tonsillar fossa on either side of the oropharynx between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches. Their surface is pitted by crypts that increase contact area with antigens. The adenoid lies on the roof of the nasopharynx; the lingual tonsils stud the posterior third of the tongue; the tubal tonsils surround the openings of the Eustachian tubes.

Function

Tonsils contain B and T lymphocytes and M cells that transport antigens to underlying lymphoid follicles, initiating antibody production — particularly secretory IgA — early in life. They are most immunologically active in childhood and tend to involute after puberty.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the palatine tonsils is tonsillitis, usually viral or due to group A Streptococcus. Complications include peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) and, rarely, rheumatic fever. Enlarged tonsils and adenoids are a leading cause of obstructive sleep apnoea in children. Surgical removal (tonsillectomy, often with adenoidectomy) is among the most common operations in childhood, though indications have narrowed to recurrent severe infection or airway obstruction.
