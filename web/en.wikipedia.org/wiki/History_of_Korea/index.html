History of Korea

The history of Korea begins with the legendary founding of Gojoseon and proceeds through the Three Kingdoms, the unified dynasties of Silla, Goryeo, and Joseon, Japanese colonial rule, and the division of the peninsula in 1945 into North and South Korea, which persists today.

Early kingdoms

According to tradition, Dangun founded Gojoseon in 2333 BC. By the first centuries AD the peninsula and Manchuria were divided among the Three Kingdoms — Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla — alongside the Gaya confederacy. Buddhism and Chinese writing, government, and technology spread through the peninsula. Silla, allied with Tang China, unified most of the peninsula in 668; the northern kingdom of Balhae succeeded Goguryeo in Manchuria.

Goryeo and Joseon

Wang Geon founded Goryeo in 918, giving Korea its English name. Goryeo is famed for celadon pottery, the Tripitaka Koreana carved on some 80,000 wooden blocks, and early movable metal type printing. It endured Mongol invasions in the 13th century. In 1392 Yi Seong-gye founded the Joseon dynasty, a Neo-Confucian kingdom that lasted over five centuries. King Sejong the Great commissioned the Hangul alphabet (promulgated 1446). Joseon repelled Japanese invasions in 1592–98 under Admiral Yi Sun-sin, famed for his "turtle ships."

Colonial rule and division

Japan annexed Korea in 1910. Colonial rule (1910–1945) combined economic exploitation with cultural suppression and provoked resistance, notably the March First Movement of 1919. Liberation in 1945 was followed by division at the 38th parallel between Soviet and American occupation zones. The Korean War (1950–53) entrenched the split between the communist North (DPRK) and the South (ROK), ending in an armistice rather than a peace treaty.

Modern Korea

South Korea industrialised rapidly under authoritarian governments and democratised after 1987, becoming a major global economy and cultural exporter. North Korea under the Kim dynasty developed nuclear weapons and remains one of the world's most closed states.
