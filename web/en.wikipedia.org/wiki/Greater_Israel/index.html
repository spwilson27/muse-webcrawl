Greater Israel (Hebrew: ארץ ישראל השלמה, Eretz Yisrael Hashlema) is an expression with several different biblical and political meanings over time. It is often used in an irredentist fashion to refer to the historic or desired borders of Israel. The term is used both by religious Zionists who ground Jewish sovereignty in the biblical Promised Land and, in varying forms, by political movements favouring permanent Israeli control of territories captured in 1967.

Meanings

In the Bible, the boundaries of the Promised Land are described in several places, most expansively in Genesis 15:18 — "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" — and more modestly in Numbers 34, from the Mediterranean to the Jordan and beyond. Historically the term's political use focused on the maximal territory of the ancient Israelite kingdoms. In contemporary political discourse it most often means Israeli sovereignty over the territory between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea, sometimes including the Gaza Strip; in Arab and critical discourse the maximal Nile-to-Euphrates reading is frequently cited.

Political history

The idea informed Revisionist Zionism — Ze'ev Jabotinsky's movement claimed both banks of the Jordan. After the Six-Day War of 1967, the Movement for Greater Israel advocateded retaining the captured territories; its ideas fed into the settlement movement Gush Emunim (founded 1974) and the Likud party, whose 1977 platform declared that between the sea and the Jordan there would be only Israeli sovereignty. The Oslo Accords, the 2005 Gaza disengagement, and two-state proposals have been opposed by adherents of the concept, which remains a live theme in Israeli politics and in debates over annexation of parts of the West Bank.
