Abu Simbel temples

Abu Simbel
Location: Nubia, southern Egypt
Builder: Ramesses II (19th Dynasty)
Built: c. 1264–1244 BC
Type: Rock-cut temples
UNESCO World Heritage Site (1979)
Relocated: 1964–1968

The Abu Simbel temples are two massive rock-cut temples in Nubia, southern Egypt, built by Pharaoh Ramesses II in the 13th century BC as a lasting monument to himself and his queen Nefertari, and to commemorate his victory at the Battle of Kadesh.

The Great Temple

The Great Temple, dedicated to Amun, Ra-Horakhty, Ptah, and the deified Ramesses, is fronted by four colossal seated statues of the king, each about 20 metres tall. Twice a year, around 22 February and 22 October, sunlight penetrates the sanctuary to illuminate statues of the gods inside — traditionally linked to Ramesses' birthday and coronation.

The Small Temple

The Small Temple is dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Nefertari, whose statues stand equal in size to the king's — a rare honour in Egyptian art.

Relocation

Threatened by flooding from the Aswan High Dam's reservoir, Lake Nasser, the temples were dismantled and reassembled 65 metres higher and about 200 metres back from the river in an UNESCO-led international campaign (1964–1968), in which the temples were cut into large blocks and moved — one of the greatest feats of archaeological engineering.

Discovery

Long buried in sand, the site was noted by Johann Ludwig Burckhardt in 1813 and cleared by Giovanni Belzoni in 1817.
