The Mongol conquest of Khwarezmia (1219–1221) was the military campaign by which the Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan overthrew the Khwarezmian Empire, a large Muslim state ruling over much of Central Asia and Persia. The invasion, provoked by the Khwarezmian governor of Otrar's seizure of a Mongol caravan and Shah Muhammad II's execution of Mongol envoys, resulted in the near-total destruction of the empire within two years and is remembered as one of the most devastating campaigns of the medieval world.

Background

In 1218 the Khwarezmshah Ala ad-Din Muhammad II ruled an empire stretching from Iraq to Transoxiana, with major cities including Samarkand, Bukhara, Urgench and Merv. Genghis Khan, having unified the Mongol tribes and conquered Western Xia and the Jin dynasty in northern China, sought trade relations with Khwarezm. He sent a large caravan of merchants to Otrar, whose governor, Inalchuq, accused them of espionage, seized their goods and had them executed. When Genghis Khan's ambassadors demanded redress, Muhammad II had one envoy killed and the others humiliated, an insult that made war inevitable.

The invasion

In the autumn of 1219 Genghis Khan advanced with an army modern estimates place between 100,000 and 150,000 men, including Chinese siege engineers. Rather than concentrate his larger forces, Muhammad II dispersed them as garrisons in his cities, allowing the Mongols to defeat them in detail. The border city of Otrar was besieged first; its governor was captured and executed. Mongol columns under Jochi moved down the Syr Darya while Chagatai and Ögedei reduced Otrar, and Genghis Khan himself crossed the Kyzyl Kum desert to strike Bukhara from an unexpected direction.

Fall of Bukhara and Samarkand

Bukhara fell in February 1220 after a short siege; its population was driven out, artisans were deported, and much of the city burned. Samarkand, defended by a large garrison, fell the following month. Urgench, the old capital, resisted a lengthy and bitter siege before falling in 1221. In Khorasan, Tolui's army took Merv and Nishapur; Herat surrendered and was initially spared. Muhammad II fled westward before the pursuing generals Jebe and Subutai and died on an island in the Caspian Sea in late 1220 or early 1221.

Resistance of Jalal al-Din

The shah's son, Jalal al-Din Mangburni, rallied resistance in Afghanistan, defeating a Mongol force at Parwan in 1221 — the Mongols' first serious reverse of the campaign. Genghis Khan personally pursued him and crushed his army at the Battle of the Indus in November 1221, after which Jalal al-Din escaped into India. Khwarezmian resistance effectively ended, though Jalal al-Din campaigned in Persia and the Caucasus for another decade.

Consequences

The conquest annihilated the Khwarezmian Empire and opened Persia and the steppe westward to Mongol expansion. Cities such as Merv, Nishapur and Urgench suffered catastrophic losses of life; contemporary chroniclers give figures in the hundreds of thousands or millions, which modern historians treat as exaggerations but agree the demographic and economic damage — including the destruction of irrigation systems — was severe and long-lasting. Transoxiana and Khorasan were incorporated into the Mongol Empire, and the campaign established the pattern of Mongol operations against the Islamic world that continued under Hülegü a generation later.
