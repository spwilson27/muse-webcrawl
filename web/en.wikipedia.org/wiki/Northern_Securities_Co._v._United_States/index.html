Northern Securities Co. v. United States, 193 U.S. 197 (1904), was a landmark United States Supreme Court decision in which the Court held that a holding company formed to control competing railroad lines violated the Sherman Antitrust Act and ordered its dissolution. It was the first great triumph of President Theodore Roosevelt's "trust-busting" campaign.

Background

In 1901 rival railroad magnates James J. Hill and Edward H. Harriman fought for control of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, triggering a stock market panic. To end the struggle, financier J. P. Morgan helped create the Northern Securities Company, a New Jersey holding company that controlled the Great Northern and Northern Pacific railways — parallel lines between the Great Lakes and the Pacific Northwest. The Roosevelt administration sued, although earlier precedent, United States v. E. C. Knight Co. (1895), had limited the Sherman Act's reach.

Decision

By 5–4, the Court upheld the government's suit. Justice Harlan's plurality held that the combination restrained interstate commerce and that Congress's power over such commerce justified dissolving the holding company. Justices Holmes and White dissented in notable opinions; Holmes wrote that "great cases like hard cases make bad law".

Legacy

The decision revived the Sherman Act as an effective instrument of federal economic regulation and established Roosevelt's reputation as a trust-buster. The stocks of the two railroads were distributed pro rata to Northern Securities shareholders, leaving the lines under overlapping ownership but formally separate.
