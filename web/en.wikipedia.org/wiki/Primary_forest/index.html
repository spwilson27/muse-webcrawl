Primary forest

Primary forest
SynonymsOld-growth forest, virgin forest, primeval forest
Global shareAbout one-third of all forest area (FAO)
Key traitsNative species, no visible human disturbance, ecological processes intact

A primary forest is a naturally regenerated forest of native tree species, where there are no clearly visible indications of human activities and ecological processes are not significantly disturbed. Primary forests are also commonly called old-growth, virgin, or primeval forests, though definitions differ: "old-growth" emphasises the age structure and ecological maturity of the stand rather than the absence of human impact. They are contrasted with secondary forests, which regrow after logging, clearing, or other disturbance.

Characteristics

Primary forests typically contain trees of many ages, including very old, large individuals; abundant standing dead trees (snags) and fallen logs; a multi-layered canopy; and complex understorey vegetation. These structural features create habitat for a disproportionate share of forest biodiversity, including species that cannot survive in managed or young forests.

Distribution

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, primary forests cover roughly 1.1 billion hectares — about one-third of the world's forest area. The largest tracts lie in the Amazon basin, the Congo Basin, boreal Russia and Canada, and Southeast Asia. Intact forest landscapes have shrunk markedly in recent decades, mainly through logging, road-building, agriculture, and mining.

Ecological importance

Primary forests store enormous quantities of carbon in biomass and soils — considerably more, hectare for hectare, than degraded or planted forests — and continue to absorb carbon as they grow. They regulate water cycles, protect soils, support Indigenous peoples and local communities, and act as refuges for endangered species such as orangutans, jaguars, and many endemic plants.

Threats and conservation

Once a primary forest is cleared or heavily logged, its composition and carbon stores cannot be restored on human timescales, even if trees regrow. Conservation measures include protected-area designation, recognition of Indigenous land rights, moratoria on road construction in intact landscapes, and international initiatives such as REDD+ that pay for avoided deforestation.
