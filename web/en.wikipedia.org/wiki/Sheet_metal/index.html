Sheet metal

Sheet metal
FormFlat metal stock

Thickness rangeFoil (<0.2 mm) to plate (>6 mm)

Sheet metal is metal formed by an industrial process into thin, flat pieces. It is one of the fundamental forms used in metalworking and it can be cut and bent into a variety of shapes. Countless everyday objects are fabricated from sheet metal, including automobile bodies, aircraft wings, medical tables, roofs for buildings, and machine housings.

Thickness and gauging

Sheet metal thickness is conventionally specified by a traditional, non-linear measure known as the gauge. The larger the gauge number, the thinner the metal. Commonly used steel sheet metal ranges from 30 gauge (about 0.30 mm) to about 7 gauge (about 4.55 mm). Whether a piece qualifies as sheet rather than foil or plate depends on the thickness range: stock thinner than about 0.2 mm is called foil or leaf, while stock thicker than about 6 mm is called plate.

Materials

Many different metals can be made into sheet metal, including aluminium, brass, copper, steel, tin, nickel and titanium. For decorative uses, important sheet metals include silver, gold, and platinum. Sheet metal can be produced from these metals by rolling, in which a hot metal slab is passed through a series of rollers that progressively reduce its thickness.

Forming processes

Sheet metal is shaped by a wide variety of processes, most of them cold-working operations performed at room temperature:

  • Bending – forcing the sheet over a die to create V-shapes, U-shapes and channels.
  • Punching and blanking – cutting holes or outlines with a punch and die.
  • Stamping – forming complex three-dimensional shapes, widely used for car body panels.
  • Deep drawing – stretching sheet into cup-like shapes such as sinks and cans.
  • Roll forming – passing the sheet through successive roller pairs to produce long profiles.
  • Spinning – rotating the sheet on a lathe against a former to make cones and dishes.

Applications

Sheet metal is used in automobile and truck bodies, airplane fuselages and wings, building roofing and cladding, ductwork for heating and ventilation, food and beverage cans, and enclosures for electronics. Its combination of low weight, strength, and formability makes it one of the most versatile engineering materials.
