g-factor (physics)

A g-factor (also called g value or dimensionless magnetic moment) is a dimensionless quantity that characterizes the magnetic moment and gyromagnetic ratio of a particle, atom, or nucleus. It relates the particle's magnetic dipole moment to its angular momentum and a unit of magnetic moment appropriate to its mass and charge.

Electron g-factor

Classically, a spinning charged sphere would have g = 1, but the electron's spin g-factor is approximately 2.0023. The Dirac equation predicts exactly g = 2 for the electron's spin; the small excess — the anomalous magnetic moment — arises from quantum electrodynamic corrections and is one of the most precisely measured and successfully predicted quantities in physics, agreeing with theory to better than one part in a trillion.

Muon g−2

The muon's g-factor differs from 2 by a larger amount, and its measurement is sensitive to virtual particles in the vacuum. Experiments at Brookhaven and Fermilab (the Muon g−2 experiment) measured the muon anomalous magnetic moment with high precision; comparison with Standard Model predictions has been an important test for physics beyond the Standard Model.

Other g-factors

The proton's g-factor is about 5.586 and the neutron's about −3.826, values explained by their internal quark structure. Nuclei and atoms have their own g-factors, central to nuclear magnetic resonance and atomic spectroscopy, and the Landé g-factor describes the splitting of atomic energy levels in a magnetic field.
