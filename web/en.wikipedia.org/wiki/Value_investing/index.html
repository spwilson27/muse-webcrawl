Value investing is an investment paradigm that involves buying securities that appear underpriced by some form of fundamental analysis. The strategy derives from the investment philosophy taught by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd at Columbia Business School from 1928 onward and codified in their text Security Analysis (1934) and Graham's The Intelligent Investor (1949).

Core principles

The central idea is that of intrinsic value: the value of a business as determined by its underlying economic characteristics—earnings, assets, dividends and financial strength—rather than by its market quotation. When the market price falls sufficiently below an estimate of intrinsic value, the value investor buys, relying on the gap to close over time. Graham insisted on a margin of safety: purchasing at a significant discount to intrinsic value so that errors in analysis or adverse developments do not produce permanent loss. Graham also popularised the allegory of "Mr. Market", a manic-depressive business partner whose fluctuating offers the investor may accept or ignore.

Notable practitioners

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger of Berkshire Hathaway are the best-known modern practitioners; Buffett has credited Graham as his mentor while acknowledging Munger's influence in shifting toward buying "wonderful companies at fair prices" rather than merely cheap ones. Other prominent value investors include Seth Klarman, Joel Greenblatt, Howard Marks and the late Walter Schloss. Academic research, notably by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French, has documented a long-run "value premium" of high book-to-market stocks over growth stocks, though value strategies have also endured long periods of underperformance, notably in the decade after 2010.

Metrics

Value investors commonly screen for low price-to-earnings ratios, low price-to-book ratios, high dividend yields, strong balance sheets and consistent free cash flow, then perform detailed analysis of individual businesses rather than relying on the metrics alone.
