Computer monitor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A computer monitor is an output device that displays information in pictorial or textual form, generated by a computer's graphics adapter. A monitor usually comprises a visual display, supporting circuitry, an enclosure, and a power supply. Monitors were originally monochrome text terminals; modern monitors are colour flat panels used for computing, gaming, design, and professional work.

Technologies

  • Cathode-ray tube (CRT) — the standard from the 1970s to the mid-2000s: bulky, heavy, but with fast response and flexible resolutions.
  • Liquid-crystal display (LCD) — dominant from the 2000s, using TN, IPS, or VA panels with LED backlighting.
  • OLED — self-emissive pixels with high contrast, increasingly common in premium monitors.

Specifications

Key specifications include screen size (diagonal, in inches), resolution (e.g. 1920×1080 "Full HD", 2560×1440 "QHD", 3840×2160 "4K UHD"), aspect ratio (commonly 16:9, with 21:9 and 32:9 ultrawides, and 16:10 productivity models), refresh rate (60 Hz standard; 144 Hz and above for gaming), response time, brightness, contrast ratio, and colour gamut coverage (sRGB, DCI-P3, Adobe RGB). Connectivity is typically by HDMI, DisplayPort, or USB-C with DisplayPort alternate mode.

History

Early computers used teletype printers and then CRT terminals such as the DEC VT100 (1978). IBM PC monitors standardised resolutions through CGA, EGA, and VGA (1987). MultiSync CRTs allowed multiple resolutions; LCDs overtook CRTs in sales around 2003–2004 as prices fell. Subsequent trends added LED backlights, high-refresh gaming panels, HDR, curved and ultrawide formats, and high-pixel-density "Retina-class" displays.

Ergonomics and health

Monitors are evaluated for flicker (PWM dimming), blue-light emission, glare, and adjustability (height, tilt, swivel, pivot). VESA mounts standardise attachment to arms and walls.
