Copy-number variation

Copy number variation (CNV) is a phenomenon in which sections of the genome are repeated and the number of repeats in the genome varies between individuals. Copy number variation is a type of structural variation: specifically, it is a type of duplication or deletion event that affects a considerable number of base pairs. Approximately two-thirds of the entire human genome may be composed of repeats, and 4.8–9.5% of the human genome can be classified as copy number variants.

Mechanisms

CNVs arise by mechanisms such as non-allelic homologous recombination between repeated sequences, non-homologous end joining, and replication errors such as fork stalling and template switching. They can span from about one kilobase to several megabases and include deletions (copy number less than two), duplications (more than two) and more complex rearrangements.

Association with disease

Copy number variation can be associated with a disorder. For example, deletion of a 3-million-base-pair region on chromosome 22 causes 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome); duplication of the PMP22 gene causes Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A, while its deletion causes hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies. CNVs have also been implicated in autism, schizophrenia, and susceptibility to infections and autoimmune diseases. Conversely, variation in the number of copies of the AMY1 (salivary amylase) gene correlates with historical starch consumption in populations.

Detection

Methods for detecting CNVs include comparative genomic hybridization, array CGH, SNP genotyping arrays, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and analysis of next-generation sequencing read depth.
