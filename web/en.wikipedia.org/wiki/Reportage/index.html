Reportage

Reportage is a style and genre of journalism — and, by extension, of documentary photography and film — in which a reporter gives an eyewitness, on-the-scene account of events, emphasising observed detail, atmosphere and narrative rather than a bare summary of facts. The term derives from the French reportage, and the German tradition of the literary Reportage flourished in the Weimar era.

Characteristics

Unlike straight news reporting, reportage immerses the reader in a scene: the reporter is present, describes what is seen, heard and felt, and often uses scene-by-scene construction, dialogue and a distinctive voice. It overlaps with literary journalism and the "New Journalism" of the 1960s and 1970s, and with immersive or "gonzo" styles, though classical reportage keeps the reporter's persona subordinate to the subject.

History

Precursors include the dispatches of war correspondents in the nineteenth century. Egon Erwin Kisch, the "raging reporter", defined the German-language reportage of the 1920s, while writers such as Joseph Roth and later Ryszard Kapuściński, Martha Gellhorn and John Hersey (whose Hiroshima is a landmark) extended the form. In photography, reportage became the standard style of picture magazines such as Life and agencies such as Magnum Photos, built around candid, unstaged images.
