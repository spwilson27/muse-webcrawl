Local loop unbundling

Local loop unbundling (LLU) is a regulatory policy that requires the incumbent telecommunications operator to lease its local loop — the connection between customers' premises and the local exchange — to competing providers at regulated rates. Competitors can then install their own equipment in the exchange and offer telephone and broadband services over the incumbent's copper lines.

Rationale

The local loop is widely regarded as a natural monopoly: duplicating the buried copper network to every home is uneconomic. Unbundling gives new entrants access to customers without building a parallel access network, encouraging competition in services while the incumbent retains the infrastructure.

Forms of access

Full unbundling gives the competitor exclusive use of the copper pair; shared access (line sharing) splits voice and data frequencies between incumbent and competitor; and bitstream access is a wholesale product in which the incumbent also provides much of the transmission network. Prices are typically set by the national regulator on a cost-oriented basis.

Adoption and effects

The European Union mandated unbundling in 2000, and it was associated with rapid growth in DSL competition and lower broadband prices in countries such as the United Kingdom and France. The policy has been debated as fibre-to-the-home networks spread, since regulators must decide how access obligations should apply to new infrastructure.
