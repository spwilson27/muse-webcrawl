Blind experiment

In a blind or blinded experiment, information which may influence the participants of the experiment is withheld until after the experiment is complete. Good blinding can reduce or eliminate experimental biases that arise from a participant's expectations, observer effects on participants, observer bias, confirmation bias and other sources. A blind can be imposed on any participant of an experiment, including subjects, researchers, technicians, data analysts and evaluators.

Types

In a single-blind experiment, the subjects do not know whether they belong to the experimental group or the control group, though the researchers do. In a double-blind experiment, neither subjects nor the experimenters interacting with them know which treatment each subject receives; allocation is concealed, often by coded preparations prepared by a third party. The term "triple-blind" is sometimes used where the data analysts are also unaware of group assignments.

Uses

Blinding is a cornerstone of randomized controlled trials in medicine, where it guards against the placebo effect and biased assessment of outcomes. It is also used in physics and other fields — for example, in blind analyses of particle physics data and in forensic laboratories — to prevent analysts' expectations from shaping results. Unblinding occurs at the end of the study, or earlier if safety requires it.

Limitations

Blinding is not always possible (for example, in surgery or physiotherapy trials) and can fail when treatments have distinctive side effects that reveal assignments. Reports therefore often assess whether blinding was maintained.
