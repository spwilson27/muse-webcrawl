The white-faced capuchin refers to two species of gracile capuchin monkey: the Colombian white-faced capuchin (Cebus capucinus) and the Panamanian white-faced capuchin (Cebus imitator), formerly considered a single species. They are small New World monkeys with black bodies and white or cream fur on the face, neck, and shoulders, native to the forests of Central America and northwestern South America.

Behaviour

White-faced capuchins live in multi-male, multi-female troops of up to about 40 individuals. They are highly intelligent and are known for tool use, including using stones to crack nuts and other hard foods, and for rubbing plants and millipedes on their fur, possibly as insect repellent. Their diet is omnivorous, dominated by fruit but including insects, eggs, and small vertebrates. They are an important seed disperser in tropical forests and a frequent subject of long-term primatology field studies, notably in Costa Rica's Santa Rosa and Lomas Barbudal reserves.
