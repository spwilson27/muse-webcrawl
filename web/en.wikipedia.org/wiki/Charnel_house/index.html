Charnel house

A charnel house is a vault or building where human skeletal remains are stored. They are often built near churches for storing bones from graves that have been dug up and reused, a common practice where burial space in consecrated ground was limited.

History

In medieval and early modern Europe, when a churchyard filled, older graves were opened and the exhumed bones gathered into a charnel house so that the ground could serve new burials. Some charnel houses were substantial stone buildings with chapels above them, and storing the dead in this way was connected with prayers for their souls and with the doctrine of the resurrection of the body.

Famous examples include the Sedlec Ossuary in the Czech Republic, where the bones of tens of thousands of people were arranged decoratively in the nineteenth century, and the charnel house at Hallstatt in Austria, where painted skulls of named villagers are still displayed. The Paris Catacombs originated in the transfer of bones from overflowing cemeteries, including the Cemetery of the Innocents, in the late eighteenth century.

Related practices

Charnel houses are related to ossuaries, bone churches and the practice of secondary burial, in which the bones of the dead are collected after decomposition and placed in a communal resting place. In some Asian traditions, similar bone repositories exist in Buddhist and Zoroastrian practice; the Zoroastrian astudan served as a receptacle for bones after exposure of the body.
