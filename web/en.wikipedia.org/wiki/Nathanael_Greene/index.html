Nathanael Greene (1742–1786) was a major general of the Continental Army in the American Revolutionary War. A self-educated Rhode Island ironmaster of Quaker background, he became one of George Washington's most trusted officers and, as commander of the Southern Department from late 1780, conducted the campaign that wore down British forces in the Carolinas and paved the way to Yorktown.

Early war service

Greene helped raise Rhode Island's Army of Observation in 1775 and was made a brigadier general in the Continental Army despite his lack of experience, rising to major general in 1776. He fought at Boston, Long Island, Trenton, Brandywine and Germantown, and served as quartermaster general from 1778 while retaining field command, presiding controversially over the army's supply system. After the fall of Charleston and the disaster at Camden, Washington gave Greene command in the South in December 1780.

Southern campaign

Greene rebuilt the shattered southern army, cooperating with partisan leaders and Daniel Morgan, whose victory at Cowpens in January 1781 opened the campaign's decisive phase. Greene then drew Cornwallis deep into North Carolina in the "Race to the Dan", fought him at Guilford Court House in March 1781 — a costly British tactical win that forced Cornwallis toward Virginia — and recovered South Carolina through battles at Hobkirk's Hill, Ninety Six and Eutaw Springs. His strategy of attrition is credited with breaking British control of the southern interior; he is often quoted, "We fight, get beat, rise, and fight again."

Later life

Granted plantations in Georgia in gratitude for his service, Greene settled at Mulberry Grove near Savannah but was saddled with debts from wartime supply guarantees. He died of sunstroke on 19 June 1786, aged 43. Counties, towns, statues in Washington and several United States Navy ships commemorate him.
