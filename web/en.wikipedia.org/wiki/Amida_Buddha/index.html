Amida Buddha (Japanese: 阿弥陀仏) is the Japanese name of Amitābha, the principal Buddha of Pure Land Buddhism. According to the Mahayana sutras, Amitābha was once a monk named Dharmākara who, after eons of practice, fulfilled a series of vows — most importantly the Primal Vow — and became a Buddha presiding over the Western Pure Land of Sukhāvatī, where conditions are ideal for attaining enlightenment.

Doctrine

Central to devotion to Amida is the practice of the nembutsu — the recitation of his name ("Namu Amida Butsu", "I take refuge in Amida Buddha"). In the teachings of Hōnen and Shinran, founders of the Japanese Jōdo-shū and Jōdo Shinshū schools, rebirth in the Pure Land is assured not by the practitioner's own efforts ("self-power", jiriki) but through reliance on Amida's vow ("other-power", tariki). Shinran taught that a single sincere recitation, arising from entrusting faith (shinjin), suffices.

Iconography

Amida is usually depicted seated in meditation or standing with hands in the raigō-in mudra, welcoming the dying believer. Famous images include the Great Buddha of Kamakura (Kamakura Daibutsu), a bronze statue over 13 metres tall cast in the 13th century, and the Amida Hall (Phoenix Hall) of Byōdō-in in Uji, built in 1053, which appears on the Japanese 10-yen coin.
