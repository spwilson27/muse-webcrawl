C-ration

The C-ration (officially Ration, Type C) was a prepared and canned wet combat ration intended to be issued to US military personnel in combat when fresh food (A-ration) or packaged unprepared food (B-ration) was not available, and on a short notice as a survival ration. Developed in 1938, it served through the Second World War and the Korean War, and was replaced in 1958 by the similar Meal, Combat, Individual (MCI), which remained in service until superseded by the MRE in the early 1980s.

Composition

A day's ration consisted of three "M" (meat) units and three "B" (bread) units in cans. M units contained entrées such as meat and beans, meat and vegetable stew, chicken and noodles, or ham and lima beans — the last widely disliked by troops. B units held crackers or biscuits, a spread, and coffee, sugar and candy or a fruit bar. A separate accessory packet provided cigarettes, matches, toilet paper, chewing gum, water-purification tablets and a can opener (the famous P-38).

Service history

The C-ration was heavier and less popular than the lighter K-ration but far more nutritious as a sustained diet. Soldiers traded cans, devised recipes, and heated meals with improvised stoves. In Korea the ration was little changed; its successor, the MCI (1958–1980), kept the canned format with revised menus. Vietnam-era troops called MCIs "C-rats" long after the official name had changed.
