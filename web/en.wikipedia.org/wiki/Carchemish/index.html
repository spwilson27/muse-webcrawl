Carchemish (Hittite: Kargamiš) was an important ancient city in the northern part of modern Syria and the far south of Turkey, on the west bank of the Euphrates River. It was a major crossing point of the Euphrates and a strategic prize contested by the Hittites, Egyptians, Assyrians and Babylonians.

History

Carchemish is mentioned in Egyptian and Hittite records of the second millennium BC. After the collapse of the Hittite Empire around 1175 BC, it became the centre of an important Neo-Hittite kingdom, ruled by a dynasty descended from Hittite royalty, and flourished as a Syro-Hittite city-state. The Assyrian king Sargon II conquered the city in 717 BC. In 605 BC the famous Battle of Carchemish was fought nearby, in which Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon decisively defeated the Egyptian army of Pharaoh Necho II and its Assyrian remnants — an event recorded in the Babylonian Chronicles and the Book of Jeremiah.

Archaeology

The site was excavated by the British Museum between 1911 and 1914, with a team that included T. E. Lawrence, and again by a joint Turco-Italian expedition from 2011. Finds include the Long Wall of Sculpture and processional reliefs now largely in the Museum of Anatolian Civilizations in Ankara.
