James Madison

James Madison (March 16, 1751 – June 28, 1836) was the fourth president of the United States, serving from 1809 to 1817. A Founding Father, he is hailed as the "Father of the Constitution" for his pivotal role in drafting and promoting the Constitution of the United States and the Bill of Rights. A protégé of Thomas Jefferson, he co-wrote The Federalist Papers with Alexander Hamilton and John Jay, helped found the Democratic-Republican Party, and served as Jefferson's secretary of state before succeeding him as president. His presidency was dominated by the War of 1812 against Britain — a war that began badly, saw Madison flee the burning of Washington, the only president to exercise the duties of commander-in-chief in the field, yet ended in a surge of national confidence that vindicated the republic.

Early life and the Constitution

Madison was born to a wealthy Virginia planter family at Port Conway and raised at Montpelier in Orange County. Small, sickly and bookish — he stood about 5 feet 4 inches and suffered lifelong ill health and episodes he called "epileptoid" attacks — he studied at the College of New Jersey (Princeton) and read voraciously in Latin, Greek, history and political theory. Drawn into politics by the struggle over religious freedom in Virginia (he helped secure Jefferson's Statute for Religious Freedom), he served in the Virginia legislature and the Continental Congress, where he became convinced the Articles of Confederation were fatally weak.

At the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in 1787 — aged 36, the best-prepared delegate — Madison's Virginia Plan provided the framework for the new Constitution, and he spoke more than 200 times. His meticulous notes of the debates, published posthumously, remain the principal record of the Convention. In The Federalist he wrote the most celebrated essays, including No. 10, on controlling the mischief of faction in an extended republic, and No. 51, on checks and balances. As a congressman in the First Congress he shepherded through the amendments that became the Bill of Rights (1791).

Party founder and secretary of state

Alarmed by Hamilton's financial programme and pro-British tilt, Madison broke with Washington's administration and, with Jefferson, organised the Democratic-Republican Party in opposition to the Federalists; in 1798 he secretly authored the Virginia Resolutions against the Alien and Sedition Acts. As Jefferson's secretary of state (1801–09) he managed the diplomacy of the Louisiana Purchase and the fruitless struggle to keep American trade out of the Napoleonic Wars by embargo — the policy he inherited as president.

Presidency and the War of 1812

Madison's two terms were consumed by the slide into war with Britain over impressment, trade restrictions and frontier conflict. In June 1812 Congress, at his request, declared war — the narrowest war vote in American history. The war's opening years brought humiliation: failed invasions of Canada and, in August 1814, the rout at Bladensburg and the British burning of the Capitol and President's House, from which Madison — who had ridden to the battlefield — escaped on horseback, while Dolley Madison rescued the portrait of Washington. But the repulse at Baltimore, the victory at Plattsburgh, and finally the Treaty of Ghent and the triumph at New Orleans transformed the ending: Madison left office in 1817 popular, with the Federalist opposition in ruins and a post-war programme of national development (a national bank, protective tariffs, internal improvements) under way — though he vetoed, on constitutional grounds, the Bonus Bill for roads and canals on his last day in office.

Retirement and legacy

Madison retired to Montpelier, served as rector of Jefferson's University of Virginia after his friend's death, and lent his prestige to moderate causes at Virginia's constitutional convention of 1829. He died in 1836, the last surviving signer of the Constitution. History remembers him above all as the Constitution's chief architect and expositor — the small, uncharismatic thinker whose writings frame American government to this day — and as the wartime president who, having nearly lost his capital, left the Union more secure than he found it.
