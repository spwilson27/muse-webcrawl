Tokugawa Iemitsu (徳川 家光, August 12, 1604 – June 8, 1651) was the third shōgun of the Tokugawa dynasty. He was the eldest son of Tokugawa Hidetada and the grandson of Tokugawa Ieyasu. Iemitsu ruled from 1623 to 1651, during which time he consolidated the shogunate's power, formalized the system of alternate attendance (sankin-kōtai), and enacted the policy of national isolation (sakoku) that defined Japan's foreign relations for more than two centuries.

Early life and succession

Iemitsu was born in Edo, the shogunate's capital, and was raised largely under the care of his wet nurse, Lady Kasuga (Kasuga no Tsubone), who became a powerful figure at court. Although his younger brother Tadanaga was favoured by their mother, their grandfather Ieyasu's wishes and the support of key retainers secured Iemitsu's succession; in 1623 Hidetada abdicated in his favour, continuing to rule as retired shōgun (ōgosho) until his death in 1632, after which Iemitsu governed alone. Tadanaga was eventually forced to commit seppuku in 1633.

Consolidation of power

Iemitsu centralized the administration, creating a more bureaucratic system of councils and inspectors, and curtailed the privileges of the imperial court in Kyoto. The revised Laws for the Military Houses (Buke shohatto, 1635) required daimyō to reside in Edo in alternate years (sankin-kōtai), leaving their families there permanently as hostages — a costly obligation that drained domain finances and bound the feudal lords to the shogunate.

Sakoku and persecution of Christianity

Fearing the influence of Spain and Portugal and their Catholic missions, Iemitsu issued a series of edicts (1633–1639) that expelled the Portuguese, forbade Japanese to travel abroad or return from overseas, and confined foreign trade to the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki (the Dutch were moved to the artificial island of Dejima in 1641). Christianity was brutally suppressed; the Shimabara Rebellion (1637–38) of largely Christian peasants was crushed with great loss of life. Iemitsu also lavishly rebuilt the mausoleum complex of his grandfather at Nikkō (the present Nikkō Tōshō-gū) as a statement of dynastic legitimacy.
