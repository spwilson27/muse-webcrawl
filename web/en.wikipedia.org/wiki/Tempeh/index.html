Tempeh (tempe)

Tempeh (Javanese: ꦠꦺꦩ꧀ꦥꦺ, témpé) is a traditional Indonesian food made by fermenting soybeans with the mould Rhizopus oligosporus (or R. oryzae), which binds the beans into a firm, sliceable cake. Originating in Java several centuries ago, it is a staple protein source in Indonesia and has become popular worldwide as a meat substitute in vegetarian and vegan cooking.

Production

Soybeans are soaked, dehulled, partially cooked, then inoculated with a starter culture (ragi tempe) and spread in shallow layers — traditionally wrapped in banana leaves, today often in perforated plastic. Incubated at around 30 °C for 24–48 hours, the beans are knitted together by a dense mat of white mycelium. The fresh product has a nutty, mushroom-like aroma and a firm, chewy texture that absorbs marinades well.

Nutrition and use

Tempeh is high in protein (about 19 g per 100 g, with all essential amino acids), fibre, and B vitamins; fermentation reduces phytates and oligosaccharides, improving digestibility. In Indonesia it is commonly fried, grilled, stewed in coconut milk, caramelised with sweet soy sauce (tempe kering), or crumbled into sambals. In Western kitchens it is marinated and baked or pan-fried for sandwiches, salads and stir-fries, and fashioned into products such as tempeh bacon. Varieties made from other legumes, grains or okara also exist, as does the related West Javanese oncom.
