Nation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nation is a type of social organisation where a collective identity, a national identity, has emerged due to a combination of shared features across a given population, such as language, history, ethnicity, culture, territory or society. Some nations are constructed around ethnic or ancestral ties (ethnic nationalism), others around shared citizenship and institutions (civic nationalism); most combine both in varying proportions.

Nations are not the same as states: a nation may lack a state of its own (as with Kurds or Catalans in much of their history), a state may contain several nations, and a nation may span several states. The modern doctrine of nationalism holds that the nation and the state should be congruent — that each nation should have its own nation-state. Scholars debate whether nations are ancient (“perennialist” and “ethnosymbolist” views, associated with Anthony D. Smith) or specifically modern creations of print, capitalism and state-building (the “modernist” accounts of Ernest Gellner and Benedict Anderson).

Definitions and theories

Benedict Anderson defined the nation as an “imagined political community — imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign”: members will never know most of their fellows, yet hold them in a deep horizontal comradeship. Gellner argued that industrial society's need for a literate, mobile workforce made a shared “high culture”, spread by universal education, functionally necessary, and nations its by-product. Ethnosymbolists reply that modern nations built on much older ethnic myths, memories and symbols, which explains their emotional power.

Nation and state

The vocabulary is slippery: “nation” in international law often means the whole population of a state (as in the United Nations), while in domestic politics it may denote a constituent people (as in the United Kingdom's “home nations”). National minorities, indigenous peoples and diasporas complicate any simple mapping of nations onto states.
