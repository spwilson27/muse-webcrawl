Batting average (baseball)

In baseball, the batting average (abbreviated BA or AVG) is a statistic measuring a batter's performance, defined as the number of hits divided by the number of at bats. It is usually rounded to three decimal places and read without the decimal point: a player with a batting average of .300 is said to be "batting three hundred".

AVG = Hits ÷ At bats

At bats exclude bases on balls (walks), hit by pitch, sacrifices, and interference or obstruction calls, so batting average measures only the outcome of plate appearances in which the batter is given the chance to hit. A batting average of .300 or higher over a season is considered excellent; the modern league average is typically between .240 and .250. In Major League Baseball, the player with the highest batting average in each league (with sufficient plate appearances) is awarded the batting title.

History

The statistic was developed in the 19th century under the influence of the English cricket statistician Henry Chadwick, who adapted cricket's batting average to baseball; initially, walks were sometimes counted as hits against the batter, but the modern definition was standardised by the early 20th century.

Records

The highest single-season batting average in Major League Baseball history belongs to Hugh Duffy, who hit .440 in 1894. The highest in the modern (post-1900) era is Rogers Hornsby's .424 in 1924. Ted Williams's .406 season in 1941 remains the last time an MLB player hit .400 or better in a season. The career batting average record is held by Ty Cobb, at .366. In Nippon Professional Baseball, the single-season record is held by Randy Bass (.389 in 1986), among others, and in the KBO League by Baek In-cheon (.412 in 1982).

Criticism and modern usage

Sabermetricians regard batting average as an incomplete measure of a hitter because it ignores walks and power—a hit is a hit, whether single or home run, and a walk contributes nothing. On-base percentage and slugging percentage (and their sum, OPS) are therefore generally preferred for evaluating offensive value, though batting average remains one of the most widely cited statistics in the sport.
