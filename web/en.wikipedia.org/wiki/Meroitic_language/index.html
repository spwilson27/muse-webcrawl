Meroitic was the language of the Kingdom of Kush during its Meroitic phase (from about 300 BC to the 4th century AD), spoken and written between the region of Aswan in the north and the vicinity of modern Khartoum in the south. It is written in the Meroitic script, which survives in two forms — monumental hieroglyphs adapted from Egyptian, and a cursive script — and although the script's sound values were deciphered in the early 20th century, the language itself remains largely untranslated and is one of the great undeciphered languages of the ancient world.

Decipherment of the script

The British Egyptologist Francis Llewellyn Griffith established the phonetic values of the Meroitic signs between 1909 and 1911, using bilingual material and known royal names, showing that the script is an alphabet-like system of 23 signs (an alphasyllabary in which consonant signs carry an inherent vowel). Scholars can therefore read Meroitic texts aloud, but the vocabulary and grammar are understood only in fragments: words for rulers (qore, king), deities such as Amun and Isis, offering formulas and funerary expressions.

Classification

For much of the 20th century Meroitic was treated as a language isolate. Work by Claude Rilly and others since the 2000s has argued that Meroitic belongs to the Northern East Sudanic branch of the Nilo-Saharan family, related to the Nubian languages, Tama, Nyima and Nara. This classification, if correct, offers a comparative route toward reading longer texts, though decipherment remains incomplete.

Texts

The corpus comprises roughly two thousand texts, mostly short funerary stelae and offering tables, plus temple inscriptions and graffiti. Royal stelae — including the long inscription of King Tanyidamani — and administrative ostraca promise richer information once the language can be fully read. The language disappeared with the fall of Meroë in the 4th century AD, succeeded in the region by Old Nubian.
