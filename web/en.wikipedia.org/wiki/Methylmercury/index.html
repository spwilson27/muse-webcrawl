Methylmercury

Methylmercury
Cation[CH3Hg]+
TypeOrganomercury compound
Main exposure routeConsumption of fish and seafood
Primary targetCentral nervous system

Methylmercury is an organometallic cation with the formula [CH3Hg]+, the principal form of mercury that accumulates in aquatic food webs. It is a potent neurotoxin. Inorganic mercury released from natural sources and human activity — notably coal combustion and artisanal gold mining — is converted by microorganisms in water and sediments into methylmercury, which then bioaccumulates in organisms and biomagnifies up the food chain, reaching its highest concentrations in long-lived predatory fish such as tuna, swordfish, shark, and king mackerel.

Health effects

Methylmercury is readily absorbed from the gut, crosses the blood–brain barrier and the placenta, and attacks the central nervous system. The developing fetal brain is especially vulnerable; prenatal exposure is associated with impaired cognition, motor function, and attention. Severe poisoning causes the syndrome seen in the Minamata disaster in Japan, where industrial discharge contaminated seafood consumed by local communities, producing numbness, ataxia, constriction of the visual field, and in congenital cases profound developmental disability. Another mass poisoning occurred in Iraq in 1971–72 from grain treated with a methylmercury fungicide.

Regulation and advice

Health agencies issue fish-consumption advisories for pregnant women and children, recommending limiting high-mercury species while retaining the nutritional benefits of low-mercury fish. The Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013) commits signatories to reducing mercury emissions and releases. Reference doses, such as the U.S. EPA's 0.1 µg/kg body weight per day, are used in risk assessment.
