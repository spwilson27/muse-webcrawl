Serial links transmit data one bit at a time over a single channel.

In telecommunication and data transmission, serial communication is the process of sending data one bit at a time, sequentially, over a communication channel or computer bus. This contrasts with parallel communication, in which several bits are sent simultaneously over multiple channels.

Characteristics

Serial links require fewer wires and connectors than parallel links of comparable capacity, reducing cost and avoiding problems such as clock skew between parallel lanes. Although sending bits one at a time was once slower, modern serial interfaces achieve extremely high signalling rates—often exceeding what parallel buses can sustain—and dominate modern interconnect design. Examples include USB, Ethernet, SATA, PCI Express, FireWire, and Fibre Channel, as well as low-speed embedded buses such as I²C, SPI, and the classic RS-232 standard.

Synchronisation

Serial communication may be synchronous, with a shared clock signal (or clock embedded in the data through line coding), or asynchronous, in which each byte is framed by start and stop bits and both ends agree in advance on the bit rate (baud). Asynchronous framing is used by UARTs, the hardware underlying traditional serial ports. Clock recovery techniques and encodings such as 8b/10b allow very high-speed synchronous links to embed timing in the data stream.

Comparison with parallel

Historically, parallel interfaces were preferred for short, high-throughput connections (printer ports, IDE/PATA, PCI). As data rates rose, maintaining alignment among many simultaneous signals became difficult, and the industry shifted to serial designs with one or more differential pairs; multiple serial "lanes" can be bonded to scale bandwidth linearly. Serial communication is now the near-universal choice from on-chip interconnects to wide-area networks.
