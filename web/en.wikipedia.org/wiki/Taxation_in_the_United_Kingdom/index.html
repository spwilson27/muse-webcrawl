Taxation in the United Kingdom involves payments to at least three levels of government: central government (through HM Revenue and Customs), devolved governments, and local government. The largest sources of revenue are income tax, National Insurance contributions, value added tax (VAT) and corporation tax.

Income tax and National Insurance

Income tax is charged at progressive rates — in England, Wales and Northern Ireland typically a basic rate of 20%, a higher rate of 40% and an additional rate of 45%, above a tax-free personal allowance (frozen at £12,570 in recent years). Scotland sets its own income-tax bands on non-savings income. National Insurance, originally a contributory social-security levy, functions economically as a second income tax on employees, employers and the self-employed. Most employment income is taxed at source under the PAYE (Pay As You Earn) system.

Indirect and other taxes

VAT is charged at a standard rate of 20%, with reduced (5%) and zero rates for categories such as domestic fuel and most food, and exemptions for financial services and health. Other major taxes include corporation tax on company profits (main rate 25% since April 2023), capital gains tax, inheritance tax, stamp duty land tax on property purchases, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, business rates, and council tax on domestic property, collected by local authorities.

History

A temporary income tax was introduced by William Pitt the Younger in 1799 to finance the Napoleonic Wars and made permanent in the 1840s. VAT replaced purchase tax on the UK's entry to the European Economic Community in 1973; the Community Charge ("poll tax") of 1989–90 provoked riots and was replaced by council tax in 1993.
