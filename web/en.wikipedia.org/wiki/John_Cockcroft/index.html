John Cockcroft

Sir John Douglas Cockcroft (27 May 1897 – 18 September 1967) was a British physicist who shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Physics with Ernest Walton for their pioneering work on the transmutation of atomic nuclei by artificially accelerated atomic particles.

Early life and education

Cockcroft was born in Todmorden, Yorkshire. He studied electrical engineering at Victoria University of Manchester and mathematics at St John's College, Cambridge, where he worked under Ernest Rutherford at the Cavendish Laboratory.

The Cockcroft–Walton accelerator

With Ernest Walton, Cockcroft built a voltage-multiplier accelerator capable of producing high-energy protons. In 1932 they used it to split the lithium nucleus, the first artificial disintegration of an atomic nucleus by accelerated particles — an experiment that confirmed Einstein's mass–energy equivalence.

Later career

During World War II, Cockcroft worked on radar and directed the Montreal Laboratory on the atomic bomb project. After the war he became the first director of the Atomic Energy Research Establishment at Harwell, and was a key figure in Britain's nuclear programme. He was knighted in 1948.
