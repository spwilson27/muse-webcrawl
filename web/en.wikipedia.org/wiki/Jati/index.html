Jati

A jati: a birth-ascribed community within the caste system

Jati (Sanskrit: जाति, jāti, "birth") is the term for the thousands of endogamous, hereditary social groups that form the working fabric of the caste system in India and elsewhere in South Asia. Where varna supplies a fourfold theoretical scheme (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra), jatis are the concrete communities into which people are born, traditionally associated with an occupation, a marriage circle, shared food customs and a position in local ritual hierarchy.

Characteristics

Jatis are usually endogamous: members marry within the group (while observing gotra exogamy within it). Historically many jatis were linked to a hereditary craft or service — potters (Kumhar), barbers (Nai), washermen (Dhobi), leatherworkers (Chamar), merchants and scribes of particular regions — and participated in local exchange arrangements sometimes called the jajmani system, in which service castes served landholding patrons. Each jati may have a caste council (panchayat) enforcing norms, and ranks vary by region: a jati's local standing can differ markedly between villages and states.

History and change

The proliferation of jatis is attributed by scholars to occupational specialisation, absorption of tribal and regional communities into Hindu society, sectarian movements and historical migration. Colonial censuses attempted to enumerate and rank castes, hardening identities that had been more fluid. In modern India, caste-based discrimination is constitutionally prohibited, and affirmative-action ("reservation") policies classify many jatis as Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes or Other Backward Classes. Yet jati remains socially and politically salient: marriage advertisements, caste associations and voting blocs are frequently organised along jati lines, and diaspora communities carry jati identities abroad.
