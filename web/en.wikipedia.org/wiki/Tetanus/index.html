Tetanus, also known as lockjaw, is a serious infection characterised by prolonged contraction of skeletal muscle fibres (tetany) and violent muscle spasms. It is caused by the neurotoxin tetanospasmin, produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani when its spores germinate in anaerobic conditions, typically in a contaminated wound. Tetanus is not contagious between people.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of usually about eight days (range 3–21), symptoms often begin with stiffness of the jaw (trismus, "lockjaw") and the facial expression known as risus sardonicus, progressing to generalised spasms in which the back arches (opisthotonus). Spasms are triggered by trivial stimuli and can fracture bones and compromise breathing; autonomic nervous system dysfunction — labile blood pressure, tachycardia, sweating — is a major cause of death. Localised and cephalic forms occur, and neonatal tetanus follows infection of the umbilical stump.

Mechanism

Tetanospasmin travels retrogradely along motor axons to the spinal cord and brainstem, where it cleaves synaptobrevin (VAMP) in inhibitory interneurons, blocking release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters glycine and GABA. Loss of inhibition leaves motor neurons unchecked, producing rigidity and spasms. A second, weaker toxin, tetanolysin, is of unclear significance.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical; the "spatula test" (touching the posterior pharynx, which provokes jaw spasm rather than gagging) is suggestive. Treatment involves wound debridement, human tetanus immunoglobulin to neutralise unbound toxin, antibiotics (metronidazole preferred), benzodiazepines and other agents to control spasms and autonomic instability, and often prolonged intensive care with ventilation. Recovery takes weeks as new nerve terminals grow; the disease does not confer immunity.

Prevention

Tetanus toxoid vaccine — among the most effective vaccines in use — is given in childhood combination vaccines (DTaP, and the older DPT) with boosters, and in maternal immunisation programmes which, with clean delivery practices, have greatly reduced neonatal tetanus worldwide. Global deaths fell from hundreds of thousands annually in the 1980s to tens of thousands by the late 2010s, though the disease persists where vaccination coverage is poor.
