Satpanth

Satpanth (Gujarati: સતપંથ, "the true path") is a tradition that refers to a cluster of beliefs and practices originating in medieval Gujarat and Sindh, associated with the Ismaili Muslim missionaries known as pirs who preached among Hindu trading and artisan communities of western India from roughly the 12th to the 15th centuries.

Origins and teaching

Satpanth tradition credits Pir Sadruddin, a missionary (da'i) of the Nizari Ismaili imams of Persia, with organising the converts into the Khoja community and composing many of the devotional hymns called ginans. The ginans, written in Gujarati, Hindi, and other vernaculars, express Ismaili doctrine in imagery drawn from Hindu mythology: the Imam is identified with the awaited tenth avatar (Dashavatara) Kalki, Ali is equated with Vishnu, and the Quran is presented as the fulfilment of the Atharva Veda. This idiom allowed Hindu converts to retain much of their familiar religious vocabulary while accepting the authority of the Imam.

Communities

The principal inheritors of the Satpanth tradition are the Khoja Ismaili Muslims, who follow the Aga Khan. Over time, especially under Aga Khan III in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the community increasingly emphasised its Shia Ismaili identity, and distinctly Hindu elements of Satpanth practice were reformed or set aside. Related traditions, such as those of the Imamshahi (Satpanthi) community centred on Pir Muhammad Shah's shrine near Ahmedabad, diverged and developed separately; some Satpanth groups eventually identified fully with Hinduism or with Sunni Islam.

Scholarship

Modern scholars such as Dominique-Sila Khan and Ali S. Asani have studied Satpanth as an example of religious synthesis and "liminal" identity on the frontier between Hindu and Muslim worlds, and have debated how far its categories correspond to the modern labels "Hindu" and "Muslim".
