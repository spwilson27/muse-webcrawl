Tonalpohualli

The tonalpohualli (Nahuatl: "count of days") is the 260-day sacred calendar used throughout pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, including by the Aztec (Mexica) and Maya peoples — among the Maya it corresponds to the calendar known as the Tzolkʼin. It was used for divination, ritual scheduling and naming children, and its day-by-day omens were consulted by priests called tonalpouhque using painted books called tonalamatl.

Structure

The cycle is generated by combining the numbers 1 to 13 with a rotating sequence of 20 named day signs, yielding 260 uniquely named days before the sequence repeats. The twenty signs include Cipactli (crocodile), Ehecatl (wind), Calli (house), Cuetzpalin (lizard), Coatl (serpent), Miquiztli (death), Mazatl (deer), Tochtli (rabbit), Atl (water), Itzcuintli (dog), Ozomahtli (monkey), Malinalli (grass), Acatl (reed), Ocelotl (jaguar), Cuauhtli (eagle), Cozcacuauhtli (vulture), Ollin (movement), Tecpatl (flint), Quiahuitl (rain) and Xochitl (flower). The 260 days are also grouped into twenty 13-day periods called trecenas, each presided over by a deity.

Use

The calendar's origin is debated; proposed explanations include the human gestation period of roughly nine months and the interval between zenith passages of the sun at certain latitudes, as well as the synodic period of Venus (224–263 days). Interlocking with the 365-day solar calendar (xiuhpohualli), the tonalpohualli produced the 52-year Calendar Round central to Aztec and Maya timekeeping. Traditional day-keepers in highland Guatemala still maintain the count today.
