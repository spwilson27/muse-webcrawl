Krein–Milman theorem

Placeholder image: a convex set as the closed convex hull of its extreme points

In the branch of mathematics known as functional analysis, the Krein–Milman theorem is a proposition about convex sets in topological vector spaces. Informally, it says that given a convex, compact set in a well-behaved locally convex space, the set can be reconstructed from its extreme points: the set is equal to the closed convex hull of its extreme points. The theorem is named for Mark Krein and David Milman, who published it in 1940; a partial converse in finite dimensions goes back to Hermann Minkowski.

Statement

Let X be a locally convex topological vector space (over the real or complex numbers), and let K be a non-empty compact convex subset of X. A point of K is called an extreme point if it cannot be written as a proper convex combination of two distinct points of K — that is, if it does not lie strictly between any two other points of the set. The Krein–Milman theorem states that K is equal to the closure of the convex hull of the set of its extreme points. In particular, K necessarily has extreme points — in fact, an abundance of them.

Background and proof idea

For convex compact sets in finite-dimensional space, Minkowski had shown that the set is the convex hull of its extreme points (no closure is needed, because the hull is already closed). In infinite dimensions the convex hull of the extreme points need not be closed, and the theorem requires both the closure operation and the local convexity hypothesis, which guarantees that there are enough continuous linear functionals to "separate" points from convex sets via the Hahn–Banach theorem. The proof proceeds by considering faces of K: using separation, every supporting hyperplane exposes a non-empty face, and by a Zorn's lemma argument one reduces to minimal faces, which must be single extreme points; if the closed convex hull of the extreme points were smaller than K, separation would then yield a contradiction.

Applications

The theorem is a basic tool in functional analysis and convex geometry. A standard application is the proof of the existence of pure states in the algebraic formulation of quantum mechanics: the state space of a C*-algebra is a weak-* compact convex set whose extreme points are exactly the pure states. Related results include the Choquet theory, which refines the theorem by representing points of K as "averages" (barycenters of probability measures) concentrated on the extreme points — a direct generalization of the finite-dimensional fact that every point of a convex compact set is a convex combination of extreme points (Carathéodory's theorem). The Krein–Milman theorem is also used in proving the existence of invariant measures and fixed points, and in the theory of Banach algebras, for instance in characterizing Banach spaces such as C(K) and in harmonic analysis.

Related results

The converse question — which sets can be recovered from their extreme points — led to work by Milman himself showing that, under mild conditions, if a compact convex set is the closed convex hull of a subset, then that subset must contain all the extreme points. The Banach–Alaoglu theorem supplies the compactness of dual unit balls that makes the theorem applicable in dual spaces.
