Hyperreal number

The system of hyperreal numbers is a way of treating infinite and infinitesimal quantities. The hyperreals, or nonstandard reals, *R, are an extension of the real numbers R that contains numbers greater than anything of the form 1 + 1 + … + 1 (any finite number of terms). Such numbers are called infinite, and their reciprocals are called infinitesimals. The system was introduced by Abraham Robinson in the 1960s as the foundation of nonstandard analysis, giving a rigorous footing to the intuitive infinitesimal arguments of Leibniz and Newton.

Properties

The hyperreals form an ordered field satisfying the transfer principle: every first-order statement true of the reals is true of the hyperreals, so algebraic and analytic manipulations carry over unchanged. Every finite hyperreal x is uniquely close to a real number, its standard part st(x); two hyperreals are infinitely close if their difference is infinitesimal. The halo (monad) of a real number consists of all hyperreals infinitely close to it.

Construction

Hyperreals can be constructed as an ultrapower: sequences of real numbers modulo a free ultrafilter on the natural numbers. Two sequences represent the same hyperreal if they agree on a set belonging to the ultrafilter. The existence of such an ultrafilter depends on a weak form of the axiom of choice. Alternative approaches, such as Edward Nelson's Internal Set Theory, axiomatise the hyperreal behaviour directly.

Applications

Using infinitesimals, the derivative of a function f at x is st((f(x + dx) − f(x)) / dx) for infinitesimal dx, which many find more intuitive than epsilon–delta limits. Nonstandard analysis has been applied to probability theory, mathematical economics, and differential equations.
