The Académie Goncourt (Goncourt Academy) is a French literary organisation based in Paris, founded in accordance with the will of the writer Edmond de Goncourt (1822–1896), who wished to create a society of writers independent of the Académie Française. It is best known as the awarding body of the Prix Goncourt, France's most prestigious literary prize for prose fiction.

History

Edmond de Goncourt bequeathed his estate to fund an academy of ten writers, each receiving an annuity, charged with awarding an annual prize to a work of imaginative prose. Litigation by Goncourt's relatives delayed the project, but the Academy was formally constituted on 19 April 1900, and the first Prix Goncourt was awarded in 1903 to John-Antoine Nau for Force ennemie.

Membership and workings

The ten members, known as les Dix, are co-opted for life and traditionally meet on the first Tuesday of each month over lunch at the restaurant Drouant in Paris. The Prix Goncourt is announced in early November after a final lunch meeting; the winner receives a symbolic sum (historically 50 francs, today €10), but the award's prestige typically produces a major increase in sales. Laureates have included Marcel Proust (1919), Simone de Beauvoir (1954) and Michel Houellebecq (2010).
