The German nuclear weapons program (informally the Uranverein, "Uranium Club") was Nazi Germany's effort during the Second World War to exploit nuclear fission for military purposes. Although German scientists had made the key discovery of fission itself, the program was fragmented, under-resourced and scientifically misdirected, and it never came close to producing either a bomb or a self-sustaining reactor.

Origins

In December 1938 in Berlin, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann chemically identified barium among the products of neutron-bombarded uranium; their exiled colleague Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch explained the result as the splitting — fission — of the nucleus, releasing enormous energy. In April 1939 physicists including Paul Harteck alerted the German War Office to the possibility of an explosive of unprecedented power, and in September 1939 the Army Weapons Office convened leading physicists — among them Werner Heisenberg, Otto Hahn, Hans Geiger, Walther Bothe and Kurt Diebner — in the group jokingly called the Uranverein.

Research

Work centred on building a "uranium machine" (an experimental reactor) and on isotope separation. Heisenberg's theoretical group pursued reactor physics; Diebner's rival army team at Gottow pursued its own reactor designs. Walther Bothe's measurements wrongly concluded that graphite absorbed too many neutrons to serve as a moderator (the error was caused by impurities in his sample), so the program became dependent on scarce heavy water from the Norsk Hydro plant at Vemork in occupied Norway — a supply repeatedly disrupted by Norwegian resistance and Allied bombing, including the famous commando raid of February 1943 and the sinking of the ferry SF Hydro in 1944.

The program also suffered from the regime's expulsion of Jewish scientists, rivalry between agencies, competing priorities for resources, and a 1942 decision — after Heisenberg briefed armaments minister Albert Speer — that a bomb could not be delivered in time to affect the war, keeping the effort at modest research scale. The final reactor experiment, B-VIII at Haigerloch in early 1945, used uranium cubes suspended in heavy water but remained subcritical.

End and assessment

The Allied Alsos Mission seized German uranium, equipment and documents in 1945, and ten leading German nuclear scientists were interned at Farm Hall in England, where their secretly recorded reactions to the Hiroshima bombing (released decades later as the Farm Hall transcripts) showed genuine surprise at the Allied achievement. Historians have debated how far short the Germans fell and why — cost, organisation, emigration of talent, and scientific errors all played a part — but the consensus is that Germany was never near a weapon: unlike the industrial-scale Manhattan Project, the Uranverein remained a laboratory research effort.
