Battle of Carham

The Battle of Carham was fought in 1018 (some sources suggest 1016) at Carham on the River Tweed, on the present-day Anglo-Scottish border, between the Kingdom of Scotland under King Malcolm II and the Northumbrians under Uhtred the Bold's successor forces led by Eadwulf Cudel, Earl of Bamburgh. The Scottish victory — won with the support of Malcolm's ally, the king of Strathclyde — is traditionally credited with securing Scottish control of Lothian and fixing the Tweed as the border between Scotland and England.

Background and battle

Since the 10th century, the kings of Scots had pressed southward into Lothian, the Anglian-speaking territory between the Forth and the Tweed held by the earls of Bamburgh. In 1018 Malcolm II, together with Owain Foel (Owen the Bald), king of Strathclyde, invaded Northumbria and met the local forces, raised after the death of the formidable Earl Uhtred (murdered in 1016), near Carham. The Northumbrians were heavily defeated. The near-contemporary sources are sparse — the battle is recorded in the Historia Regum attributed to Symeon of Durham and later chronicles — but tradition holds that the church at Carham was left filled with the slain, and monastic chroniclers treated the day, the feast of St Cuthbert in some accounts, as momentous.

Significance

Historians have long regarded Carham as the battle that confirmed the Tweed frontier and Scotland's possession of Lothian, including Edinburgh, although modern scholarship cautions that the transfer of Lothian may have been a gradual process — possibly beginning with King Edgar's grant to Kenneth II in the 970s — rather thanthe direct result of a single battle. Either way, Carham is remembered as a landmark in the formation of the Anglo-Scottish border, and its 1000th anniversary was commemorated on the battlefield site in 2018.
