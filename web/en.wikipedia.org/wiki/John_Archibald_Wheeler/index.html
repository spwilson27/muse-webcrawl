John Archibald Wheeler (9 July 1911 – 13 April 2008) was an American theoretical physicist. He was largely responsible for reviving interest in General relativity in the United States after World War II, and he coined or popularised the terms 'Black hole', 'Wormhole', 'quantum foam' and 'it from bit'. Wheeler worked with Niels Bohr on the mechanism of Nuclear fission (1939), with Richard Feynman (his student) on absorber theory and the path integral's ancestry, and mentored a generation including Feynman, Kip Thorne, Jacob Bekenstein and Hugh Everett.

His programme of 'geometrodynamics' sought to build matter itself from curved spacetime; though it failed as unification, it created modern relativity research — his Princeton school, the Wheeler–DeWitt equation of quantum gravity (1967), and the monumental textbook Gravitation ('MTW', 1973, with Misner and Thorne). In later life Wheeler pursued the participatory universe — 'it from bit': reality arising from yes/no questions posed by observers — the most philosophical turn of a career that had also included designing hydrogen bombs at Los Alamos and Princeton's Project Matterhorn.

Naming black holes

At a 1967 lecture Wheeler, tired of repeating 'gravitationally completely collapsed object', accepted an audience member's suggestion of 'black hole' — and made it immortal. His instinct for the telling phrase ('a charge without charge', 'mass without mass', 'no hair') shaped how physics talks about its strangest objects; the 'no-hair theorem' for black holes is his coinage too.
