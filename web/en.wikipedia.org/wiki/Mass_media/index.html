Mass media

Mass media refers to technologies and organisations that communicate information, entertainment and culture to large, heterogeneous audiences, typically through media channels such as print (newspapers and magazines), broadcast (radio and television), film, and digital and online media. The term emerged in the 1920s with the rise of nationwide radio networks and mass-circulation newspapers.

History

Mass communication has roots in early print culture. The invention of the movable-type printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 dramatically lowered the cost of books and pamphlets. In the 19th century, steam-powered presses and cheap wood-pulp paper enabled the "penny press" and mass-circulation newspapers. Radio broadcasting began commercially in the 1920s, and television became the dominant household medium in the 1950s. The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw the rise of cable television, satellite broadcasting and the internet.

Forms

Print media

Newspapers, magazines, books and other printed materials. Print was the earliest mass medium and remains influential, though circulations have declined in many countries with the growth of digital alternatives.

Broadcast media

Radio and television transmit audio and audiovisual content to dispersed audiences simultaneously. Broadcasting is often regulated by national authorities because it uses the electromagnetic spectrum.

Digital media

Websites, streaming services, social media platforms and podcasts deliver content on demand. Digital media blur the line between producers and audiences, enabling user-generated content and many-to-many communication.

Functions and effects

Scholars such as Harold Lasswell identified key functions of media: surveillance of the environment, correlation of society's parts, transmission of culture, and entertainment. Agenda-setting theory holds that media influence which issues the public considers important. Critics have raised concerns about media concentration, misinformation, advertising influence and the economics of attention.
