Sannyasa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sannyasi, recognisable by the ochre (saffron) robes symbolising renunciation of the world.

Sannyasa (Sanskrit: संन्यास, saṃnyāsa, "renunciation") is the life stage of formal renunciation within the Hindu tradition of the ashrama system. A sannyasi (male) or sannyasini (female) is one who has renounced worldly and material pursuits in order to devote their life wholly to spiritual realisation and the attainment of moksha (liberation from the cycle of rebirth).

The ashrama system

Classical Hindu texts divide an ideal life into four stages (ashramas): brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder), vanaprastha (forest-dweller or hermit, a stage of gradual withdrawal) and sannyasa. In the orthodox scheme, sannyasa was traditionally restricted mainly to Brahmin men in later life; in practice, renunciation has long been open to people of varied backgrounds, and some traditions allow it to be taken at any age by one gripped by dispassion (vairagya).

Rites and way of life

Entering sannyasa is marked by initiation (sannyasa diksha) from a guru: the renouncer performs their own symbolic funeral rites — signifying death to their former social identity — abandons the sacred thread and family ties, shaves the head (often keeping a tuft), and takes the ochre or saffron robe, a staff (danda) and a water pot (kamandalu). Many renouncers take a new name, typically ending in titles such as Saraswati, Puri, Giri, Tirtha or Bharati, according to the monastic order (notably the Dashanami Sampradaya founded in the tradition of Adi Shankaracharya).

The sannyasi owns little or nothing, begs for food, wanders or resides in a monastery (matha) or hermitage, and observes vows of celibacy, non-possession and non-violence. Their days are given to meditation, scriptural study, teaching and pilgrimage. Figures such as Swami Vivekananda carried the renouncer ideal to a global audience in the modern era.

Related traditions

Renunciation is also central to Jainism and Buddhism — the Buddha's own "going forth" from palace life is the paradigm — where monastic communities (sangha) institutionalised the renouncer life earlier and more inclusively than in most Hindu orders. Within Hinduism itself, the bhakti traditions produced renouncer-saints and orders with their own customs, dress and lineages.
