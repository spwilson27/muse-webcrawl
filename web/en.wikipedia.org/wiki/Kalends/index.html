Kalends

The kalends (Latin: kalendae) was the first day of each month in the Roman calendar. The word derives from calare, "to call out", because on that day the pontifices publicly announced the dates of the month's festivals and the number of days until the nones. The English word "calendar" comes from this term.

Reckoning of dates

Romans named dates by counting backwards, inclusively, from three fixed points in the month: the kalends (1st), the nones (5th or 7th) and the ides (13th or 15th). Thus 26 December was "the seventh day before the kalends of January".

Financial significance

The kalends was the day on which debts fell due and interest was paid; account books (kalendaria) took their name from it. The expression "at the Greek kalends" (ad Kalendas Graecas) meant "never", since the Greeks had no kalends — a phrase used, among others, by Suetonius of Augustus.

Religious observance

Each kalends was sacred to Juno, and the first kalends of the year, in January, had particular festivity. The kalends of January were celebrated with gift-giving (strenae), a custom ancestral to modern New Year presents.
