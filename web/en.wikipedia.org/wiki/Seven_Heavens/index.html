Seven Heavens

The Seven Heavens is a part of religious cosmology found in some religions and mythologies, such as Islam, Judaism and Hinduism, and in ancient Mesopotamian and Greek thought. The concept holds that the heaven or sky is divided into seven distinct levels or spheres, often associated with the seven classical "planets" visible to the naked eye: the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn.

Mesopotamian and Jewish tradition

The idea of multiple heavens appears in ancient Mesopotamian cosmology and passed into Judaism, where rabbinic literature names the seven heavens — among them Vilon, Rakia and Shehaqim — with the highest, Araboth, containing the Throne of God. The Apostle Paul's reference to being caught up to the "third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2) reflects this scheme.

Islam

In Islam the Qur'an states that God created seven heavens, and Islamic tradition describes the Prophet Muhammad's night journey (Mi'raj) ascending through each heaven, meeting earlier prophets in turn. The lowest heaven is adorned with stars; the seventh is the limit beyond which lies the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary (Sidrat al-Muntaha).

Hinduism and other traditions

Hindu cosmology describes a series of lokas or worlds stacked above the earth, commonly grouped in sevens, with Satyaloka, the world of Brahmā, at the summit. Medieval Christian cosmology, synthesised with Ptolemaic astronomy, likewise placed seven planetary spheres beneath the firmament and the Empyrean, an arrangement reflected in Dante's Paradiso.
