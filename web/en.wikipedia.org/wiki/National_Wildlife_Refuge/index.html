National Wildlife Refuge

System: National Wildlife Refuge System
Administered by: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
First refuge: Pelican Island, Florida (1903)
Number: More than 560 refuges

A National Wildlife Refuge is a protected area of the United States managed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as part of the National Wildlife Refuge System, a network of lands and waters set aside for the conservation of fish, wildlife and plants. The system's mission puts wildlife conservation first: other public uses are allowed only when compatible with that purpose.

History

President Theodore Roosevelt established the first refuge, Pelican Island in Florida, by executive order in 1903 to protect egrets and other wading birds from plume hunters. The system grew through the Migratory Bird Conservation Act, Duck Stamp funding from 1934, and major statutes including the National Wildlife Refuge System Administration Act of 1966 and the Improvement Act of 1997, which designated six priority public uses: hunting, fishing, wildlife observation, photography, environmental education and interpretation.

Scope

Today the system includes more than 560 refuges and some 850 million acres, the great majority in Alaska, together with vast marine national monuments. Refuges protect habitats from Arctic tundra and prairie potholes to desert wetlands and coral atolls, and are found in every state.
