George Armstrong Custer (5 December 1839 – 25 June 1876) was a United States Army officer and cavalry commander in the American Civil War and the American Indian Wars. He rose to fame as a flamboyant Union general during the Civil War and was killed, along with all the men of his immediate command, at the Battle of the Little Bighorn — remembered as "Custer's Last Stand".

Civil War

Custer graduated last in his class at West Point in 1861, just as the Civil War began. As a cavalry officer he distinguished himself by aggressive, daring leadership, earning brevet promotions that made him, at 23, one of the youngest generals in the Union Army. His Michigan Cavalry Brigade fought at Gettysburg, in Sheridan's Shenandoah Valley campaign, and at Appomattox, where Custer received the first flag of truce from the Confederate army.

Indian Wars

After the war Custer became lieutenant colonel of the newly formed 7th Cavalry. He led campaigns on the Great Plains, including the controversial 1868 attack on Black Kettle's Cheyenne village at the Washita River. In 1874 his expedition into the Black Hills — sacred to the Lakota and guaranteed to them by treaty — confirmed the presence of gold, triggering a rush that helped ignite the Great Sioux War of 1876.

Little Bighorn and legacy

On 25 June 1876, attacking a large encampment of Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors led by chiefs including Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse, Custer divided his regiment; the five companies under his direct command were annihilated. Custer's image was afterwards promoted by his widow, Elizabeth Custer, in bestselling memoirs, and he remains one of the most debated figures of the American West — celebrated as a gallant hero or condemned as a reckless glory-seeker whose campaigns targeted Native peoples.
