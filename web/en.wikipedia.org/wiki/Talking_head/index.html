Talking head

A talking head is a broadcasting industry term for a person, usually an expert or pundit, who appears on television or in film speaking directly to the audience, typically shown in close-up or medium shot from the shoulders up. By extension, the term is used pejoratively for commentators — especially political pundits on news and discussion programmes — and for documentary sequences composed of such interview shots.

Uses

Broadcast journalism

In television news, talking heads are guests or analysts who give opinion and background, often from a remote studio or via satellite link, with the speaker's name and affiliation shown in a lower-third caption. Cable news formats rely heavily on panels of talking heads debating current events; the phrase "talking head" is frequently used dismissively to suggest superficial or predictable commentary.

Documentary film

Documentary filmmakers use talking-head interviews as a standard expository device, intercutting testimony with archive footage and observational material. Critics since the cinéma vérité movement have sometimes criticised over-reliance on talking heads as visually unambitious, while filmmakers such as Errol Morris developed devices like the Interrotron to have subjects address the camera directly. Retrospective series such as VH1's I Love the... franchise popularised a comic talking-head format with celebrity commentators.

Video communication

The term also applies to video conferencing and online video: a framing showing only a speaker's head and shoulders, and to early experiments in video compression — the "talking head" sequences used to test low-bitrate codecs, such as the standard "Miss America" and "Claire" test videos.

In popular culture

The American rock band Talking Heads took their name from the term after reading it in a television guide; musician David Byrne later played on the phrase in the concert film Stop Making Sense.
