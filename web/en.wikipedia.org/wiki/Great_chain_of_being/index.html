Great chain of being

The great chain of being (Latin: scala naturae, "ladder of nature") is a hierarchical structure of all matter and life, believed in medieval and early modern thought to have been decreed by God. The chain begins with God and descends through angels, humans, animals, plants, and minerals, each rank subdivided into further levels. It strongly influenced Western philosophy, theology, and natural history from antiquity until the 18th and 19th centuries.

Origins

The idea derives from Plato's principle of plenitude — that the universe, being the work of a perfect creator, contains every possible kind of being — and from Aristotle's classification of nature by degrees of soul and complexity in works such as the History of Animals. Neoplatonists such as Plotinus organized these notions into a continuous descending scale from the One.

Principles

The classic account, analyzed by historian Arthur O. Lovejoy in The Great Chain of Being (1936), rests on three principles: plenitude (every possible form of existence is realized), continuity (the ranks shade into one another without gaps), and gradation (beings form a single ordered hierarchy). Humans stood between angels and animals, and social hierarchies were often justified as reflections of the cosmic order.

Decline

The static chain was undermined by geological evidence for extinction, by the temporalization of the scale into ideas of development and progress, and ultimately by Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection, which replaced the ladder with a branching tree of life. The related medieval concept of the ladder of nature nevertheless survives as an influence on taxonomy and on popular images of a "march of progress."
