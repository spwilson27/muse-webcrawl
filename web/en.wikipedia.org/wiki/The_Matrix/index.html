The Matrix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Neo in a black coat and sunglasses in the lobby, in the bullet-time still that froze an era's idea of cool]

The Matrix is a 1999 science fiction action film written and directed by the Wachowskis. Keanu Reeves plays Neo, a hacker who learns that the world of 1999 is a simulated reality — the Matrix — built by machines that farm unconscious humans for energy, and that he may be 'the One' prophesied to free humankind. With Laurence Fishburne's Morpheus and Carrie-Anne Moss's Trinity, it fused cyberpunk, Hong Kong action choreography (Yuen Woo-ping's wire work), anime and philosophy into a phenomenon grossing over $460 million and winning four Academy Awards.

Its visual signature — 'bullet time' (a ring of still cameras creating frozen-time pans), cascading green code, black leather and slow-motion gunplay — was instantly everywhere, from parodies to a thousand imitators. The red pill/blue pill choice became a universal (and hotly contested) political metaphor.

Ideas and making

The Wachowskis pitched the film with a comic-book storyboard and required the cast to read Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation (a hollowed copy serves as Neo's hiding place on screen) — while the film's debts run equally to Descartes, Plato's cave, Gnosticism and Ghost in the Shell. Shot in Sydney, its effects (by John Gaeta's team) genuinely advanced the art: bullet time was built for it.

Sequels and afterlife

Two sequels filmed back-to-back (2003) disappointed relative to the original's myth and a fourth (Resurrections, 2021) deconstructed the whole enterprise. The original's standing is untouched: it is the rare blockbuster that made philosophy seminar material, and its anxieties — simulation, control, waking up — have only sharpened in the social-media age.
