The Battle of Roncevaux Pass (also Roncesvalles) was fought on 15 August 778, when the rearguard of Charlemagne's army, returning from an expedition into Muslim Spain, was ambushed and annihilated in the Pyrenees by Basque forces. Among the dead was Hruodland (Roland), prefect of the Breton March — whose legendary last stand was immortalised three centuries later in the Chanson de Roland, one of the oldest works of French literature.

Historical events

Charlemagne had invaded the Ebro valley in 778 at the invitation of dissident Muslim governors, but failed to take Zaragoza and withdrew after destroying the walls of Pamplona — an act that likely provoked the Basques. As the Frankish column crossed the Pyrenees, Basque fighters, lightly armed and knowing the terrain, attacked the baggage train and rearguard in the narrow pass, killed them to the last man according to the royal annals, looted the baggage, and dispersed. Charlemagne's biographer Einhard names the fallen, including Roland, and attributes the disaster to the terrain and the enemy's mobility.

Legend

In the Song of Roland (c. 1100), the ambushers became 400,000 Saracens, and Roland a peerless hero who, betrayed by his stepfather Ganelon, refuses to blow his horn (the olifant) until too late, then dies facing the enemy with his sword Durendal. The epic transformed a frontier skirmish into a foundational myth of Christian chivalry and French identity. A second battle of Roncesvalles in 824 led to the establishment of the Kingdom of Pamplona.
