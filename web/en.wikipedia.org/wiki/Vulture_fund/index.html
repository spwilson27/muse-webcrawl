Vulture fund

A vulture fund is a hedge fund, private equity fund, or other investment fund that invests in debt considered to be very weak or in default, such as that of bankrupt or near-bankrupt companies and distressed sovereign states. The name is a metaphor: like vultures, such funds are said to circle dying issuers, buying their debts for a fraction of face value in the secondary market and then seeking to recover the full amount — plus interest and costs — through negotiation or litigation.

Strategy

Vulture funds buy distressed securities at steep discounts, betting that restructuring or litigation will return more than the purchase price. In corporate debt, investors may convert debt into equity and take control of the reorganised company. In sovereign debt, "holdout" creditors refuse to participate in a restructuring accepted by most bondholders, then sue for full payment. A landmark case involved Elliott Management's affiliate NML Capital against Argentina: after Argentina's 2001 default, NML bought bonds cheaply, rejected exchanges in 2005 and 2010, and won favourable rulings in New York courts — including an interpretation of the pari passu clause that blocked Argentina from paying restructured bondholders, precipitating a further default in 2014 — before settling in 2016 for about $2.4 billion.

Criticism and defence

Critics, including the IMF, World Bank, and many governments, argue that vulture funds undermine debt relief for poor countries — cases involving Zambia and the Republic of Congo prompted legislation such as the UK's Debt Relief (Developing Countries) Act 2010 — and obstruct orderly restructurings. Defenders respond that secondary markets provide liquidity, that funds merely enforce contracts voluntarily made, and that their activity disciplines profligate borrowers. Several jurisdictions have since introduced collective action clauses and anti-holdout measures in sovereign bond contracts.
