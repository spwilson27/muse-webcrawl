Hepatitis C

SpecialtyInfectious disease, gastroenterology, hepatology
SymptomsOften none; fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, jaundice in later stages
CausesHepatitis C virus (HCV), spread mainly by blood-to-blood contact
TreatmentDirect-acting antivirals (e.g., sofosbuvir-based regimens)
Frequency~50 million people chronically infected worldwide

Hepatitis C is an infectious disease caused by the hepatitis C virus (HCV) that primarily affects the liver. During initial infection people often have mild or no symptoms. Over many years it frequently leads to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and liver cancer. The virus is spread mainly through blood-to-blood contact associated with intravenous drug use, poorly sterilised medical equipment, needlestick injuries, and transfusions (now rare in countries that screen blood).

Signs and symptoms

Only about 15–30% of people develop noticeable symptoms during acute infection — fatigue, nausea, muscle aches, and sometimes jaundice. Roughly 70–85% of those infected develop chronic hepatitis C, which is usually silent for decades while scarring accumulates. About 10–30% of chronically infected people develop cirrhosis over 20–30 years, with risks of liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma; extrahepatic manifestations include cryoglobulinemia and an association with non-Hodgkin lymphoma.

Cause and transmission

HCV is a small, enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus of the family Flaviviridae, genus Hepacivirus. It has at least six major genotypes with differing geographic distributions. Transmission requires contact with infected blood; sexual and mother-to-child transmission occur but are comparatively uncommon. There is no vaccine, owing to the virus's high variability.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with an antibody test for anti-HCV, followed by an HCV RNA test to confirm active infection and measure viral load; genotyping may guide therapy.

Treatment

Modern treatment uses direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — combinations such as sofosbuvir/velpatasvir or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir — taken orally for 8–12 weeks and curing more than 95% of cases with few side effects. These replaced older interferon-based regimens that were poorly tolerated and far less effective.

History

In the 1970s Harvey J. Alter showed that most post-transfusion hepatitis was neither hepatitis A nor B ("non-A, non-B hepatitis"). In 1989 a team at Chiron led by Qui-Lim Choo, with Michael Houghton, isolated the virus's genome. Alter, Houghton, and Charles M. Rice received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of the hepatitis C virus.
