Leishmaniasis is a wide array of clinical manifestations caused by parasites of the trypanosome genus Leishmania. It is generally spread through the bite of phlebotomine sandflies, specifically the genera Phlebotomus in the Old World and Lutzomyia in the New World. More than twenty Leishmania species can infect humans.

Forms

Cutaneous leishmaniasis, the most common form, causes skin sores that may leave disfiguring scars. Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis destroys the mucous membranes of the nose, mouth and throat. Visceral leishmaniasis, known as kala-azar ("black fever"), attacks internal organs — with fever, weight loss and enlargement of the spleen and liver — and is fatal in over 95% of untreated cases.

Epidemiology and treatment

The disease is endemic in some 90 countries across the tropics, subtropics and southern Europe, with an estimated 700,000 to 1 million new cases annually; it is classified as a neglected tropical disease. Treatments include liposomal amphotericin B, miltefosine and pentavalent antimonials, varying by species and region. No vaccine is in routine human use, so prevention relies on vector control and protection from sandfly bites.
