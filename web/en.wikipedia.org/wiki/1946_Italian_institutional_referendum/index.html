The 1946 Italian institutional referendum (referendum istituzionale) was held on 2 June 1946 to decide whether Italy should remain a monarchy under the House of Savoy or become a republic. Voters chose the republic by 54.3% to 45.7%, ending the Kingdom of Italy, which had existed since unification in 1861. The same day, Italians elected a Constituent Assembly to draft the constitution of the new Italian Republic. It was the first national vote in Italy in which women participated.

Background

The monarchy had been gravely compromised by its association with Mussolini's Fascist regime and by Victor Emmanuel III's flight from Rome after the 1943 armistice. In 1944 the King transferred his powers to his son Umberto as Lieutenant General of the Realm, and on 9 May 1946 he abdicated in Umberto's favour in a last attempt to save the throne. King Umberto II reigned for just over a month, earning the nickname Re di Maggio ("the May King").

Voting showed a deep geographical split: the north and centre voted mainly for the republic, while the south — Sicily, Campania and Lazio included — voted predominantly to keep the monarchy, with Naples returning an especially strong monarchist majority.

Aftermath

Monarchists alleged counting irregularities and appealed to the Court of Cassation, but after tense days the government of Alcide De Gasperi proclaimed the result, and the Court confirmed the republican victory on 18 June. Umberto II left for exile in Portugal on 13 June. The Constitution of 1948 formally established the Republic and barred male members of the House of Savoy from Italian soil — a provision repealed only in 2002. Italy's national day, the Festa della Repubblica, is celebrated every 2 June in commemoration of the vote.
