Princeps

Augustus, the first and defining princeps of Rome.

Princeps (plural: principes) is a Latin word meaning "first in time or order; the first, foremost, chief, the most eminent, distinguished, or noble; the first person". In the Roman Republic, the princeps senatus was the leading member of the Senate. Augustus adopted princeps as his preferred style from 27 BC, and it became the defining title of the early Roman emperors' constitutional position — the period of the "Principate" (27 BC – AD 284), contrasted with the later "Dominate".

EtymologyLatin princeps, from primus + capio ("first to take")
Republican usePrinceps senatus — leader of the Senate
Imperial useFrom Augustus, 27 BC
Derivatives"Prince" in most European languages

Republican origins

Under the Republic, princeps denoted informal pre-eminence rather than a legal office. The princeps senatus, chosen by the censors from among ex-consuls, spoke first in debates and headed the Senate's membership roll. Figures such as Scipio Africanus and Marcus Aemilius Scaurus were described as princeps of the Senate and of the citizenry at large.

Augustus and the Principate

After his victory in the civil wars, Octavian deliberately avoided kingly or dictatorial titles. In the settlement of 27 BC he took the name Augustus, and styling himself simply princeps — "first citizen" — he presented his rule as leadership within a restored republic, even as he accumulated the powers of tribune, consul, and commander of the legions. Emperors down to Diocletian governed within this fiction; his reforms after AD 284, in which the emperor ruled openly as dominus ("lord"), mark the start of the Dominate.

Legacy

Via Old French, princeps gives English "prince" and cognates in most European languages. The word also survives in terms such as princeps edition (editio princeps, the first printed edition of a work).
