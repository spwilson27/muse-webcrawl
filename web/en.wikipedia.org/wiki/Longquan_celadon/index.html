Longquan celadon (Chinese: 龍泉青瓷) is a type of Chinese celadon-glazed stoneware produced in and around the city of Longquan in south-western Zhejiang province. The kilns there are among the most famous in Chinese ceramic history; their wares reached their peak under the Southern Song dynasty (1127–1279) and were exported in enormous quantities across Asia and as far as East Africa and the Middle East.

Characteristics

Longquan ware is prized for its thick, unctuous glazes in blue-green shades produced by iron in a reduction atmosphere. The finest Southern Song glazes are the powder-blue fenqing ("powder green") and plum-green meizi qing ("green plum") colours, often essentially opaque and crackle-free, applied in multiple layers over a pale grey body. Many shapes imitate archaic bronzes and jades — tripod incense burners, mallet vases and "lotus bowls" — reflecting court taste. The kilns around Dayao were associated with the legendary potter brothers Zhang Shengyi and Zhang Sheng'er: "elder brother" (Ge) ware with its pronounced crackle, and "younger brother" (Di) ware with smooth glazes.

Production and trade

Hundreds of kiln sites operated in the region, using long hillside "dragon kilns" (longyao). Wares travelled down the Ou River to Wenzhou for shipment abroad; Longquan celadons have been excavated from shipwrecks, including the Sinan wreck off Korea, and from port cities from Japan to Egypt. Production declined in quality and quantity in the later Ming period as blue-and-white porcelain from Jingdezhen took over export markets, but firing never wholly ceased.

Recognition

The traditional firing technology of Longquan celadon was inscribed in 2009 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — the first ceramic craft so listed.
