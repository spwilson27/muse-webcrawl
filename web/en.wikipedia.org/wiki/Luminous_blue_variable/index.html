Luminous blue variable

Luminous blue variables (LBVs) are massive, evolved stars that show unpredictable and sometimes dramatic variations in both their spectra and their brightness, with occasional giant outbursts in which the star ejects large amounts of mass. They are among the most luminous stars known, radiating millions of times the Sun's power, and are thought to be a short-lived transitional phase in the evolution of the most massive stars.

Characteristics

Confirmed LBVs include Eta Carinae, P Cygni, S Doradus (the prototype of the "S Doradus variables"), AG Carinae, and the Pistol Star. In their quiescent state they are hot, blue supergiants or hypergiants close to the Eddington limit; during outbursts they expand and cool, appearing as cooler A- or F-type supergiants while their total luminosity stays roughly constant. Rare "giant eruptions" such as Eta Carinae's Great Eruption of the 1840s can eject several solar masses and rival supernovae in energy — so-called "supernova impostors".

Evolutionary significance

LBV mass loss is believed to strip the hydrogen envelopes of very massive stars, allowing them to evolve into hydrogen-poor Wolf–Rayet stars before ending as core-collapse supernovae or collapsing directly to black holes. The Humphreys–Davidson limit, an observed upper luminosity boundary for cool supergiants, is associated with the onset of LBV instability.

Discovery

P Cygni was observed to flare in 1600, and S Doradus's variability was characterised in the early 20th century. Edwin Hubble and Allan Sandage identified five such "irregular variables" in M31 and M33 in 1953; the class was unified under the name "luminous blue variable" following work by Peter Conti in the 1980s.
