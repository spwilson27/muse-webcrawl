Mars

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun. The surface of Mars is orange-red because it is covered in iron(III) oxide dust — rust — giving it the nickname "the Red Planet". Mars is among the brightest objects in Earth's sky, and its high-contrast albedo features have made it a common subject of telescope viewing and of speculation about life for centuries.

Mars hosts Olympus Mons, the largest volcano and highest known mountain in the Solar System, and Valles Marineris, one of its largest canyon systems. Ancient river valleys, lakebeds and minerals that form in water show that early Mars was warmer and wetter, with a thicker atmosphere that has since largely been stripped away to space.

Exploration

Mariner 4 returned the first close-up photographs in 1965; Viking 1 and 2 landed in 1976 and searched for life with inconclusive results. Later missions include orbiters Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Express (ESA) and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and the NASA rovers Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity and Perseverance. Perseverance, landed in Jezero Crater in February 2021, is collecting samples for a possible future return to Earth and carried the Ingenuity helicopter, the first aircraft to fly on another planet. China's Tianwen-1 mission landed the Zhurong rover in 2021.

Habitability and human missions

No conclusive evidence of past or present Martian life has been found, though subsurface ice, seasonal methane variations and organic molecules keep the question open. Human missions to Mars are a long-term goal of space agencies and companies; NASA's Artemis programme to return to the Moon is framed as a stepping stone, and SpaceX's Starship is being developed with Mars ambitions.

Moons

Mars has two small, irregular moons, Phobos and Deimos, probably captured asteroids or debris from an impact. Phobos orbits so close that it is slowly spiralling inward and will eventually break up into a ring.
