Pine tar is a form of wood tar produced by the high-temperature carbonization of pine wood in anoxic conditions (dry distillation or destructive distillation). It is a sticky, dark brown or black liquid with a distinctive smoky odour, used since antiquity as a wood preservative, waterproofing agent, and mild antiseptic.

Production and history

Pine tar was traditionally made by burning pine stumps and roots slowly in covered pits or kilns and collecting the tar that ran out. It was an essential naval store: Viking and later European shipbuilders used it to preserve rigging, ropes, and hulls against rot and seawater. Scandinavian tar production, especially from Finland and Sweden ("Stockholm tar"), was a major export industry, and naval demand for tar was one reason Britain encouraged tar production in its American colonies — an association preserved in the nickname "Tar Heels" for North Carolinians.

Uses

Pine tar is still used to preserve wooden boats and roofs, in pine tar soap and shampoos for skin conditions such as psoriasis and eczema, and in veterinary care for horse hooves. In baseball, batters apply pine tar to bat handles for grip; its use is regulated, and the famous 1983 "Pine Tar Incident" involving George Brett arose when his home run was initially disallowed because tar extended too far up his bat.

Composition

Pine tar is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, phenols, resin acids, and turpentine-like compounds, varying with the wood and the production method.
