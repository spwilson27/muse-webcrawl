Punched card

A punched card (or punch card) is a piece of stiff paper that holds digital data through the presence or absence of holes in predefined positions. Punched cards were widely used throughout the 19th century to control textile looms and, from the late 19th century and through much of the 20th century, as the primary medium for data input, processing, and storage in unit record equipment and early computers.

History

Card-controlled looms, culminating in the Jacquard machine of 1804, inspired Herman Hollerith, whose punched-card tabulating machines processed the 1890 United States census. Hollerith's company eventually became IBM. IBM's 80-column card format, introduced in 1928, became the industry standard, encoding one character per column. Generations of programmers wrote source code one line per card, submitting decks to be read by card readers and processed by mainframes; the terms "Hollerith card" and "IBM card" became synonymous with the medium.

Formats and decline

Other formats included the 90-column Remington Rand (UNIVAC) card and marked-sense cards. From the 1970s, interactive terminals and magnetic media displaced cards, though punched cards lingered in voting systems — where "hanging chads" achieved notoriety in the 2000 United States presidential election recount in Florida.
