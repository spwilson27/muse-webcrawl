Orphan tsunami

An orphan tsunami is a tsunami whose parent earthquake is unknown at the time the wave is reported — there is no obvious local seismic event to account for the inundation. The term is the mirror image of an "orphan earthquake", an earthquake known only from the tsunami it generated. Orphan tsunamis are typically the product of distant subduction-zone megathrust earthquakes whose waves cross an entire ocean before striking a coast where nobody felt the shaking.

The 1700 Cascadia tsunami in Japan

The classic example is the tsunami that struck the Pacific coast of Japan on 26–27 January 1700. Japanese records describe waves up to several metres high damaging villages in Iwate, Miyagi and Ibaraki prefectures, flooding rice paddies and destroying houses, yet no earthquake was felt in Japan itself. Because the event had no local parent, it was long treated as a puzzle. In the 1990s, researchers matched the Japanese records with geological evidence and Indigenous oral traditions from the Pacific Northwest of North America, showing the tsunami had been generated by a magnitude ~9 earthquake on the Cascadia subduction zone off the coast of present-day British Columbia, Washington and Oregon. The precise Japanese arrival times allowed the earthquake's date and size to be pinned down — an achievement sometimes called "the orphan tsunami" detective story.

Causes and significance

Orphan tsunamis can arise from trans-oceanic megathrust earthquakes, from submarine landslides, or from very slow "tsunami earthquakes" whose shaking is too weak to be noticed locally. They matter for hazard assessment because a coast may be inundated with no felt warning: modern tsunami warning centres therefore rely on deep-ocean pressure sensors and seismic networks rather than local shaking alone. Records of historical orphan tsunamis are used to identify active subduction zones and to estimate the recurrence interval of giant earthquakes.
