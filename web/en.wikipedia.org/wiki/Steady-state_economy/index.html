Steady-state economy

A steady-state economy is an economy made up of a constant stock of physical wealth (capital) and a constant population size. In effect, such an economy does not grow in the course of time. The term usually refers to the national economy of a particular country, but it is also applicable to the economic system of a city, a region, or the entire world. Early in the history of economic thought, classical economist Adam Smith of the 18th century developed the concept of a stationary state of an economy: Smith believed that any national economy in the world would sooner or later settle in a final state of stationarity.

Since the 1970s, the concept of a steady-state economy has been associated mainly with the work of leading ecological economist Herman Daly. As Daly's concept of a steady state includes the ecological analysis of natural resource flows through the economy, his concept differs from the classical notion of a stationary state. One other difference is that Daly recommends immediate political action to establish the steady state by imposing permanent government restrictions on all resource use, whereas economists of the classical period believed that the final stationary state of any economy would evolve by itself without any government intervention.

Throughput of matter and energy is kept constant and within ecological limits in a steady-state economy.

Definition

Herman Daly defines a steady-state economy as an economic system made up of a constant stock of physical wealth (capital) and a constant population size, maintained by a throughput of matter and energy that is as low as possible and within regenerative and assimilative ecological limits. The steady state is not static in every respect: births equal deaths, production equals depreciation, and qualitative development (such as technological improvement and better design) continues even though quantitative growth in throughput does not.

Historical antecedents

The idea of a stationary state appears in classical economics. Adam Smith anticipated that economic growth would eventually be checked by the scarcity of land and resources. David Ricardo developed a theory in which accumulation drives the economy toward a stationary state as profits fall. John Stuart Mill, in his Principles of Political Economy (1848), welcomed the prospective stationary state of capital and population, arguing that it could coincide with "a very considerable improvement" in the art of living, once minds ceased to be engrossed by the struggle for riches. In the 20th century, John Maynard Keynes speculated in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930) that within a hundred years humanity might solve its economic problem and turn to leisure and culture.

Daly's steady state

Herman Daly, formerly an economist at the World Bank, became the leading modern proponent of the steady state. He proposed three institutional arrangements to bring it about: (1) stabilising population by transferring the right to bear children through a system of tradable birth licenses; (2) stabilising the stock of capital by a system of tradable depletion quotas on resource use; and (3) limiting income and wealth inequality by setting minimum and maximum incomes and a maximum on wealth. Daly distinguished growth (quantitative increase in throughput) from development (qualitative improvement), arguing that the economy should develop without growing, much as the Earth develops without expanding materially.

Degrowth and related concepts

The steady-state concept is closely related to degrowth, a movement that advocates reducing production and consumption in wealthy countries to bring economies back within ecological limits; a steady state might be the destination once an equitable downscaling has occurred. It also connects with ecological economics, post-growth economics, the economics of Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen on entropy, and E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful (1973). Donut economics, proposed by Kate Raworth, similarly seeks an economic system operating between a social foundation and an ecological ceiling.

Criticism

Critics argue that a steady-state economy would be difficult to achieve politically, that it might entrench existing inequalities if distribution is not addressed, and that technological progress could decouple growth from resource use, making physical limits less binding. Others contend that in a world of poverty, halting growth in aggregate would harm the poor unless accompanied by strong redistribution. Supporters reply that the steady state explicitly includes distributive institutions and that continued aggregate growth in already wealthy economies is ecologically unsustainable.
