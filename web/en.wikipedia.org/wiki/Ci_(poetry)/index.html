Ci (Chinese: 詞) is a type of lyric Chinese poetry that developed in the Tang dynasty and reached its height in the Song dynasty. Unlike the regulated verse of shi poetry, ci were written to pre-existing musical tunes, so their lines are of deliberately unequal length, following fixed patterns called cipai (詞牌, "tune names"). Each tune prescribes the number of characters, the pattern of line lengths, tonal arrangement, and rhyme scheme of the poem.

History

The genre grew out of popular songs and the music of the entertainment quarters in the late Tang. The earliest surviving collection is the Huajian ji ("Among the Flowers"), compiled in 940. Wen Tingyun was among the first literati poets to write ci in quantity. In the Song dynasty the form became a major vehicle of elite literary expression; masters such as Liu Yong, Su Shi, Zhou Bangyan, Li Qingzhao, and Xin Qiji transformed it from a primarily romantic song form into a genre capable of historical reflection, philosophy, and patriotic lament.

Form

More than 800 tune patterns are recorded. Poems are usually divided into two stanzas (shangpian and xiapian), though some tunes have one, three, or four. Because the lyrics were composed to fit melodies, ci are sometimes called "song words" or "lyrics to tunes." Most melodies are now lost, but the textual patterns survive and poets continued to write to them for a millennium.

Style and themes

Early ci tended toward the graceful and restrained wanyue style, often in a feminine voice. Su Shi and Xin Qiji pioneered the bolder haofang ("heroic abandon") manner. The Qing-dynasty critic's canon, and anthologies such as the Song Ci San Bai Shou ("Three Hundred Song Ci"), kept the form central to Chinese literary culture.
