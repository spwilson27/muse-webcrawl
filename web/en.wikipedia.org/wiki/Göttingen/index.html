Göttingen

Göttingen (German: [ˈɡœtɪŋən]) is a university town in Lower Saxony, Germany, on the Leine river. With a population of roughly 120,000, of whom a large fraction are students, the town is dominated by the University of Göttingen, founded in 1737 by George II, King of Great Britain and Elector of Hanover.

History

The town is first mentioned in 953 as the village of Gutingi. It received town rights around 1200 and flourished as a member of the Hanseatic League before declining during the Thirty Years' War. The founding of the university in the 18th century reversed its fortunes. Göttingen became a world centre of mathematics and physics: Carl Friedrich Gauss, Bernhard Riemann, David Hilbert, and Felix Klein all worked there, and in the 1920s the university was central to the creation of quantum mechanics under Max Born, Werner Heisenberg, and others. In 1837 the dismissal of the "Göttingen Seven" professors by King Ernest Augustus I became a famous cause for academic freedom.

Present day

Göttingen escaped heavy bombing in the Second World War, so its medieval old town, ringed by a preserved rampart walk, survives largely intact. The town's landmark is the Gänseliesel ("Goose Girl") fountain of 1901, traditionally kissed by newly graduated doctoral students. The university, the Max Planck Society institutes, and a cluster of scientific publishers and instrument firms make Göttingen one of Germany's leading research locations; more than forty Nobel laureates have lived or worked there.
