Bicycle gearing

Bicycle gearing is the aspect of a bicycle drivetrain that determines the relation between the cadence (the rate at which the rider pedals) and the rate at which the drive wheel turns. A bicycle's gearing allows the rider to trade force for speed: low gears make climbing easier at low speed, while high gears permit fast riding on the flat at the cost of greater pedalling force.

Chainrings and a rear cassette provide a range of ratios.

Gear ratios

The gear ratio is the ratio of teeth on the chainring to teeth on the rear sprocket, multiplied by the wheel diameter in traditional measures such as "gear inches", or expressed as metres of development — the distance travelled per crank revolution. Gain ratio, popularised by Sheldon Brown, additionally accounts for crank length.

Mechanisms

The most common system uses derailleurs to move the chain between multiple chainrings and a cassette of sprockets, giving as many as 30 nominal combinations on some bicycles. Alternatives include hub gears, which enclose planetary gears in the rear hub; single-speed and fixed-gear drivetrains; belt drives; and continuously variable transmissions.

Range and stepping

Two qualities matter: the overall range between the lowest and highest gear, and the steps between successive gears. Touring and mountain bicycles favour wide ranges with low climbing gears, while racing bicycles use closer steps at higher ratios.
