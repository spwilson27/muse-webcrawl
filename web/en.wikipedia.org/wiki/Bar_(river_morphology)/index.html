Bar (river morphology)

A bar in a river is an elevated region of sediment, typically sand or gravel, that has been deposited by the flow. Bars form within channels where the local competence of the current drops enough for transported material to settle out. They are characteristic features of braided and meandering rivers, and they continually migrate, grow, and erode as discharge and sediment supply change.

Types of bars

Point bars form on the inside (convex bank) of meander bends, where flow velocity is lowest and deposition occurs as the channel migrates outward. Mid-channel bars develop where a channel widens or where flow competence suddenly decreases; vegetated and stabilized mid-channel bars may become permanent islands, splitting the channel in the braided pattern. Unit bars are large, simple depositional forms that migrate downstream in trains. At river mouths, mouth bars form where the current's energy is dissipated on entering standing water, and along coasts, wave action builds shore-parallel barrier bars and spits.

Formation

Bar formation is governed by the balance between sediment supply and transport capacity. When the load of bed material exceeds what the flow can carry—because of a local decrease in slope, velocity, or depth—deposition begins. In meanders, helicoidal secondary flow sweeps bedload toward the inner bank, accreting the point bar laterally as the bend migrates. Gravel bars commonly show downstream fining and imbricated clasts; sand bars display current ripples and cross-bedding that record paleoflow directions used by sedimentologists to reconstruct ancient rivers.

Significance

Bars influence navigation, flood behavior, and river ecology. Exposed gravel bars provide nesting habitat for birds and spawning substrate for fish, and point-bar sequences preserved in the rock record are important hydrocarbon reservoirs because of their porous, laterally accreting sand bodies.
