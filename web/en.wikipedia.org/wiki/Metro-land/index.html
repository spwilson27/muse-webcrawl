Metro-land

Metro-land is a name given to the suburban areas north-west of London that were developed in the early 20th century by the Metropolitan Railway. The term was coined in 1915 by the railway's marketing department in its annual Metro-land guide, promoting the countryside of Middlesex, Hertfordshire, and Buckinghamshire along the Metropolitan line as a rural retreat within commuting distance of the capital — "a land of lovely villages, beautiful beechwoods and pleasant country lanes."

Development

Uniquely among the Underground companies, the Metropolitan was permitted to retain surplus land and develop it for housing through its Metropolitan Railway Country Estates Company (founded 1919). Estates were built at places such as Wembley Park, Ruislip, Pinner, Rickmansworth, Chorleywood, and Chesham, typically featuring mock-Tudor, tree-lined streets of semi-detached houses marketed to clerks and professionals seeking a healthier life outside the smoky city. By the 1930s the Metropolitan line had effectively created a new commuter suburbia.

In culture

Metro-land was celebrated by John Betjeman, whose 1973 television documentary Metro-Land traced the line from Baker Street to Buckinghamshire and helped fix the term in affectionate public memory. The concept also inspired satire of suburban life, as in Evelyn Waugh's Vile Bodies and in Betjeman's own ambivalent verse.
