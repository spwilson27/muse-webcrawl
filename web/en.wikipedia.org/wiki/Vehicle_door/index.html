Vehicle door

A vehicle door is a movable panel used to cover an opening in the body of a vehicle, allowing access to the interior for occupants and cargo. Car doors typically contain a window (which may be lowered into the door structure), a latch and lock, hinges, an interior trim panel with controls, and side-impact protection beams.

Conventional doors and components

Most cars use front-hinged doors that swing outward. A modern door is a complex assembly: outer and inner steel or aluminium pressings, a window regulator (manual or electric), speakers, wiring looms, a side airbag in some models, weatherstripping, and a steel intrusion beam designed to resist side collisions. Door checks limit the swing and hold the door at intermediate positions.

Types

Numerous alternatives to the conventional door exist. Suicide doors are hinged at the rear (coach doors in Rolls-Royce terminology). Scissor doors, popularised by the Lamborghini Countach, rotate upward and forward. Gullwing doors are hinged at the roof, as on the Mercedes-Benz 300SL. Butterfly doors move up and outward. Sliding doors are standard on vans and minivans, running on tracks along the body side. Falcon wing doors, used on the Tesla Model X, are double-hinged gullwing variants with sensors for obstacle clearance. Canopy doors lift the entire roof section as one piece.

Safety

Doors are a regulated safety component: standards govern latch strength (to prevent ejection in crashes), side-impact resistance and, increasingly, protection of cyclists against "dooring" — opening a door into a rider's path. Child safety locks prevent rear doors being opened from inside, and many modern vehicles add soft-close latches and obstacle detection for powered doors.

Other vehicles

Buses and trains use pneumatically or electrically powered sliding or plug doors, often with sensitive edges. Aircraft doors are structural pressure vessels with complex locking mechanisms, while spacecraft and submarines use hatches rather than doors.
