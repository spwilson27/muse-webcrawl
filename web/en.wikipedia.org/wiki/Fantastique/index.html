Fantastique

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The fantastique is a French literary and cinematic genre (and mode) in which supernatural or uncanny events intrude into an otherwise realistic world. Unlike fantasy, whose secondary worlds accept magic as normal, the fantastique preserves uncertainty: for the theorist Tzvetan Todorov, the "fantastic" is defined by the hesitation experienced by a character (and the reader) who cannot decide whether an apparently impossible event has a natural or a supernatural explanation.

Literary history

The French fantastique grew from the Gothic and from E. T. A. Hoffmann's tales, which were enormously popular in France; foundational French texts include Charles Nodier's Smarra (1821), Théophile Gautier's stories, Prosper Mérimée's La Vénus d'Ille (1837), and the work of Gérard de Nerval and Villiers de l'Isle-Adam. Guy de Maupassant's Le Horla (1887) is often taken as the genre's masterpiece. In the twentieth century the fantastique shaded into surrealism and into the work of Belgian writers such as Jean Ray.

Cinema

In French film criticism, fantastique is an umbrella term covering horror, science fiction, and fantasy; the Festival de Gérardmer and the former Avoriaz festival were dedicated to it. French fantastique cinema spans Jean Cocteau's La Belle et la Bête (1946), Georges Franju's Les Yeux sans visage (1960), and a later wave of graphically extreme horror sometimes called the "New French Extremity".
