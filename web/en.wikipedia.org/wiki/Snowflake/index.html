Snowflake

A snowflake is a single ice crystal that has achieved a sufficient size, and may have amalgamated with others, which falls through the Earth's atmosphere as snow. Each flake nucleates around a dust particle in supersaturated air masses by attracting supercooled cloud water droplets, which freeze and accrete in crystal form. Complex shapes emerge as the flake moves through differing temperature and humidity regimes in the atmosphere; as a result, individual snowflakes differ in detail while following broadly identifiable classifications.

Formation

Snow crystals form when tiny supercooled cloud droplets freeze around a nucleus such as a dust or pollen particle. The initial crystal takes a hexagonal prism form because of the arrangement of water molecules in ice Ih. As the crystal grows, instabilities cause branches to sprout from the six corners, producing the familiar six-fold dendritic patterns; temperature and humidity determine whether growth produces plates, columns, needles or elaborate stellar dendrites.

Uniqueness

Popularly, it is said that no two snowflakes are alike. Because a growing flake's path through the cloud subjects it to an intricate, constantly changing sequence of environments, the probability of two large, complex flakes being identical is vanishingly small — though small, simple crystals can be effectively indistinguishable, and near-identical flakes have been produced in laboratory conditions.

Classification

Meteorologists classify snow crystals into systems of categories and subcategories — the Magono and Lee classification lists 80 distinct types — ranging from simple prisms and hexagonal plates to stellar dendrites and capped columns. Wilson Bentley, a Vermont farmer, was the first person to photograph individual snowflakes, attaching a microscope to a bellows camera beginning in 1885.
