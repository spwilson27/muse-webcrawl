Yarkovsky effect

The Yarkovsky effect is a force acting on a rotating body in space caused by the anisotropic emission of thermal photons, which carry momentum. It is usually considered in relation to meteoroids and small asteroids, whose orbits it can measurably change over long periods.

Mechanism

A rotating asteroid absorbs sunlight on its day side and re-radiates the heat after a delay, when the surface has rotated. Because the afternoon side is warmer than the morning side, more radiation is emitted in one direction, producing a tiny thrust. For a prograde rotator the effect adds energy to the orbit and pushes the asteroid outward; for a retrograde rotator it removes energy, spiralling the body inward.

Consequences

Named after the Polish engineer Ivan Osipovich Yarkovsky, who proposed it around 1900, the effect was first directly measured on the asteroid 6489 Golevka in 2003. It is a major mechanism delivering asteroids from the main belt into near-Earth space, and must be modelled to predict impact hazards accurately. A related phenomenon, the YORP effect, changes an object's spin rate.
