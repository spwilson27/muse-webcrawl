Sub-brown dwarf

A sub-brown dwarf or planetary-mass brown dwarf is an astronomical object that formed in the same manner as stars and brown dwarfs — by the gravitational collapse of a cloud of gas — but has a mass below the limit for the thermonuclear fusion of deuterium, roughly 13 Jupiter masses.

Definition

The distinction between giant planets and sub-brown dwarfs is based on formation mechanism rather than location: a sub-brown dwarf forms by cloud collapse, whereas a planet forms by accretion in a protoplanetary disk. Free-floating examples drift through interstellar space alone and are sometimes called rogue planets, though the term sub-brown dwarf emphasizes their star-like origin.

Examples and detection

Because they emit little light, sub-brown dwarfs are detected mainly in the infrared, in young star-forming regions where they are still warm from formation. Objects such as PSO J318.5−22 and members of young moving groups are studied to understand both star formation and giant-planet atmospheres.
