An interstellar object is an astronomical body, other than a star, located in interstellar space and not gravitationally bound to any star. The term applies most commonly to small bodies such as asteroids and comets that formed around other stars and were later ejected into the galaxy, where they drift until — rarely — they pass through the Solar System on unmistakably hyperbolic trajectories.

Detection

An interstellar visitor is recognised by an orbit with eccentricity clearly greater than 1 relative to the Sun, too extreme to result from planetary perturbations. The first confirmed example, ʻOumuamua, was found by the Pan-STARRS survey in October 2017, already outbound. The second, 2I/Borisov, discovered by amateur astronomer Gennady Borisov in August 2019, behaved as an ordinary comet and was observed for months. A third object, 3I/ATLAS, was discovered in July 2025 by the ATLAS survey.

Origin and significance

Interstellar objects are thought to be planetesimals ejected from young planetary systems during planet formation and migration; models suggest every star may eject trillions of such bodies, so the galaxy should contain vast numbers of them. Because they are samples of material formed around other stars, their composition — inferred from spectroscopy of bodies like Borisov, which proved surprisingly rich in carbon monoxide — offers a direct test of how other planetary systems formed. Statistical arguments from the detections imply that roughly one such object may be inside Neptune's orbit at any given time, and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's survey is expected to increase the discovery rate substantially.

Nomenclature

The International Astronomical Union created the "I" designation series for these objects, numbering them in order of discovery much like periodic comets: 1I/ʻOumuamua, 2I/Borisov, and 3I/ATLAS. Proper names may be assigned following the conventions for small bodies.
