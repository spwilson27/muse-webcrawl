Hyperbolic comet

A hyperbolic comet is a comet whose orbit has an eccentricity greater than 1, meaning its trajectory through the Solar System is an open hyperbola rather than a closed ellipse. Such a comet passes through the inner Solar System only once and is then ejected into interstellar space, never to return.

Orbital characteristics

Comets are conventionally classified by orbital eccentricity: periodic comets have eccentricities below 1 and return on elliptical orbits, while hyperbolic comets exceed 1. A slightly hyperbolic orbit can result when a long-period comet from the Oort cloud is perturbed by the gravity of Jupiter or another giant planet, gaining enough energy to escape the Sun entirely. Genuinely interstellar comets, such as 2I/Borisov, arrive already on strongly hyperbolic trajectories because they formed around other stars.

Detection

Because hyperbolic comets are visible only during their single passage near the Sun, they must be characterized quickly. Astronomers measure their positions over days to weeks to fit an orbit; if the best-fit eccentricity exceeds 1 by more than the measurement uncertainty, the comet is classified as hyperbolic. Modern surveys such as Pan-STARRS and the Catalina Sky Survey have greatly increased the rate of discovery.

Significance

Interstellar hyperbolic comets offer a rare chance to study material formed in other planetary systems. Spectroscopic observations reveal their composition, including water, carbon monoxide, and organic molecules, which can be compared with comets native to our own Solar System.
