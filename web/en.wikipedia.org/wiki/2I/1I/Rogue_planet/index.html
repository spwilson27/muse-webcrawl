A rogue planet, also called an interstellar planet, nomad planet, or free-floating planet, is an interstellar object of planetary mass that is not gravitationally bound to any star. Such objects either formed around a star and were subsequently ejected from their planetary system, or never became bound to a star or brown dwarf in the first place, collapsing directly from a cloud of gas and dust.

Discovery and population estimates

The first candidate rogue planets were reported in the early 2000s in young star-forming regions such as the Sigma Orionis cluster. Because rogue planets emit almost no light of their own, they are extremely difficult to detect directly. The most productive detection technique is gravitational microlensing, in which a rogue planet briefly magnifies the light of a background star as it passes in front of it.

A 2011 microlensing survey published in Nature estimated that there may be roughly twice as many Jupiter-mass rogue planets as main-sequence stars in the Milky Way. Later studies revised the numbers downward for the most massive objects, but surveys with the OGLE project have detected candidate Earth-mass rogue planets, suggesting that low-mass ejected worlds may be very numerous. The upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is expected to conduct a dedicated microlensing census of rogue planets.

Formation

Computer simulations of planetary system formation show that gravitational interactions between young giant planets, or between planets and a passing star, can eject planets into interstellar space. In the Solar System's early history it has been proposed that a fifth giant planet was ejected during the instability described by the Nice model. Alternatively, objects at the low-mass end of star formation may collapse directly, blurring the boundary between rogue planets and sub-brown dwarfs.

Atmosphere and habitability

Although a rogue planet receives no starlight, internal heat from formation and radioactive decay could keep a subsurface ocean liquid beneath an insulating ice layer, and a sufficiently thick hydrogen atmosphere could trap enough heat to maintain liquid water at the surface. Such environments have been speculated as possible refuges for life independent of any star.
