Comet

Comet

Composition: Ice, dust, and rocky material ("dirty snowball")

Parts: Nucleus, coma, ion tail, dust tail

Origins: Kuiper belt and Oort cloud

Famous examples: Halley, Hale–Bopp, Tempel–Tuttle

A comet is an icy small Solar System body that, when passing close to the Sun, warms and begins to release gases, a process called outgassing. This produces a glowing coma and sometimes a tail, features visible from Earth in the case of bright "great comets." Comet nuclei range from a few hundred metres to tens of kilometres across and are composed of loose collections of ice, dust, and small rocky particles.

Physical characteristics

Fred Whipple's "dirty snowball" model describes the nucleus as a mixture of ices (mostly water, with carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and others) and dust. As a comet approaches the Sun, solar heating sublimates the ices, dragging dust away. The solar wind and radiation pressure shape the released material into two tails: a curved dust tail and a straighter, bluish ion tail pointing directly away from the Sun.

Orbital characteristics

Short-period comets, with orbital periods under 200 years, originate mainly in the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune; Halley's Comet, with a period of about 76 years, is the most famous. Long-period comets come from the distant spherical Oort cloud, and may take millions of years to orbit the Sun. Interstellar comets such as 2I/Borisov arrive from other star systems on hyperbolic paths.

Comets in history and exploration

Comets were long regarded as omens; Halley's appears in the Bayeux Tapestry. Spacecraft have visited several comets: ESA's Giotto flew by Halley in 1986, NASA's Deep Impact struck Tempel 1 in 2005, and ESA's Rosetta orbited 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko and landed the Philae probe on it in 2014.
