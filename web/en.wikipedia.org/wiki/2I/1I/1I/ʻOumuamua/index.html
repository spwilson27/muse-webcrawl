ʻOumuamua is the first interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. Formally designated 1I/ʻOumuamua, it was discovered by Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope at Haleakalā Observatory, Hawaii, on 19 October 2017, approximately 40 days after it passed its closest point to the Sun on 9 September. When it was first observed, it was about 33 million kilometres (21 million miles) from Earth and already heading away from the Sun.

ʻOumuamua is a small object, estimated to be between 100 and 1,000 metres (300 and 3,000 feet) long, with its width and thickness at least five to ten times less. Based on its light curve, the object appears to be tumbling rather than rotating about a single fixed axis. Its name comes from the Hawaiian word ʻoumuamua, meaning "a messenger from afar arriving first" or "scout".

Observations

ʻOumuamua was first imaged on 19 October 2017 by the Pan-STARRS 1 telescope during a survey for near-Earth objects. The object was moving unusually fast and, once its orbit was computed, it became clear that its eccentricity of about 1.20 placed it on a hyperbolic trajectory, meaning it was not gravitationally bound to the Sun. It approached the Sun from roughly the direction of the star Vega in the constellation Lyra.

The object was initially classified as a comet (C/2017 U1), then as an asteroid (A/2017 U1), because no coma was detected even when it passed close to the Sun. On 6 November 2017, the International Astronomical Union announced the new designation 1I/ʻOumuamua, the first object ever classified with the "I" designation for interstellar objects.

Physical characteristics

Observations with telescopes including the Very Large Telescope indicated that ʻOumuamua varies dramatically in brightness, by a factor of about ten, with a period of roughly 7 to 8 hours. This implies an elongated shape, with a length-to-width ratio of at least 5:1 and possibly as high as 10:1, unlike any known large asteroid in the Solar System. Analysis of its light curve suggested it tumbles chaotically, likely excited by its acceleration through the inner Solar System.

A notable puzzle was the detection of a small non-gravitational acceleration as the object receded from the Sun, similar to the outgassing-driven acceleration seen in comets, yet no dust or gas tail was observed. Proposed explanations included outgassing of molecular hydrogen ice, nitrogen ice (similar to surface material on Pluto), or, more controversially, radiation pressure on an extremely thin object. The hydrogen iceberg hypothesis, proposed by astronomers at the University of Chicago and Yale, holds that ʻOumuamua consisted largely of hydrogen ice formed in a cold molecular cloud.

Origin

Based on its velocity relative to the Sun and the local standard of rest, ʻOumuamua's trajectory is consistent with an origin in the local galactic neighbourhood. It may have been ejected from an unknown planetary system during planet formation. Its relatively low velocity compared with nearby stars suggests it may be a comparatively young object, perhaps a few hundred million years old.
