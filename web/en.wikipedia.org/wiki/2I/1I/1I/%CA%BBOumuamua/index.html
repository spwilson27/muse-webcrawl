ʻOumuamua

ʻOumuamua is the first known interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. Discovered on 19 October 2017 by the Pan-STARRS telescope in Hawaii, it was designated 1I/2017 U1 — the "1I" marking it as the first identified interstellar visitor. Its Hawaiian name, suggested by the Pan-STARRS team, means roughly "a messenger from afar arriving first" or "scout".

Discovery19 October 2017, Pan-STARRS (Robert Weryk)
Designation1I/2017 U1
OrbitHyperbolic; eccentricity c. 1.2
Perihelion0.256 AU (9 September 2017)
SizeEstimated c. 100–400 m long, highly elongated or flattened

Observations

ʻOumuamua showed no visible coma or tail, yet its trajectory displayed a small non-gravitational acceleration away from the Sun, as if outgassing — though no gas or dust was detected. Its light curve varied dramatically, indicating tumbling rotation and an extreme shape, either a long cigar or a thin disc, and a reddish surface colour. It was already receding and too faint for detailed study within weeks.

Interpretations

Proposed explanations include a hydrogen or nitrogen iceberg fragment, a fractal dust aggregate, and ordinary cometary outgassing of invisible gases. The Harvard astronomer Avi Loeb controversially suggested an artificial light-sail origin, a claim most astronomers dispute. Whatever its nature, ʻOumuamua's passage spurred new surveys for interstellar objects, rewarded in 2019 with the clearly cometary 2I/Borisov.
