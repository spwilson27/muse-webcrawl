ʻOumuamua

1I/ʻOumuamua is the first known interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. Discovered by Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope on October 19, 2017, about 40 days after its closest approach to the Sun, it was already heading away and fading rapidly. Its name — Hawaiian for roughly "scout" or "first distant messenger" — reflects its discovery from Hawaii and its role as a first representative of interstellar visitors.

Properties

ʻOumuamua's light curve varied by a factor of about ten with a period near 8 hours, implying an extreme, tumbling, elongated shape hundreds of metres long but only tens of metres wide. It showed no coma, yet its trajectory exhibited a small but significant non-gravitational acceleration away from the Sun. Proposed explanations include outgassing of volatile ices (including hydrogen or nitrogen icebergs) and radiation pressure on a thin body; a minority of researchers, most prominently Avi Loeb, suggested artificial origin, a hypothesis most astronomers reject in favour of natural explanations.

Origin and legacy

Its inbound velocity matched the local standard of rest, suggesting it may have wandered the galaxy for a very long time. The detection prompted new observing campaigns and mission concepts — such as the European Space Agency's planned Comet Interceptor — designed to chase future interstellar visitors.
