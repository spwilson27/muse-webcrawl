Interstellar object

An interstellar object is an astronomical body — such as an asteroid, comet, or rogue planet — located in interstellar space that is not gravitationally bound to a star. Such objects originate in other planetary systems and pass through the Solar System on hyperbolic trajectories, typically never to return. The term also covers objects that originated in the Solar System but were ejected into interstellar space.

Detection

Interstellar objects passing through the Solar System betray themselves through their orbital eccentricity exceeding 1 and their high velocity relative to the Sun. Because they are small and faint, confirmed detections did not occur until the 21st century, despite astronomers having predicted such visitors for decades.

Before the interstellar designation existed, a handful of comets placed on marginally hyperbolic orbits were retroactively debated, but none had an eccentricity clearly high enough to prove an extrasolar origin.

Nomenclature

In November 2017 the International Astronomical Union created the "I" numbering prefix for interstellar objects, analogous to "C/" for comets and "A/" for asteroids. Objects receive a permanent number in discovery order, so the first interstellar object is 1I/ʻOumuamua, the second 2I/Borisov, and the third, discovered by the ATLAS survey in July 2025, is 3I/ATLAS.

Population and significance

Statistical estimates from the detections suggest that the Galaxy is filled with enormous numbers of ejected planetesimals — perhaps trillions of objects for every star — shed during planet formation. Interstellar objects offer the only opportunity to study material from other planetary systems at close range without interstellar travel, and space agencies have prepared mission concepts, such as ESA's Comet Interceptor, to visit a future interstellar visitor on short notice. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time is expected to greatly increase the detection rate.
