Gravitational microlensing

Gravitational microlensing is an astronomical phenomenon caused by the gravitational lens effect. It occurs when a massive foreground object — the lens — passes almost directly in front of a more distant source star. The lens's gravity bends and focuses the source's light, producing a characteristic temporary brightening that typically lasts days to months. Unlike large-scale gravitational lensing, the multiple images produced are too closely spaced to be resolved by telescopes, so only the brightening is observed.

Theory

The theory of gravitational light deflection follows from Einstein's general theory of relativity. For microlensing, the alignment is described by the Einstein radius, the angular scale at which the source would be imaged into a perfect ring. As the lens drifts across the line of sight due to the relative motion of the lens, source and observer, the source's apparent brightness follows a smooth, symmetric, achromatic light curve whose shape depends on the impact parameter and the Einstein crossing time.

If the lensing star hosts a planet, the planet can create a brief additional spike or dip in the light curve, which is how the microlensing method of exoplanet detection works. The technique is sensitive to planets at wide separations from their stars, and even to rogue planets with no host star at all.

Observations

Microlensing was proposed as an observational tool by Bohdan Paczyński in 1986, originally as a way to search for dark matter in the galactic halo in the form of massive compact halo objects (MACHOs). Dedicated survey projects — OGLE, MOA and EROS among them — began monitoring millions of stars in the galactic bulge and the Magellanic Clouds in the 1990s. Their results showed that MACHOs make up only a small fraction of dark matter, but the surveys turned microlensing into a powerful exoplanet technique, responsible for discovering dozens of planets, including cold super-Earths and candidate free-floating planets.

Notable events

Microlensing surveys have detected candidate rogue planets of Earth-like mass through extremely short events lasting only hours. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, planned for launch in the late 2020s, will carry out a large space-based microlensing survey expected to detect hundreds of bound exoplanets and constrain the population of unbound planets.
