The Ngoni are an ethnic group living in the present-day countries of Malawi, Zambia, Mozambique, and Tanzania. They are descended from the Nguni peoples of southern Africa who migrated northward during the Mfecane, the period of upheaval associated with the rise of Shaka and the Zulu kingdom in the early 19th century.

History

The largest migration was led by Zwangendaba, a general of the Ndwandwe, whose followers crossed the Zambezi River — tradition dates the crossing to 19 November 1835, during a solar eclipse — and eventually settled in the region around present-day Malawi and eastern Zambia. After Zwangendaba's death in 1848, his kingdom split into several chiefdoms. Another major Ngoni group, led by Mpezeni, established itself near modern Chipata in Zambia and resisted British expansion until its defeat in 1898.

Although the Ngoni arrived as conquerors with Zulu-style military organization — age regiments, short stabbing spears, and large cowhide shields — they gradually adopted the languages of the peoples they ruled, principally Chichewa (Chewa) and Tumbuka, retaining Zulu mainly in songs and praise poetry. Their kingdoms were absorbed into the colonial states of Nyasaland and Northern Rhodesia.

Culture

The Ngoni are renowned for the Ingoma dance, performed by men in animal skins with bells on their ankles, and for ceremonies such as the Incwala first-fruits festival maintained by the Mpezeni paramountcy in Zambia, and Umhlanga observances among Malawi's Mmbelwa kingdom. Ngoni identity remains politically and culturally significant in northern Malawi and eastern Zambia.
