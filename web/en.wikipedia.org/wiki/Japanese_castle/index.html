Japanese castles (城, shiro or jō) are fortresses constructed primarily of wood and stone. They evolved from the wooden stockades of earlier centuries and came into their best-known form in the late 16th century during the Azuchi–Momoyama period, when they served as the fortified residences of daimyō (feudal lords) and centres of administration.

History

Early Japanese fortifications were wooden palisades on hilltops. During the Sengoku period (1467–1603), competing daimyō built increasingly elaborate castles. Oda Nobunaga's Azuchi Castle (1579) set the pattern of a grand multi-storey central keep (tenshu) on massive stone foundations, followed by Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Osaka Castle. After the Tokugawa shogunate consolidated power, it restricted castle construction — the "one castle per province" rule of 1615 — and ordered many destroyed. In the Meiji Restoration most remaining castles were demolished or fell into ruin; the few surviving originals, such as Himeji, Matsumoto, and Hikone, are now national treasures, and several are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Structure and defence

A castle typically stood on a base of sloping stone walls (ishigaki) topped by white-plastered wooden buildings resistant to fire and firearms. Defences included moats, concentric baileys (honmaru, ninomaru, sannomaru), turrets (yagura), gates arranged to expose attackers to fire, and openings for dropping stones (ishi-otoshi) or firing arrows and guns. The tenshu, though a symbol of the lord's power, was rarely used as a residence; daily life centred on palaces in the baileys.

Types

Castles are classified by site: mountain castles (yamajiro), hill castles (hirayamajiro), and flatland castles (hirajiro). Many famous castles, including those destroyed in the Second World War to bombing (Hiroshima, Nagoya) or earlier by fire, have been reconstructed in concrete as museums, while others remain as stone-wall ruins in public parks.
