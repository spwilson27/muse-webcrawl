Initiated byCaliph al-Ma'mun; continued by al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq; ended under al-Mutawakkil

The Mihna (Arabic: محنة, "ordeal" or "inquisition") was a period of religious persecution instituted by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in 833 AD, under which judges, scholars, and officials were required to affirm the doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an — the Mu'tazilite teaching that God's speech was created in time rather than being an eternal attribute co-existing with God. Those who refused faced dismissal, imprisonment, flogging, and in a few cases death. The inquisition was continued by al-Ma'mun's successors al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq and was abandoned by al-Mutawakkil around 848–851.

Background

The question whether the Qur'an was God's eternal speech or a created book had divided theologians for generations and carried a constitutional stake: if the Qur'an was uncreated and fully accessible to scholarly tradition, the religious scholars (ulama) stood as its custodians against the caliph; if it was created, the caliph — as God's deputy — could claim greater authority in defining doctrine. Al-Ma'mun, victor of the Fourth Fitna and patron of rationalist Mu'tazilite theology, proclaimed the createdness doctrine in 827 and, shortly before his death in 833, launched the Mihna by ordering his governors to test the judges and traditionists of the empire.

The inquisition

Most scholars submitted, at least outwardly. A few resisted. In Baghdad, the traditionist Ahmad ibn Hanbal refused to affirm the doctrine under interrogation by al-Mu'tasim and was flogged — by tradition until he fell unconscious — and imprisoned, emerging as a popular hero whose defiance cemented the prestige of the Hanbali school and of hadith-based traditionalism generally. Others suffered worse: Muhammad ibn Nuh al-Ijli died in chains on the way to the caliph's camp, and Ahmad ibn Nasr al-Khuza'i was executed by al-Wathiq in 846 after refusing to recant. In the provinces, enforcement was uneven, and traditionists developed the practice of dissimulation under compulsion while privately holding the Qur'an uncreated.

End and significance

Al-Mutawakkil reversed the policy from about 848, releasing prisoners, restoring traditionists to favor, and eventually banning theological disputation on the question altogether. The Mihna's deepest consequence was paradoxical: intended to assert caliphal supremacy over religious doctrine, its failure instead settled the matter the other way. After the Mihna, no caliph again attempted to define dogma by inquisition; authority over doctrine passed durably to the ulama, and Sunni Islam crystallized around the traditionalist position that the Qur'an is God's uncreated speech. The episode is frequently described as the moment the caliphate lost the religious leadership of Islam, retaining political and symbolic headship instead.
