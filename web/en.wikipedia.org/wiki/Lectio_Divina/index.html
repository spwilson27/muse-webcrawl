Lectio Divina

Lectio Divina (Latin for "Divine Reading") is a traditional monastic practice of scriptural reading, meditation and prayer intended to promote communion with God and to increase knowledge of God's word. It does not treat Scripture as a text to be studied analytically, but as the living word to be savoured slowly and prayerfully.

The four movements

The classic formulation, described in the twelfth century by the Carthusian Guigo II in his Ladder of Monks, distinguishes four rungs or movements:

  • Lectio (reading) — slow, repeated reading of a short passage of Scripture, listening for a word or phrase that strikes the reader.
  • Meditatio (meditation) — pondering and "ruminating" on that word or phrase, letting it engage memory, imagination, and desire.
  • Oratio (prayer) — responding to God in prayer arising from the meditation: praise, petition, contrition, or intercession.
  • Contemplatio (contemplation) — resting silently in God's presence beyond words and concepts.

A later tradition, reflected in modern practice, sometimes adds actio — resolving to act on what has been received.

History and practice today

Rooted in the prayer of the Desert Fathers and the Rule of Saint Benedict, which prescribed daily periods of lectio for monks, the practice was maintained especially in Benedictine and Cistercian monasteries. Endorsed by the Second Vatican Council's constitution Dei Verbum and promoted by Pope Benedict XVI, lectio divina has since the late twentieth century spread widely among lay Christians of many denominations, both individually and in groups, sometimes in adapted forms centred on slow, reflective reading.
