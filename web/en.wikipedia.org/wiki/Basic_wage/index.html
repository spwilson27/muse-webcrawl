Basic wage

The basic wage concept arose in Australian industrial law

The basic wage is a legally prescribed minimum rate of pay intended to meet the normal needs of an average employee living in a civilized community. The concept is most closely associated with Australia, where it formed the foundation of the national wage-fixing system for much of the twentieth century.

The Harvester judgment

The doctrine traces to the 1907 Harvester case, in which Justice H. B. Higgins of the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration determined a "fair and reasonable" wage for an unskilled labourer. Higgins calculated the amount needed to support a man, a wife, and three children in modest comfort, setting the benchmark at seven shillings per day. The decision linked wage-fixing to household needs rather than to the market value of labour alone.

Operation

The basic wage was periodically adjusted, including for movements in the cost of living, and margins for skill were added for qualified trades. It applied through federal and state industrial awards and influenced wage outcomes across the economy. A separate, lower basic wage was set for female workers until moves toward equal pay in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

Decline

From the 1960s, Australian wage tribunals moved toward a "total wage" and later to national wage case principles emphasizing economic capacity alongside needs. The basic wage as a distinct needs-based floor gradually lost its central role, though minimum wage setting in Australia retains echoes of the Harvester philosophy.
