Block cipher

[Image placeholder: Block cipher]

In cryptography, a block cipher is a deterministic algorithm that operates on fixed-size groups of bits, called blocks, using a transformation specified by a symmetric key. Block ciphers are fundamental components of many cryptographic protocols and are widely used to encrypt large amounts of data, including in data exchange protocols.

Design

Most block ciphers are constructed by repeatedly applying a simpler round function. Claude Shannon proposed combining confusion and diffusion, typically through substitution–permutation networks or Feistel networks. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), adopted in 2001, uses 128-bit blocks and keys of 128, 192 or 256 bits; its predecessor DES used 64-bit blocks and an effective 56-bit key.

Modes of operation

Because a block cipher handles only one block at a time, modes of operation such as ECB, CBC, CTR and GCM define how to process longer messages and, in authenticated modes, how to provide integrity as well as confidentiality.
