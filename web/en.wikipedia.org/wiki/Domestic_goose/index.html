Domestic goose

A domestic goose is a goose that humans have domesticated and kept for their meat, eggs, or down feathers. Domestic geese have been derived through selective breeding from the wild greylag goose (Anser anser domesticus in Europe) and swan goose (Anser cygnoides domesticus in Asia).

History

Geese were domesticated at least 5,000–7,000 years ago; recent archaeological evidence from China suggests domestication of the swan goose may date to around 5000 BC. In ancient Egypt geese were kept and depicted in art, and in Rome geese were credited with warning of the Gallic attack on the Capitol in 390 BC. Geese were also historically kept as guard animals and for weeding crops.

Characteristics

Domestic geese are much larger than their wild ancestors, with bulkier bodies, upright posture and a reduced ability to fly. They are grazers, hardy and long-lived, often living 20 years or more. Breeds include the Embden, Toulouse, Pilgrim, Chinese and African goose. Goose down and feathers are used in bedding and clothing; goose meat and foie gras are culinary specialities in parts of Europe and Asia.
