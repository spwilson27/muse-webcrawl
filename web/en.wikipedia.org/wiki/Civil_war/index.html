Civil war

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A civil war is a war between organised groups within the same state (or, less commonly, a former state) over control of the government, secession, or fundamental changes to policy. Academic definitions (such as the Correlates of War and Uppsala conflict data projects) typically require sustained combat with substantial casualties — often a threshold of about 1,000 battle deaths — distinguishing civil wars from riots, coups and one-sided massacres, though the boundaries are contested.

Causes and conduct

Research associates civil war onset with poverty and weak state capacity, ethnic and religious polarisation, political exclusion, rough terrain and external support; the influential "greed vs. grievance" debate (Collier and others) weighs rebels' economic opportunities against grievances, with subsequent work stressing political exclusion and horizontal inequality. Civil wars are often long, recur frequently, internationalise through foreign intervention, and produce mass displacement and atrocities governed (in law) by Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions and Additional Protocol II. Famous examples include the English Civil War, the American Civil War, the Russian and Chinese civil wars, the Spanish Civil War, and post-Cold War conflicts in the Balkans, Rwanda, Syria and Ethiopia.

Termination

Civil wars end by military victory or negotiated settlement; power-sharing, security-sector reform and transitional justice are standard settlement instruments, and the scholarly and policy literatures examine why settlements hold or collapse — with UN peacekeeping shown to reduce recurrence substantially.
