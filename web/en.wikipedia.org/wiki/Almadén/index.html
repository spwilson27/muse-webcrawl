Almadén is a town in the province of Ciudad Real in Castile-La Mancha, Spain, famed for possessing the largest deposit of cinnabar — mercury ore — ever known. Its name derives from the Arabic al-maʿdin, "the mine". Together with Idrija in Slovenia it is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the "Heritage of Mercury".

History

Mercury has been extracted at Almadén since antiquity: the Romans mined there (calling the place Sisapo), and the mines passed through Moorish and Castilian hands. From the 16th century the Spanish Crown, and later the Fugger banking family who leased the mines, used Almadén's mercury in the patio process for refining silver from the mines of Spanish America, making the town strategically vital to the empire. Over two millennia the deposit yielded roughly a third of all the mercury ever produced worldwide.

Conditions underground were notoriously deadly — mercury poisoning ("mercurialism") destroyed the health of miners, and for a period convicts were sentenced to labour in the mines. Reforms in the 18th century, including a mining academy founded in 1777, improved matters. Mining finally ceased in 2003, and the site has been developed as a mining park and museum within the UNESCO listing of 2012.
