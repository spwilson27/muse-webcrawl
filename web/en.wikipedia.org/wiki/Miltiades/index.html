Miltiades

Miltiades the Younger (c. 555 – 489 BC) was an Athenian statesman and general, best known for his role in the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC, where his strategy secured the Athenian victory over the invading Persian Empire. A member of the wealthy Philaid family, he had earlier ruled as tyrant of the Athenian colony in the Thracian Chersonese under Persian overlordship, giving him first-hand knowledge of Persian tactics that proved decisive at Marathon.

Career

Miltiades succeeded his uncle (also Miltiades) as ruler of the Chersonese, a strategically vital peninsula controlling the Hellespont. As a Persian vassal he accompanied Darius I on his Scythian expedition (c. 513 BC) and, according to Herodotus, urged the Ionians to destroy the Danube bridge and strand the Persian army — advice not taken. Compromised by the failed Ionian Revolt, he fled to Athens in 493 BC, escaping Phoenician pursuit and losing a son. Tried for tyranny, he was acquitted and elected one of the ten strategoi for 490/89 BC.

Marathon

When Datis and Artaphernes landed at Marathon with a Persian expeditionary force, Miltiades persuaded the polemarch Callimachus to give battle rather than await attack. On the chosen day the Athenians and their Plataean allies advanced at a run; Miltiades had thinned his centre and strengthened his wings, which closed around the Persian centre after it broke through — a double envelopment. Herodotus gives Persian dead at 6,400 against 192 Athenian. A bronze helmet excavated at Olympia, inscribed "Miltiades dedicated me to Zeus", is traditionally linked to the victory.

Fall

In 489 BC Miltiades led an expedition against Paros that failed; he returned wounded and was prosecuted by Xanthippus for deceiving the people. Fined 50 talents, he died of his wound (gangrene) in prison or soon after; his son Cimon paid the fine. His reputation recovered: later Athens honoured him as the hero of Marathon in the Painted Stoa.
