Mercaptopurine (6-MP), sold under the brand name Purinethol among others, is a medication used for cancer and autoimmune diseases. Specifically it is used to treat acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL), acute promyelocytic leukemia, Crohn's disease, and ulcerative colitis. For acute lymphocytic leukemia it is generally used with methotrexate. It is taken by mouth.

It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. It is available as a generic medication.

Medical uses

In addition to its use in leukemia, mercaptopurine is used as an immunosuppressant in inflammatory bowel disease, usually in patients who do not tolerate or respond to other therapies, and occasionally in other autoimmune conditions. It is the active metabolite framework related to azathioprine, which is converted to 6-MP in the body.

Side effects

Common side effects include bone marrow suppression (myelosuppression), liver toxicity, nausea, vomiting, and increased risk of infection. Toxicity is strongly influenced by thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) and NUDT15 activity; genetic testing or enzyme assays are often recommended before treatment. The xanthine oxidase inhibitor allopurinol markedly increases 6-MP toxicity because xanthine oxidase is a major route of inactivation, so dose reduction is required when they are combined.

Mechanism of action

Mercaptopurine is a purine analogue antimetabolite. It is converted intracellularly to thioguanine nucleotides, which are incorporated into DNA and RNA and inhibit de novo purine synthesis, interfering with the growth of rapidly dividing cells such as malignant lymphocytes and activated immune cells.

History

Mercaptopurine was developed by Gertrude B. Elion and George H. Hitchings at Burroughs Wellcome in the early 1950s, work that contributed to their 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
