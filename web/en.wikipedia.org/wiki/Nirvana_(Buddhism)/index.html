Nirvana (Sanskrit: निर्वाण, nirvāṇa; Pali: nibbāna) is a concept in Indian religions denoting the extinguishing of desire, hatred and ignorance, and with it the end of suffering and of the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra). In Buddhism it is the ultimate goal of the path taught by the Buddha.

Etymology and metaphor

The word derives from the Sanskrit roots nir ("out") and vā ("to blow"), hence "blown out" — as a flame is extinguished. Early texts describe the liberated mind as "cooled" once the "three fires" of greed (rāga), aversion (dveṣa) and delusion (moha) have gone out.

In early Buddhism

The Buddha's first sermon set the goal in terms of the Four Noble Truths: suffering arises from craving, and ceases when craving ceases along the Noble Eightfold Path. Texts distinguish nirvana "with remainder" — awakening attained in life, as by the Buddha at Bodh Gaya — from parinirvana, the final passing at death, after which the awakened one is not reborn and is described by negation as "unborn, unbecome, unmade, uncompounded". An arhat is one who has attained nirvana.

Mahayana interpretations

Mahayana schools developed further readings: the Madhyamaka teaching that nirvana and samsara are not ultimately different, since both are empty of inherent nature (Nagarjuna); the Yogācāra doctrine of the transformation of consciousness; and the tathāgatagarbha ("Buddha-nature") literature, which speaks of nirvana's qualities as permanence, bliss, self and purity. In East Asian and Tibetan practice nirvana is approached through the bodhisattva path, postponing final exit to liberate all beings.

Related concepts

Similar liberation concepts include mokṣa in Hinduism and kevala in Jainism. Within Buddhism, nirvana is linked to the doctrine of dependent origination and to meditative attainments (jhāna) without being identical to any of them.
