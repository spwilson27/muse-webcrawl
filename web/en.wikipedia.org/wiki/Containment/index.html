Period1947 – end of the Cold War (1991)

Containment was a geopolitical strategic foreign policy pursued by the United States during the Cold War to prevent the spread of communism after the end of World War II. The theory was first laid out by George F. Kennan, a U.S. diplomat in Moscow, in his famous "Long Telegram" of February 1946 and the subsequent "X Article" ("The Sources of Soviet Conduct") published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947. Kennan argued that the Soviet Union was ideologically committed to expansion but was cautious and patient, and that it could be "contained by the adroit and vigilant application of counter-force at a series of constantly shifting geographical and political points."

Origins

The policy was operationalized by President Harry S. Truman's address to Congress on 12 March 1947 — the Truman Doctrine — which pledged support to Greece and Turkey against communist pressure and declared that the United States would support free peoples resisting subjugation. It was underpinned economically by the Marshall Plan (1948) for the reconstruction of Western Europe, and militarily by the creation of NATO in 1949.

Development

Containment evolved through successive administrations. NSC-68 (1950) framed the conflict as a global, zero-sum struggle and justified a massive military build-up; the Korean War (1950–53) was containment's first major military test. Under Dwight D. Eisenhower, Secretary of State John Foster Dulles spoke of "roll-back" and massive retaliation, though practice remained largely containment. The policy was invoked to justify U.S. involvement in Vietnam, a conflict that gravely discredited it; later variants included Nixon's détente, Carter's human-rights emphasis, and Reagan's more confrontational "Reagan Doctrine" of supporting anti-communist insurgencies.

Criticism and legacy

Critics such as Walter Lippmann charged that Kennan's strategy was a "strategic monstrosity" that would commit the United States to defending indefensible positions everywhere. Kennan himself later insisted he had meant political and economic, not primarily military, containment. After the Cold War, the concept was adapted — with mixed success — to policies toward Iraq ("dual containment"), Iran, China, and North Korea.
