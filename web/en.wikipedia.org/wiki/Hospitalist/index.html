Hospitalist

A hospitalist is a physician, typically an internist, whose primary professional focus is the general medical care of hospitalized patients. Their activities include patient care, teaching, research, and leadership related to hospital medicine. The term was coined by Robert Wachter and Lee Goldman in a 1996 New England Journal of Medicine article, "The Emerging Role of 'Hospitalists' in the American Health Care System".

Role

Unlike primary care physicians who divide their time between office practice and hospital rounds, hospitalists work full-time in the hospital, often in shift-based schedules (commonly seven days on, seven off). They admit and manage patients for other physicians, co-manage surgical patients, respond to rapid-response calls, and coordinate discharge planning with case managers, specialists, and primary care doctors, to whom care returns at discharge.

Growth and evidence

Hospital medicine grew rapidly in the United States from the mid-1990s: the specialty expanded from a few hundred practitioners to tens of thousands, and the Society of Hospital Medicine (founded 1998) represents the field. Studies associate hospitalist care with modest reductions in length of stay and cost per admission, with little or no change in mortality or readmission rates, though discontinuity with the patient's own physician at admission and discharge is a recognized drawback. Variants include nocturnists (night hospitalists), pediatric hospitalists, and surgical co-managers. Hospital medicine became the first specialty recognized through "focused practice" designation by the American Board of Internal Medicine.
