Maiolica

Maiolica (also spelled majolica) is tin-glazed pottery decorated with colourful painted designs on a white opaque glaze, produced notably in Italy during the Renaissance. The name is thought to derive from Majorca, the island through which Hispano-Moresque lustreware from Spain passed on its way to Italy, or from the Spanish term for lustre work.

History and technique

Italian potters developed the technique from the thirteenth century onwards, coating earthenware with a glaze made opaque and white with tin oxide, which provided a ground for painting with metal-oxide pigments before firing. Great centres of production included Faenza (from which "faience" takes its name), Deruta, Gubbio, Urbino, Siena, Orvieto and Castel Durante. The craft reached its height in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

Styles

Peak-period maiolica featured istoriato ware — whole surfaces painted with narrative scenes from mythology, the Bible and classical history, often copied from engravings. Other celebrated techniques included the ruby and gold lustres of Deruta and Gubbio, berettino ware with designs on a blue ground, and armorial services made for noble families. Maiolica was produced as display plate, apothecary jars (albarelli), tiles, and tableware for wealthy patrons.

Decline and related wares

Maiolica declined in Italy in the seventeenth century as porcelain and other wares took over its market, but related tin-glazed traditions continued elsewhere as faience in France, delftware in the Netherlands and talavera in Spain and Mexico. The term "majolica" was revived in the nineteenth century for a different, brightly coloured lead-glazed earthenware exhibited by Minton in 1851.
