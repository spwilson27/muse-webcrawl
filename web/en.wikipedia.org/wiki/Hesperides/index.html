The Hesperides (Ancient Greek: Ἑσπερίδες) are the nymphs of evening and the golden light of sunsets in Greek mythology. They were entrusted with the care of a blissful garden in a far western corner of the world, where a tree bearing golden apples grew. The garden, often called the Garden of the Hesperides, was one of the mythical locations at the edge of the known world, sometimes placed near the Atlas Mountains in North Africa, beyond the Pillars of Heracles, or on islands in the Atlantic Ocean.

Mythology

The golden apples of the Hesperides were a wedding gift from Gaia to Hera at her marriage to Zeus. Hera planted the tree in her garden and set the Hesperides to guard it, assisted by the hundred-headed dragon Ladon, who never slept. The apples conferred immortality on those who ate them, making the garden a coveted prize.

Stealing the apples was the eleventh of the Twelve Labours of Heracles. After a long journey — during which he wrestled the sea god Nereus for directions, freed Prometheus, and encountered Atlas — Heracles arrived at the garden. In the best-known version, he persuaded Atlas, the father of the Hesperides in some accounts, to fetch the apples while Heracles temporarily held up the sky. Atlas returned with three apples but refused to resume his burden; Heracles tricked him into taking the sky back for a moment and departed with the fruit. In other versions, Heracles slew Ladon and took the apples himself.

Number and names

Sources differ on the number of the Hesperides: three, four, or seven are commonly given. Named Hesperides include Aegle ("dazzling light"), Erytheia (or Erytheis), Hesperia (or Hespere), and Arethusa; a sevenfold list adds Asterope, Chrysothemis, and Lipara. Their names generally evoke evening, sunset, and radiance.

The garden's location

Ancient authors placed the garden ever further west as Greek geographical knowledge expanded: first in Libya, then beyond the Strait of Gibraltar, and eventually on the "Fortunate Isles" in the Atlantic, sometimes identified with the Canary Islands. The ambiguity of "Hesperia" — a term applied to both Italy and Iberia as western lands from a Greek perspective — reflects this shifting geography.

Legacy

The Hesperides and their garden recur throughout classical literature, from Hesiod's Theogony to the works of Apollonius of Rhodes and Diodorus Siculus. The golden apples have been linked in later interpretation to citrus fruits, and the botanical family name Hesperidia was once applied to citrus; the name also survives in the chemical compound hesperidin, found in citrus peel.
