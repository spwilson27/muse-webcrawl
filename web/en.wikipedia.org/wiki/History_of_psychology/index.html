History of psychology

The history of psychology reaches from ancient philosophical speculation about the mind and soul to the founding of experimental psychology in the nineteenth century and the discipline's expansion in the twentieth. Wilhelm Wundt's laboratory at Leipzig (1879) is conventionally taken as the discipline's birth. Major schools — structuralism, functionalism, psychoanalysis, behaviourism, Gestalt psychology, humanistic psychology and cognitive psychology — succeeded one another, and psychology is now a diverse science spanning brain, behaviour and society.

Wilhelm Wundt's Institute for Experimental Psychology at Leipzig, founded 1879.

Origins

Ancient Egyptian, Indian, Chinese, Greek and Islamic thinkers — from the Edwin Smith Papyrus to Aristotle's De Anima and Ibn al-Haytham's optics — addressed perception, memory and emotion. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Descartes' dualism, Locke's empiricism and associationism framed questions later taken up experimentally by Weber, Fechner (psychophysics) and Helmholtz.

Founding of the discipline

Wundt's laboratory trained the first generation of experimentalists. His student Edward Titchener developed structuralism in America, while William James's Principles of Psychology (1890) and John Dewey's work advanced functionalism, influenced by Darwin. In Germany, the Gestalt school of Wertheimer, Köhler and Koffka argued the whole of experience differs from the sum of its parts.

Psychoanalysis and behaviourism

Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis, and the rival schools of Jung and Adler, dominated clinical practice in the early twentieth century. In reaction, John B. Watson's behaviourist manifesto (1913) and B. F. Skinner's radical behaviourism restricted psychology to observable behaviour and learning, dominating American laboratories until mid-century. Humanistic psychologists Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers offered a "third force" centred on growth and the self.

The cognitive revolution and after

From the 1950s, work by George A. Miller, Noam Chomsky's critique of Skinner (1959), and the rise of computing reframed the mind as an information-processing system. Cognitive, social, developmental, clinical and biological psychology all expanded; the American Psychological Association (founded 1892) grew into the world's largest psychological society. Debates over replication, methods and diversity continue in the twenty-first century.
