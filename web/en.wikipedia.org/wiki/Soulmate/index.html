Soulmate

A soulmate is a person with whom one has a feeling of deep and natural affinity. This is generally understood to involve similarity in love, romance, platonic relationships, comfort, intimacy, sexuality, spirituality, compatibility, and trust. In modern usage, the term is most often applied to a romantic partner believed to be uniquely suited to oneself, as if destined; related concepts include the "twin flame" in esoteric traditions and the Jewish concept of bashert (destined spouse).

The idea that each person has one destined partner has ancient roots in mythology.

Origins

The classical source most often cited is Plato's Symposium, in which Aristophanes recounts a myth that humans were originally beings with four arms, four legs, and two faces, whom Zeus split in two; each half thereafter longs for its other half, and love is the desire for wholeness. Variants of a destined-match idea appear in many traditions: in Hindu mythology, Shiva and Parvati exemplify divine union; in Chinese folklore, the god Yue Lao ties destined couples with an invisible red thread; and in Jewish tradition, matches are said to be decreed in heaven forty days before a child is formed.

Modern usage and psychology

The word entered English in the early nineteenth century; its first recorded use is in an 1822 letter by Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Contemporary psychology distinguishes between "destiny beliefs" (that relationships succeed when partners are meant for each other) and "growth beliefs" (that good relationships are built through effort); research by C. Raymond Knee and others associates strong destiny beliefs with quicker disengagement when relationships face difficulties, though they may predict satisfaction in relationships that are going well. Sociologists note that the soulmate ideal has become a dominant expectation for marriage in Western cultures, raising both the personal significance and the demands placed on the institution.

In popular culture

The search for a soulmate is a perennial theme of romance novels, films, and popular music, and dating services frequently market themselves as tools for finding "the one."
