Porphyrin

Porphyrins are a group of heterocyclic macrocycle organic compounds, composed of four modified pyrrole sub-units interconnected at their α carbon atoms via methine bridges (=CH−). In vertebrates, an important member is heme, which is a porphyrin with an iron atom in its centre; chlorophyll, central to photosynthesis, is a related compound (a chlorin) containing magnesium. The parent porphyrin is porphine; substituted derivatives, including protoporphyrin IX, are the porphyrins proper. The name derives from the Greek porphyra (purple), because many porphyrins are intensely coloured.

Structure and chemistry

The porphyrin macrocycle is a planar, aromatic ring system of 26 atoms with an 18-electron conjugated pathway, satisfying Hückel's rule. The four nitrogen atoms point inward and bind metal ions — Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ in hemes, Mg²⁺ in chlorophylls, Co in the corrin ring of vitamin B₁₂ (a contracted relative), and nickel or vanadium in geologically derived petroporphyrins. Binding a metal changes the compound's colour and reactivity; the strong visible absorption bands of porphyrins (notably the intense Soret band near 400 nm) make them easily detected spectroscopically. In synthesis, porphyrins are commonly prepared by the condensation of pyrrole with aldehydes (the Rothemund and Adler–Longo methods).

Biosynthesis and biology

All living cells synthesise porphyrins through a conserved pathway: glycine and succinyl-CoA condense to form 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), two molecules of which form the pyrrole porphobilinogen; four porphobilinogen units assemble into the linear tetrapyrrole hydroxymethylbilane, which cyclises to uroporphyrinogen III and is then modified step by step to protoporphyrin IX, the immediate precursor of heme. Defects in the pathway's enzymes cause the group of diseases known as the porphyrias. Beyond heme and chlorophyll, porphyrin-derived cofactors include siroheme and cofactor F430 in archaea.

Applications

Porphyrins and their synthetic analogues (metalloporphyrins and porphyrin-like phthalocyanines) are used as catalysts, dyes and pigments, sensors, and materials in organic electronics and dye-sensitised solar cells. In medicine, porphyrin derivatives such as porfimer sodium accumulate in tumours and are used in photodynamic therapy, in which light activates the drug to generate reactive singlet oxygen that destroys target tissue. Fluorescent porphyrins also serve as probes in imaging and diagnostics.
