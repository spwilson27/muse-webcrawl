Detonator

A detonator, frequently a blasting cap, is a small sensitive device used to trigger an explosive device. Detonators can be chemically, mechanically, or electrically initiated, the last being the most common. They contain a small amount of a primary explosive such as lead azide, lead styphnate or mercury fulminate, which is highly sensitive and produces the shock needed to initiate a much larger, less sensitive secondary charge.

Types

  • Fuse caps — ignited by a safety fuse inserted into the open end of the cap.
  • Electric blasting caps — fired by an electric current through a bridge wire.
  • Non-electric (shock tube) detonators — initiated by a hollow plastic tube coated with reactive powder.
  • Electronic detonators — use a microchip for precise, programmable delay timing, improving fragmentation and reducing vibration in mining.
  • Exploding-bridgewire and slapper detonators — used where extreme speed and safety are required, e.g. in nuclear weapons and aerospace.

History

Blasting caps became practical in the 1860s alongside dynamite; Alfred Nobel's mercury fulminate detonator of 1867 made high explosives reliable for mining and warfare. Numbers on commercial caps (e.g. No. 8) denote their strength.

Safety

Because primary explosives are extremely sensitive to shock, friction, heat and static electricity, detonators are handled separately from main charges until the moment of use and are subject to strict transport regulations.
