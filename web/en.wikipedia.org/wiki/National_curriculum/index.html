National curriculum

A national curriculum is a set of standards and prescribed content that a nation's government requires schools to teach, specifying what pupils should learn at each stage of schooling. National curricula are used to promote consistency and equity across schools, guide textbook and examination development, and enable monitoring of educational standards. Their scope varies: some countries specify detailed lesson content, while others set broad attainment goals and leave methods to teachers.

England

The best-known example in the English-speaking world is the national curriculum for England, introduced by the Education Reform Act 1988 under Secretary of State Kenneth Baker. It established core subjects — English, mathematics, and science — and other foundation subjects, taught through four "key stages" with attainment targets and national testing (SATs) at certain stages. It applies to maintained schools; academies and free schools are not legally required to follow it, though many use it as a benchmark. Major revisions took effect in 2014.

Other countries

France has a long tradition of a centralized national curriculum administered by the Ministry of National Education. Australia introduced a national Australian Curriculum, phased in from 2011, developed by the Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority. Japan sets a national Course of Study revised roughly every decade, while Finland's national core curriculum sets goals and core content with strong local autonomy. The United States has no national curriculum, education being a state responsibility, though the Common Core State Standards were adopted by many states as a form of shared standards.

Debates

Supporters argue national curricula guarantee a common entitlement and raise standards; critics contend they can overload teachers, narrow instruction, or reflect political interference in content such as history teaching.
