Superbase

A superbase is a chemical compound or reagent of exceptionally high basicity — that is, an extremely strong affinity for protons. Superbases are stronger than the hydroxide ion measured on a common scale, and they are used in organic synthesis when ordinary bases are not strong enough to deprotonate weakly acidic substrates.

Classes of superbases

Organic superbases

Neutral organic superbases are typically nitrogen compounds engineered so that protonation yields a resonance-stabilised, internally hydrogen-bonded cation. Examples include the amidine DBU (1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene), the guanidine TBD, "proton sponges" such as 1,8-bis(dimethylamino)naphthalene, and the phosphazene bases developed by Schwesinger, whose strongest members approach pKa values (in acetonitrile) of around 42 or higher. Verkade's proazaphosphatranes are another prominent family.

Organometallic bases

Organolithium compounds such as n-butyllithium, and potassium bases such as potassium hydride and potassium tert-butoxide, behave as superbases. Mixtures can be stronger still: the Lochmann–Schlosser base (n-butyllithium with potassium tert-butoxide) deprotonates very weak acids. Lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) and lithium tetramethylpiperidide are widely used hindered superbases for enolate formation.

Inorganic superbases

Metal nitrides such as lithium nitride, and some hydrides and oxides, are also classed as superbases. On the Hammett acidity-function scale, superbases are orders of magnitude beyond concentrated sodium hydroxide.

Uses and handling

Superbases are used to generate carbanions, enolates and other reactive intermediates, to catalyse polymerisations and isomerisations, and in some cases for carbon dioxide capture. Many are moisture-sensitive, pyrophoric or expensive, and must be handled under inert atmosphere with anhydrous solvents.
