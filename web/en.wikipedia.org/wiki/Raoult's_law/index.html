Raoult's law

Raoult's law is a law of thermodynamics concerning the vapour pressure of a solution, proposed by the French chemist François-Marie Raoult in 1887. It states that the partial vapour pressure of each component of an ideal mixture of liquids is equal to the vapour pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction in the mixture.

Vapour pressure of a binary ideal solution obeying Raoult's law

Statement

For a solution of components A and B, Raoult's law gives pA = xA pA* and pB = xB pB*, where pi is the partial pressure of component i, pi* is the vapour pressure of the pure component, and xi is its mole fraction in the liquid. The total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures. A solution that obeys the law over the whole concentration range is an ideal solution, in which intermolecular forces between unlike molecules match those between like molecules.

Consequences and deviations

Raoult's law explains colligative properties: dissolving a non-volatile solute lowers the solvent's vapour pressure, producing boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression. Real solutions deviate when interactions between components differ from those within the pure liquids. Positive deviations (higher pressure than predicted) occur in mixtures such as ethanol–water at high ethanol content, while negative deviations occur where unlike molecules attract strongly, as in acetone–chloroform. Large deviations can produce azeotropes — constant-boiling mixtures that cannot be separated by simple distillation. For very dilute solutions, the solute instead follows Henry's law.
