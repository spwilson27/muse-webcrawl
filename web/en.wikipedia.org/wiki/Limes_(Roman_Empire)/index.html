Limes (Latin for "path" or "boundary") is the modern term for the system of frontier defences and border zones that protected the Roman Empire. Rather than a single continuous wall, a limes typically combined natural barriers such as rivers with chains of watchtowers, forts, fortlets and — where no river ran — artificial barriers of palisades, ditches and walls.

Function

The limites controlled movement into and out of the empire: trade was channelled through official crossing points where it could be taxed and monitored, raiding parties could be detected early, and troops were positioned to respond. Military roads ran behind or along the frontier, linking the installations. Over time many frontier forts grew civilian settlements (vici) and became the nuclei of later towns.

Major sections

In Britain, Hadrian's Wall and the turf-built Antonine Wall marked the northern frontier. In Germany, the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes ran for some 550 km between the Rhine and the Danube, defended by a palisade, ditch and stone wall with about 900 watchtowers. Along the Danube, the frontier followed the river through Raetia, Noricum, Pannonia and Moesia. In the east, the Limes Arabicus guarded the desert fringe of Syria and Arabia, while in North Africa systems such as the Fossatum Africae protected the settled coastal strip.

Legacy

Since 1987, sections of the limes have been inscribed as the transnational UNESCO World Heritage Site "Frontiers of the Roman Empire". Reconstructed towers, gates and forts — such as the Saalburg near Bad Homburg — anchor museums along the frontier lines.
