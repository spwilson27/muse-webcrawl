U-boat campaign (World War I)

The U-boat campaign from 1914 to 1918 was the naval campaign fought by German U-boats against the trade routes of the Allies during the First World War. It took place largely in the seas around the British Isles, in the Mediterranean Sea, and in the Atlantic, and was, together with the British blockade of Germany, the principal economic warfare struggle of the war at sea.

Early phase (1914–1915)

Germany possessed only a small submarine force in 1914. U-boats achieved early successes against warships — U-9 sank three British armoured cruisers in a single action in September 1914 — and in February 1915 Germany declared the waters around Britain a war zone in which merchant ships would be sunk without warning. The sinking of the liner Lusitania in May 1915, with the loss of 1,198 lives including 128 Americans, caused a diplomatic crisis with the United States. Further protests after the sinkings of Arabic and Sussex led Germany to restrict the campaign under the Sussex pledge of 1916.

Unrestricted submarine warfare (1917)

On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, gambling that Britain could be starved into submission before American intervention became decisive. Losses soared: in April 1917 Allied and neutral shipping losses reached about 860,000 tons, the worst month of the war, and one in four ships leaving British ports was sunk. The campaign succeeded in bringing the United States into the war in April 1917, but failed in its primary aim.

The convoy system and defeat of the campaign

The introduction of ocean convoys from May 1917, together with improved depth charges, hydrophones, air patrols and mine barrages, sharply reduced losses while U-boat losses mounted. Germany lost 178 U-boats during the war, along with some 5,000 officers and men. Although the campaign sank roughly 5,000 merchant ships totalling over 12 million gross tons, Britain was never starved into surrender, and the blockade of Germany tightened until the Armistice of November 1918.
