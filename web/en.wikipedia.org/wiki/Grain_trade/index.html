Grain trade

The grain trade refers to the local and international trade in cereals such as wheat, maize (corn), rice, barley, oats, rye, sorghum, and millet, together with oilseeds such as soybeans. Grain is among the most important commodities in world trade, providing staple calories for much of the world's population and feed for livestock.

History

Grain was traded in antiquity: Rome depended on grain fleets from Egypt, Sicily, and North Africa to feed its capital, and the cura annonae administered this supply. In the nineteenth century, railways, steamships, and grain elevators transformed the trade, linking the North American prairies and the Ukrainian steppe to European markets. Chicago became a centre of the trade, and futures contracts standardized at the Chicago Board of Trade allowed hedging of price risk.

Modern trade

Today a small number of large trading companies — historically nicknamed the "ABCD" firms (ADM, Bunge, Cargill, and Louis Dreyfus), joined by firms such as COFCO and Viterra — handle much of the international grain trade. Major exporters include the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Russia, Ukraine, Canada, Australia, and the European Union. Prices are set on exchanges and are sensitive to weather, energy costs, export restrictions, and conflict, as illustrated by the disruption of Black Sea exports after 2022 and the subsequent Black Sea Grain Initiative.
