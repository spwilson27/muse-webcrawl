Nanban trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nanban trade (南蛮貿易, Nanban-bōeki, "Southern barbarian trade") or the Nanban trade period (南蛮貿易時代, Nanban-bōeki-jidai) was a period in the history of Japan from the arrival of Europeans in 1543 to the first Sakoku Seclusion Edicts of isolationism in 1614–1616. Nanban (南蛮, "Southern barbarian") is a Sino-Japanese word originally applied to peoples from South Asia and Southeast Asia, and later to the Portuguese, then to all Europeans arriving from the south by sea.

First contact

In 1543, a Chinese junk carrying Portuguese traders — among them António Mota, Francisco Zeimoto, and António Peixoto — was blown off course to the island of Tanegashima, south of Kyushu. The Portuguese introduced the matchlock arquebus to Japan; within decades, Japanese gunsmiths were producing firearms in large numbers, transforming warfare during the Sengoku period's final unification campaigns under Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Trade

The Portuguese operated an immensely profitable trade between China and Japan: Chinese raw silk, in great demand in Japan, was exchanged for Japanese silver, since direct China–Japan trade was prohibited by the Ming haijin sea bans. The annual Portuguese "Great Ship" (kurofune, black ship) sailed from Macau to Nagasaki, carrying silk, porcelain, and other Chinese goods, and returning with silver. Jesuit missionaries, including St. Francis Xavier (who arrived in 1549), accompanied the traders, and Christianity spread notably in Kyushu, where daimyo such as Ōmura Sumitada patronised the mission and the port of Nagasaki.

The Dutch (1609, at Hirado) and English (1613) also established factories, and Spanish ships came from Manila. Japanese "red seal ships" traded throughout Southeast Asia under shogunal licence until the 1630s.

Decline and end

Relations with the Portuguese soured as the Tokugawa shogunate came to view Christianity as a political threat. Edicts expelled missionaries (1614), persecuted converts, and finally, after the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38, the Portuguese were expelled entirely in 1639; a returning embassy from Macau in 1640 was executed. The Dutch, who had assisted the shogunate and confined their activity to trade, were moved to the artificial island of Dejima in Nagasaki harbour in 1641, where Dutch trade continued throughout Japan's two centuries of sakoku.

Cultural impact

Nanban trade introduced to Japan firearms, Christianity, Western shipbuilding, tobacco, bread, sponge cakes (castella), tempera painting, and clocks. It produced the genre of Nanban art — folding screens depicting exotic foreigners and ships, lacquerware for export combining mother-of-pearl inlay with Christian motifs, and new vocabulary in Japanese, such as pan (bread, from Portuguese pão) and tempura.
