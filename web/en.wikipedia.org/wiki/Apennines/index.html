The Apennines are the great mountain system that runs the length of Italy, extending about 1,200 km from the Ligurian coast near Savona down the spine of the peninsula through Calabria, with the chain being continued by the mountains of northern Sicily. In Italian they are called the Appennini. Together with the Alps in the north they define the physical geography of Italy, and their name survives in administrative usage such as the former Abruzzo provinces and in the Italian expression for the peninsula's mountain backbone.

Character of the range

Unlike the Alps, the Apennines are of moderate altitude — only a handful of summits exceed 2,000 m and the highest, Corno Grande in the Gran Sasso group, reaches 2,912 m. The mountains are composed largely of folded and faulted sedimentary rocks, limestones in the central ranges and sandstones and clays further north and south, and are consequently more rounded and eroded than the crystalline Alps. Historic routes across them, from the Roman Via Flaminia and Via Appia to modern motorway tunnels under passes such as the Futa and the Gran Sasso tunnel, have shaped Italian settlement for two millennia.

People and environment

The Apennine interior has historically been a zone of small hill towns, transhumant shepherding, and emigration, a theme of much Italian literature and cinema. Today several national parks protect its beech forests, wolves, bears, and endemic flora, while depopulated villages face recurring earthquake risk owing to the active extensional faults along the range's crest.
