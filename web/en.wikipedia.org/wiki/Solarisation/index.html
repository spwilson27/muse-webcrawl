Solarisation

Solarisation may refer to several distinct topics:

In photography

The Sabattier effect, often loosely called solarisation, is a phenomenon in photography in which an image recorded on a negative or print is partially reversed in tone when the material is briefly exposed to light during development: dark areas appear light or vice versa, and a characteristic bright line (the Mackie line) forms at tonal boundaries. True solarisation is instead the reversal of tone caused by extreme overexposure of the film in the camera. The effect was described by Armand Sabattier in 1862 and was popularised as an artistic technique by Man Ray and Lee Miller in the late 1920s, whose "solarised" portraits became icons of surrealist photography. In digital imaging the effect is simulated with tone-curve manipulation.

In agriculture

Soil solarisation is a method of pest control in which moist soil is covered with clear plastic sheeting during hot weather; solar heating raises soil temperatures enough to kill many soil-borne pathogens, nematodes, weed seeds, and fungi. The technique was developed in Israel in the 1970s and is used especially in warm climates as a non-chemical alternative to fumigation.

In physics and glass

"Solarisation" also describes changes in a material under prolonged solar radiation — for example, the gradual purple discolouration of old manganese-containing glass exposed to ultraviolet light, and degradation of optical fibres or lenses. In physics the term has also been applied historically to the apparent effect of sunlight on certain chemical and photographic reactions.
