Ultra-distance cycling

Ultra-distance cycling is a form of long-distance bicycle riding in which events typically cover at least 200 kilometres, and often many hundreds or thousands of kilometres, ridden continuously or over multiple days. It includes organised randonnées and brevets, ultra-endurance races, and self-supported adventure events.

Forms

In randonneuring, riders complete fixed routes within generous time limits, passing through checkpoints; the flagship event is Paris–Brest–Paris, a 1,200 km ride held every four years since 1891. In ultra-endurance racing, the clock runs continuously: events such as the Race Across America (about 4,800 km) and the self-supported Transcontinental Race across Europe reward riders who manage sleep, nutrition and navigation over many days. Gravel and bikepacking ultras, such as the Tour Divide along the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route, take place largely off-road.

Physiology and strategy

Riders commonly cover 300–500 km per day in multi-day races, sleeping only a few hours. Performance depends on pacing at low intensity, constant fuelling, equipment reliability and coping with sleep deprivation, saddle sores and repetitive-strain injuries.

Records

Governing bodies such as the UltraMarathon Cycling Association ratify records for transcontinental crossings and 24-hour time trials, in which leading riders exceed 800 km in a day.
