Glycosidic bond

A glycosidic bond or glycosidic linkage is a type of covalent bond that joins a carbohydrate (sugar) molecule to another group, which may or may not be another carbohydrate. The bond is formed between the hemiacetal or hemiketal group of a saccharide (or a molecule derived from a saccharide) and the hydroxyl group of a compound such as an alcohol, with the loss of a molecule of water in a condensation reaction.

Types

Glycosidic bonds are classified by the atoms involved and by the stereochemistry at the anomeric carbon. An O-glycosidic bond links a sugar to an oxygen atom, as in disaccharides such as lactose and sucrose. An N-glycosidic bond links a sugar to a nitrogen atom, notably in the nucleosides of DNA and RNA, where ribose or deoxyribose is joined to a nitrogenous base. Less common S-glycosidic and C-glycosidic bonds link through sulfur and carbon atoms respectively.

Alpha and beta bonds

Depending on whether the substituent at the anomeric carbon lies on the same side (cis) or the opposite side (trans) of the ring oxygen relative to the CH2OH group, the bond is designated alpha (α) or beta (β). This distinction has major biological consequences: humans digest the α-1,4 bonds of starch readily with amylase enzymes, but lack enzymes to hydrolyse the β-1,4 bonds of cellulose, which passes through the gut as dietary fibre.

Hydrolysis

Glycosidic bonds are cleaved by a family of enzymes called glycoside hydrolases (glycosidases), specific to the bond type and sugar involved; for example, lactase hydrolyses the β-galactosidic bond in lactose. Under laboratory conditions the bonds can also be broken by acid hydrolysis.
