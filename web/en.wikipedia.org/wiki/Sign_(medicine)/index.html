Sign (medicine)

In medicine, a sign is an objective, observable indication of a disease, injury, or abnormal physiological state that may be detected during a physical examination, by a healthcare provider, or by laboratory and imaging tests. A sign is distinguished from a symptom, which is a subjective experience reported by the patient, such as pain or fatigue. Examples of signs include elevated blood pressure, fever measured with a thermometer, a heart murmur heard on auscultation, clubbing of the fingers, and abnormal blood test results.

Signs versus symptoms

The distinction dates to classical medicine and remains central to diagnosis: symptoms are what the patient notices and describes, whereas signs are elicited or measured by the clinician. Some findings can be both — a rash may be a symptom (noticed and reported by the patient) and a sign (characterized by the examiner). Vital signs — temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, and blood pressure — are the standardized measurements taken at nearly every clinical encounter.

Named signs

Clinical medicine recognizes hundreds of eponymous and descriptive signs, each associated with particular conditions: Kernig's sign and Brudziński's sign in meningitis; Murphy's sign in acute cholecystitis; Babinski's sign indicating upper motor neuron lesions; Trousseau's sign of latent tetany in hypocalcemia; and the Auspitz sign in psoriasis. Many such signs were described in the era before imaging and laboratory medicine, and their diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity and specificity) varies widely.

Use in diagnosis

Signs are gathered through inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation, and are combined with symptoms and history to form a differential diagnosis. Movements such as the "Rational Clinical Examination" series in JAMA have sought to quantify the evidence behind individual signs, showing that some traditional signs contribute little while combinations of findings can be highly informative.
