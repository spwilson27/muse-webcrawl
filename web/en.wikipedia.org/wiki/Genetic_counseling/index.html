Genetic counseling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic counseling is the process of investigating individuals and families affected by, or at risk of, genetic disorders, in order to help them understand and adapt to the medical, psychological and familial implications of genetic contributions to disease. Genetic counselors are health professionals with specialised graduate training in medical genetics and counseling.

What it involves

A genetic counseling session typically includes:

  • Interpreting family and medical histories, often by constructing a pedigree, to assess the chance of disease occurrence or recurrence;
  • Explaining inheritance patterns, such as autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and X-linked transmission;
  • Education about the natural history of a condition, available testing, management, prevention, and resources;
  • Counseling to promote informed choices — traditionally on a non-directive basis — and adaptation to the risk or condition;
  • Ordering and interpreting genetic tests, and communicating results and their implications for relatives.

Settings and indications

Counseling is offered in prenatal care (advanced maternal age, abnormal screening results, family history of birth defects), paediatrics (developmental delay, congenital anomalies), adult medicine (hereditary cancer syndromes such as BRCA1/2-related breast and ovarian cancer, Huntington's disease, inherited cardiac conditions), and increasingly in pharmacogenomics and direct-to-consumer testing follow-up. Referrals are also common for consanguineous couples and for individuals of ancestries with elevated carrier frequencies for conditions such as Tay–Sachs disease or sickle cell disease.

Profession and history

The term "genetic counseling" was coined by Sheldon Reed in 1947. The first master's-level training programme was established at Sarah Lawrence College in 1969, and the (American) National Society of Genetic Counselors was founded in 1979. Certification is provided in the United States by the American Board of Genetic Counseling; similar professional structures exist in many other countries, where the scope and title of practice vary. The growth of genomic medicine and exome/genome sequencing has greatly expanded demand for the profession.
