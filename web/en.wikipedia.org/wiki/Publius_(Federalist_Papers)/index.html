Publius (Federalist Papers)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Publius was the pseudonym used by Alexander Hamilton, John Jay and James Madison in writing The Federalist Papers, the eighty-five essays published in New York newspapers in 1787–88 to persuade the state to ratify the proposed United States Constitution. The name honoured Publius Valerius Publicola, a founder of the Roman Republic celebrated by Plutarch for establishing popular government. Hamilton organised the project and wrote the majority (fifty-one essays by the standard attribution); Madison wrote twenty-nine; Jay, incapacitated by illness after an early contribution, wrote five.

The shared mask was functional: it presented the Constitution's defence as a single civic voice, not the ambitions of individuals. Authorship of a dozen essays was long disputed between Hamilton's and Madison's lists; twentieth-century stylometry (Mosteller and Wallace's Bayesian study, 1964) assigned the disputed essays to Madison, a conclusion broadly accepted. “Publius” conventions persisted in American political writing (and in later productions such as Common Sense's successors and the “Publius” of modern blogs), while the Anti-Federalists answered with Roman masks of their own (Brutus, Cato, Cincinnatus) — the classical pseudonym being the ratification era's standard dress.
