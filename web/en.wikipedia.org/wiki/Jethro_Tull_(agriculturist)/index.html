Jethro Tull (1674 – 21 February 1741) was an English agriculturist from Berkshire who helped to bring about the British Agricultural Revolution. He perfected a horse-drawn seed drill in 1701 that economically sowed seeds in neat rows, and later developed a horse-drawn hoe. Tull's methods were adopted by many large landowners and helped to provide the basis for modern agriculture.

Although his underlying theory of plant nutrition — that plants feed on fine particles of soil, so that pulverising the soil could replace manure — was wrong, his practical innovations (drilling in rows, inter-row cultivation, and mechanised weeding) were influential and widely imitated.

Early life

Tull was born at Basildon, Berkshire, the son of a prosperous farmer, also named Jethro Tull. He matriculated at St John's College, Oxford, at seventeen and later studied law at Gray's Inn, though illness prevented a legal career. He married Susannah Smith of Burton Dassett, Warwickshire, and worked his father's farm at Howberry in Oxfordshire before moving in 1709 to Prosperous Farm near Hungerford, Berkshire, where he conducted most of his experiments.

Agricultural career

Frustrated by the waste and unevenness of broadcast sowing and by unreliable hired labour, Tull developed his drill after observing how organ pipes distribute air — an inspiration often credited to his interest in music and mechanics. On a tour of France and Italy (1711–1714) undertaken for his health, he observed Languedoc vineyards cultivated without manure, which reinforced his conviction that thorough tillage alone nourished plants. He then systematised "horse-hoeing husbandry": crops sown in rows wide enough for a horse-drawn hoe to destroy weeds and loosen soil between them.

Inventions

Seed drill (1701): Tull's drill used a rotating cylinder with grooves to drop seeds at a controlled rate into a furrow cut by a ploughshare, covering them with a following harrow. It sown in three rows at once, dramatically reducing seed use (from bushels per acre to a few pounds) and enabling mechanical weeding between rows. Earlier seed drills existed (in China for two millennia and in Europe since the 16th century), but Tull's design and publicity made the concept practical and known.

Horse-drawn hoe and cultivators: Tull designed hoes and ploughs for inter-row cultivation, including a four-coulter plough. His turnip drill and related implements were taken up by improvers such as Viscount "Turnip" Townshend's circle and later mechanised by makers like James Small.

Horse-Hoeing Husbandry

Tull published The New Horse-Hoeing Husbandry in 1731, expanded in 1733 as An Essay on the Principles of Tillage and Vegetation. The book set out his system and theory: plants absorb minute particles of earth through their roots, hence repeated pulverisation exposes new particles and manure is unnecessary; weeds rob crops of this food; rows and hoeing restore it. The theory was criticised by contemporaries and refuted by later agricultural chemistry (notably Liebig), yet the book ran to many editions and translations and spread drill husbandry across Britain and France.

Legacy

Tull died at Hungerford in 1741 and was buried at St Lawrence's Church, Lower Basildon. Historians rank him among the most influential practical improvers of the Agricultural Revolution: seed rates, row culture, and mechanised inter-row tillage descend from his advocacy, even where his science was mistaken. The progressive rock band Jethro Tull, formed in 1967, took its name from him.
