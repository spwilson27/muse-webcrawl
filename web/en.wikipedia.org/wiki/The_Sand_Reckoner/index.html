The Sand Reckoner (Greek: Ψαμμίτης, Psammites) is a work by Archimedes, the 3rd-century BC Greek mathematician of Syracuse, in which he set out to determine an upper bound for the number of grains of sand that would fill the entire universe. To do so he had to invent a system for expressing extremely large numbers and to estimate the size of the universe according to contemporary astronomical models.

Content

Addressed to King Gelon II, the work begins by noting that Greek number names, based on the myriad (10,000), could not conveniently express very large quantities. Archimedes therefore built a positional-like system of "orders" and "periods" capable of writing numbers up to 10(8×1016) — vastly beyond any practical need.

He then adopted the heliocentric hypothesis of Aristarchus of Samos — the earliest surviving description of it — and, making deliberately generous assumptions (for example, that a poppy seed contains no more than 10,000 grains of sand, and that the sphere of the fixed stars is enormously larger than the Earth's orbit), calculated that the universe could contain no more than about 1063 grains of sand. The treatise is thus both a landmark in the history of numeration and a key source for ancient Greek astronomy and cosmology.
