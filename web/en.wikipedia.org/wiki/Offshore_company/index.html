Offshore company

An offshore company is a company or corporation that is incorporated in a jurisdiction other than the jurisdiction in which it operates or in which its directors and shareholders reside. The term is generally used to describe a company formed in a jurisdiction with low or no taxation, often called a tax haven, where the company conducts little or no local business. Typical characteristics include minimal reporting requirements, low formation fees, and legal frameworks designed to attract foreign incorporation.

Characteristics

Offshore companies are typically formed in jurisdictions such as the British Virgin Islands, the Cayman Islands, Panama, Belize, Seychelles and Delaware in the United States. A common legal form is the International Business Company (IBC). Most offshore jurisdictions charge little or no corporate tax on income earned outside the jurisdiction, do not require public disclosure of directors or shareholders (though this has changed substantially under international pressure), and allow incorporation within days.

Uses

Legitimate uses of offshore companies include international trade, holding intellectual property, asset protection, estate planning, operating shipping fleets under flags of convenience, and structuring cross-border investment. International joint ventures frequently use neutral jurisdictions so that neither party operates under the other's legal system.

Criticism and regulation

Offshore companies have been widely criticised for facilitating tax avoidance, tax evasion, money laundering and the concealment of assets. Leaks such as the Panama Papers (2016), the Paradise Papers (2017) and the Pandora Papers (2021) exposed the scale of offshore structures used by politicians, celebrities and criminals. In response, regimes such as beneficial-ownership registers, the OECD Common Reporting Standard for automatic exchange of tax information, and economic-substance legislation have been introduced in most major offshore centres, significantly reducing anonymity.
