Mu-metal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A multi-layer mu-metal shield used to protect a sensitive detector from magnetic fields.

Mu-metal is a nickel–iron soft ferromagnetic alloy with very high magnetic permeability, commonly composed of approximately 77% nickel, 16% iron, 5% copper, and 2% chromium or molybdenum. Its name derives from the Greek letter mu (μ), the symbol for magnetic permeability. Mu-metal typically has a relative permeability of 80,000–100,000, compared with a few thousand for ordinary steel, making it extremely effective at attenuating low-frequency magnetic fields.

Properties and manufacture

The alloy's high permeability comes from its carefully controlled composition and, crucially, from a final anneal in a hydrogen atmosphere after all forming work is complete. Bending, stamping, or welding the finished material damages its crystal structure and drastically reduces its permeability, so mu-metal parts are shaped first and annealed last; even dropping a finished shield can degrade its performance. Mu-metal saturates at a relatively low magnetic flux density (about 0.75 tesla), so strong fields may require outer layers of a higher-saturation material such as ordinary steel or a 50% nickel alloy.

Applications

Because mu-metal shields work by providing a low-reluctance path that diverts magnetic flux around a protected volume, the material is used to shield cathode-ray tubes, photomultiplier tubes, magnetic resonance imaging equipment, sensitive electronic instruments, audio transformers, and the chambers used in atomic physics and biomagnetism experiments. Multiple nested layers separated by gaps shield far more effectively than a single thick layer. Alloy families marketed under names such as Mumetall, Permalloy, HyMu 80, and AD-MU are closely related.

History

Mu-metal was developed in Britain in the 1920s by the Telegraph Construction and Maintenance Company (Telcon) for shielding submarine telegraph cables, where its use allowed cables to carry signals faster and with less distortion; the underlying research built on the earlier discovery of permalloy by Gustav Elmen at Bell Laboratories.
