The Place Charles de Gaulle, historically known as the Place de l'Étoile, is a large circular road junction in Paris, France, at the western end of the Champs-Élysées. At its centre stands the Arc de Triomphe. Twelve straight avenues radiate from it, forming the star (étoile) that gave the square its original name.

History

The junction was laid out from 1670 on the axis of the Tuileries garden, and its star plan took shape under the urban renovations of the 18th and 19th centuries, notably those of Baron Haussmann, who regularised the ring of hôtels particuliers around it. The Arc de Triomphe, commissioned by Napoleon in 1806 and completed in 1836, occupies the centre. Beneath it lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, installed in 1921, with an eternal flame rekindled daily.

In 1970, following the death of Charles de Gaulle, the square was renamed in his honour, though many Parisians still call it l'Étoile. It marks the meeting point of the 8th, 16th and 17th arrondissements.

Layout and traffic

The twelve avenues include the Champs-Élysées, Avenue Foch, Avenue Victor Hugo, Avenue Carnot and Avenue de la Grande Armée. The roundabout is famous for its chaotic traffic, in which vehicles already circulating do not have priority — priority belongs to those entering (priorité à droite). Pedestrians reach the arch only by an underground passage. Below the square is the Charles de Gaulle–Étoile station, serving several Métro lines and the RER A.
