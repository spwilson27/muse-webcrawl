Process art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Process art is an artistic movement that emerged in the United States and Europe in the mid-1960s, in which the process of making the work — gathering, arranging, cutting, pouring, dropping, and the natural behaviour of materials over time — is more important than any predetermined finished object. It is closely related to postminimalism and sometimes overlaps with land art and arte povera.

Characteristics

Process artists deliberately used perishable, unstable or everyday materials — latex, felt, wax, ice, fat, lead, sawdust, grease — and allowed gravity, evaporation, melting, rust and decay to shape the result. Works were often arranged directly on the floor and meant to change, sag or disintegrate after exhibition, challenging the idea of art as a permanent, saleable commodity.

Artists

Key figures include Robert Morris, whose felt pieces hang according to gravity; Eva Hesse, known for latex and fibreglass works shaped by material behaviour; Richard Serra, famous for splashed-lead and thrown-rubber works and his "Verb List" of actions (to roll, to crease, to pour...); Bruce Nauman; Lynda Benglis, who poured brightly coloured polyurethane foam directly onto gallery floors; and Barry Le Va, who scattered broken glass and felt.

Legacy

The movement influenced installation art, performance art and contemporary practices that foreground making, entropy and the temporality of materials over the finished product.
