Environmental impact of the automobile

The environmental impact of the automobile encompasses the effects of cars and other road vehicles across their life cycle: extraction and manufacture, fuel production, exhaust and non-exhaust emissions during use, noise, land taken by roads and parking, and disposal. Road transport accounts for roughly a sixth of global greenhouse-gas emissions, of which passenger cars are the largest share.

Air pollution and climate

Internal-combustion vehicles emit carbon dioxide (proportional to fuel burned), nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons and particulate matter. In cities, traffic is a principal source of the NO₂ and fine particles (PM2.5) associated with respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Non-exhaust emissions — from tyre, brake and road wear — have grown in relative importance as exhaust controls tightened, and continue even with electric vehicles. Catalytic converters, particulate filters and successive Euro emission standards have greatly reduced per-vehicle toxic emissions in developed countries.

Life cycle and electrification

Manufacturing a car, particularly the battery of an electric vehicle, generates substantial emissions and demand for metals such as lithium, cobalt and nickel. Life-cycle analyses nevertheless generally find battery-electric cars produce significantly lower lifetime CO₂ emissions than comparable petrol cars, with the advantage growing as electricity grids decarbonise. End-of-life vehicles are widely recycled for steel and aluminium, though plastics and battery materials pose challenges.

Other impacts

Roads fragment habitats and roadkill affects wildlife; runoff carries oil, heavy metals and tyre-derived microplastics into waterways; and car-oriented development consumes land and encourages sprawl. Noise pollution from traffic is linked to sleep disturbance and cardiovascular risk. Responses include fuel-economy and CO₂ standards, low-emission zones, public-transport and cycling investment, and policies to reduce car dependence.
