Moloch

Moloch (Hebrew: מֹלֶךְ, Mōlek) is a name or term that appears in the Hebrew Bible, associated in biblical tradition with a type of sacrifice — particularly the sacrifice of children by fire — and with a deity of the Ammonites. Biblical texts strongly condemn rites involving Moloch, describing them as an abomination practised in the Valley of Hinnom (Gehenna) outside Jerusalem.

Biblical accounts

Leviticus forbids "giving any of your children to Moloch" on pain of death, and the Books of Kings recount that kings Ahaz and Manasseh "made their sons pass through the fire", while Josiah defiled Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom so that no one might burn a son or daughter "to Moloch". Jeremiah and Ezekiel likewise condemn the practice. Solomon is said to have built a high place for "Milcom, the abomination of the Ammonites", whose name may be related.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether Moloch was the name of a god — possibly a form of the Semitic word melek, "king" — or a term for a sacrificial rite (the Punic molk sacrifice). Phoenician and Carthaginian tophets, cemeteries of cremated infants, have been linked to the cult condemned in the Bible, though whether these represent child sacrifice or infant burial remains contested among archaeologists.

Cultural legacy

In later literature Moloch became a symbol of a devouring power demanding human sacrifice: Milton's Paradise Lost lists Moloch among the fallen angels; John Stuart Mill and later writers used "Moloch" for oppressive systems; Allen Ginsberg's poem Howl (1956) famously personifies Moloch as the destructive spirit of modern America.
