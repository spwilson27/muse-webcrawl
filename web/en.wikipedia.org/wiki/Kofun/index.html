Kofun

Kofun (古墳, "old tomb") are megalithic tombs built in Japan between the 3rd and 7th centuries AD, which give their name to the Kofun period of Japanese history. They range from simple round and square mounds to the enormous keyhole-shaped imperial tombs (zenpō-kōen-fun) of the Yamato kings, some of which are among the largest tombs in the world by area.

Forms

The earliest kofun, such as Hashihaka in Nara Prefecture, date to the late 3rd century and already show the characteristic keyhole plan: a circular rear mound joined to a trapezoidal front. Other shapes include round (enpun), square (hōfun), scallop-shaped and octagonal mounds. Mounds were often surrounded by moats and decorated with haniwa — terracotta figures of houses, animals, warriors and ritual objects placed on and around the tomb.

Burial chambers and goods

Early kofun contain pit-style stone chambers with wooden coffins; from the 5th century, corridor-style horizontal chambers influenced by the Korean peninsula allowed repeated burials. Grave goods included bronze mirrors imported from China, iron weapons and armour, gilt-bronze crowns and horse trappings, and curved jewels (magatama), reflecting the power and continental connections of the buried elite.

The great imperial tombs

The largest kofun, the Daisenryō Kofun in Sakai, Osaka — traditionally attributed to Emperor Nintoku — is about 486 m long and covers some 46 hectares with its moats. The Mozu–Furuichi Kofun Group, a cluster of such tombs in Osaka Prefecture, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2019. Many imperial kofun are administered by the Imperial Household Agency and have never been excavated, leaving their occupants' identities a matter of tradition rather than archaeology.
