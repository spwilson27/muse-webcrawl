Anti-psychiatry

Anti-psychiatry, also known as critical psychiatry, is a movement that questions the medical model of mental illness and the practices of psychiatry, including involuntary treatment, psychiatric diagnosis and the use of psychoactive medication. The term was coined by psychiatrist David Cooper in 1967.

Key figures and ideas

Prominent contributors include R. D. Laing (The Divided Self, 1960), Thomas Szasz (The Myth of Mental Illness, 1961), Michel Foucault (Madness and Civilization, 1961) and Franco Basaglia, who led Italy's deinstitutionalisation reforms. Critics argued that psychiatric diagnoses lack objective validity, that "mental illness" is a metaphor or a social label for problematic behaviour, and that asylums were coercive institutions.

Legacy

The movement influenced deinstitutionalisation, patients' rights and informed-consent reforms, and survives in the psychiatric-survivor movement and academic critical psychiatry. Mainstream psychiatry responds that severe mental disorders have demonstrable biological correlates and that treatments, while imperfect, relieve suffering; critics within psychiatry itself continue to debate diagnostic validity, such as controversies surrounding the DSM.
