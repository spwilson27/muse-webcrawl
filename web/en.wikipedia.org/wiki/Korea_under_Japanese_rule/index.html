Korea under Japanese rule

The Government-General of Chōsen headquarters, Keijō (Seoul) (image placeholder)

Korea under Japanese rule was the period of Japanese imperial rule over the Korean Peninsula from 1910 to 1945, beginning with the Japan–Korea Annexation Treaty of 1910 and ending with Japan's surrender in World War II. Korea (Japanese: Chōsen) was administered by a Governor-General answerable to the Emperor, seated in Keijō (Seoul). The period brought infrastructure, industrialisation in the north and expanded schooling, alongside political repression, land expropriation, exploitation of Korean labour, forced assimilation policies, and wartime mobilisation including the conscription of soldiers, labourers and "comfort women" for the Japanese military — grievances that still shape relations between Japan and the two Koreas.

Background and annexation

Japan forced Korea open with the Treaty of Ganghwa (1876), and after victories over Qing China (1895) and Russia (1905) made Korea a protectorate by the Eulsa Treaty of 1905, stripping it of diplomatic rights. The Korean Emperor Gojong was forced to abdicate in 1907, the Korean army was disbanded, and in August 1910 the last prime minister, Yi Wan-yong, signed the annexation treaty; Emperor Sunjong was deposed and Korea became a Japanese colony. Armed resistance by "righteous armies" (uibyeong) was suppressed in a brutal pacification campaign before and after annexation.

Military rule and the March First Movement

The first decade (1910–1919) was one of direct military rule (budan seiji): Koreans were denied freedom of assembly and press, the military police (gendarmerie) supervised civilian life, and a land survey (1910–1918) consolidated Japanese and corporate ownership of Korean farmland. On 1 March 1919 millions of Koreans joined nationwide peaceful demonstrations for independence, inspired by Wilsonian self-determination and the death of Gojong; the March First Movement was crushed with mass arrests and killings. Korean leaders formed a Provisional Government in exile in Shanghai.

Cultural rule

In the 1920s the administration shifted to "cultural rule" (bunka seiji), permitting Korean newspapers, magazines and cultural associations under censorship, while continuing surveillance and arrests. Korean literature, cinema and scholarship flourished within limits, and modernist movements emerged in Seoul. Meanwhile the colonial economy was integrated into the Japanese empire: rice exports to Japan grew even as Korean per-capita rice consumption fell, and in the 1930s heavy and chemical industries (hydroelectric power, fertiliser, mining) were built in the north to supply Japan's war economy in Manchuria.

Wartime mobilisation

After 1937 total-war mobilisation brought forced assimilation: Koreans were pressured to worship at Shinto shrines, adopt Japanese names (sōshi-kaimei, from 1940) and speak Japanese; Korean-language instruction and newspapers were abolished by 1943. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans were drafted as labourers to mines and factories in Japan, Sakhalin and the Pacific, from 1944 as conscript soldiers; tens of thousands of (mostly Korean) women were forced into military sexual slavery as "comfort women".

End of rule and legacy

Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945 ended colonial rule; Korea was divided at the 38th parallel between Soviet and American occupation zones, leading to two states in 1948 and the Korean War. Colonial-era disputes — apologies and compensation for comfort women and forced labour, the legality of the annexation treaties, textbook portrayals and the Dokdo/Takeshima dispute — continue to complicate Japan–Korea relations; Japan and South Korea normalised ties in 1965, but controversies over historical memory recur.
