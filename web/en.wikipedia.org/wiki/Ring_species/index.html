Concept developed byErnst Mayr and others, 20th century

In biology, a ring species is a connected series of neighbouring populations, each of which interbreeds with closely sited related populations, but for which there exist at least two "end" populations in the series which are too distantly related to interbreed, though there is a potential gene flow between each "linked" population and the next. Such non-breeding, though genetically connected, "end" populations may co-exist in the same region (sympatry), thus closing a "ring." Ring species are often cited as striking evidence of gradual evolution and speciation, because they capture, in space, the kind of divergence that usually unfolds over time.

Definition and significance

The concept illustrates the difficulty of drawing sharp boundaries for the biological species concept. Around a geographic barrier (a mountain range, valley, or polar ice cap), populations diverge as they spread in two directions; where the two fronts meet again, the terminal forms may behave as separate species even though they are linked by a chain of interbreeding intermediates. Ernst Mayr highlighted ring species in developing the theory of allopatric speciation, and they remain a classic teaching example — though modern genetic studies have shown several celebrated cases to be more complicated than the simple textbook ring.

Examples

  • Ensatina eschscholtzii salamanders around California's Central Valley: subspecies intergrade along both the coastal and inland arms, while the forms that meet in southern California rarely hybridise.
  • The gull complex around the Arctic — the herring gull and lesser black-backed gull were the classic example — though DNA studies have shown the history is not a simple ring, and the case is now considered disputed.
  • Greenish warblers (Phylloscopus trochiloides) around the Tibetan Plateau: two Siberian forms, viridanus and plumbeitarsus, differ in song and do not interbreed where they meet, being connected by gradations through Central Asia; often regarded as the best-supported case.
  • Euphorbia tithymaloides in the Caribbean and deer mice and other rodents in North America have also been proposed.

Problems and debate

Perfect rings require uninterrupted gene flow around the barrier and simultaneous divergence; gaps, local extinctions, and secondary contact can blur the pattern. Some purported rings (gulls, and arguably the herring gull complex and some warbler and salamander details) have been reclassified after genomic analysis, prompting debate over whether any case is a "perfect" ring — while the underlying phenomenon of clinal divergence with terminal reproductive isolation remains well documented.
