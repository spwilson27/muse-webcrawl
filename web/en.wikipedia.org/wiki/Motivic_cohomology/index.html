Motivic cohomology

Motivic cohomology is a cohomology theory in algebraic geometry whose existence was first conjectured by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s. It is intended to be a universal cohomology theory for algebraic varieties that simultaneously refines and relates other theories such as singular cohomology, de Rham cohomology, étale cohomology, and algebraic K-theory. The theory was developed decisively by Vladimir Voevodsky, who received the Fields Medal in 2002 in part for this work and its applications.

Motivation

Grothendieck introduced the notion of a motive as a kind of "generalized space" underlying the various cohomology groups of a variety. Weil's conjectures, proved by Deligne, showed that the different Weil cohomologies of a smooth projective variety have the same Betti numbers and Frobenius eigenvalues, suggesting a common source. Motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(X) are bigraded: the degree p is cohomological, while q is a "weight" or Tate twist.

Definition

For smooth varieties, motivic cohomology can be defined using Bloch's higher Chow groups:

Hp,q(X, Z) = CHq(X, 2q − p)

Voevodsky gave an alternative construction using complexes of sheaves in the Zariski or Nisnevich topology built from finite correspondences, producing the motivic complexes Z(q). For a field k, motivic cohomology relates to Milnor K-theory: Hn,n(Spec k, Z) ≅ KMn(k).

Key results

Voevodsky used motivic cohomology to prove the Milnor conjecture (that Milnor K-theory modulo 2 of a field is isomorphic to its Galois cohomology) and, with collaborators, the more general Bloch–Kato conjecture, now the norm residue isomorphism theorem. These results were major achievements connecting algebraic cycles, K-theory, and Galois cohomology.
