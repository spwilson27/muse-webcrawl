Smectite

Smectite is a mineral mixture of mainly clay-sized hydrous aluminium phyllosilicates (such as montmorillonite and beidellite). In geology, the term is used as an umbrella term for a group of clay minerals that share a characteristic swelling behaviour when in contact with water.

Smectites are a three-layer clay (2:1 clay) consisting of a central octahedral layer of aluminium oxide surrounded by two tetrahedral layers of silica. The silicate sheets are only weakly linked, allowing water and other molecules to enter between the sheets and expand the lattice, which causes smectite clays to swell considerably when wet.

Terminology

In mineralogy, smectite is the name of a group of sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron and lithium aluminium silicates. In soil science and ceramics, the older term montmorillonite group is often used interchangeably. The principal members of the group include montmorillonite, beidellite, nontronite, saponite and hectorite.

Properties

Smectite clays are known for their high cation-exchange capacity, plasticity and shrink–swell behaviour. They are the main constituent of bentonite, a rock type formed from altered volcanic ash, and of Fuller's earth. Cation exchange in smectite is facilitated by the substitution of lower-valence cations for higher-valence ones within the mineral lattice, producing a net negative charge balanced by exchangeable cations between layers.

Occurrence and uses

Smectites form by weathering of silicate rocks and volcanic ash, and are abundant in many soils. Commercially, smectite-rich clays are used as drilling muds, cat litter, foundry sands, and as adsorbents and clarifying agents in industry. In agriculture, smectite-rich soils are fertile but can be difficult to work because of cracking when dry and stickiness when wet (see vertisols).
