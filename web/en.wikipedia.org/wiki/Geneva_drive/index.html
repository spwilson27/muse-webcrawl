Geneva drive

Other namesMaltese cross mechanism
TypeGear mechanism
FunctionConverts continuous rotation into intermittent rotary motion
ApplicationsFilm projectors, watches, indexing machinery

The Geneva drive or Maltese cross is a gear mechanism that translates a continuous rotation movement into intermittent rotary motion. The rotating drive wheel is equipped with a pin that reaches into a slot of the driven wheel (the "star wheel"), advancing it by one step for each revolution of the drive wheel. For most of the revolution, a circular blocking disc on the drive wheel engages the curved sides of the star wheel to lock it in position.

Applications

The mechanism is named after Geneva, Switzerland, where it was used in watchmaking to prevent overwinding of mainsprings. Its best-known application is in mechanical film projectors, where the film must advance one frame at a time and dwell stationary while each frame is projected. Geneva mechanisms are also used in automatic indexing tables, assembly machinery, plotters, and sample changers, where precise stepwise positioning is required.

Characteristics

The number of slots in the star wheel determines the step angle: a four-slot wheel advances 90 degrees per cycle. Because the pin enters the slot with an unfavourable velocity profile, the mechanism experiences shock at engagement and is generally suited to low-to-moderate speeds; modified designs reduce this effect. An "internal" variant, in which the drive wheel is inside the ring-shaped driven wheel, is more compact but less common.
