Italian-American cuisine

Italian-American cuisine is a style of Italian cuisine adapted by Italian immigrants in the United States. Developed mainly by immigrants from southern Italy — Naples, Sicily, Calabria, and Apulia — who arrived in large numbers between the 1880s and 1920s, it reflects the greater availability of meat, cheese, and canned tomatoes in America.

Characteristics

Dishes are typically more richly sauced and meat-heavy than their Italian counterparts. Signature creations include spaghetti and meatballs, chicken and veal parmigiana, baked ziti, Italian-American pizza styles, the submarine sandwich, and "Sunday gravy", a long-simmered tomato sauce with several meats. Red-checkered-tablecloth restaurants serving these dishes became a fixture of American cities.

History

Immigrants adapted recipes to new ingredients: wheat was cheaper, and tomatoes, scarce fresh in Italy's off-season, were available canned year-round. Figures such as Chef Boyardee (Ettore Boiardi) popularised canned Italian-American food nationally in the twentieth century.
