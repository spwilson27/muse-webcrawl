Incongruity theory

Incongruity theory is one of the major theories of humour, holding that laughter and amusement result from the perception of something incongruous — a violation of one's mental patterns and expectations. It is generally regarded as the dominant theory of humour in modern philosophy and psychology, contrasted with the older superiority theory and relief theory.

History

The idea appears in the work of early modern thinkers and was developed by Immanuel Kant, who described laughter as arising from a strained expectation dissolved into nothing, and by Arthur Schopenhauer, who located laughter in the sudden perception of incongruity between a concept and the reality subsumed under it. Søren Kierkegaard also treated incongruity as central to the comic.

Formulations

Modern versions, such as the incongruity-resolution model, propose a two-stage process: the perceiver first detects an incongruity (as in a joke's punchline) and then resolves it by finding a rule that makes the surprising element fit after all. Pure, unresolved incongruity may instead be experienced as puzzling or absurd. Psychologists use the theory to explain children's humour development, from peekaboo games in infancy to verbal riddles.

Criticism

Critics note that not all incongruity is funny — surprises can be frightening or confusing — so the theory must specify the conditions (such as playfulness, safety, and resolution) under which incongruity produces amusement rather than other responses.
