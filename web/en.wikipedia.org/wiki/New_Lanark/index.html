New Lanark

LocationSouth Lanarkshire, Scotland
Founded1786
FoundersDavid Dale and Richard Arkwright
DesignationUNESCO World Heritage Site (2001)

New Lanark is a village on the River Clyde in South Lanarkshire, Scotland, founded in 1786 by David Dale, who built cotton mills powered by the Falls of Clyde in partnership with the inventor Richard Arkwright. It became world famous through the social and industrial reforms of Dale's son-in-law, the Welsh reformer Robert Owen, who managed the mills from 1800 to 1825 and turned the village into a model industrial community — an experiment that made him a founder of utopian socialism and the cooperative movement.

Owen's reforms

Owen raised wages, reduced working hours, and refused to employ children under ten, at a time when pauper apprentices as young as five worked in many mills. He built decent housing, provided free medical care, opened a village store selling quality goods at near-cost prices (a forerunner of the cooperative store and the source of the "divi"), and founded the Institution for the Formation of Character (1816), whose school offered what is often described as Britain's first infant school, with an emphasis on music, dancing, and learning without corporal punishment. The "silent monitor" system recorded each worker's daily conduct by coloured blocks rather than by fines. Visitor numbers ran into the tens of thousands, including royalty and reformers from across Europe and America; Owen later attempted a comparable experiment at New Harmony, Indiana.

Decline and revival

The mills continued under later owners into the twentieth century, finally closing in 1968. After a period of decline, the New Lanark Conservation Trust, founded in 1974, restored the village; it is now a major visitor attraction and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001, recognised as an outstanding example of an early planned industrial community and of Owen's social philosophy in built form.
