The classical, or traditional, Mongolian script, also known as Hudum Mongol bichig, is the first writing system created specifically for the Mongolian language, derived from the Old Uyghur alphabet, and was the only script of Mongolia until the introduction of Cyrillic in the 20th century. It is written vertically in columns running from left to right — a rotation of its originally horizontal Uyghur model — making it the only major vertical script whose columns proceed in that direction.

History

According to tradition, after the defeat of the Naiman in 1204, Genghis Khan captured a Uyghur scribe, Tata-tonga, who adapted the Old Uyghur alphabet to write Mongolian. The script was used for the empire's administration and for the Secret History of the Mongols. In 1269 Kublai Khan commissioned the 'Phags-pa script for all the empire's languages, but the Uyghur-derived script outlasted it. In the late 16th and 17th centuries the script was reformed for the translation of Buddhist texts; the Oirat scholar Zaya Pandita devised the Clear script (Todo bichig) in 1648, a modified version still associated with Oirat and Kalmyk. Ligdan Khan's court produced the great canonical translations that fixed the classical orthography.

The Manchu alphabet of the Qing dynasty was adapted from the Mongolian script in 1599 and modified in 1632.

Characteristics

The script is alphabetic, with letters for consonants and vowels whose shapes vary according to position in the word (initial, medial, final), as in Arabic. Words are built along a vertical "spine". Its classical orthography is highly conservative and often diverges from modern pronunciation: many vowels that are no longer pronounced remain written, and several letters are ambiguous — notably t/d, k/g, and the vowels o/u and ö/ü are written identically in many positions. These ambiguities, together with the vertical direction, long complicated its adaptation to typewriters, computers and the internet; the script was encoded in Unicode in 1999 (block U+1800–U+18AF), but rendering and input remain more complex than for Cyrillic.

Modern use

The script remains in everyday use in Inner Mongolia, where it appears on shop signs, newspapers and official documents. In Mongolia proper, after decades in which Cyrillic predominated, the traditional script has been revived as a symbol of national identity: it is taught in schools, used ceremonially, and since 2020 the government has promoted its wider use, with a stated goal of dual-script use in official documents from 2025.
