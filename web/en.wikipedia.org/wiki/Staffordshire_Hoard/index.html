Staffordshire Hoard

Discovered: July 2009, near Hammerwich, Staffordshire
Period: 7th century AD (Anglo-Saxon)
Material: gold, silver, garnet
Present location: Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery; Potteries Museum, Stoke-on-Trent

The Staffordshire Hoard is the largest hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold and silver metalwork yet found. It consists of almost 4,600 items and metal fragments, amounting to more than 5.1 kg of gold and 1.4 kg of silver, together with nearly 3,500 pieces of garnet cloisonné jewellery. It was discovered by metal detectorist Terry Herbert in a field near the village of Hammerwich, Staffordshire, in July 2009.

Contents

Remarkably, the hoard contains almost entirely martial items — sword and seax hilts, pommel caps, helmet pieces, and fittings stripped from weapons — and no coins or items of female jewellery. Many pieces are decorated with filigree and garnet inlays of the highest quality. One gold strip bears an inscription from the Book of Numbers (10:35) in Latin: "Rise up, LORD, and let thine enemies be scattered." A large gold cross, folded by its depositors, suggests the objects may have been treated as pagan trophies or that the burial was not itself a Christian act.

Dating and interpretation

The objects are dated to the seventh century AD, probably the period around AD 650–675, and are associated with the kingdom of Mercia. Archaeologists believe the items were deliberately stripped from prestigious weapons — handles and fittings removed while blades, presumably of steel, were kept or discarded — and buried as a single deposit, perhaps as war booty or a ritual offering. Excavations in 2012 recovered a further 81 items from the same field.

Valuation and display

The hoard was declared treasure and valued at £3.285 million, shared between the finder and the landowner. It was acquired jointly by Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery and the Potteries Museum and Art Gallery in Stoke-on-Trent, where selections are on permanent display.
