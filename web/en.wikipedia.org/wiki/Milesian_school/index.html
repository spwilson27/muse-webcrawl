Milesian school

The Milesian school was a school of thought founded in the 6th century BC in Miletus, a prosperous Greek city on the Ionian coast of Anatolia. Its three thinkers — Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes — are traditionally regarded as the first philosophers and scientists of the Western tradition, because they sought natural explanations of the world in terms of a single underlying principle, the archê, rather than appealing to mythology.

The three Milesians

  • Thales (c. 624 – c. 546 BC) held that the archê of all things is water. Aristotle reports his reasoning — water is the source of life and nourishment, and the earth rests on water. Thales is also credited with geometrical theorems and with predicting the solar eclipse of 585 BC.
  • Anaximander (c. 610 – c. 546 BC), Thales' pupil, proposed instead the apeiron — the "boundless" or indefinite — as the origin from which opposites separate out. He drew the first known map of the world, described the earth as a free-floating cylinder, and offered an early account of the origin of living things, including humans.
  • Anaximenes (c. 585 – c. 528 BC) chose air (aer) as the archê, explaining change through rarefaction and condensation: air rarefied becomes fire; condensed, it becomes wind, cloud, water, earth, and stone.

Significance

The Milesians' importance lies less in their particular answers than in the kind of question they asked: what is the world made of, and by what natural processes does it change? Their work marks the conventional beginning of the Pre-Socratic tradition and influenced later Greek thought, notably the Eleatics and the atomists, who took up the problem of the one and the many.
