Boyle's law

Boyle's law
StatementAt constant temperature, the pressure of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume
EquationpV = k (constant), or p1V1 = p2V2
Named afterRobert Boyle
Published1662
Related lawsCharles's law, Gay-Lussac's law, ideal gas law

Boyle's law, also referred to as the Boyle–Mariotte law or Mariotte's law (especially in France), is a gas law stating that the absolute pressure exerted by a given mass of an ideal gas is inversely proportional to the volume it occupies, provided the temperature and amount of gas remain unchanged within a closed system. Mathematically, pV = k, where p is pressure, V is volume, and k is a constant.

History

The relationship was first noted by Richard Towneley and Henry Power in the 1650s and was published by the Anglo-Irish natural philosopher Robert Boyle in 1662, based on experiments with a J-shaped tube of trapped air and mercury, many performed by his assistant Robert Hooke. The French physicist Edme Mariotte independently discovered the same law in 1679, and in French-speaking countries it commonly bears his name. Boyle called the phenomenon the "spring of the air".

Relation to other laws

Boyle's law is a special case of the ideal gas law at constant temperature and amount. Combined with Charles's law (volume proportional to temperature) and Gay-Lussac's law (pressure proportional to temperature), it forms the combined gas law. Kinetic theory later explained the law: halving a gas's volume doubles the frequency of molecular collisions with the container walls, and hence the pressure.

Applications

Boyle's law governs the behaviour of air in the human lungs during breathing, the operation of syringes and bicycle pumps, and the expansion of gases in scuba diving, where it explains why a diver must exhale during ascent to avoid lung over-expansion injury.
