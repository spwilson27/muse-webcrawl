Official Languages Act

The Official Languages Act is the name of legislation relating to official languages in several countries. Most prominently, it refers to the Canadian statute first enacted in 1969 that established English and French as the official languages of Canada. Acts of the same name exist or have existed in Ireland, Sri Lanka, and other jurisdictions.

Canada

The Canadian Official Languages Act (1969) was enacted under the government of Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, following the recommendations of the Royal Commission on Bilingualism and Biculturalism. It gave English and French equality of status in the Parliament and federal courts of Canada and in federal institutions, guaranteeing the public the right to communicate with and receive services from the federal government in either language. A substantially revised Act in 1988 implemented language rights entrenched in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982), and created the office of the Commissioner of Official Languages to oversee compliance. The Act was modernised again by legislation receiving royal assent in 2023, strengthening protections for French and recognising Indigenous language rights separately.

Ireland

Ireland's Official Languages Act 2003 (Acht na dTeangacha Oifigiúla) gave effect to the constitutional status of Irish as the first official language, requiring public bodies to provide services in Irish and establishing the office of An Coimisinéir Teanga (the Language Commissioner). Amendments enacted in 2021 set targets for Irish-language recruitment to the public service.

Sri Lanka

The Official Language Act No. 33 of 1956 in Ceylon (now Sri Lanka), popularly known as the "Sinhala Only Act", replaced English with Sinhala as the sole official language. It was deeply controversial, contributing to ethnic tensions; Tamil was later recognised as an official language by constitutional amendment in 1987.
