Gioachino Rossini

Born: February 29, 1792, Pesaro, Papal States

Died: November 13, 1868 (aged 76), Paris, France

Occupation: Composer

Notable works: The Barber of Seville, William Tell, La Cenerentola

Gioachino Antonio Rossini (February 29, 1792 – November 13, 1868) was an Italian composer who gained fame for his 39 operas, although he also wrote many songs, some chamber music and piano pieces, and some sacred music. He set new standards for both comic and serious opera before retiring from large-scale composition while still in his thirties, at the height of his popularity.

Life and career

Rossini was born in Pesaro, the son of a trumpeter and an opera singer. He studied at the Bologna conservatory and had his first opera staged in Venice in 1810. A string of successes across Italy — including Tancredi (1813) and The Italian Girl in Algiers (1813) — made him Italy's pre-eminent opera composer while still in his early twenties. His opera buffa masterpiece The Barber of Seville premiered in Rome in 1816; though its opening night was a fiasco, it quickly became one of the most beloved operas ever written. La Cenerentola followed in 1817.

As director of the Neapolitan theatres under the impresario Domenico Barbaja, Rossini wrote a series of serious operas for the soprano Isabella Colbran, whom he married in 1822, including Otello, Armida, and Semiramide (1823), his last Italian opera. Moving to Paris, he became director of the Théâtre-Italien and then composed for the Paris Opéra, adapting earlier works and culminating in Guillaume Tell (William Tell, 1829), a grand opera of unprecedented scale whose overture became one of the most famous in classical music.

Retirement and late years

After William Tell, aged only 37, Rossini effectively abandoned opera, worn down by ill health, contractual disputes, and changing tastes. He wrote little for two decades apart from the Stabat Mater (completed 1841). In his final years in Paris he hosted celebrated Saturday-evening salons (samedis soirs) and composed the witty piano pieces and songs he called Péchés de vieillesse ("Sins of Old Age"), including the Petite messe solennelle (1863). A celebrated wit and gourmet — dishes such as tournedos Rossini are named for him — he died in Passy, Paris, in 1868 and was buried in Père Lachaise Cemetery; his remains were moved to Florence's Basilica of Santa Croce in 1887.

Music and legacy

Rossini's style is noted for melodic abundance, rhythmic vitality, brilliant orchestration, and the trademark "Rossini crescendo," in which a repeated phrase builds from a whisper to a climax. He standardized the structure of Italian opera overtures and scenes, and his works dominated the bel canto era, influencing Donizetti, Bellini, and the young Verdi.
