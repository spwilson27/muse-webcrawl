Kalman filter

The Kalman filter is an algorithm that uses a series of measurements observed over time, including statistical noise and other inaccuracies, to produce estimates of unknown variables that tend to be more accurate than those based on a single measurement alone. It is named after Rudolf E. Kálmán, who published the method in 1960, and it is optimal — in the minimum-variance sense — for linear systems with Gaussian noise.

Overview

The filter operates in two repeating steps. In the predict step, the current state estimate is projected forward through a process model, along with its uncertainty (covariance). In the update step, a new measurement is blended with the prediction, weighted by the Kalman gain, which trusts the measurement more when the prediction is uncertain and vice versa:

x̂k = x̂k− + Kk(zk − H x̂k−)

Only the previous estimate is needed, so the algorithm requires little memory and is well suited to real-time computation.

Applications

The Kalman filter was developed for and famously used in the Apollo program's navigation computer, and is now ubiquitous: in GPS and inertial navigation, spacecraft and missile guidance, radar tracking, robot motion planning, computer vision, signal processing, econometrics, and the smoothing of sensor data such as battery state-of-charge estimates. Non-linear versions include the extended Kalman filter (EKF), the unscented Kalman filter (UKF), and related particle filters.

History

Kálmán's 1960 paper built on earlier work by Thorvald Thiele, Peter Swerling and others, reformulating Wiener filtering in state-space terms. Stanley Schmidt's implementation at NASA Ames made the method practical, and it guided Apollo spacecraft to the Moon.
