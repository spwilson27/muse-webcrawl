Oda Nobunaga

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Oda Nobunaga in later Japanese painting]

Oda Nobunaga (23 June 1534 – 21 June 1582) was a Japanese daimyo and the first of the three great unifiers of Japan, who rose from a minor house in Owari province to conquer central Japan and lay the foundations of national unification. A revolutionary commander — his arquebus volleys at Nagashino (1575) shattered the Takeda — and a ruthless enemy of entrenched privilege, he destroyed the warrior-monks of Enryaku-ji and the Ikkō-ikki leagues, abolished guilds and toll barriers in his domains, and made free markets and merit his instruments. On the verge of supremacy he was betrayed by his general Akechi Mitsuhide and died at the Honnō-ji temple in Kyoto in 1582, by seppuku as it burned, leaving unification to his lieutenant Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

Rise

Succeeding his father in 1551 amid fraternal strife, Nobunaga first united Owari, then at Okehazama (1560) destroyed the invading army of Imagawa Yoshimoto in a surprise attack during a thunderstorm — the classic Japanese underdog victory. In 1568 he marched the Ashikaga pretender Yoshiaki into Kyoto, then deposed him (1573), ending the Ashikaga shogunate. His wars against the Azai, Asakura, Takeda and Mōri, and his decade-long Ishiyama Hongan-ji War against the Ikkō-ikki confederation, subdued the centre of Japan; his castle at Azuchi (1576) — seven storeys of gold and revolutionary painting — advertised the new order.

Honnō-ji and legacy

In June 1582, with the conquest of the Mōri under way and Nobunaga's armies on every front, Akechi Mitsuhide — for reasons still argued (ambition, grievance, fear, others' conspiracy) — turned his army on Kyoto and trapped Nobunaga at Honnō-ji with a token escort. Nobunaga's body was never found. Mitsuhide was dead within thirteen days, killed after Hideyoshi's lightning march and victory at Yamazaki. Nobunaga's reputation — demon king (maō) to the monks he burned, moderniser to posterity — anchors the standard narrative of Japan's unification: Nobunaga pounded the rice cake, Hideyoshi kneaded it, Tokugawa Ieyasu ate it.
