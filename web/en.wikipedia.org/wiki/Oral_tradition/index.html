Oral tradition

Oral tradition, or oral lore, is a form of human communication in which knowledge, art, ideas and cultural material are received, preserved and transmitted orally from one generation to another — through speech or song, including folktales, ballads, epics, proverbs, genealogies and ritual texts. It is the principal medium of cultural transmission in societies without writing and remains significant everywhere alongside it.

Characteristics

Oral transmission is not mere rote memorisation. In living traditions, performance is compositional: trained specialists — West African griots, South Slavic guslars, rhapsodes — recompose works in performance using formulaic phrases, metre, melody and mnemonic structures. Texts therefore exist in fluid multiform versions rather than a single fixed original, though some traditions (such as Vedic chanting in India) developed elaborate error-correction techniques to preserve enormous texts verbatim over millennia.

Scholarship

In the 1930s Milman Parry and Albert Lord developed the oral-formulaic theory from fieldwork with Bosnian epic singers, arguing that the Homeric poems were products of oral composition. Walter Ong's Orality and Literacy (1982) explored how the technology of writing restructures thought. Oral traditions pose methodological questions for historians, who must weigh living memory and the "feedback" of written sources, as Jan Vansina established for African historiography. UNESCO recognises many oral traditions as Intangible Cultural Heritage. Orality also underpins scripture: the Vedas, much of the Bible, the Qur'an's recitation and the rabbinic "Oral Torah" all began or persist as oral transmission.
