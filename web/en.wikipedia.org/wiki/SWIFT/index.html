SWIFT, the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, is a Belgian cooperative that operates a global messaging network used by banks and other financial institutions to send and receive standardised, secure information about financial transactions — most notably payment orders. Founded in 1973 and live from 1977, SWIFT does not itself hold funds or accounts: it provides the trusted "language" and rails by which member institutions instruct one another. Its standards include the familiar BIC ("SWIFT code") identifying each institution, and the MT message formats now migrating to ISO 20022 (MX).

History and operation

SWIFT was created by 239 banks in 15 countries to replace the slower, error-prone telex system. The network went live in 1977 and expanded to connect more than 11,000 institutions in over 200 countries, carrying on the order of 45 million messages per day at peak. Members are identified by an 8- or 11-character Business Identifier Code. Message categories cover customer payments, interbank transfers, treasury and securities trading, trade finance, and more. Oversight is provided by the central banks of the G-10 countries, led by the National Bank of Belgium.

Sanctions and geopolitics

Because disconnection from SWIFT severely hampers a country's banks, exclusion has become a major sanctions tool. Iranian banks were disconnected in 2012 and again after 2018, and in 2022, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the EU, US, UK and allies removed most major Russian banks from the network. Such episodes have spurred alternative systems, including Russia's SPFS and China's CIPS, and wider debates about the neutrality of financial infrastructure. SWIFT has also been a target of high-profile cybercrime, notably the 2016 Bangladesh Bank heist, in which attackers used stolen credentials to send fraudulent SWIFT messages and steal $81 million.
