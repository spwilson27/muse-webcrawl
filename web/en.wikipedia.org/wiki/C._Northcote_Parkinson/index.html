C. Northcote Parkinson

Cyril Northcote Parkinson (30 July 1909 – 9 March 1993) was a British naval historian and author of some sixty books, the most famous of which was Parkinson's Law (1957), in which he advanced the satirical law that "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion" and described the self-perpetuating growth of bureaucracies.

Life and career

Educated at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, and King's College London, Parkinson taught history in Malaya and at the University of Liverpool, served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, and wrote respected histories of trade and naval warfare in the age of sail, including the Hornblower-influenced fictional series about Richard Delancey.

Parkinson's law

The law first appeared as a humorous essay in The Economist in 1955 and was expanded in the 1957 book. Parkinson illustrated bureaucratic growth with the Admiralty and Colonial Office, whose staff increased as their responsibilities shrank, and derived a satirical "mathematical" formula for the expansion of committees. He also formulated the "law of triviality" — that organizations spend disproportionate time on trivial issues (the "bike-shed effect").

Later work

Parkinson lectured and wrote on management and politics into his eighties, dividing his later years between Guernsey and Canterbury.
