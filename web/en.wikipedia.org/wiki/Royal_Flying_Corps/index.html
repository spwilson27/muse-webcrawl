Royal Flying Corps

An RFC biplane, c. 1917

The Royal Flying Corps (RFC) was the air arm of the British Army before and during the First World War. Formed in May 1912, it was responsible for reconnaissance, artillery spotting, bombing and air fighting over the Western Front and other theatres. On 1 April 1918 the RFC merged with the Royal Naval Air Service to form the Royal Air Force.

Origins

The RFC was established by royal warrant on 13 April 1912 and came into being on 13 May 1912, superseding the Air Battalion of the Royal Engineers. It consisted of a Military Wing, a Naval Wing, the Central Flying School at Upavon and the Royal Aircraft Factory at Farnborough. At the outbreak of war in August 1914, four squadrons accompanied the British Expeditionary Force to France.

First World War

The corps expanded enormously during the war, growing from a few dozen aircraft to a force of tens of thousands of men and thousands of machines. Its squadrons performed photographic reconnaissance, ranged artillery by wireless, bombed enemy airfields and supply lines, and fought German aircraft for control of the skies. Losses were heavy: training accidents and combat together killed thousands of airmen, and periods such as "Bloody April" 1917 saw severe attrition among new pilots.

Commanders included David Henderson, Hugh Trenchard and John Salmond. In 1917 the corps adopted offensive patrol doctrine deep over enemy lines, accepting high losses to guarantee support for the army.

Legacy

The RFC's traditions — its motto Per ardua ad astra and many of its squadrons — passed to the Royal Air Force on its formation.
