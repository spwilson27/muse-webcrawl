Tzedakah

Tzedakah (Hebrew: צְדָקָה) is the Hebrew word for philanthropy in the Jewish tradition. The word derives from the Hebrew root Tzade-Dalet-Qof, meaning righteousness, justice, or fairness. In Judaism, giving to the poor is not viewed as a generous, magnanimous act; instead it is an act of justice and righteousness, the performance of a duty, and a religious obligation (a mitzvah).

Principles

Unlike voluntary philanthropy, tzedakah is understood as obligatory: Jewish law holds that one must give even if one is poor oneself, and traditionally specifies giving a tithe of one's income (ma'aser). Recipients have a right to receive, and giving should preserve their dignity. The 12th-century sage Maimonides codified a famous hierarchy of giving, the "Ladder of Tzedakah": the lowest rung is giving begrudgingly, while the highest is helping a person become self-sufficient — for example through a job, a partnership, or an interest-free loan — so that they no longer need charity.

Practice

Tzedakah is traditionally given before Shabbat and holidays, and a tzedakah box (pushke) is kept in many Jewish homes and synagogues. Communal institutions historically organized collection and distribution to the poor. The concept encompasses not only money but also food, clothing, and other material help, and it is closely related to gemilut chasadim, acts of loving-kindness performed personally rather than financially.
