Scientific notation

Scientific notation is a way of expressing numbers that are too large or too small in a decimal form. It is commonly used by scientists, mathematicians, and engineers, in part because it can simplify certain arithmetic operations. On scientific calculators it is usually known as "SCI" display mode.

In scientific notation, nonzero numbers are written in the form m × 10n, where n is an integer and the coefficient m is a nonzero real number between 1 and 10 in absolute value (it is called the significand or mantissa). For example, the number 6,022,140,760,000,000,000,000,000 (Avogadro's number in molecules per mole) can be written as approximately 6.02214076×1023, and the mass of an electron, 0.00000000000000000000000000000091093837015 kg, can be written as 9.1093837015×10−31 kg.

Normalised notation

Any real number can be written in the form m × 10n in many ways: for example, 350 can be written as 3.5 × 102, 35 × 101, or 350 × 100. In normalised scientific notation (called "standard form" in the United Kingdom), the exponent n is chosen so that the absolute value of m remains greater than or equal to one but less than ten (1 ≤ |m| < 10). Thus 350 is written as 3.5 × 102.

Engineering notation

Engineering notation differs from normalised scientific notation in that the exponent n is restricted to multiples of 3, so the value of m is in the range 1 ≤ |m| < 1000. This matches the SI prefixes, so 3.5 × 102 would be written 350 × 100 or with a prefix as appropriate, and 12,300 would be written 12.3 × 103.

E notation

Most calculators and many computer programs present very large and very small results in scientific notation. Because superscripted exponents like 107 cannot always be displayed, the letter "E" or "e" is often used instead: 3.5e2 means 3.5 × 102.

Significant figures

Scientific notation also makes the number of significant figures unambiguous: all digits of the significand are significant. Thus 1.230 × 103 has four significant figures, distinguishing it from 1.23 × 103, which has three.
