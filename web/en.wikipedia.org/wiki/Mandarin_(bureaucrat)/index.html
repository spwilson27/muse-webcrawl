Mandarin (bureaucrat)

A mandarin (Chinese: 官, guān) was a bureaucrat scholar-official in the government of imperial China, and later Vietnam. The word entered English via Portuguese mandarim, from Malay menteri ("counsellor"), ultimately from Sanskrit mantrin ("minister"). From the 7th century onward, mandarins were recruited largely through the imperial examination system (keju), which tested candidates on the Confucian classics.

The system

The examination system, formalised under the Sui and Tang dynasties and reaching its mature form under the Song, was in principle open to almost any male candidate, making the mandarinate one of history's earliest large-scale meritocracies, though wealth for tutoring and years of study strongly favoured landed gentry families. Officials were graded into nine ranks, indicated by insignia including hat buttons and embroidered rank badges (buzi) showing birds for civil officials.

Mandarins administered counties, provinces, taxation, law and public works throughout the empire. The system endured, with interruptions, until its abolition in 1905, shortly before the fall of the Qing dynasty in 1912.

Legacy

"Mandarin" became the name of the standard administrative language of the empire — hence Mandarin Chinese — and in modern English the word is used figuratively for senior civil servants and, pejoratively, for an elite, self-perpetuating bureaucracy. The ideal of examination-based recruitment influenced civil-service reforms in Britain, France and elsewhere in the 19th century.
