Gaucho

A gaucho of the Argentine pampas in traditional attire.

A gaucho (Spanish: [ˈɡawtʃo]) or gaúcho (Portuguese) is a skilled horseman, reputed to be brave and unruly. Gauchos became greatly admired and renowned in legend, folklore and literature and became an important part of the regional cultural tradition of the pampas of Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, the Brazilian state of Rio Grande do Sul, and parts of Bolivia and southern Chile. The gaucho is comparable to the North American cowboy, the Chilean huaso, the Peruvian chalan and the Venezuelan/Colombian llanero.

History

Gauchos emerged in the 18th century from the mixed Spanish, Portuguese and Indigenous population of the Río de la Plata grasslands, living semi-nomadically by hunting the vast herds of feral cattle and horses descended from colonial stock, and trading in hides and tallow. Colonial authorities often treated them as vagabonds and smugglers. In the wars of independence (1810s–1820s) gaucho cavalry — notably Martín Güemes's forces in Salta — were decisive, and gauchos filled the armies of the caudillos during the civil wars that followed. The fencing of the pampas, the spread of large estancias and immigration in the late 19th century ended the free gaucho way of life, turning gauchos into ranch hands (peones).

Culture

Traditional dress includes a poncho, a wide-brimmed hat or beret-like boina, baggy trousers (bombachas), a sash (faja), a leather belt (rastra) often decorated with coins, a long knife (facón) and leather boots with spurs. Signature customs include drinking mate (yerba mate through a metal straw, the bombilla), grilled meat (asado), horsemanship displays and the payada — improvised sung duels with guitar.

In literature

The gaucho became an Argentine national symbol in epic poems such as José Hernández's Martín Fierro (1872, 1879), the classic of gauchesque literature, and in works by Esteban Echeverría, Domingo Sarmiento (who in Facundo treated the gaucho as both barbarism and raw material of the nation) and Ricardo Güiraldes's novel Don Segundo Sombra (1926). In modern Brazil, gaúcho is simply the demonym for natives of Rio Grande do Sul.
