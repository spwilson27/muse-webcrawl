Solidarity (Polish: Solidarność, pronounced [sɔliˈdarnɔɕt͡ɕ]), officially the Independent Self-Governing Trade Union "Solidarity", is a Polish trade union founded in August 1980 at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk under the leadership of Lech Wałęsa. It was the first trade union in a Warsaw Pact country not controlled by a communist party, and it grew into a broad social movement of some ten million members — a third of Poland's working population — that played a central role in ending communist rule in Poland and, by example, across Eastern Europe.

Origins and martial law

Solidarity emerged from strikes in the summer of 1980 sparked by food price rises and the dismissal of crane operator and activist Anna Walentynowicz. The Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980 conceded the strikers' 21 demands, including the right to independent unions and to strike. The movement's rise alarmed the Soviet bloc; on 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski imposed martial law, interning thousands of activists and delegalising the union in 1982. Wałęsa received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983, and Solidarity survived underground with support from the Catholic Church and Western labour movements.

Fall of communism and after

Renewed strikes in 1988 forced the government into the Round Table Talks of early 1989, leading to partially free elections on 4 June 1989, which Solidarity won overwhelmingly. Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc, and in 1990 Wałęsa was elected President of Poland. Solidarity later became a conventional trade union and a political force of the right; its distinctive red-and-white logo, designed by Jerzy Janiszewski, remains an iconic symbol of peaceful resistance.
