Computer-generated imagery

Computer-generated imagery (CGI) is a specific technology or application of computer graphics for creating or improving images in art, printed media, simulators, videos, and video games. These images may be static (still images) or dynamic (moving images). CGI technology is used in visual effects for films and television, in architectural visualisation, advertising, medical imaging, and flight simulation, and refers in film contexts especially to 3D computer graphics used to create characters, scenes, and effects.

History

Early CGI appeared in experimental films of the 1960s and 1970s; Futureworld (1976) featured the first use of 3D wireframe imagery in a feature film, and Tron (1982), Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan (1982, the Genesis effect), and The Last Starfighter (1984) expanded its role. Milestones include the stained-glass knight in Young Sherlock Holmes (1985), the water pseudopod in The Abyss (1989), the liquid-metal T-1000 in Terminator 2 (1991), and the dinosaurs of Jurassic Park (1993), which convinced the industry that photorealistic CGI characters were viable. Toy Story (1995) was the first fully computer-animated feature film. Subsequent decades brought performance capture (The Lord of the Rings' Gollum, Avatar), large-scale simulation of water, fire, cloth, and crowds, and real-time rendering in virtual production.

Techniques

CGI production typically involves modelling (creating 3D geometry), texturing and shading, rigging and animation, lighting, simulation (fluids, particles, cloth, hair), and rendering — the computation of final images, historically by ray tracing or rasterisation on large render farms, increasingly with physically based rendering and GPU and real-time engines. Two-dimensional CGI (digital compositing and paint) is also widely used to combine live-action footage with generated elements.

Applications and reception

Beyond entertainment, CGI supports product visualisation, forensic reconstruction, scientific visualisation, and virtual prototyping. Criticism of CGI in cinema sometimes targets inconsistent quality, the displacement of practical effects, and uncanny-valley characters; surveys nonetheless show CGI is now the default approach for spectacle filmmaking. The distinction between "CGI" and live action continues to blur with digital doubles and de-ageing.
