Daniel Bernoulli (8 February 1700 – 27 March 1782) was a Swiss mathematician and physicist, a member of the celebrated Bernoulli family of Basel. He is remembered above all for his applications of mathematics to mechanics, especially fluid mechanics — his name attaches to Bernoulli's principle, the statement that an increase in the speed of a fluid occurs simultaneously with a decrease in its pressure — and for pioneering work in probability, statistics and the kinetic theory of gases in his book Hydrodynamica (1738).

Early life and education

Daniel was born in Groningen, where his father Johann Bernoulli held a chair of mathematics; his uncle Jacob Bernoulli was likewise a leading mathematician. Johann intended Daniel for a business career and then, when that failed, for medicine; Daniel studied medicine at Heidelberg, Strasbourg and Basel, taking his doctorate in 1721 with a dissertation on respiration — but he taught himself mathematics throughout, publishing his first work, Exercitationes quaedam mathematicae, in 1724. His relationship with his father was permanently soured in 1734 when they were declared joint winners of a Paris Academy prize and Johann, unable to accept equality with his son, had Daniel banished from his house; Johann later published his own Hydraulica with a false early date to claim priority over Daniel's Hydrodynamica.

St Petersburg and Basel

In 1725 Daniel and his brother Nicolaus II took up chairs at the new Imperial Academy in St Petersburg. Nicolaus died within a year; Daniel stayed until 1733, working alongside the young Leonhard Euler, before returning to Basel, where he successively held chairs of anatomy and botany, physiology and finally physics (1750), which he kept until 1776. He won the Paris Academy's grand prize ten times, a record he shares with Euler, for memoirs on tides, magnetism, ocean currents and the efficiency of ship propulsion.

Hydrodynamica and Bernoulli's principle

Hydrodynamica (written by 1734, published 1738) organised the study of fluids around the conservation of vis viva (living force, essentially kinetic energy). From it follows Bernoulli's principle: along a streamline in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, the sum of pressure energy, kinetic energy and potential energy per unit volume is constant. The principle explains, in simplified treatments, the lift of aerofoils, the operation of Venturi meters and carburettors, and the curve of a spinning ball. The same book derived Boyle's law from the collisions of tiny particles in motion — an early statement of the kinetic theory of gases — and treated the flow of blood and the work of the heart.

Probability and economics

In a 1738 memoir written in St Petersburg, Bernoulli introduced the St. Petersburg paradox: a lottery whose expected monetary value is infinite, yet which nobody will pay much to play. His resolution — that utility grows only logarithmically with wealth, so expected utility is finite — founded the theory of utility and risk aversion, central to later economics. He also applied statistical reasoning to the controversy over smallpox inoculation (1760), computing from life tables how many years of life expectancy inoculation would add, an early classic of mathematical epidemiology.

Later years

Bernoulli remained intellectually active into old age, corresponding with Euler and Goldbach, and contributed to the theory of vibrating strings and to tidal theory (shared with Euler and Maclaurin in a 1740 prize essay). He died in Basel on 27 March 1782.
