Feminist sociology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Feminist sociology is a conflict theory and theoretical perspective which observes gender in its relation to power, both at the level of face-to-face interaction and reflexivity within social structures at large. Feminist sociologists focus on how gender and intersectionality relates to social constructs and how power is distributed based on systemic bias. The field developed largely in the second half of the twentieth century, as scholars such as Dorothy Smith, Patricia Hill Collins, and Arlie Russell Hochschild challenged the male-centered assumptions of mainstream sociology.

History

Although women such as Harriet Martineau contributed to the founding of sociology in the nineteenth century — Martineau translated Auguste Comte's work into English and wrote early analyses of marriage, education, and religion — their contributions were long marginalized by the discipline. The modern feminist sociology emerged alongside the second-wave feminist movement of the 1960s and 1970s, when women entered academia in greater numbers and began questioning why the canonical sociological theorists had largely ignored gender as a fundamental organizing principle of social life.

In 1975 Jessie Bernard described the emergence of a "sex-role" literature and the growing recognition that mainstream sociology amounted to the study of men. Dorothy Smith's concept of the "relations of ruling" and her development of standpoint theory argued that sociology should begin from women's everyday experiences. By the 1980s and 1990s, feminist journals, professional sections, and textbooks had institutionalized the perspective.

Key concepts

Gender as a social structure

Feminist sociologists treat gender not merely as an individual attribute but as a social structure embedded in institutions, interaction patterns, and cultural expectations. Judith Lorber's notion of "gender as an institution" and Barbara Risman's gender structure theory both frame gender as operating simultaneously at individual, interactional, and institutional levels.

Standpoint theory

Standpoint theory holds that knowledge is socially situated, and that marginalized groups may possess distinctive insight into social relations precisely because of their subordinate position. Nancy Hartsock developed a feminist standpoint drawing on Marxist theory, while Sandra Harding elaborated the related concept of "strong objectivity."

Emotional labour

Arlie Hochschild's The Managed Heart (1983) introduced the concept of emotional labour — the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display — and showed how service work demands fall disproportionately on women.

Waves and strands

Feminist sociology encompasses several strands, including liberal feminism, which focuses on legal equality and equal access to education and employment; Marxist and socialist feminism, which connect gender inequality to capitalism and unpaid domestic labour; radical feminism, which centers patriarchy as a fundamental system of power; and poststructuralist and queer approaches, which question fixed gender categories altogether. Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity, developed in Gender Trouble (1990), has been especially influential in this last strand.

Intersectionality

The concept of intersectionality, coined by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, holds that gender cannot be understood apart from race, class, sexuality, disability, and other axes of identity, because systems of oppression interlock. Patricia Hill Collins's Black Feminist Thought (1990) developed the idea of a "matrix of domination" and became a foundational text for intersectional sociology.

Criticism

Critics have argued that some feminist sociology is overly theoretical, that early work generalized from the experiences of white, middle-class women, and that the perspective can shade into advocacy rather than analysis. Feminist sociologists respond that all sociology reflects standpoints, and that making them explicit strengthens rather than weakens scholarship.
