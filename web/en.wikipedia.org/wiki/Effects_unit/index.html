Effects unit

An effects unit or effects pedal is an electronic device that alters the sound of a musical instrument or other audio source through audio signal processing. Effects units are most commonly used with electric and electronic instruments, especially the electric guitar, but may also be used with vocals and in studio recording. Common effects include distortion and fuzz, dynamics effects such as compression, filters such as the wah-wah pedal, modulation effects such as chorus and flanging, pitch effects such as octave generation, and time-based effects such as delay and reverb.

Formats

Effects are available in several formats. Stompboxes are compact pedals operated by a footswitch, designed to sit on the floor in front of the performer. Rack-mounted units are used in studios and large live rigs. Multi-effects processors combine many effects in a single floor or rack unit, while modern digital devices and software plug-ins emulate classic effects in computers and digital audio workstations.

History

Early electronic effects were built into amplifiers, such as the tremolo and spring reverb of 1950s guitar amplifiers. Stand-alone units followed, including tape echo machines and the fuzz pedals popularised in the 1960s, such as the Maestro Fuzz-Tone used on the Rolling Stones' "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" (1965). In the 1970s companies such as Electro-Harmonix, MXR, and Boss introduced a wide range of affordable pedals, and digital technology in the 1980s and 1990s brought programmable multi-effects and modelling amplifiers.

Signal chain

The order of effects in a chain affects the resulting sound. A typical order places dynamics effects first, followed by gain effects, modulation, and finally time-based effects such as delay and reverb, though musicians frequently experiment with placement.
