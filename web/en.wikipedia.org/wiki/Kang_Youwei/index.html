Kang Youwei

Born19 March 1858, Foshan, Guangdong, Qing China
Died31 March 1927, Qingdao, China
OccupationScholar, reformer, calligrapher
Known forHundred Days' Reform (1898); Datong shu

Kang Youwei (Chinese: 康有為; 1858–1927) was a Chinese scholar and political reformer of the late Qing dynasty. A brilliant and controversial Confucian thinker, he reinterpreted Confucius as a reformer and visionary of progress, and became the intellectual leader of the movement that produced the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898.

Reform career

After witnessing China's weakness following defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War, Kang and his student Liang Qichao organised petitions and study societies urging modernisation. In the summer of 1898 the young Guangxu Emperor adopted many of their proposals — reforms of education, the military, administration and the economy — in a rapid series of edicts. The programme was crushed after 103 days by the coup of the Empress Dowager Cixi; Kang escaped abroad, narrowly avoiding execution, and his brother was among those executed.

Thought and later life

In exile Kang founded a society to protect the emperor and continued to advocate constitutional monarchy, opposing the republican revolution of Sun Yat-sen. His most radical work, the Datong shu ("Book of the Great Unity"), sketched a utopian future world without nations, classes or family boundaries, though it was published in full only after his death. Kang returned to China in his later years, remained a monarchist, and died in Qingdao in 1927.
