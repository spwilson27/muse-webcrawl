Bagan (Burmese: ပုဂံ, formerly Pagan) is an ancient city in the Mandalay Region of Myanmar, on the east bank of the Irrawaddy River. From the 9th to the 13th centuries it was the capital of the Kingdom of Pagan, the first kingdom to unify the regions that would later constitute modern Myanmar. The Bagan Archaeological Zone — a plain of about 100 square kilometres dotted with more than 2,000 surviving Buddhist temples, pagodas and monasteries — is one of the great archaeological sites of Southeast Asia, comparable in scale to Angkor in Cambodia.

History

According to Burmese chronicles, Bagan was founded in the 2nd century CE and fortified in 849 CE by King Pyinbya. Its rise began under King Anawrahta (r. 1044–1077), who united the Irrawaddy valley, conquered the Mon kingdom of Thaton, and sponsored Theravada Buddhism, which his successors spread with enormous building campaigns. Between the 11th and 13th centuries, over 10,000 religious monuments were built on the plain, of which roughly 2,200 survive. The city's wealth drew scholars and artisans from India, Ceylon and the Khmer world.

The kingdom weakened through the 13th century under the strain of its own tax-exempt religious endowments and fell amid the Mongol invasions; the chronicles' dramatic account of the king fleeing and temples being demolished before the Mongols is now partly doubted, but Bagan ceased to be a royal capital after 1287. It remained a place of pilgrimage and scholarship.

Architecture

Bagan's monuments fall into two main forms: the solid stupa (pagoda, zeidi), housing relics, and the hollow gu-style temple, used for worship and meditation, often with a central massif bearing a Buddha image and murals of Jataka tales. Outstanding examples include the Shwezigon Pagoda, prototype of later Burmese stupas; the Ananda Temple (c. 1105), a cruciform masterpiece with four standing Buddhas; Thatbyinnyu, the tallest temple at 61–66 m; the massive Dhammayangyi, known for its fine brickwork and tyrannical founder legend; and the Sulamani and Htilominlo temples. Inscriptions in Old Burmese, Mon, Pali and Pyu are a major source for early Burmese history.

Modern era

Earthquakes have repeatedly damaged the monuments, notably in 1975 and on 24 August 2016, when about 400 temples were damaged. Restoration, some of it criticised by archaeologists in the 1990s, preceded Bagan's inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage Site on 6 July 2019. Today the zone is divided into Old Bagan, New Bagan and the market town of Nyaung-U, with tourism centred on sunrise and sunset viewing and hot-air balloon flights from October to March. Bagan is also known for its traditional lacquerware.
