Credit spread

The credit spread is the difference in yield between a corporate bond and a benchmark government bond.

Credit spread is a term used in finance with several related meanings. In bond markets, it is the difference in yield between two bonds of similar maturity but different credit quality — most commonly the yield on a corporate bond minus the yield on a benchmark government bond (such as a U.S. Treasury) of the same maturity. The spread compensates investors for the additional default risk of the corporate issuer, as well as for lower liquidity.

Bond credit spreads

Spreads are usually quoted in basis points. Investment-grade bonds typically trade at modest spreads over governments, while high-yield ("junk") bonds trade at much wider spreads. Spreads widen sharply in times of financial stress — as during the 2008 financial crisis and the March 2020 market turmoil — and narrow when investors are willing to take risk, making the credit spread a widely watched indicator of financial conditions and risk appetite.

Options

In options trading, a credit spread is a strategy in which the trader sells one option and buys another of the same type (calls or puts) on the same underlying, with the same expiration but different strike prices, receiving a net premium (credit) when opening the position. Bull put spreads and bear call spreads are common examples; the maximum profit is the net credit received and the maximum loss is the difference between strikes minus that credit.

Credit default swaps

The term is also used for the annual premium, quoted in basis points, on a credit default swap (CDS) referencing a borrower; a widening CDS spread indicates the market perceives a higher probability of default.
