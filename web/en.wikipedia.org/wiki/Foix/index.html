Foix (French pronunciation: [fwa]; Occitan: Fois) is a commune in southwestern France, the prefecture (capital) of the Ariège department in the Occitanie region. It lies south of Toulouse, in the foothills of the Pyrenees, at the confluence of the Ariège and Arget rivers. Although one of the smallest departmental capitals in France, it has a long history as the seat of the Counts of Foix.

History

The town grew up around an oratory founded by Charlemagne and the abbey of Saint-Volusien. Its castle, the Château de Foix, perched on a rocky outcrop above the old town, was the stronghold of the Counts of Foix, who resisted both the Albigensian Crusade and the expansion of the French crown. Gaston III, Count of Foix, known as Gaston Fébus, was a notable fourteenth-century ruler and author of the Livre de chasse, a famous treatise on hunting. Foix was repeatedly besieged but its castle was never taken by storm; it later served as a garrison and prison before becoming a museum of the department.

Geography

Foix sits where the Ariège emerges from the Pyrenean foothills into a broader valley. The medieval old town of narrow streets clusters beneath the castle rock, while modern districts spread along the valley floor. The surrounding countryside of the Plantaurel hills and the high Ariège valleys is popular for walking, climbing and caving.

Main sights

  • Château de Foix – the medieval castle with three towers dominating the town, now housing the Musée départemental de l'Ariège.
  • Abbatiale Saint-Volusien – the abbey church, rebuilt in the fourteenth century.
  • The old town's covered market hall and half-timbered streets.

Population

The commune has roughly 9,500 inhabitants, making the urban area one of the smaller prefectures in metropolitan France. Its economy rests largely on public administration, services and tourism.
