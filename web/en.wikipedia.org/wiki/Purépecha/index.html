Purépecha

The Purépecha (also spelled P'urhépecha) are an Indigenous people of the Mexican state of Michoacán, centred on the highlands around Lake Pátzcuaro. Historically known as the Tarascans, they built the Purépecha Empire, the second-largest state in Mesoamerica at the time of the Spanish conquest, and the only one never defeated by the Aztecs. Today there are roughly 140,000–180,000 Purépecha people, and their language, a language isolate unrelated to any other known language, is spoken by well over 100,000 people.

History

The Purépecha state took shape in the 14th century under the irecha (king) Tariácuri, who unified the Lake Pátzcuaro basin from his capital at Tzintzuntzan. The empire became renowned for its metalworking — copper, bronze, and gold — unusual in Mesoamerica, and for its stepped pyramidal platforms called yácatas. Purépecha armies repeatedly repelled Aztec invasions, including a crushing victory over the emperor Axayacatl in the 1470s. After the Spanish conquest, the last king, Tangaxuan II, was executed by Nuño de Guzmán in 1530, but the Franciscan Vasco de Quiroga organized the region's towns, each specializing in a craft — a pattern that survives today.

Culture

Purépecha communities are known for pottery, copper work, lacquerware, embroidery, and wood carving, as well as the pirekua, a traditional song form inscribed by UNESCO on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2010. The Day of the Dead celebrations around Lake Pátzcuaro, especially on Janitzio island, are among Mexico's most famous. In 2011 the community of Cherán expelled its municipal government and organized an autonomous self-government based on Indigenous customary law, a landmark in Mexican Indigenous rights.

Language

The Purépecha language is an isolate with no demonstrated relatives. It is agglutinative, with a rich system of suffixes, and is one of Mexico's more widely spoken Indigenous languages, with growing bilingual education and media.
