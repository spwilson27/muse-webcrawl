Cnut the Great

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Cnut and Queen Emma presenting a cross to the New Minster, Winchester, from the Liber Vitae]

Cnut (c. 990 – 12 November 1035), also spelled Canute and called Cnut the Great, was king of England (1016–1035), Denmark (1018–1035) and Norway (1028–1035) — the only ruler of the North Sea Empire that briefly united the three crowns. Son of Sweyn Forkbeard, he completed his father's conquest of England, married Emma of Normandy (widow of the defeated Æthelred), and ruled England not as a foreign occupier but as a conspicuously Christian, law-giving king: he issued law codes drafted with Archbishop Wulfstan, endowed churches, went on pilgrimage to Rome (1027), and cultivated the famous image — preserved in later chronicle — of the king who set his throne on the shore and commanded the tide to stop, to rebuke his flattering courtiers.

Conquest and consolidation

After Sweyn's sudden death (1014) and Edmund Ironside's resistance, Cnut won at Assandun (1016) and partitioned, then inherited, England. The early reign was ruthless — executed ealdormen, heavy taxation (the great Danegeld/hergeld), Danish earls installed — including Godwin of Wessex's rise. But Cnut soon governed through English institutions: he kept the shire courts, minted a uniform coinage, married into the old regime, and in his 1027 letter from Rome presented himself as a pilgrim king concerned for his subjects' fair treatment. His sons by Ælfgifu of Northampton (Svein, Harold Harefoot) and by Emma (Harthacnut) divided his inheritance as he died at Shaftesbury in 1035.

The tide and the legend

The tide story — from Henry of Huntingdon, a century later — inverts in popular memory: Cnut did not vainly command the sea expecting obedience; in the original moral, he staged the failure to teach that only God commands nature. The empire dissolved immediately at his death (Norway reverted at once, England split between his sons, the Norman claim through Emma led in time to 1066), but Cnut's England — integrated, taxed, monetised — was the richest kingdom of northern Europe, and his reign remains the great might-have-been of Anglo-Scandinavian history.
