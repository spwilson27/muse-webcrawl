Mount Hiei (Japanese: 比叡山, Hiei-zan) is a mountain to the northeast of Kyoto, lying on the border between Kyoto and Shiga prefectures in Japan. It is the site of Enryaku-ji, the head temple of the Tendai school of Japanese Buddhism, founded by the monk Saichō in 788. Because of its position guarding the north-eastern (demon gate) approach to the imperial capital, the mountain was long regarded as a spiritual bulwark of Kyoto.

Enryaku-ji and the warrior monks

Saichō established his temple with imperial support from Emperor Kanmu. Enryaku-ji grew into one of the most powerful institutions in Japan, its estates worked by thousands and its armies of warrior monks (sōhei) descending on Kyoto to press demands on the court. Many founders of later schools — Hōnen (Pure Land), Shinran (Jōdo Shinshū), Eisai (Rinzai Zen), Dōgen (Sōtō Zen), and Nichiren — trained on Mount Hiei before breaking away, earning it the title "mother mountain" of Japanese Buddhism.

Destruction and survival

In 1571 the warlord Oda Nobunaga, enraged by the monks' support of his enemies, burned Enryaku-ji and massacred thousands on the mountain in one of the most notorious acts of the Sengoku period. The temple was rebuilt under Tokugawa patronage and today forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto. Mount Hiei is also famed for the "marathon monks" (gyōja) of the Tendai school, who undertake the kaihōgyō — an ascetic practice of walking enormous distances around the mountain and holy sites over periods of up to 1,000 days.

Access

The mountain is reached by two funicular/cable-car systems — the Eizan line from the Kyoto side and the Sakamoto Cable from Ōtsu — and by a toll drive parkway with views over Lake Biwa and Kyoto.
