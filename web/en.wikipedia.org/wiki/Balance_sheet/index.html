Balance sheet

Balance sheet

Also calledStatement of financial position
Core equationAssets = Liabilities + Equity
One ofThe four basic financial statements (with income statement, cash-flow statement, statement of changes in equity)

In financial accounting, a balance sheet (also known as a statement of financial position) is a summary of the financial balances of an individual or organisation at a specific point in time — a snapshot of what the entity owns, what it owes, and the residual interest of its owners. It is one of the core financial statements used by investors, managers and creditors.

The accounting equation

The balance sheet is built on the fundamental accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity

Because every asset is financed either by borrowing (liabilities) or by owners' funds (equity), the two sides must always "balance" — hence the name. Equity is the residual: Assets − Liabilities.

Contents

Assets

Listed broadly in order of liquidity: current assets (cash and equivalents, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses) expected to be converted to cash within a year, and non-current assets (property, plant and equipment, long-term investments, intangible assets such as goodwill and patents).

Liabilities

Current liabilities due within a year (accounts payable, short-term debt, accrued expenses, deferred revenue) and non-current liabilities (long-term debt, bonds payable, pension obligations, deferred tax).

Equity

Share capital, additional paid-in capital, retained earnings and accumulated other comprehensive income (less treasury stock).

Standards and formats

Public companies prepare balance sheets under standards such as US GAAP or IFRS, with prescribed classifications; IFRS calls the statement a "statement of financial position". Presentation may be in account form (two columns) or report form (a single vertical list). Banks and other regulated entities follow additional formats reflecting regulatory capital.

Analysis and limitations

Analysts use the balance sheet to assess liquidity (working capital, current ratio), leverage (debt-to-equity) and solvency. Its limitations include the mixture of measurement bases (historical cost versus fair value), the omission of internally generated intangibles and human capital, and its being a single-date snapshot that can be dressed up at period-end ("window dressing").
