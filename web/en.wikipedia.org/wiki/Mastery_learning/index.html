Mastery learning

Mastery learning is an educational philosophy and instructional strategy, proposed by Benjamin Bloom in 1968, based on the premise that students must achieve a level of mastery in prerequisite knowledge before moving on to subsequent information. If a student does not achieve mastery, they are given additional support, time, and alternative instruction, and are then tested again; the cycle continues until the learner accomplishes mastery and only then moves to the next stage.

Principles

Mastery learning rests on the idea that aptitude is largely a matter of the time a learner needs to reach a standard, not a fixed ceiling on achievement. Building on John B. Carroll's 1963 model of school learning, Bloom argued that if learners are given appropriate instruction and sufficient time, most — Bloom suggested up to 90–95% — can master material that only a fraction reach under conventional group instruction with a fixed pace.

Method

In practice, instruction is broken into small units with clear objectives. After each unit, a brief formative assessment checks understanding; students who have not met the mastery criterion (often 80–90% correct) receive corrective activities and retake a parallel assessment, while students who have mastered the unit take on enrichment activities. Related implementations include the Keller Plan (Personalized System of Instruction) and modern competency-based education and flipped-classroom approaches. Research reviews generally find positive effects on achievement, though implementation is demanding in time and organisation.
