The Battle of Poltava (Swedish: Slaget vid Poltava) was fought on 8 July 1709 between the army of King Charles XII of Sweden and that of Tsar Peter I of Russia, near Poltava in Ukraine. It was the decisive battle of the Great Northern War: the Swedish invasion army, already ruined by the terrible winter of 1708–09 and by defeat and supply failure, was destroyed, and the remnants surrendered at Perevolochna. Poltava ended Sweden's era as a great power and announced Russia's arrival in its place.

Background

Charles XII invaded Russia in 1708, intending to march on Moscow. Peter's scorched-earth retreat, the destruction of Lewenhaupt's supply column at Lesnaya, the defection politics of the Cossack hetman Ivan Mazepa (who brought far fewer men than promised), and the "Great Frost" — the coldest European winter in centuries — reduced the Swedish army to a shadow. In spring 1709 Charles besieged Poltava, hoping to seize its stores. Peter advanced to relieve it with a much larger, modernised army and fortified a camp protected by a line of redoubts. Charles, shot in the foot days before the battle, had to command from a litter, leaving field direction to Rehnskiöld.

The battle

Before dawn on 8 July the Swedes attacked the redoubts, intending to slip past them in the dark; confusion and Russian resistance split the assault, and General Roos's detached columns were surrounded and destroyed in the woods. The main Swedish infantry, with only four guns serviceable, then advanced on the Russian camp and was met by Peter's full army drawn up in line, its 102 guns firing into the Swedish ranks. After a short, savage close-quarters fight the Swedish line broke; cavalry pursuit under Menshikov turned defeat into catastrophe. Charles escaped south across the Dnieper with Mazepa and a small escort; Lewenhaupt surrendered the remaining 16,000 men at Perevolochna three days later.

Aftermath

Charles fled into five years of exile in the Ottoman Empire; Sweden's Baltic empire was progressively dismantled by Russia, Prussia, Hanover and Denmark, confirmed by the Treaty of Nystad (1721), by which Peter took Livonia, Estonia and Ingria and proclaimed the Russian Empire. Poltava remains a central monument of Russian and Ukrainian memory — celebrated in Pushkin's poem Poltava and Tchaikovsky's opera Mazeppa.
