Neo-Calvinism

Neo-Calvinism
Key figuresAbraham Kuyper, Herman Bavinck
EraLate 19th century – present
OriginNetherlands

Neo-Calvinism is a movement within Reformed Protestantism, originating in the Netherlands in the late nineteenth century, that seeks to apply Calvinist theology to every sphere of modern life — politics, science, art, and culture — rather than confining it to the church. Its principal founder was the theologian and statesman Abraham Kuyper, who served as Prime Minister of the Netherlands from 1901 to 1905, and its leading systematic theologian was Herman Bavinck.

Key ideas

Sphere sovereignty (souvereiniteit in eigen kring) holds that God has ordained distinct spheres of life — family, church, state, university, business — each with its own authority directly accountable to God, so that no sphere, including the state, may dominate the others. Common grace teaches that God restrains sin and bestows gifts on all humanity, enabling cultural cooperation between Christians and non-Christians. The cultural mandate, drawn from Genesis, calls Christians to develop creation's potentials, and Kuyper's antithesis described a fundamental spiritual opposition in scholarship and culture between belief and unbelief.

Institutions and legacy

Kuyper founded the Free University of Amsterdam (1880), the Anti-Revolutionary Party, and a daily newspaper, and led the Doleantie church secession of 1886. Neo-Calvinism influenced Reformed communities in South Africa, North America, and Korea, and thinkers such as Herman Dooyeweerd developed its philosophical programme. Some of its Dutch South African adherents' ideas were later invoked — controversially and, its critics and many adherents argue, distortingly — in theological defences of apartheid.
