The Huangpu River (Chinese: 黄浦江; pinyin: Huángpǔ Jiāng, lit. "Yellow Bank River"), formerly romanized as the Whangpoo River, is a 113-kilometer (70 mi)-long river flowing north through Shanghai. It is the last significant tributary of the Yangtze River before the Yangtze empties into the East China Sea.

The Huangpu divides Shanghai into two regions: Pudong ("east of the Huangpu") to its east and Puxi ("west of the Huangpu") to its west. The Bund and Lujiazui are located along the river.

Course

The Huangpu is formed from waters flowing out of Dianshan Lake in Qingpu District, fed in turn by inflows from Tai Lake. It flows northeast through Shanghai, merging with the Suzhou Creek (Wusong River) at the northern end of the Bund, passes Yangpu, Hongkou and Huangpu districts, curves around Lujiazui, and continues to Wusongkou in Baoshan District, where it joins the Yangtze estuary. The river is on average about 400 metres wide and 9 metres deep.

History

Traditionally, the Huangpu's lower course was dredged and widened in the early Ming dynasty under the direction of the official Ye Zongxing (1405) and the minister Xia Yuanji, redirecting the flow of the Wusong system and making the Huangpu the principal navigable channel to Songjiang and later Shanghai. The river is named for Lord Chunshen (Huang Xie), a statesman of Chu to whom the waterway was fiefed in legend. With Shanghai's growth as a treaty port after 1842, the Huangpu became one of the busiest shipping channels in the world and central to the city's identity as a port.

Bridges, tunnels and transport

The river is crossed in central Shanghai by major bridges including the Nanpu, Lupu, Yangpu and Xupu bridges, and by numerous metro and road tunnels linking Puxi with Pudong — there were no bridges across the river in the city centre until the Nanpu Bridge opened in 1991. The Bund Tourist Tunnel and Huangpu river cruises are popular tourist attractions, and ferries continue to cross at many points. The river also supplies a large share of Shanghai's drinking water and hosts the older reaches of the Port of Shanghai, much of whose deep-water cargo traffic shifted to the Yangshan deep-water port.

Events

In March 2013, more than 16,000 pig carcasses — later linked to a disease outbreak upstream in Jiaxing, Zhejiang — were retrieved from the river, drawing national attention to water quality and agricultural waste disposal.
