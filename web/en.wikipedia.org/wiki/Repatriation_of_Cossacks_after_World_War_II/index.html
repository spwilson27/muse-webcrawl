Repatriation of Cossacks after World War II

The repatriation of Cossacks after World War II refers to the forced return to the Soviet Union of Cossacks and other Russian émigrés and collaborators by British and American forces at the end of the Second World War. The policy followed from agreements reached at the Yalta Conference in February 1945, under which the Western Allies undertook to return Soviet citizens found in areas under their control.

Background

During the war, tens of thousands of Cossacks had served in German uniform, notably in the XV SS Cossack Cavalry Corps and in units associated with Pyotr Krasnov and Andrei Shkuro. Many had fled Russia after the Russian Civil War and had never been Soviet citizens. When Germany collapsed in 1945, Cossack formations in Austria surrendered to British forces, expecting to be treated as anti-communist émigrés rather than Soviet nationals.

The handovers

In May and June 1945, British forces in Carinthia — around Lienz and Judenburg — disarmed the Cossacks and transferred them to the Red Army and the NKVD. Officers were separated from their men under false pretences and handed over first; many were executed or sent to the Gulag. Ordinary soldiers and their families, including women and children who had accompanied the formations, were loaded into trains and shipped east. Some committed suicide rather than be repatriated.

Aftermath and historiography

The events, sometimes called the "Betrayal of the Cossacks", were documented in works such as Nicholas Bethell's The Last Secret (1974) and Nikolai Tolstoy's Victims of Yalta (1977). Tolstoy's claims about the role of British officials led to a noted libel trial in 1989. Historians continue to debate the extent to which the British government knowingly exceeded the Yalta terms by returning people who were not Soviet citizens.
