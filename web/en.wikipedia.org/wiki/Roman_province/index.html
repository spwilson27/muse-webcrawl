In ancient Rome, a province (Latin: provincia, plural provinciae) was the basic and, until the Tetrarchy (from AD 293), the largest territorial and administrative unit of the empire's territorial possessions outside Italy. The word originally denoted a magistrate's sphere of assigned responsibility — which could be a task rather than a place — before coming to mean the conquered overseas territories governed by Roman commanders. Rome's first province was Sicily, acquired after the First Punic War in 241 BC; by the death of Augustus in AD 14 there were about forty, and the number grew as the empire expanded to its greatest extent under Trajan.

Government under the Republic

Republican provinces were assigned by lot or by the Senate to ex-consuls (proconsuls) and ex-praetors (propraetors), who held near-absolute imperium for a year, commanding the army, administering justice and farming out tax collection to companies of publicani. Governors were assisted by quaestors (financial officers) and legates. The system invited extortion — famously prosecuted by Cicero in his speeches against Verres, governor of Sicily — though provincials could sue for recovery in Rome. Client kingdoms and free cities often coexisted with directly governed provinces.

The imperial settlement

Augustus divided the provinces between himself and the Senate. The imperial provinces — mostly frontier regions with legions, such as Syria, Gaul and Hispania Tarraconensis — were governed by the emperor's deputies, legati Augusti pro praetore, serving at his pleasure; the senatorial (public) provinces — older, peaceful regions such as Africa, Asia and Baetica — continued to be allotted to proconsuls for annual terms. Egypt held a special status, administered by an equestrian prefect, and senators were forbidden to enter it without the emperor's permission. Over time the emperors assumed control of more provinces, and practices standardised: governors were salaried, tax collection passed to imperial officials, and appeals to the emperor became routine.

Administration and society

Day-to-day government rested with the cities of the provinces, whose councils maintained order, collected taxes and funded public works; Rome ruled its vast empire with only a few hundred senior officials. Provinces supplied grain (Egypt, Africa, Sicily), metals (Spain), and recruits; roads, colonies of veterans and Roman law gradually Romanised local elites, a process capped by the Constitutio Antoniniana of AD 212, which extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants. Diocletian's reforms at the end of the third century split the provinces into roughly a hundred smaller units grouped into dioceses, beginning the late-antique system of administration.

Notable provinces

  • Sicilia — Rome's first province and a granary of the Republic.
  • Asia — wealthy senatorial province in western Anatolia.
  • Gallia Narbonensis — so Romanised it was simply called Provincia, origin of the name Provence.
  • Roman Britain (Britannia) — the north-western frontier province.
  • Judaea — annexed in AD 6 and repeatedly rebellious.
  • Dacia — Trajan's conquest, abandoned under Aurelian.
