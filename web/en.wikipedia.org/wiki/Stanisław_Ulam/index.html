Stanisław Ulam

Born: 13 April 1909, Lemberg, Austria-Hungary (now Lviv, Ukraine)
Died: 13 May 1984, Santa Fe, New Mexico, US
Fields: Mathematics, physics
Known for: Teller–Ulam design, Monte Carlo method, Ulam spiral, Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem

Stanisław Marcin Ulam (13 April 1909 – 13 May 1984) was a Polish-American mathematician who participated in the Manhattan Project, originated the Teller–Ulam design of thermonuclear weapons, co-invented the Monte Carlo method of computation, and worked in set theory, topology, ergodic theory and mathematical biology.

Early life

Ulam was born in Lwów (then Lemberg) into a wealthy Polish-Jewish family and studied mathematics at Lwów Polytechnic, where he was part of the Lwów School of Mathematics centred on Stefan Banach and the Scottish Café. He emigrated to the United States in the 1930s, working with John von Neumann at Princeton before joining the University of Wisconsin.

Los Alamos

Von Neumann recruited Ulam to Los Alamos in 1943. Ulam's work on hydrodynamical implosion calculations contributed to the plutonium weapon. After the war he returned to Los Alamos, where with Cornelius Everett he performed calculations showing Edward Teller's original "Super" hydrogen bomb design was unworkable, and in 1951 he and Teller produced the breakthrough Teller–Ulam configuration — radiation implosion using a fission primary to compress a separate fusion secondary — which made practical thermonuclear weapons possible. With Nicholas Metropolis, John von Neumann and others he developed the Monte Carlo method, inspired by his thinking about solitaire while recovering from brain surgery in 1946. With Enrico Fermi, John Pasta and Mary Tsingou he ran the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou experiment, one of the first computer experiments, whose unexpected non-thermalisation launched the study of nonlinear systems and chaos.

Other contributions

Ulam's pure-mathematical work includes the Ulam spiral (a pattern revealing structure in the primes, doodled during a boring lecture), Ulam numbers, the Borsuk–Ulam theorem (proved by Karol Borsuk from his conjecture), measure theory, and the first correct proof methods in several areas of topology. He proposed nuclear pulse propulsion (Project Orion) and wrote a celebrated memoir, Adventures of a Mathematician (1976).
