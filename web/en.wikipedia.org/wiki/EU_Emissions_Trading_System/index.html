European Union Emissions Trading System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Launched: 1 January 2005

Participants: EU member states, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway

Coverage: Power generation, energy-intensive industry, aviation, maritime (from 2024)

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is a carbon trading system — a "cap and trade" scheme — covering greenhouse gas emissions from installations in the European Union and associated countries. Launched in 2005, it was the world's first major carbon market and remains the largest, covering around 40% of EU emissions.

How it works

A declining cap sets the total quantity of emissions allowed. Companies receive or buy emission allowances — each permitting one tonne of CO2 equivalent — and must surrender enough allowances each year to cover their verified emissions, or face heavy penalties. Allowances can be traded, so reductions happen where cheapest. A share of allowances is auctioned, with revenues flowing to member states and EU climate funds; historically, many industrial installations received free allocation to guard against "carbon leakage" to jurisdictions without carbon pricing.

Phases and reform

The system ran in phases: a pilot phase (2005–07) marred by overallocation and price collapse, a Kyoto phase (2008–12), a third phase (2013–20) with an EU-wide cap and more auctioning, and a fourth phase (2021–30). A Market Stability Reserve, operating from 2019, withdraws surplus allowances to support prices, which rose from under €10 per tonne in the mid-2010s to peaks near €100 in 2023. Under the "Fit for 55" reforms, the cap tightens faster, free allocation for sectors covered by the new Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is phased out, shipping is included, and a separate ETS2 for buildings and road transport fuels is due to start later in the decade. Aviation within Europe has been covered since 2012, and the EU ETS has been linked with the Swiss system since 2020.

Assessment

Studies generally find the EU ETS has reduced emissions in covered sectors beyond what would otherwise have occurred, though debates continue over the role of free allocation, price volatility and competitiveness effects.
