Switching cost

Switching costs are the costs that a consumer or business incurs as a result of changing suppliers, brands, products or services. They may be monetary — termination fees, new equipment, retraining — or non-monetary, such as the time, effort and psychological discomfort of learning a new system or losing accumulated data, loyalty points or relationships.

Categories

Researchers commonly divide switching costs into procedural costs (searching, learning, setting up), financial costs (fees, lost discounts, replacing compatible assets) and relational costs (breaking personal or brand bonds). When switching costs are high, customers become "locked in", giving incumbents pricing power even if a competitor offers a better product; economists have studied the phenomenon since Michael Porter's work on competitive strategy, and Paul Klemperer's models of consumer switching.

Examples and policy

Classic examples include mobile-phone contracts and number portability rules, bank account switching services, proprietary software file formats and ecosystems of apps and accessories, and frequent-flyer programmes designed to raise the perceived cost of changing airlines. Regulators often act to lower switching costs — through portability mandates, interoperability requirements and standardised switching services — in order to make markets more competitive.
