Expressionism (theatre)

Distorted, angular scenery typical of expressionist stage design.

Expressionism in the theatre was a movement that developed principally in Germany in the early 20th century, as one strand of the wider expressionist movement in the arts. Rejecting naturalism's faithful depiction of external reality, expressionist drama sought to dramatise the inner, subjective experience of its characters — above all the crisis and spiritual rebirth of the individual in a mechanised, dehumanising society.

Characteristics

Expressionist plays are typically structured not in conventional acts but in "stations" (after Strindberg's station drama), following a central figure's journey of awakening. Language is often ecstatic and telegraphic; characters are types rather than individuals (the Father, the Son, the Cashier); and staging employs distorted scenery, sharp angles, exaggerated lighting, masks, and mechanical, gestural acting. Protagonists frequently deliver long, feverish monologues.

Playwrights and works

Precursors included August Strindberg (To Damascus, A Dream Play) and Frank Wedekind. The movement's central German figures were Georg Kaiser (From Morn to Midnight, The Burghers of Calais), Ernst Toller (Man and the Masses), and the early Bertolt Brecht. In America, Eugene O'Neill's The Emperor Jones (1920) and The Hairy Ape (1922), and Elmer Rice's The Adding Machine (1923), brought expressionist technique to the English-speaking stage.

Influence

Though the movement faded in Germany by the mid-1920s, its devices — episodic structure, direct address, symbolic sets, and the staging of mental states — were absorbed into modern theatre practice worldwide, and into expressionist cinema such as The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari.
