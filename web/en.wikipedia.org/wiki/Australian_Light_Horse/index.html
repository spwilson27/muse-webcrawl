The Australian Light Horse was a highly mobile mounted infantry force of the Australian Army that served with distinction in the Second Boer War and the First World War. Light horsemen typically rode to the battlefield on horseback and then dismounted to fight on foot, with one man in four holding the horses — a versatility that proved ideal in the wide open spaces of the Middle East.

First World War

Light horse regiments were organised into brigades within the Australian Imperial Force. Although their horses were left behind when they served as infantry reinforcements at Gallipoli in 1915 — where the 8th and 10th Light Horse regiments were devastated in the charge at the Nek — the light horse achieved its greatest fame in the Sinai and Palestine campaign. Operating in the Egyptian Expeditionary Force under General Allenby, they fought at Romani, Magdhaba and Rafa, and in October 1917 the 4th and 12th Light Horse regiments made the celebrated charge at Beersheba, often described as the last successful great cavalry charge in history, capturing the town and its vital wells.

Horses and equipment

The light horse rode the hardy Australian stock horse known as the Waler. Troopers carried the standard .303 Lee–Enfield rifle, bayonet, and — unusually for mounted infantry — were trained in mounted shock action with sword or bayonet when required. Their slouch hats, adorned with emu plumes in many units, became an enduring national symbol.

Later history

Between the wars the light horse regiments were progressively mechanised. In the Second World War some regiments served as cavalry reconnaissance and commando units, while others converted to armour. The lineage of the light horse continues today in the Royal Australian Armoured Corps regiments that retain "Light Horse" in their titles.
