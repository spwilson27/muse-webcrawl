Lower Egypt (Arabic: مصر السفلى; ancient Egyptian: Ta-Mehu, "land of the papyrus plant") is the northernmost region of Egypt, consisting of the fertile Nile Delta between the Mediterranean Sea and the area south of modern Cairo. Its name derives from the flow of the Nile from the highlands of Upper Egypt in the south "down" to the Mediterranean.

History

In Predynastic times Lower Egypt was one of two kingdoms — its ruler wore the Red Crown (Deshret) and its patron goddess was Wadjet, the cobra. Around 3100 BC it was united with Upper Egypt under the pharaohs of the First Dynasty, traditionally by Narmer or Menes, creating ancient Egypt. The combined land was symbolised by the Double Crown and the intertwined papyrus (Lower Egypt) and lotus/sedge (Upper Egypt).

Lower Egypt's capitals included Memphis, near the apex of the Delta, while important Delta cities included Tanis, Sais, Bubastis and Buto. In later periods the region was ruled from Alexandria, founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BC, and its fertile farmland made it a breadbasket of the Roman and Byzantine empires.

Geography

The Delta is a broad, flat fan of silt deposited by the Nile's distributaries, historically the Rosetta and Damietta branches among others. It is intensively cultivated and densely populated, containing the cities of Alexandria, Mansoura, Tanta and Zagazig.
