Mikhail Zoshchenko

Mikhail Mikhailovich Zoshchenko (Russian: Михаил Михайлович Зощенко; 9 August [O.S. 28 July] 1894 – 22 July 1958) was a Soviet and Russian writer and satirist. He is best known for his short satirical stories depicting everyday life, bureaucracy, and housing shortages in the early Soviet Union, written in a distinctive skaz style — the voice of a semi-literate, naïve narrator whose language satirizes both Soviet officialdom and philistine attitudes.

Life

Zoshchenko was born in Saint Petersburg (some sources say Poltava) into the family of an artist of Ukrainian origin. He volunteered for the Imperial Russian Army in World War I, was wounded and gassed, and after the revolutions of 1917 held various jobs — including postmaster, criminal investigator, and animal trainer — that furnished material for his fiction. In the 1920s, as a member of the Serapion Brothers literary group, he became one of the most popular writers in the Soviet Union with collections such as The Tales of Nazar Ilyich, Mr. Sinebryukhov (1922).

Persecution

His semi-autobiographical novella Before Sunrise (1943), an attempt to analyze his own chronic depression, was condemned by the authorities. In 1946 Zoshchenko was a principal target of the Zhdanov decree: a resolution of the Communist Party's Central Committee "On the Journals Zvezda and Leningrad" denounced him as a "vulgar" and "ideologically harmful" writer, and he was expelled from the Union of Soviet Writers, effectively banned from publishing. He was readmitted to the Writers' Union after Stalin's death in 1953 but never regained his former position or popularity, and died in Leningrad in 1958 in poverty.

Legacy

Zoshchenko's stories are now classics of Russian literature, widely reprinted and translated; his flat in St. Petersburg is preserved as a museum. His satirical mode influenced later Soviet humorists, and his fate is emblematic of the pressures on writers under Stalinist cultural policy.
