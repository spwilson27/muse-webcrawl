Rewilding (conservation biology)

Rewilding is a form of ecological restoration aimed at increasing biodiversity and restoring natural processes, typically by reintroducing apex predators and keystone species, reconnecting fragmented habitats, and reducing human management so that ecosystems become self-sustaining. The term emerged from North American conservation biology in the 1990s, associated with figures such as Dave Foreman and Michael Soulé, who emphasised large core reserves, corridors and carnivores — the "3 Cs".

Approaches

Pleistocene rewilding proposes introducing ecological proxies for extinct megafauna, inspired by the Pleistocene Park experiment in Siberia. In Europe, organisations such as Rewilding Europe have promoted the return of species including the European bison, and the passive rewilding of abandoned farmland. Trophic rewilding focuses on restoring top-down food-web interactions; the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is frequently cited, though the magnitude of its cascading effects is debated. More recently, "rewilding" has also been applied at small scales to urban greening and even to the human gut microbiome.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about conflicts with farming communities, the welfare of reintroduced animals, disease, the shifting baselines chosen as restoration targets, and the fact that much of the evidence for trophic cascades is correlational. Supporters argue rewilding can deliver carbon sequestration, flood mitigation and nature-based tourism alongside biodiversity gains.

Notable projects

  • Knepp Wildland, England
  • Yellowstone wolf reintroduction, United States
  • Pleistocene Park, Russia
  • Iberá wetlands rewilding, Argentina
