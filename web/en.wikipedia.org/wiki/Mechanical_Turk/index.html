The Mechanical Turk, also known as the Automaton Chess Player, was a chess-playing machine constructed in 1770 by the Hungarian inventor Wolfgang von Kempelen. Presented as an automaton that could play a strong game of chess against human opponents, it was in fact an elaborate hoax: a human chess master concealed inside the machine operated its movements.

Construction and exhibition

The Turk consisted of a life-sized figure dressed in Ottoman robes and a turban, seated behind a large wooden cabinet topped with a chessboard. Before each game, Kempelen opened the cabinet doors to reveal clockwork gears and empty compartments, then closed them; the doors were opened in a sequence that concealed the operator, who shifted position inside using a system of sliding panels and a candle for light. The hidden operator followed the game using magnets beneath the board and moved the Turk's arm with a pantograph-like mechanism.

Career

The Turk toured Europe and the Americas for 84 years, defeating many challengers including statesmen such as Napoleon Bonaparte and Benjamin Franklin. Owners after Kempelen included Johann Nepomuk Mälzel, inventor of the metronome. Writers including Edgar Allan Poe attempted to expose its method in print. The original was destroyed in a fire at a Philadelphia museum in 1854; its last operator's son later published the full secret.

Legacy

The Turk inspired numerous imitations and influenced early thinking about machine intelligence. Its name was adopted in 2005 by Amazon for its crowdsourcing platform, Amazon Mechanical Turk, which similarly conceals human labor behind an apparently automated interface. A working reconstruction was built in the 1980s by John Gaughan.
