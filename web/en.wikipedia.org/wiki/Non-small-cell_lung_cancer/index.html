Non-small-cell lung cancer

Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is any type of epithelial lung cancer other than small-cell lung carcinoma (SCLC). NSCLC accounts for about 85% of all lung cancers. Because its cells are larger and it generally grows and spreads more slowly than small-cell carcinoma, it is treated differently, with surgery playing a central role in early-stage disease.

Types

The principal histological types are adenocarcinoma — the most common lung cancer overall and the most frequent type in non-smokers — squamous-cell carcinoma, strongly linked to smoking and usually arising centrally, and large-cell carcinoma, a diagnosis of exclusion for poorly differentiated tumours.

Causes and symptoms

Tobacco smoking is the dominant risk factor, with radon exposure, asbestos, air pollution and second-hand smoke also contributing. Symptoms include persistent cough, haemoptysis (coughing blood), breathlessness, chest pain, weight loss and fatigue, though early disease is often asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging.

Treatment

Early-stage NSCLC is treated with surgical resection, sometimes with adjuvant chemotherapy. Advanced disease is treated systemically. Modern management depends heavily on molecular profiling: tumours with activating EGFR mutations respond to tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as osimertinib; ALK rearrangements to agents such as alectinib; and many other tumours to immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1 blockers) such as pembrolizumab, alone or with chemotherapy. Despite these advances, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death worldwide.
