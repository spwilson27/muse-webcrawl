Uri Geller

Uri Geller (born 20 December 1946) is an Israeli-British illusionist, magician, television personality, and self-proclaimed psychic. He is known for his trademark television performances of spoon bending and other apparent feats of psychokinesis and telepathy. Geller uses conjuring tricks to simulate the effects of psychic powers.

Early life

Geller was born in Tel Aviv, in what was then Mandatory Palestine, to Hungarian and Austrian Jewish parents. He served in the Israeli Army's Golani Brigade and was wounded in action during the Six-Day War in 1967. He worked as a photographic model before beginning to perform as a nightclub entertainer.

Career

Geller first gained attention in Israel and then internationally in the 1970s, appearing on television programs in Europe and the United States demonstrating spoon bending, describing hidden drawings, and making watches stop or start. In 1973 he was tested at the Stanford Research Institute by physicists Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff, whose published results were disputed by other scientists. That same year, an appearance on The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson — in which magician and skeptic James Randi had arranged for props to be supplied independently — resulted in Geller failing to perform any of his feats on air.

Criticism

Magicians and skeptics, most prominently James Randi, have argued that Geller's performances are stage magic presented as genuine psychic ability. Randi replicated Geller's effects using conjuring methods and documented the dispute in his book The Truth About Uri Geller. Geller has been involved in several lawsuits against his critics, most of which he lost or withdrew.

Later work

Geller has written numerous books, both fiction and non-fiction, appeared in reality television programs including the Israeli show The Successor, in which contestants competed to be his heir, and has promoted spoon bending as an art form; a large spoon sculpture of his stands outside his museum in Jaffa.
