On the Genealogy of Morality

On the Genealogy of Morality: A Polemic (German: Zur Genealogie der Moral: Eine Streitschrift) is an 1887 book by the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. It consists of a preface and three interrelated essays that expand and follow through on the ideas Nietzsche sketched in Beyond Good and Evil (1886).

The three essays

In the First Essay, Nietzsche distinguishes "master morality" from "slave morality". Aristocratic cultures, he argues, first called themselves "good" and the common "bad"; the priestly caste, driven by ressentiment, inverted these values, making humility, weakness, and meekness virtues and branding strength "evil".

The Second Essay traces the origins of guilt and bad conscience: the feeling of debt (Schuld) toward ancestors and gods became internalized when human beings were confined within society, turning their instincts inward. Punishment, memory, and the "sovereign individual" are analysed.

The Third Essay examines the meaning of ascetic ideals — in priests, philosophers, and artists — arguing that the ascetic ideal gave suffering a meaning, and that even modern science and atheism remain under its spell. Nietzsche concludes that man would rather will nothingness than not will at all.

Influence

The book is among the most influential works of modern moral philosophy, shaping genealogical methods later used by Michel Foucault and many others. It is widely taught and is often considered Nietzsche's most systematic work.
