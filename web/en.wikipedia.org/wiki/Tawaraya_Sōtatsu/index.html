Tawaraya Sōtatsu

Tawaraya Sōtatsu (俵屋 宗達)
Born: c. 1570
Died: c. 1640
Nationality: Japanese
Known for: Painting; co-founder of the Rinpa school

Tawaraya Sōtatsu (c. 1570 – c. 1640) was a Japanese furniture designer and painter of the Edo period. He is regarded, together with Hon'ami Kōetsu, as one of the founders of the Rinpa school of Japanese painting, characterised by bold, decorative compositions, lavish use of gold and silver leaf, and subjects drawn from classical Japanese literature.

Career

Sōtatsu ran the Tawaraya atelier in Kyoto, which produced painted fans, screens and other luxury goods for the imperial court and wealthy townspeople. Around 1615 the shogun Tokugawa Ieyasu presented him with a set of valuable classical calligraphy scrolls, prompting a collaboration with Kōetsu on illustrated handscrolls of works such as the Tales of Ise and the Tale of Genji.

Works

His best-known works are the pair of folding screens Wind God and Thunder God (Fūjin Raijin-zu) — later famously copied by Ogata Kōrin — and screens depicting scenes from the Tale of Genji and the bugaku dances. Sōtatsu developed the tarashikomi technique, in which a second layer of paint is applied before the first has dried, producing puddled, blurred effects exploited with great delicacy in his depictions of flowers and water.

Legacy

Almost nothing is documented about Sōtatsu's personal life, including his exact dates, but his workshop methods and compositions shaped Japanese decorative painting for centuries, and the Rinpa style he pioneered continues to be celebrated as a pinnacle of Japanese art.
