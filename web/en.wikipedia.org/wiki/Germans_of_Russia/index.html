Germans of Russia

The Germans of Russia (German: Russlanddeutsche) are ethnic Germans who settled in the Russian Empire from the 18th century onward and their descendants. Invited by Catherine the Great's manifestos of 1762–63, tens of thousands emigrated to farm the Volga steppe and later the Black Sea coast, forming compact German-speaking colonies while retaining their language, faiths (Lutheran, Catholic and Mennonite) and customs.

Settlement

Volga Germans established more than a hundred colonies near Saratov; Black Sea Germans settled around Odessa and in Crimea, and Mennonite colonies arose at Chortitza (1789) and Molotschna (1804). Settlers received land, tax privileges, religious freedom and exemption from military service — privileges gradually revoked in the later 19th century, prompting emigration to the Americas.

Soviet period

In 1924 the USSR created the Volga German Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic, with its capital at Engels. After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, Stalin ordered the wholesale deportation of ethnic Germans to Siberia and Kazakhstan; the autonomous republic was abolished and hundreds of thousands were conscripted into the forced-labour "Trudarmee".

Emigration and present day

Restrictions eased after 1955, but full rehabilitation never came. From the late 1980s, over two million ethnic Germans and their families emigrated to Germany as Aussiedler (resettlers). Smaller Russia-German communities remain in Russia, Kazakhstan and Siberia today.
