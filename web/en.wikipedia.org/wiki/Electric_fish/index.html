An electric fish is any fish that can generate an electric field. Species able to produce powerful shocks for predation or defence are called strongly electric, while those that generate weak fields for electrolocation and communication are weakly electric. Many electric fish are also electroreceptive — able to sense electric fields produced by other organisms. Electric organs evolved independently at least six times among fishes.

Electric organs

Electric organs are built from stacks of modified muscle or nerve cells called electrocytes. Each cell produces only a fraction of a volt, but thousands arranged in series — like batteries — can sum to a large voltage, while parallel arrays increase the current. In the electric eel (Electrophorus), the organs occupy most of the body and can deliver discharges around 860 volts, the strongest of any animal.

Weakly electric fish

The knifefishes of South America (Gymnotiformes) and the elephantfishes of Africa (Mormyridae) continuously emit weak pulses or waves. Distortions of this self-generated field reveal nearby objects — a form of active electrolocation analogous to echolocation — and modulations of the discharge serve in courtship and territorial signalling. These fish typically have huge brains relative to body size, devoted to processing electrosensory information.

Strongly electric fish

Besides the electric eel, strong discharges are produced by the African electric catfish, by marine torpedo rays, and by some stargazers. Such shocks stun prey and deter predators; electric eels can even leap partially from the water to press a shock directly against a perceived threat.
