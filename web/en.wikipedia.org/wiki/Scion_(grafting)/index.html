Scion (grafting)

In grafting, the scion is the piece of a plant — typically a short shoot or a single bud — that is inserted into a rooted plant, the stock or rootstock, so that the two tissues unite and grow as one plant. The resulting organism is composed of genetically distinct parts: the roots and lower stem from the stock, and the shoots, leaves, flowers and fruit from the scion.

Types of graft

Different grafting techniques use the scion in different forms:

  • Whip and tongue grafting — scion and stock of similar diameter are joined with interlocking cuts.
  • Cleft grafting — one or two scions are inserted into a split cut in a larger stock, used for top-working established trees.
  • Bark grafting — scions are slipped under the bark of the stock in spring, when the bark separates easily.
  • Budding (bud grafting) — the "scion" is a single bud with a small shield of bark, inserted under the bark of the stock; widely used for roses and fruit trees.
  • Approach grafting — two independently rooted plants are joined and the top of one becomes the scion after union.

Requirements for success

A successful graft requires close contact between the cambium of scion and stock, compatible partners (usually closely related plants), protection of the union from drying out, and correct timing — most grafting is done in late winter or early spring just before growth resumes, using dormant scion wood collected earlier and kept refrigerated.

Uses

Grafting with scions makes possible the mass production of named fruit cultivars (apples, grapes, citrus), the combination of fruiting varieties with rootstocks resistant to soil pests such as phylloxera, the dwarfing of orchard trees, and the repair of girdled trunks by bridge grafting.
