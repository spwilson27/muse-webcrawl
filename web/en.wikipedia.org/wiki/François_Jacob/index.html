François Jacob

Born17 June 1920, Nancy, France
Died19 April 2013 (aged 92), Paris, France
FieldGenetics, molecular biology
Known forOperon model of gene regulation (with Jacques Monod)
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1965)

François Jacob (17 June 1920 – 19 April 2013) was a French biologist who, together with Jacques Monod, originated the idea that control of enzyme levels in cells occurs through the regulation of transcription. In 1961 Jacob and Monod proposed the operon model, based on studies of the lac operon in E. coli, introducing concepts such as the repressor, the operator, and messenger RNA. Jacob shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Monod and André Lwoff "for their discoveries concerning genetic control of enzyme and virus synthesis".

Life

Jacob's medical studies were interrupted by the Second World War; he escaped to Britain, joined the Free French forces, and served as a medical officer in North Africa and Normandy, where he was badly wounded, receiving the Croix de Guerre and the Ordre de la Libération. After the war he turned to research at the Pasteur Institute under Lwoff, initially studying bacterial lysogeny, and later collaborating with Monod and with Elie Wollman on bacterial conjugation and genetic mapping (the "interrupted mating" experiments).

Jacob later headed the Pasteur Institute's department of cellular genetics and held a chair at the Collège de France. His book The Logic of Life (1970) is a noted history of heredity and modern biology; his autobiography The Statue Within appeared in 1987.
