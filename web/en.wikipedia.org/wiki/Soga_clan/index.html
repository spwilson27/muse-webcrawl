The Soga clan (Japanese: 蘇我氏, Soga-shi) was one of the most powerful clans of the Asuka period of ancient Japanese history and played a major role in the spread of Buddhism in Japan. Through political marriage and control of the treasury (the office of Ōomi), the Soga dominated the imperial court in the 6th and early 7th centuries.

Rise to power

Under Soga no Iname (c. 506–570), the clan gained great influence: Iname served as Ōomi, married his daughters into the imperial family, and accepted a Buddhist statue and sutras from the king of Baekje in 552 — the traditional date for the introduction of Buddhism to Japan. The Soga championed Buddhism against the conservative Mononobe and Nakatomi clans; their victory at the Battle of Shigisan in 587, under Soga no Umako, secured the new religion's position.

Zenith and fall

Umako installed Emperor Sushun, later had him assassinated (592), and placed his niece on the throne as Empress Suiko, ruling alongside Prince Shōtoku. His son Soga no Emishi and grandson Soga no Iruka continued the family's dominance until 645, when Prince Naka no Ōe (later Emperor Tenji) and Nakatomi no Kamatari assassinated Iruka at court in the Isshi Incident; Emishi burned the family residence and its records, ending Soga power.

Legacy

Soga patronage introduced continental culture, Buddhism, and temple architecture — notably Asuka-dera (Hōkō-ji), Japan's first great temple — shaping the Asuka period. Later historiography, written under the winners of 645, portrayed the Soga as usurpers, a view modern scholars treat with caution.
