Omophorion

In the Eastern Orthodox Church and Eastern Catholic Churches, the omophorion (Greek: ὠμοφόριον, meaning "[something] borne on the shoulders") is the distinguishing vestment of a bishop and symbol of his metropolitan or primatial authority. It is a broad band of cloth, usually wool, decorated with crosses, and is worn about the neck and shoulders.

Form and symbolism

The omophorion is arranged so that one end hangs down in the front and the other down in the back. It is traditionally made of white wool, symbolising the lost sheep — the bishop's flock — which the Good Shepherd carries on his shoulders, and the bishop's duty to care for those who have gone astray. There are two forms: the great omophorion, worn at the Divine Liturgy and other solemn services, and the small omophorion, a shorter form worn at less formal services such as vespers and ordinations.

Use

The omophorion corresponds to the pallium worn by metropolitans in the Western Church. Only bishops may wear it; during the liturgy the bishop puts it on and removes it at specified moments, assisted by deacons, most notably before the reading of the Gospel and during the Great Entrance, times when its symbolism of episcopal authority is adjusted for humility before the mysteries being celebrated.
