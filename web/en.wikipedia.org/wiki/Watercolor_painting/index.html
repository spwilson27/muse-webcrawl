Watercolor painting

Watercolor painting
Medium:Pigments suspended in a water-based solution
Binder:Gum arabic

Watercolor (or aquarelle) is a painting method in which the paints are made of pigments suspended in a water-based solution. Watercolor refers both to the medium and the resulting artwork. The traditional and most common support is paper, though other supports such as papyrus, bark, vellum and fabric have been used.

Materials

Watercolor paint consists of pigment bound with gum arabic, with additives such as glycerin or honey as plasticizers. Paints are sold as dry pans or in tubes. Brushes are traditionally of sable or squirrel hair, prized for holding water. The whiteness of the paper provides the highlights, since traditional watercolor is transparent.

Techniques

Characteristic techniques include wet-on-wet (applying paint to wet paper), wet-on-dry, dry brush, glazing (layering transparent washes), lifting, and masking with fluid to preserve white areas. The medium is considered demanding because mistakes are difficult to correct and the drying paint shifts in value.

History

Water-based painting is ancient, from Egyptian tomb painting to Chinese and Japanese ink and color traditions. In Europe, Albrecht Dürer's nature studies were early masterpieces. The English school of the late 18th and 19th centuries — Paul Sandby, Thomas Girtin and J. M. W. Turner — elevated watercolor to an independent art; Turner exploited its luminosity in vast atmospheric landscapes. In America, Winslow Homer and John Singer Sargent brought virtuoso immediacy to the medium.
