Aberration in optical systems

An aberration in an optical system is a defect of an image formed by a lens or mirror, arising because rays of light from a point object do not converge to (or diverge from) a single image point. Optical systems are designed so that aberrations are minimised, but they can never be eliminated entirely in real instruments.

Monochromatic aberrations

The Seidel aberrations, described by Philipp Ludwig von Seidel in the 1850s, classify the primary monochromatic (single-wavelength) defects:

  • Spherical aberration — marginal rays focus at a different distance from paraxial rays.
  • Coma — off-axis points image as comet-shaped blurs.
  • Astigmatism — tangential and sagittal rays focus at different distances, so lines in different orientations cannot be sharp simultaneously.
  • Field curvature — the image forms on a curved surface (the Petzval surface) rather than a plane.
  • Distortion — magnification varies with field position, producing barrel or pincushion shapes.

Chromatic aberration

Because refractive index varies with wavelength, a simple lens focuses different colours at different distances (longitudinal chromatic aberration) and with different magnifications (lateral chromatic aberration), producing coloured fringes. Achromatic doublets, apochromats and low-dispersion glass reduce it. Diffractive and gradient-index optics can also correct aberrations, and modern computational imaging removes some defects in software.
