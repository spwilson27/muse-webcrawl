Electret

An electret is a dielectric material that has a quasi-permanent electric charge or dipole polarisation. An electret generates internal and external electric fields, and is the electrostatic equivalent of a permanent magnet. The term was coined by Oliver Heaviside in 1892 from "electricity" and "magnet," though the phenomenon of wax retaining charge was noted earlier, and the first systematic modern study was by the Japanese physicist Mototaro Eguchi in 1919, who made electrets from carnauba wax and resin.

Polymer film electrets power most of the world's microphones.

Formation

Electrets are formed by charging, heating, or otherwise treating dielectric materials so that charges become trapped or dipoles become aligned. Common methods include corona discharge, electron-beam injection, and thermoforming under an applied electric field: a polymer is heated above its glass transition temperature in a strong field, dipoles align, and cooling "freezes" the polarization in place. Modern electrets are typically made from polymers such as PTFE (Teflon), FEP, PET, and polypropylene, which can retain charge for years to decades.

Applications

The best-known application is the electret microphone, developed at Bell Labs in the 1960s by Gerhard Sessler and James West: a charged metallized polymer foil forms one plate of the condenser capsule, eliminating the need for an external polarizing voltage. Electret condenser microphones are now produced by the billions and used in phones, hearing aids, and recording equipment. Other applications include:

  • Air filters — electret fiber mats in respirators (including N95 masks) and HVAC filters attract and capture fine particles electrostatically;
  • Electret transducers and loudspeakers/headphones;
  • Radiation dosimeters — discharge of an electret measures accumulated exposure;
  • Energy harvesters and vibration sensors;
  • Xerography research and electrostatic motors.

Types

Electrets are classified as real-charge electrets (containing trapped monopolar charge) or oriented-dipole electrets (containing aligned dipoles), and by material as organic polymer electrets, inorganic electrets (e.g. silicon dioxide/silicon nitride stacks used in MEMS), and wax or resin electrets historically. Space-charge stability, humidity, and temperature determine useful lifetime.
