Monocoque

Monocoque
TypeStructural design
Meaning'Single shell' (French)
Used inAircraft, automobiles, boats, bicycle frames

Monocoque, also called structural skin, is a structural system in which loads are supported by an object's external skin, in a manner similar to an egg shell. The word is a French term meaning 'single shell'. First used for boats, a true monocoque carries both tensile and compressive forces within the skin and is distinguished from semi-monocoque construction, in which a frame of longerons and formers shares the loads with the skin.

Applications

In aviation, monocoque and semi-monocoque fuselages became standard from the 1930s, replacing internal truss structures covered with fabric; early examples included the de Havilland Mosquito, whose fuselage was a plywood sandwich shell.

In automobiles, the unitary body (unibody) is the monocoque form now universal in mass-production cars; racing cars adopted carbon-fibre monocoque survival cells, famously introduced to Formula One by the McLaren MP4/1 of 1981. Monocoque construction is also used in bicycle frames, rockets, and pressure vessels.

Advantages

Because the skin carries the loads, monocoques can achieve high strength and stiffness for low weight and offer a large unobstructed internal volume. Their disadvantages include sensitivity to local damage and the difficulty of making cut-outs for doors and windows, which interrupt load paths.
