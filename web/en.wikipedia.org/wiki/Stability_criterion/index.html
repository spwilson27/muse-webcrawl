Stability criterion

In control theory and dynamical systems, a stability criterion is a mathematical condition that determines whether a system is stable — that is, whether its response to bounded inputs and small disturbances remains bounded and returns to equilibrium. Because directly solving a system's differential equations may be impractical, stability criteria allow engineers and mathematicians to decide stability from the coefficients of the system's characteristic equation or from frequency-response data.

Lyapunov stability

The foundational definition derives from Aleksandr Lyapunov's 1892 doctoral thesis. An equilibrium is stable if trajectories starting near it remain near it, and asymptotically stable if trajectories also converge to it. Lyapunov's direct method certifies stability by finding a positive-definite "energy-like" function whose time derivative along trajectories is negative, without solving the equations of motion.

Algebraic criteria

For linear time-invariant systems, stability is equivalent to all roots (poles) of the characteristic polynomial lying in the open left half of the complex plane. The Routh–Hurwitz criterion tests this property from the polynomial coefficients alone, using a structured array (the Routh array): the number of sign changes in its first column equals the number of right-half-plane roots, so all coefficients present and of the same sign with no sign changes implies stability. Related tests include the Hermite–Biehler theorem and Jury's criterion for discrete-time systems, where the unit circle replaces the left half-plane.

Frequency-domain criteria

The Nyquist stability criterion, developed by Harry Nyquist at Bell Labs in 1932, infers closed-loop stability from the open-loop frequency response: it counts encirclements of the −1 point in the complex plane by the Nyquist plot and relates them, via the argument principle, to the numbers of open-loop and closed-loop right-half-plane poles. Simplified frequency rules include the Bode stability criterion and phase/gain margin measures used in practical design. The circle criterion and Popov criterion extend these ideas to certain nonlinear systems.

Other uses

Similar named criteria appear in neighboring fields: in fluid mechanics, criteria such as the Richardson number condition and Rayleigh's inflection-point theorem govern the stability of flows; in numerical analysis, the CFL (Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy) condition constrains time steps so that simulations remain stable.
