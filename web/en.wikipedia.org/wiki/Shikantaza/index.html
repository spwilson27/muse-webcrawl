Shikantaza is the central meditation practice of Sōtō Zen.

Shikantaza (Japanese: 只管打坐) is a Japanese translation of a Chinese term for zazen introduced by Rujing, a monk of the Caodong school of Zen Buddhism. It generally translates as "just sitting" or "nothing but sitting", and is a form of meditation practice in which the mind is not fixed on any particular object but remains simply aware, without attachment to thoughts or sensations. In the Sōtō school founded by Eihei Dōgen, shikantaza is the defining practice, distinguished from the kōan introspection emphasized in the Rinzai school.

Doctrine

Dōgen taught that zazen is not a means to enlightenment but the expression of enlightenment itself — the "oneness of practice and realization" (shushō-ittō). In works such as the Fukanzazengi and the Shōbōgenzō, he describes sitting without striving: non-thinking (hishiryō) that is neither thinking nor the suppression of thought. Practitioners sit in full or half lotus, back straight, eyes half-open and lowered, allowing thoughts to arise and pass without grasping at them or pushing them away.

Origins

The practice descends from the Chinese Caodong (Japanese Sōtō) tradition of "silent illumination" (mòzhào) associated with Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157), who described a serene, objectless awareness. Dōgen studied under the Caodong master Tiantong Rujing in China and brought the transmission to Japan in the 1220s.

Modern practice

Teachers in the modern Sōtō lineage, including Shunryū Suzuki, have popularized shikantaza in the West, presenting it as a practice of complete presence in which the sitter "just sits" without goal or technique. Intensive periods of sitting are held during sesshin retreats. Scholarly discussion continues over how objectless shikantaza really is in practice, and how it differs from other forms of open-monitoring meditation.
