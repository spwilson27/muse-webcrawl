Smooth muscle is one of three types of muscle tissue in the human body, alongside skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle. It is an involuntary, non-striated muscle found in the walls of hollow internal organs such as the stomach, intestines, bladder, and uterus, and in the walls of blood vessels and airways. Smooth muscle contraction is controlled by the autonomic nervous system, hormones, and local chemical signals rather than by conscious effort.

Structure

Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped, typically 20–500 micrometres long, with a single central nucleus. Their contractile proteins — actin and myosin — are not arranged in regular sarcomeres, so the tissue lacks the banded (striated) appearance of skeletal and cardiac muscle. Instead, filaments anchor to dense bodies in the cytoplasm and dense plaques at the membrane, and the cells are linked by gap junctions that allow coordinated contraction across a sheet of tissue.

Contraction mechanism

Contraction is triggered by calcium ions, which bind calmodulin and activate myosin light-chain kinase; the kinase phosphorylates the regulatory light chains of myosin, allowing cross-bridge cycling with actin. Smooth muscle can maintain tension for long periods with very low energy use — the so-called "latch state" — which suits its role in maintaining vascular tone and gut motility rhythms.

Types

Smooth muscle is divided into single-unit (visceral) muscle, in which cells contract together as a syncytium via gap junctions — as in the gut wall — and multi-unit muscle, in which cells contract more independently, as in the iris of the eye and the walls of large arteries.

Functions

Smooth muscle propels food through the digestive tract by peristalsis, regulates blood flow and blood pressure through vasoconstriction and vasodilation, controls airflow in the bronchi, empties the bladder, and drives the contractions of the uterus in childbirth. Abnormal smooth muscle contraction underlies conditions such as asthma and hypertension, and benign tumours of smooth muscle (leiomyomas, e.g. uterine fibroids) are among the most common human tumours.
