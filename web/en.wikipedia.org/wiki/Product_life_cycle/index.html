Product life cycle

The product life cycle is a concept in marketing that describes the stages a product goes through from its introduction to the market until its withdrawal: typically introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. The concept, popularized in the 1960s by economists and marketing scholars such as Theodore Levitt, is used to guide pricing, promotion, distribution, and product-development decisions.

Stages

During the introduction stage, sales are low, costs per customer are high, and the marketing objective is to build awareness and trial. In the growth stage, sales rise rapidly, competitors enter, and firms aim to maximize market share and improve the product. In maturity, sales growth slows and eventually levels off; competition is intense and firms defend share through differentiation, price adjustments, and finding new users or uses. In decline, sales fall because of technological change, shifting tastes, or increased competition, and firms must decide whether to maintain, harvest, or discontinue the product.

Applications and criticism

Life-cycle thinking also underlies the Boston Consulting Group growth-share matrix, which classifies business units as stars, cash cows, question marks, and dogs partly by market stage. Critics note that real products rarely follow the smooth bell-shaped curve, that the length of stages varies enormously, and that treating decline as inevitable can become self-fulfilling. Related but distinct concepts include the industry life cycle and the technology life cycle.
