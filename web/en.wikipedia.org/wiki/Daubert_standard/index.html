Daubert standard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In United States federal law, the Daubert standard is a rule of evidence governing the admissibility of expert witnesses' testimony. It derives from the Supreme Court's decision in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), in which parents alleged that the anti-nausea drug Bendectin caused birth defects. The Court held that Federal Rule of Evidence 702 imposes a gatekeeping obligation on trial judges to ensure that scientific testimony rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the task at hand.

The Daubert factors

Writing for the majority, Justice Harry Blackmun listed non-exclusive considerations for assessing reliability: (1) whether the theory or technique can be and has been tested (falsifiability); (2) whether it has been subjected to peer review and publication; (3) its known or potential rate of error and the existence of standards controlling its operation; and (4) whether it is "generally accepted" in the relevant scientific community. The standard displaced the stricter general-acceptance test of Frye v. United States (1923) in federal courts.

Extension and influence

In Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael (1999) the Court extended Daubert gatekeeping to all expert testimony, including technical and experience-based expertise. Rule 702 was amended in 2000 and again in 2023 to codify the standard and clarify that the proponent must establish admissibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Most states have adopted Daubert or a similar framework, though some retain Frye. See also Expert witness and Federal Rules of Evidence.
