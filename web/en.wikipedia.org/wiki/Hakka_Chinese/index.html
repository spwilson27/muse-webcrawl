Hakka (Hakka: Hak-kâ-fa; Chinese: 客家話; pinyin: Kèjiāhuà) is a variety of Chinese spoken natively by the Hakka people in southern China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and in overseas Hakka diaspora communities around the world. The name "Hakka" literally means "guest families", reflecting the people's history of migration from northern China to the south over many centuries.

History

The Hakka language developed as the Hakka people migrated southward in several waves, beginning around the end of the Tang dynasty and continuing through the Song, Ming and Qing dynasties, driven by war, famine and political upheaval in the Central Plains. Isolated in the mountainous borderlands of Guangdong, Fujian and Jiangxi, their speech preserved many features of older forms of Chinese while also absorbing elements from neighbouring She, Gan, Min and Yue varieties.

Phonology

Hakka is noted for retaining entering-tone (checked) syllables ending in -p, -t and -k, and for a six-tone system in many dialects, including the prestige Meixian (Moiyan) dialect of northeastern Guangdong. It has a relatively simple initial system and preserves distinctions lost in Mandarin, such as the contrast between certain sibilants.

Dialects

Major Hakka dialect groups include Meixian, Huizhou, Wuhua, Xingning, and the Sixian and Hailu dialects of Taiwan. Mutual intelligibility between distant Hakka dialects can be limited. In Taiwan, Hakka is recognised as one of the national languages and is used in public broadcasting, including the Hakka TV station.

Writing

Hakka is written with Chinese characters, and romanisation systems such as Pha̍k-fa-sṳ were developed by Presbyterian missionaries in the 19th century for Bible translation. In Taiwan, the Ministry of Education has promoted a standardised Hakka phonetic system and character usage for education.
