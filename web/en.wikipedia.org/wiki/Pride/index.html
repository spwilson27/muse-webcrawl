Pride is a feeling of deep pleasure or satisfaction derived from one's own achievements, the achievements of those with whom one is closely associated, or from qualities or possessions that are widely admired. It is also, in many moral traditions, regarded as a vice: in Christianity, pride (superbia) is traditionally the first and most serious of the seven deadly sins, the excessive belief in one's own abilities that interferes with recognition of grace.

Psychology

Psychologists such as Jessica Tracy and Richard Robins distinguish authentic pride, arising from genuine accomplishment and associated with confidence and prosocial behaviour, from hubristic pride, a grandiose, narcissistic form associated with arrogance and aggression. Pride has a characteristic nonverbal expression — expanded posture, head tilted back, arms raised or on hips — recognised across cultures, including among congenitally blind individuals, suggesting an innate component.

In classic texts

Aristotle treated pride (megalopsychia, proper pride or magnanimity) as a virtue — the "crown of the virtues" — when grounded in real merit, with vanity and humility as its excess and deficiency. Augustine and Aquinas, by contrast, saw pride as the root of sin: Aquinas called it the beginning of all sin, echoing the biblical "Pride goes before destruction" (Proverbs 16:18). Dante's Purgatorio places the proud on the first terrace of Purgatory, bent under heavy stones.

Collective pride

Pride is also felt on behalf of groups — national pride, ethnic pride, or the pride movements of marginalised communities, most prominently LGBTQ pride (see Pride (LGBTQ culture)). Such collective pride can affirm identity and solidarity, though national pride shades into nationalism and, in excess, chauvinism.
