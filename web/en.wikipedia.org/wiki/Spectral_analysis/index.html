Spectral analysis

Spectral analysis
FieldStatistics, signal processing

Spectral analysis or spectrum analysis is the analysis of a signal, time series, or other quantity in terms of its frequency components. In statistics and signal processing, spectral analysis estimates the power spectrum (spectral density) of a signal — how its variance or power is distributed over frequency. It is a principal tool for detecting periodicities, cycles, and noise structure that are not evident in the time domain.

Methods

The classical nonparametric method is the periodogram — the squared magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform — smoothed or averaged (as in Welch's method) to obtain a consistent estimate of the spectrum. Parametric methods model the data as the output of a linear system driven by white noise, most commonly autoregressive (AR), moving-average (MA), or ARMA models, yielding smoother spectra and better resolution for short records. Multitaper methods, developed by David Thomson, reduce spectral leakage and variance by using several orthogonal data tapers.

Applications

Spectral analysis is used in economics (business-cycle analysis), climatology (e.g. detecting El Niño and sunspot cycles), seismology, acoustics and speech processing, radio astronomy, oceanography, medicine (EEG and heart-rate variability), and engineering vibration analysis. In physics and chemistry the term also overlaps with spectroscopy — the analysis of electromagnetic spectra — though in signal processing it refers specifically to frequency-domain statistical estimation.

History

The term "spectrum" for a function of frequency was introduced into statistics by Maurice Priestley. Arthur Schuster devised the periodogram at the end of the 19th century in searching for hidden periodicities in sunspot data; John Tukey and others developed modern estimation methods, and the fast Fourier transform (Cooley–Tukey, 1965) made spectral estimation routine.
