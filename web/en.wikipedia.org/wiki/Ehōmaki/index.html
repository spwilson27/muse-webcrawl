Ehōmaki

Ehōmaki (恵方巻, "lucky direction roll") is a thick, uncut makizushi sushi roll traditionally eaten on Setsubun, the day before the beginning of spring in Japan (usually 3 February). The roll is eaten whole, in silence, while facing the year's lucky direction (ehō), in the belief that doing so brings good fortune.

Custom

The roll typically contains seven fillings, said to represent the Seven Lucky Gods (Shichifukujin); common ingredients include kanpyō (dried gourd), cucumber, tamagoyaki (rolled omelette), eel, sakura denbu (sweet fish flakes), and shiitake mushrooms. The roll must not be cut, because cutting is said to sever the good fortune, and it is eaten without speaking until finished.

History

The custom originated in the Kansai region during the Edo or early Meiji period; its precise origins are debated, with theories linking it to Osaka merchants, to geisha customs in the pleasure quarters, and to shrine festivals. The practice spread nationwide in the late twentieth century, heavily promoted by convenience store and supermarket marketing campaigns from the 1980s and 1990s onward. In recent years the custom has attracted criticism for the large amounts of food waste generated by unsold ehōmaki, prompting retailers and the Japanese government to encourage more accurate ordering.
