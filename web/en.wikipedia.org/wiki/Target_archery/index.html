Target archery

Target archery is the most recognisable format of modern archery, in which archers shoot arrows at a stationary circular target from set distances. It is governed internationally by World Archery (formerly FITA) and is the form of archery contested at the Olympic Games (recurve) and the World Archery Championships.

Equipment

Two bow types are used in international target competition. The recurve bow, the only type permitted at the Olympics, has limbs whose tips curve away from the archer; the compound bow, which uses a system of cams and cables to reduce holding weight at full draw, is used at world championship and World Cup level. Archers may use sights, stabilisers, finger tabs or releases depending on division.

Targets and scoring

The standard target face has ten concentric scoring zones in five colours: white (1–2), black (3–4), blue (5–6), red (7–8) and gold (9–10), with the innermost ring, the "X" or inner ten, used as a tiebreak. Outdoor faces are 122 cm in diameter for longer distances and 80 cm for shorter ones; indoor faces are 40 or 60 cm. Arrows are shot in "ends" of three or six.

Distances and rounds

Olympic recurve competition is shot at 70 metres for both men and women. Compound archers compete at 50 metres. Competition typically combines a ranking round — 72 arrows for qualification — with head-to-head elimination matches decided by set points (recurve) or cumulative score (compound). Indoor competition is commonly shot at 18 metres.

History

Organised target archery developed in Britain and the United States in the nineteenth century, with rounds such as the York, Hereford and American rounds. Archery appeared at the Olympic Games from 1900 to 1920, was dropped, and returned permanently at Munich in 1972. The compound bow, invented in the 1960s, was admitted to world championship competition in the 1990s.
