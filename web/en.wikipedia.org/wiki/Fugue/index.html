Fugue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The opening of the Fugue in C minor from Book I of Bach's The Well-Tempered Clavier, showing the subject's successive entries.

In classical music, a fugue (from Latin fuga, "flight", related to fugere, to flee, and fugare, to chase) is a contrapuntal compositional technique in two or more voices, built on a short melody or phrase — the subject — which is introduced at the beginning in imitation and recurs frequently throughout the course of the composition.

A fugue usually begins with an exposition, in which each voice in turn states the subject; the second entry, transposed to the dominant, is called the answer, and is "real" if it reproduces the subject exactly and "tonal" if adjusted to stay in key. Material connecting later groups of entries is called an episode, and a passage in which entries overlap closely is a stretto. Counter-material heard consistently against the answer is a countersubject.

History

The fugue grew out of the imitative ricercar and canzona of the Renaissance and early Baroque. It reached its height in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach, notably The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722, 1742), The Art of Fugue, and The Musical Offering, whose concluding six-part ricercar is written over a theme by Frederick the Great. Handel's choral fugues in Messiah are also celebrated.

In the Classical era the fugue retreated to a display of learned technique: Haydn ended several Op. 20 string quartets with fugues, Mozart wrote fugal finales (as in the "Jupiter" Symphony), and Beethoven crowned works such as the Hammerklavier Sonata and the Grosse Fuge with monumental fugues. Romantic and later composers — Mendelssohn, Brahms, Reger, Hindemith, Shostakovich (in his 24 Preludes and Fugues) and Bartók — continued to use fugal writing, often as a culmination within larger forms.

Analysis

Analysts describe a typical fugue as a three-part design: exposition, middle entries separated by episodes (often sequential and modulating), and a final section returning to the tonic, sometimes with stretto and pedal points for climax. Related forms include the fughetta (a short fugue), the double and triple fugue (with two or three subjects), the permutation fugue, and the mirror (invertible) fugue.

Fugue in other senses

In psychiatry, a fugue state (dissociative fugue) is a rare disorder involving reversible amnesia and unplanned travel — an unrelated meaning of the same word, from the idea of "flight".
