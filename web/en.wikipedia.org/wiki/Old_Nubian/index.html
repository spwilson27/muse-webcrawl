Old Nubian is an extinct Nubian language, written in an adapted form of the Coptic alphabet, that was spoken and written in the medieval Christian kingdoms of Nubia — Nobatia, Makuria and Alodia — along the middle Nile. It is attested from the 8th to the 15th century AD and is the ancestor (or close relative of the ancestor) of modern Nobiin.

Corpus and script

About a hundred texts survive: translations of the Gospels and liturgy, saints' lives (notably a miracle of St Mennas), legal documents such as land sales and slave manumissions, letters, and graffiti. Many were recovered in the UNESCO salvage campaign of the 1960s at sites like Qasr Ibrim and Faras before flooding by Lake Nasser. The script takes the Coptic uncial letters, adds three characters derived from Meroitic cursive, and adds diacritics for sounds such as /ɲ/ and /ŋ/. Old Nubian is thus one of the few medieval African languages with a substantial indigenous written literature outside Ethiopia.

Language

Old Nubian is an agglutinative language with subject–object–verb word order, postpositions, and a system of verbal suffixes marking tense, aspect and question forms; it has a two-tone system reflected in modern Nobiin. Christian vocabulary drew heavily on Greek loanwords for church concepts, while everyday vocabulary is native Nubian. Scholarly study began with Leo Reinisch and was transformed by Gerald Browne's grammar and dictionary in the late twentieth century.
