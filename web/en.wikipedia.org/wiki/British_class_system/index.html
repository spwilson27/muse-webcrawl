The British class system is the social stratification of people in the United Kingdom, traditionally into upper, middle and working classes, defined by a combination of wealth, occupation, education, manners and accent. Class has been a defining theme of British politics and culture: the aristocratic landed upper class, the professional and commercial middle classes, and the industrial working class shaped the country's parties, institutions and literature from the 19th century onwards.

History

The modern class structure emerged from industrialisation, which created a factory working class and a new commercial middle class alongside the older landed aristocracy. The 19th century saw reform acts extend the franchise, while the rise of trade unions and the Labour Party gave the working class political representation. The post-1945 welfare state, comprehensive education and deindustrialisation from the 1970s blurred many older boundaries: home ownership expanded, manual work shrank, and class came to be measured as much by cultural and social capital as by occupation.

Indicators and culture

Accent — notably the contrast between Received Pronunciation and regional accents — remains a powerful class marker, as do schooling (particularly attendance at fee-paying "public schools" and Oxford or Cambridge), vocabulary ("U and non-U" in Nancy Mitford's famous formulation) and leisure tastes. Comedians and writers from John Betjeman to the makers of Class sketches have mined these distinctions.

Modern analyses

Sociologists increasingly argue the three-class model is inadequate. The 2013 Great British Class Survey, run by the BBC with academics, proposed seven classes — from an "elite" and "established middle class" to a "precariat" — based on economic, social and cultural capital. Politicians have variously proclaimed a "classless society" (John Major) and insisted that "we're all middle class now", but research continues to show that class origins strongly predict life chances in Britain.
