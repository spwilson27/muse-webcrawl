The Midday Meal Scheme, now known as PM-POSHAN, is a school meal programme in India designed to better the nutritional standing of school-age children nationwide. Under the programme, free hot cooked lunches are provided on working days to children in government and government-aided primary and upper-primary schools. It is the largest school feeding programme in the world, reaching well over 100 million children.

History

School meal programmes existed in parts of India from the 1920s (notably in Madras Presidency) and were greatly expanded in Tamil Nadu under Chief Minister K. Kamaraj and later M. G. Ramachandran. The national programme, the National Programme of Nutritional Support to Primary Education (NP-NSPE), was launched on 15 August 1995. A landmark Supreme Court order in 2001 directed states to provide cooked meals rather than dry rations, and the scheme was progressively extended to upper-primary classes. In 2021 it was renamed PM-POSHAN (Pradhan Mantri Poshan Shakti Nirman) and extended to pre-primary children.

Objectives and norms

The scheme aims to improve enrolment, retention, and attendance, especially among disadvantaged groups, while enhancing children's nutritional status. Meals are prescribed to provide at least 450 calories and 12 grams of protein at the primary level, and 700 calories and 20 grams of protein at the upper-primary level.

Implementation and criticism

Costs are shared between the central and state governments; food is cooked in school kitchens or by centralized kitchens run by NGOs such as the Akshaya Patra Foundation. The programme has been widely credited with raising school participation and reducing classroom hunger, but has faced criticism over food quality, hygiene, and occasional tragedies, notably the 2013 Bihar school meal poisoning, which killed 23 children.
