Taika Reforms
Date645–646 CE (edicts issued 646)
LocationAsuka, Yamato, Japan
Key figuresEmperor Kōtoku, Prince Naka no Ōe, Nakatomi no Kamatari
OutcomeCentralised imperial government on the Chinese model; basis of the ritsuryō system

The Taika Reforms (大化の改新, Taika no Kaishin) were a series of political and administrative reforms promulgated in Japan in 645–646, following the Isshi Incident, in which Prince Naka no Ōe and Nakatomi no Kamatari assassinated the powerful minister Soga no Iruka and destroyed the dominance of the Soga clan. Named for the Taika era proclaimed by the new emperor, Kōtoku, the reforms sought to remake Japan's government on the model of Tang-dynasty China.

The reform edicts

The edicts of 646, as recorded in the Nihon Shoki, declared that all land and people were to belong to the emperor, abolishing the private estates and hereditary subject groups of the great clans; established a system of provinces, districts, and local administration; ordered household registers and a periodic redistribution of rice land (handen-shūju); and instituted uniform taxes in grain, cloth, and labour. A new capital and a bureaucratic court ranking system on Chinese lines were also begun.

Significance

The Taika Reforms laid the ideological foundation of the ritsuryō state completed by the Taihō Code of 701. In practice, implementation was gradual and incomplete — powerful families retained much influence, and the land-allotment system decayed over the following centuries — but the principle that sovereignty resided in the emperor, rather than in clan chieftains, remained central to Japanese political thought. Modern historians debate how much of the reform narrative in the Nihon Shoki reflects 7th-century events versus 8th-century idealisation.
