Electrical conductance

Electrical conductance is a measure of how readily a material or component allows an electric current to flow. It is the reciprocal of electrical resistance and is measured in the SI unit of siemens (symbol S), formerly called the mho. Conductance G is defined by G = 1/R = I/V, where R is resistance, I current and V voltage.

Relation to conductivity

For a uniform conductor of length l and cross-sectional area A, conductance is G = σA/l, where σ is the electrical conductivity of the material, expressed in siemens per metre. High conductance characterises good conductors such as copper and silver; insulators have extremely low conductance.

AC circuits

In alternating-current circuits, conductance is the real part of the complex admittance: Y = G + jB, where Y is admittance and B is susceptance. In quantum electronics, conductance can be quantised in units of the conductance quantum G0 = 2e²/h, an effect observed in point contacts and the quantum Hall effect.
