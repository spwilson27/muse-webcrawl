Evisceration (ophthalmology)

SpecialtyOphthalmology
ICD-9-CM16.3

An evisceration is the removal of the eye's contents, leaving the scleral shell and extraocular muscles intact. The procedure is performed to reduce pain in a blind eye, as in cases of end-stage glaucoma, or to improve cosmetic appearance in a disfigured, non-seeing eye. It differs from enucleation, in which the entire eyeball is removed.

Procedure

During evisceration, the surgeon removes the cornea and the intraocular contents — the iris, lens, vitreous, retina, and uveal tissue — while preserving the sclera (the white outer coat of the eye) and the attached extraocular muscles. An orbital implant is usually placed within the scleral shell to maintain volume, and the shell is closed over it. After healing, an ocular prosthesis (artificial eye) can be fitted over the implant by an ocularist.

Indications

  • A blind, painful eye, for example from endophthalmitis or absolute glaucoma
  • A severely traumatized eye with no visual potential
  • A disfigured blind eye where cosmetic rehabilitation is desired

Comparison with enucleation

Because the sclera and muscle attachments are preserved, evisceration often provides better motility of the implant and prosthesis and is a technically simpler operation. However, evisceration is contraindicated when intraocular malignancy is suspected, since leaving the scleral shell could leave tumor tissue behind; enucleation is preferred in such cases. A historically feared complication is sympathetic ophthalmia — inflammation in the fellow eye — although modern studies suggest the risk after evisceration is extremely low.

Implants

Implants used in evisceration include porous materials such as hydroxyapatite and porous polyethylene, which allow fibrovascular ingrowth, as well as nonporous silicone or acrylic spheres. The sclera may be modified with relaxing incisions or posterior sclerotomies to accommodate a larger implant.
