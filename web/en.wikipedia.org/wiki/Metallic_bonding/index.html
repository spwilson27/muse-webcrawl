Metallic bonding

Metallic bonding is a type of chemical bonding that arises from the electrostatic attractive force between conduction electrons (in the form of an electron cloud of delocalised electrons) and positively charged metal ions. It may be described as the sharing of free electrons among a structure of positively charged ions (cations). Metallic bonding accounts for many physical properties of metals, such as strength, ductility, malleability, conductivity of heat and electricity, opacity, and lustre.

The electron sea model

In the simplest model, metal atoms contribute their valence electrons to a common "sea" that permeates the whole solid, while the remaining ion cores occupy regular lattice positions. Because the bonding electrons are not tied to any particular atom, they can move freely, explaining metals' high electrical and thermal conductivity. The non-directional character of the attraction between the electron sea and the ion cores allows layers of atoms to slide over one another, explaining malleability and ductility.

Band theory

A more complete description is given by band theory, in which the atomic orbitals of the many atoms in the solid combine into energy bands. Partially filled bands allow electrons to move in response to an electric field; the strength of metallic bonding grows across the transition metals as more electrons participate in bonding, peaking around the middle of each series.
