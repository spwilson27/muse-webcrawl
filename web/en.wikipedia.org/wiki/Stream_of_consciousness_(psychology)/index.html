Stream of consciousness (psychology)

The stream of consciousness is a metaphor describing how thoughts seem to flow through the conscious mind. The phrase was coined by Alexander Bain in 1855 and popularised by the philosopher and psychologist William James in his Principles of Psychology (1890), where he argued that consciousness does not appear as chopped-up bits but flows like a river or stream.

William James, who popularised the metaphor in 1890.

James's account

James identified characteristics of the stream of thought: it is personal, constantly changing, sensibly continuous, deals with objects independent of itself, and is selective — attending to some things and ignoring others. He contrasted "substantive" resting places of thought (images and concepts) with "transitive" fringes and feelings of relation between them.

Influence

The metaphor influenced both psychology — forming part of the background to introspectionist methods and later criticisms of them — and literature, where "stream of consciousness" became the name for a narrative technique used by writers such as James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, and William Faulkner to render characters' inner mental lives. Related Buddhist concepts, such as the mindstream (citta-santāna), describe a similar continuity of mental moments.

Contemporary views

Modern cognitive science debates whether conscious experience is genuinely continuous or a rapid sequence of quasi-independent moments — the distinction between "stream" and "cinematic" models of consciousness.
