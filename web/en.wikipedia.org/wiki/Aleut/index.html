The Aleut (Russian: Алеуты, romanized: Aleuty) are the Indigenous people of the Aleutian Islands, the western Alaska Peninsula, the Pribilof Islands and the Shumagin Islands in Alaska, and of the Commander Islands in Russia. In their own language they call themselves Unangax̂ or Unangas. Their language, Aleut, forms one branch of the Eskimo–Aleut language family.

History

Aleut ancestors have occupied the island chain for at least 9,000 years, developing a sophisticated maritime culture based on sea-mammal hunting, fishing and birding, using skin boats (the one- and two-hatch iqyax̂, known in Russian as the baidarka) and dwellings called ulax̂ (barabaras), semi-subterranean houses roofed with sod.

Contact with Russian fur traders began in the 1740s, after Vitus Bering's expedition. The ensuing Russian-American period brought violent conflict, epidemic disease and forced labor in the sea otter fur trade, reducing the Aleut population drastically. Russian influence also brought Orthodox Christianity, which remains central to Aleut identity. After the United States purchased Alaska in 1867, the Aleut came under American administration.

Second World War and after

During the Second World War, Japanese forces occupied the islands of Attu and Kiska, and Aleut villagers from the western chain were evacuated by the U.S. government to camps in Southeast Alaska, where many died of disease and neglect; the people of Attu were taken prisoner to Japan. After the war, some villages were never resettled. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 created the Aleut Corporation as one of thirteen Native regional corporations.

Culture

Traditional Aleut arts include finely woven grass basketry, bentwood hunting hats, gut-skin parkas and carved wooden masks and tools. Subsistence hunting and fishing for salmon, seals, sea lions and whales remain culturally and economically important, alongside commercial fishing. Aleut communities today work to revitalize the Unangam Tunuu language, which is severely endangered.
