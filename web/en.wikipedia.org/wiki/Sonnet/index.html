Sonnet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A manuscript page of a sonnet, fourteen lines in italic hand, the volta marked by a flourish in the margin]

A sonnet is a lyric poem of fourteen lines, traditionally in iambic pentameter, originating in Italy in the thirteenth century (Giacomo da Lentini at the Sicilian court of Frederick II) and perfected by Petrarch, whose Canzoniere — 366 poems, 317 of them sonnets, for his Laura — made the form Europe's love-poem standard for three centuries.

The Petrarchan sonnet divides octave (ABBA ABBA) from sestet (flexible rhymes) with a turn, the volta, between problem and resolution. Imported to England by Wyatt and Surrey, it was reshaped into the Shakespearean form (three quatrains and a couplet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) and practised by Spenser, Sidney, Milton, Wordsworth, Keats, Elizabeth Barrett Browning and Edna St. Vincent Millay — and, renewably, by poets ever since.

The argument in fourteen lines

The form's genius is its rhetoric: a proposition is set up, turned, and landed — sonnets argue, woo, mourn and philosophise within a discipline that forces economy. Shakespeare's 154 sonnets (1609) made the English sequence the form's summit — the Fair Youth and Dark Lady poems remaining literature's most analysed lyrics — and Milton stretched the form to politics and grief, breaking the love monopoly.

Modern sonnets

The form survived modernism (Frost, Auden, the sonnet-sequences of Lowell and Berryman) and thrives in contemporary reinvention — Terrance Hayes's American Sonnets for My Past and Future Assassin (2018) renewed it for the present tense. Fourteen lines proved the most durable technology in English poetry: room enough for a thought, small enough to memorise.
