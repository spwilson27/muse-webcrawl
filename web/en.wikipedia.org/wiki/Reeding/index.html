Reeding

Reeding is a technique used in coinage and in architecture and decorative arts. In numismatics, reeding refers to the series of grooves — reeds — milled into the edge of a coin; in architecture, reeding denotes a set of thin, parallel convex mouldings, the opposite of the concave channels of fluting.

In coinage

Reeded edges were introduced with milled (machine-struck) coinage to make clipping — shaving precious metal from a coin's edge — immediately detectable, and to make counterfeiting more difficult. Many modern coins retain reeded edges, including the US dime and quarter and the British £1 coin (in one of its variants), while others have plain, lettered, or decoratively patterned edges. The number of reeds can be a minting characteristic used by collectors to distinguish issues.

In architecture and furniture

In classical and neoclassical design, reeding is a surface ornament of parallel convex strips, used on columns, pilasters, chair and table legs, and silverware. It is the inverse of fluting: where fluting carves concave grooves, reeding applies or carves raised, rounded ribs resembling bundled reeds.
