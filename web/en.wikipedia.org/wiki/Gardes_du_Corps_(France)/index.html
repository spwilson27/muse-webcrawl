Gardes du Corps (France)

A Garde du Corps in the uniform of the Restoration period.

The Gardes du Corps ("Body Guards") were the senior formation of the cavalry of the French royal household, the Maison militaire du roi de France. Senior in precedence to all other troops of the French Army, they served as the mounted bodyguard of the King of France and as a heavy cavalry regiment in war.

History

The corps originated with a company of Scottish archers—the Garde Écossaise—raised in the early 15th century, and French companies were added later. By the reign of Louis XIV the Gardes du Corps comprised four companies, each with its own colour and identity: the Scottish Company, and three French companies. A place in the corps was highly sought after by the nobility, and its officers held high court rank. On campaign the guards fought as elite heavy cavalry, charging alongside the other household troops.

During the French Revolution the corps was associated with the defence of the monarchy; guardsmen protected the royal family during the Women's March on Versailles in October 1789, in which several were killed. The corps was disbanded in 1791, but was reconstituted by Louis XVIII during the Bourbon Restoration in 1814, serving again as the royal bodyguard until the July Revolution of 1830, when it was finally dissolved.

Organisation and dress

Each company had a nominal strength of several hundred guards, commanded by a captain who was a senior courtier. The guards wore blue coats with silver lace under the Bourbons (red in some earlier periods), with distinctive bandoliers that identified their company. In battle order they traditionally took post around the king's person.
