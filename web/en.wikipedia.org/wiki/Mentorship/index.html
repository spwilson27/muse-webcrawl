Mentorship

Mentorship
Named afterMentor, character in Homer's Odyssey
PartiesMentor (experienced) and mentee/protégé (less experienced)
SettingsWorkplace, education, youth programmes, the arts, academia
Related conceptsCoaching, apprenticeship, sponsorship, peer mentoring

Mentorship is a relationship in which a more experienced or more knowledgeable person — a mentor — helps to guide a less experienced person — the mentee or protégé — in their personal or professional development. The mentor acts as adviser, counsellor and role model, sharing knowledge, skills and networks over a sustained period. The word derives from Mentor, the friend of Odysseus in Homer's Odyssey to whom the hero entrusted the care of his son Telemachus.

Functions and models

Researcher Kathy Kram's influential 1985 study distinguished two broad functions of workplace mentoring: career functions (sponsorship, exposure, coaching, challenging assignments and protection) and psychosocial functions (role modelling, acceptance, counselling and friendship). Mentoring relationships may arise informally or be organised through formal programmes in companies, universities and professional bodies, which match mentors and mentees and set goals and durations.

Types

  • Traditional one-to-one mentoring — a senior figure guides a junior one.
  • Peer mentoring — equals or near-equals support one another.
  • Reverse mentoring — a junior person mentors a senior one, e.g. in new technology or diversity perspectives (popularised at General Electric under Jack Welch in the late 1990s).
  • Group and team mentoring — one mentor with several mentees, or a network of advisers.
  • Youth mentoring — structured programmes such as Big Brothers Big Sisters (founded 1904) pairing adult volunteers with young people.
  • E-mentoring — mentoring conducted primarily online.

Evidence and criticism

Meta-analyses of workplace mentoring find modest but consistent benefits for mentees in compensation, promotion and job satisfaction, and benefits for mentors and organisations as well. Studies of youth mentoring show small positive effects on behaviour and academic outcomes. Critics note that access to informal mentors can reproduce privilege — people tend to mentor those who resemble themselves — and that formal schemes vary widely in quality and matching. Mentorship is distinguished from coaching (shorter-term, task-focused) and from sponsorship, in which a senior person actively advocates for a protégé's advancement.
