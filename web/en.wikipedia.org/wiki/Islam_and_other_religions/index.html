Islam and other religions

Over its history, Islam has developed distinctive theological and legal frameworks governing its relationship with other religions. The Quran presents Islam as the final and complete revelation in a line of monotheistic faiths, honouring earlier prophets — including Abraham, Moses and Jesus — while holding that earlier scriptures were partly altered over time.

People of the Book

Jews and Christians are recognised as Ahl al-Kitāb, "People of the Book" — communities who received earlier revelation. In classical Islamic law, in Muslim-ruled states they were granted the status of dhimmi: protected communities permitted to practise their faith, maintain houses of worship and govern internal affairs, in exchange for payment of the jizya tax and acceptance of certain restrictions. The category was later extended in practice to Zoroastrians and, in India, to Hindus. The Quran itself both affirms common ground — "Our God and your God is one" — and criticises doctrines such as the Trinity and the divinity of Jesus, while also teaching that "there shall be no compulsion in religion" (2:256), a verse interpreted in different ways by classical and modern scholars.

Relations with particular faiths

Judaism and Christianity. Islam shares with them the Abrahamic heritage: Abraham is a central figure, Jesus is revered as a prophet and the Messiah (though not divine), and Mary is the only woman named in the Quran. Hinduism and Buddhism. Classical jurists debated their status given their non-Abrahamic character; Muslim rule in India generally accommodated Hindu practice. Sikhism arose in a Hindu-Muslim environment in Punjab, and relations in the Mughal period ranged from coexistence to persecution. Baháʼí Faith, which emerged from a Shi'a milieu in 19th-century Persia, is not recognised by most Muslim authorities, and Baháʼís have faced persecution in Iran.

Modern dialogue

Contemporary interfaith initiatives include the 2007 open letter A Common Word Between Us and You, signed by 138 Muslim scholars and addressed to Christian leaders, grounding dialogue in the shared commandments of love of God and neighbour, and the 2019 Document on Human Fraternity signed by Pope Francis and the Grand Imam of al-Azhar. Scholars continue to debate questions of pluralism, apostasy laws and religious freedom in Muslim-majority states.
