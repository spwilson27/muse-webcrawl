Saint Alban (Latin: Albanus) is venerated as the first-recorded British Christian martyr. He lived in the Roman town of Verulamium, now St Albans in Hertfordshire, and was beheaded there in the 3rd or early 4th century. His story is recorded by the Venerable Bede in the Ecclesiastical History of the English People (731).

Legend

According to the tradition, Alban was a pagan who sheltered a Christian priest (later named Amphibalus) fleeing persecution. Impressed by the priest's faith, Alban converted and, when soldiers came to arrest his guest, exchanged cloaks with him and gave himself up. Before the magistrate he refused to offer sacrifice and declared himself a Christian. He was scourged and beheaded on a hill outside the town; legend adds that the executioner's eyes fell out and that a spring flowed where Alban's head fell. The priest and later converts were also martyred.

Cult

A church stood over Alban's tomb from late antiquity; in 793 King Offa of Mercia founded St Albans Abbey, which became one of England's richest monasteries. The town of St Albans takes its name from him, and St Albans Cathedral stands on the site of his execution. Alban is the patron saint of St Albans and is commemorated on 22 June with an annual pilgrimage.
