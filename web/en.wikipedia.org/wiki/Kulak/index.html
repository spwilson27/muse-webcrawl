Kulak

Kulak (Russian: кула́к, "fist", by extension "tight-fisted") was the term used to describe prosperous peasants in the late Russian Empire and early Soviet Union. In the years before the Russian Revolution of 1917, the term was popularised and used to describe peasants who owned over 3.2 hectares (8 acres) of land and used hired labour. Under Joseph Stalin, the definition broadened: any peasant who resisted collectivisation, owned property, or employed labour could be labelled a kulak — and hundreds of thousands were arrested, executed or deported in the campaign known as dekulakization.

History

The category emerged after the Stolypin reforms (1906–1911), which sought to create a class of independent, property-owning farmers as a bulwark of the tsarist order. Kulaks grew in number and prosperity in the last decades of the Empire and during the New Economic Policy of the 1920s.

Dekulakization

In 1929, Stalin announced the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class". The OGPU secret police divided kulaks into categories: the first, deemed counter-revolutionary activists, were to be shot or sent to labour camps; the second deported to distant regions such as Siberia, the Urals and Kazakhstan; the third evicted from their districts. Around 1.8 million people were deported in 1930–31 alone, and many more fled or were resettled; their farms were seized for collective farms (kolkhozes). Historians estimate that hundreds of thousands died from execution, camp conditions, hunger and exposure during deportation. Dekulakization, combined with forced collectivisation, contributed to the catastrophic Soviet famines of 1932–33, including the Holodomor in Ukraine.

After Stalin's death, surviving deported kulaks were gradually released from special settlements, and in the post-Soviet era victims of dekulakization were formally rehabilitated in Russia and Ukraine.
