Herefordshire is a ceremonial county and unitary authority in the West Midlands of England, bordering Wales to the west, Shropshire to the north, Worcestershire to the east and Gloucestershire to the south-east. It is one of the most rural and sparsely populated counties in England. Its county town and only city is Hereford, seat of Hereford Cathedral. Between 1974 and 1998 the county was merged with Worcestershire as Hereford and Worcester, before being restored as a separate unitary county.

Geography

The county is drained mainly by the River Wye, which enters from Wales, winds through Hereford, Ross-on-Wye and the dramatic gorge at Symonds Yat, and forms part of the southern border. The west rises to the Black Mountains on the Welsh border; the Black Hill (Cat's Back) and Hay Bluff reach over 600 metres. Much of the south forms part of the Wye Valley Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty.

Economy and agriculture

Agriculture dominates the economy. Herefordshire is famous for cider: it produces about half of the United Kingdom's cider, with firms such as Bulmers (founded in Hereford in 1887) and Weston's among the largest producers, and the county is planted with extensive cider-apple orchards. The Hereford breed of beef cattle, with its characteristic white face, originated in the county and is now raised worldwide. Hops, soft fruit, potatoes and poultry are also important.

Towns

Other principal towns are Leominster, Ross-on-Wye, Ledbury, Bromyard and Kington — historic market towns, several noted for black-and-white timber-framed buildings. The book town of Hay-on-Wye lies just across the Welsh border.
