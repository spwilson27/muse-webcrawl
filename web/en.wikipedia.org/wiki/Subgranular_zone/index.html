Subgranular zone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The subgranular zone (SGZ) is a region of the brain located between the granule cell layer and the hilus of the dentate gyrus in the hippocampus. It is one of only two major sites where neurogenesis — the production of new neurons — continues throughout adult life in mammals, the other being the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles.

Adult neurogenesis

The subgranular zone contains neural stem and progenitor cells. These divide to produce new granule neurons, which migrate a short distance into the granule cell layer, extend dendrites into the molecular layer and axons (mossy fibres) toward the CA3 region, and over several weeks become functionally integrated into hippocampal circuitry. In rodents, thousands of new neurons are produced each day, though only a fraction survive long term; survival is enhanced by learning, exercise, and enriched environments, and reduced by stress, ageing, and sleep deprivation.

Function and significance

Adult-born dentate granule cells are thought to contribute to learning and memory, particularly to pattern separation — the ability to distinguish similar experiences — and to mood regulation. Altered hippocampal neurogenesis has been linked in animal studies to depression-like behaviour and to the effects of antidepressant treatments, many of which increase progenitor proliferation in the SGZ. Whether neurogenesis continues at meaningful levels in the adult human hippocampus has been debated, with studies published since 2018 reaching differing conclusions, though substantial evidence supports its persistence into old age.
