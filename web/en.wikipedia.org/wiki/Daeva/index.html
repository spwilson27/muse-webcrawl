Daeva (Avestan: daēuua) is the Zoroastrian term for a class of supernatural entities considered demons or beings to be rejected. In the Zoroastrian scriptures, the daevas are agents of Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), the destructive spirit, and stand opposed to the ahuras and yazatas, the beneficent divine beings associated with Ahura Mazda.

Etymology and Indo-Iranian background

The word derives from the Proto-Indo-Iranian *daiva-, related to the Sanskrit deva ("god") and ultimately to the Proto-Indo-European root *deiwós, "celestial one", from *dyew-, "sky, day". In the Indian Vedic tradition, the cognate deva retained a positive meaning, while the Iranian tradition underwent a striking inversion: the daevas became demons, and the asuras (Avestan ahura) became the high gods. This divergence is often interpreted as reflecting a religious reform associated with the prophet Zoroaster, who condemned the cult of the daevas.

In Zoroastrian scripture

In the Gathas, the oldest hymns attributed to Zoroaster, the daevas are "gods of the old order" who chose deceit (druj) over truth (asha). The Vendidad enumerates individual daevas, including Indra, Saurva, and Nanghaithya — figures who remained gods in India as Indra, Shiva (Śarva), and the Nāsatyas — alongside personified evils such as Aka Manah (evil thought), Aeshma (wrath), and Azhi Dahaka, the three-headed serpent later known in Persian epic as Zahhak.

Later tradition

In Middle Persian literature the daevas (dēw) became standard demons of popular folklore, and the word passed into Persian, Armenian, Georgian and Urdu usage for monsters and ogres. In the Shahnameh of Ferdowsi, divs such as the White Demon (Div-e Sepid) appear as formidable adversaries of heroes.
