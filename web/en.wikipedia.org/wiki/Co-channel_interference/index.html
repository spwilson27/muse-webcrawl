Co-channel interference

Co-channel interference or CCI is crosstalk from two different radio transmitters using the same channel. Co-channel interference can be caused by many factors, from atmospheric conditions to administrative frequency-planning decisions. It can be mitigated by various antenna and network-design schemes, but in cellular radio systems it is one of the principal limits on capacity, because it determines how closely the same frequencies can be reused across a coverage area.

Causes

Weather and propagation

Under certain atmospheric conditions — notably temperature inversions that create tropospheric ducting — VHF and UHF signals can travel hundreds of kilometres beyond their normal range, so that a distant station on the same frequency interferes with a local one. This is a familiar phenomenon to FM radio listeners and television viewers, who may occasionally receive distant stations in place of, or mixed with, local broadcasts.

Poor frequency planning

When transmitters that use the same channel are placed too close together, or transmit with too much power, their coverage areas overlap and receivers in the overlap zone cannot cleanly separate the signals. Regulators publish minimum separation distances and protection ratios to keep such interference within acceptable bounds.

Cellular networks

Cellular systems deliberately reuse the same channels in different cells. The ratio of the distance between co-channel cells to the cell radius (the reuse ratio) is chosen so that the signal-to-interference ratio stays above the threshold required by the modulation and coding scheme. Unlike thermal noise, co-channel interference cannot be overcome simply by increasing transmit power, because the interfering signals increase proportionally. Techniques used to manage CCI include frequency planning, sectorisation, power control, adaptive antennas, and interference coordination between base stations.
