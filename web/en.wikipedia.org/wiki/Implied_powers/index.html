Implied powers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Implied powers derive from the Necessary and Proper Clause of the U.S. Constitution.

Implied powers, in the law of the United States, are political powers granted to the government of the United States that are not explicitly stated in the Constitution of the United States. Rather, they are implied to exist because similar powers — the enumerated powers — are expressly granted, and because these lesser powers are necessary in order to carry the enumerated powers out.

Origins

The doctrine grows out of the final clause of Article I, Section 8, which empowers Congress "to make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers." Alexander Hamilton relied on this clause in his 1791 opinion defending the constitutionality of a national bank, arguing that the power to charter a bank was a legitimate means of executing the taxing, borrowing, and currency powers.

McCulloch v. Maryland

In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall gave the doctrine its canonical formulation. Maryland had taxed the Bank of the United States; the Court held that Congress possessed the implied power to incorporate the bank and that states could not tax a federal instrumentality. Marshall wrote: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."

Examples

Implied powers commonly cited include establishing a national bank, creating the Internal Revenue Service to collect taxes, regulating intrastate activity that substantially affects interstate commerce, instituting a military draft, and setting a federal minimum wage as a means of regulating commerce and labour.

Criticism and limits

The doctrine has been criticized since the Anti-Federalists, who feared it would swallow the limits of federalism. The Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers to the states or the people. Modern courts continue to debate how far implication extends, as in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), where the Court limited the Commerce Clause rationale for the individual mandate while upholding it under the taxing power.
