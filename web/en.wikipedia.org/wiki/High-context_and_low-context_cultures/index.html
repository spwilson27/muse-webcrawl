High-context and low-context cultures

The terms high-context and low-context cultures describe the degree to which communication relies on implicit context versus explicit verbal information. They were introduced by anthropologist Edward T. Hall in his book Beyond Culture (1976) and have become influential in intercultural communication and business studies.

Definitions

In a high-context culture, much of the meaning of a message is conveyed by shared background knowledge, relationships, nonverbal cues and the situation; direct verbal statements may be minimal or indirect, and long-standing relationships carry much of the informational load. Japanese, Arab and Mediterranean cultures are commonly given as examples. In a low-context culture, communication is expected to be explicit, precise and direct, with meaning carried primarily by the spoken or written words themselves; German, Scandinavian and North American business cultures are typical examples.

Criticism

Scholars caution that the distinction is a continuum rather than a binary, varies by situation and relationship even within one society, and can slide into stereotype if used carelessly — nevertheless it remains a widely used heuristic in cross-cultural training and negotiation research.
