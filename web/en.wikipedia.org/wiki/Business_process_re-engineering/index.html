Business process re-engineering (BPR) is a management strategy, originating in the early 1990s, that focuses on the analysis and radical redesign of workflows and business processes within an organisation. Its goal is dramatic improvement in critical measures of performance such as cost, quality, service, and speed.

Origins

The approach was popularised by Michael Hammer's 1990 article "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate" and by the 1993 bestseller Reengineering the Corporation by Hammer and James Champy. They argued that most work processes were fragmented relics of functional specialisation, and that information technology should be used not to pave over existing "cow paths" but to redesign processes from a clean slate around outcomes for the customer.

Practice and criticism

BPR projects typically identify core processes, map their current state, redesign them radically, and reorganise jobs and structures around the new design, often flattening hierarchies and empowering case teams. In practice, BPR became associated with large-scale layoffs and downsizing, and many projects failed to deliver promised gains; reported failure rates were high, and critics argued the approach neglected human and cultural factors. Its ideas nevertheless shaped later disciplines of business process management and digital transformation.
