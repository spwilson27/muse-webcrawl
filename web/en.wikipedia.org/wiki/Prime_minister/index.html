A prime minister is the head of government of a country or territory. In most parliamentary systems the prime minister chairs the cabinet, leads the executive branch, and is accountable to the legislature, from which the government is usually drawn. The prime minister is distinct from the head of state — a monarch or president — though in some systems the roles' powers overlap in complex ways.

Origins

The office evolved in Great Britain after the Hanoverian succession. Robert Walpole, who dominated the government from 1721 to 1742, is generally regarded as the first prime minister, though the title was initially one of abuse. By the nineteenth century the British model — a premier dependent on the confidence of the House of Commons, heading a cabinet of ministers collectively responsible to Parliament — was being exported through the British Empire and imitated elsewhere, notably in Belgium, the Netherlands, Scandinavia, and later across Europe, Asia and Africa.

Powers and role

Prime ministers typically appoint and dismiss ministers, set the government's agenda, direct administration and foreign and defence policy, and serve as their party's leader and public face. Their authority rests on maintaining a parliamentary majority: governments fall on lost votes of confidence, lost supply, or by resignation. In semi-presidential systems such as France, the prime minister shares executive power with a directly elected president, with the balance shifting during "cohabitation," when the presidency and parliamentary majority are held by rival camps.

Titles elsewhere

Equivalent offices include the Chancellor of Germany and Austria, the President of the Government of Spain, the Taoiseach of Ireland, the Minister of State of several Scandinavian countries, and the Premier of sub-national governments in Australia and Canada. In absolute and constitutional monarchies the office may be styled grand vizier or chief minister. As of the twenty-first century, most of the world's states have a prime minister or equivalent; notable exceptions include the United States, where the president is both head of state and head of government.
