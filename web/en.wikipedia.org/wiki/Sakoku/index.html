Sakoku (Japanese: 鎖国, "locked country") is the term for the isolationist foreign policy of the Tokugawa shogunate under which, from the 1630s to 1853, Japan severely restricted contact with the outside world: Japanese were forbidden to leave the country, foreigners were largely excluded, and trade was channelled through a handful of controlled gateways.

The exclusion edicts

Between 1633 and 1639 the shogunate issued a series of edicts motivated by fear of Christian subversion (after the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38), of Iberian colonial ambitions, and of daimyo enriched by foreign trade. The Portuguese were expelled in 1639; the English had already withdrawn; only the Dutch — valued for trading without missionaries — kept a factory, confined from 1641 to the artificial island of Dejima in Nagasaki harbour.

Four gateways

Japan was never wholly closed. Through the so-called "four portals" it maintained relations: the Dutch and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki; Korea through the Sō clan of Tsushima, with Korean embassies travelling to Edo; the Ryukyu Kingdom through the Shimazu of Satsuma; and the Ainu lands (Ezo) through the Matsumae clan. Dutch studies (rangaku) gave Japanese scholars a thin but real stream of European science.

End of seclusion

Western pressure mounted in the nineteenth century, and in 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry of the United States Navy arrived with steam warships demanding Japan open its ports. The Convention of Kanagawa (1854) opened Shimoda and Hakodate to American ships, and commercial treaties followed in 1858, ending sakoku and setting Japan on the road to the Meiji Restoration.
