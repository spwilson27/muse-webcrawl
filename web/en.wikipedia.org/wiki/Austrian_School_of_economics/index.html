Austrian school of economics

[Image: the University of Vienna's main building, home of Menger and the early Austrian school]

The Austrian school is a heterodox tradition of economic thought originating with Carl Menger's Principles of Economics (1871) in Vienna. Its hallmarks are methodological individualism, subjectivism about value, emphasis on time, capital and entrepreneurship, and scepticism of mathematical modelling and central planning.

Development

Menger's marginal-Utility theory was developed by Böhm-Bawerk (capital and interest) and Wieser, then by Ludwig von Mises — whose Human Action (1949) grounded economics in purposeful action (praxeology) — and Friedrich Hayek, whose work on dispersed knowledge and the price system became the school's most influential contribution, deployed in the socialist calculation debate. Austrian business-cycle theory blames credit expansion by central banks for boom-and-bust. Largely exiled from Vienna after 1938, the school revived in the United States from the 1970s (Murray Rothbard, Israel Kirzner) and remains a minority, largely non-university tradition influential in libertarian circles and in debates over monetary policy and Bitcoin-era free banking.
