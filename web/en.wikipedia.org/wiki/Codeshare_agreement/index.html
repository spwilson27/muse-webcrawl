Codeshare agreement

A codeshare agreement, sometimes simply codeshare, is an arrangement in the aviation industry in which two or more airlines publish and market the same flight under their own airline designator and flight number ("airline code") as part of their published timetables or schedules. Typically, a flight is operated by one airline (the operating carrier) while seats are sold by others (the marketing carriers).

History

In 1967, Richard A. Henson pioneered the first joint service with Allegheny Airlines, a forerunner of codesharing in the United States. The term "code sharing" itself was coined in 1989 by Qantas and American Airlines, and the practice spread rapidly following U.S. deregulation and the growth of global alliances—Star Alliance, SkyTeam, and Oneworld—whose members codeshare extensively.

Types

In a parallel operation, both partners operate the same route with their own aircraft but sell seats on each other's flights. In a connecting or unlocking arrangement, one carrier operates a route it does not serve itself under the partner's code. In an alliance codeshare, members place their codes on each other's networks to offer seamless connections. Blocked-space agreements guarantee the marketing carrier a fixed allocation of seats, while free-sale arrangements draw on the operating carrier's inventory in real time.

Reasons and effects

Codesharing lets airlines offer service to destinations they do not fly to, increase flight frequency, feed traffic into their hubs, and extend frequent-flyer benefits. Critics note that passengers may be confused about which airline actually operates their flight, and that different baggage, seating, and service standards may apply. Regulations in the United States and European Union therefore require disclosure of the operating carrier at booking.
