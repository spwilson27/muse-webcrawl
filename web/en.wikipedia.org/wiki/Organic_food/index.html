Organic food

Organic food, also known as ecological food or biological food, is food produced by methods complying with the standards of organic farming. Standards vary worldwide, but organic farming features practices that cycle resources, promote ecological balance, and conserve biodiversity. Organizations regulating organic products may restrict the use of certain pesticides and fertilizers; generally, organic foods are also not processed using industrial solvents, irradiation, or synthetic food additives.

Definition and regulation

In the United States, the USDA National Organic Program certifies products labelled "organic", which must contain at least 95% organically produced ingredients; the European Union, Canada, Japan and many other jurisdictions maintain similar legal regimes. Certification requires inspection of farms and handling operations, bans most synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, prohibits genetically modified organisms, and for livestock requires access to the outdoors and prohibits routine antibiotics and growth hormones.

History

The organic movement arose in the early 20th century in reaction to industrialised agriculture; pioneers included Albert Howard in Britain, Rudolf Steiner's biodynamic agriculture (1924), and J. I. Rodale in the United States. Consumer demand grew rapidly from the 1990s, and the global market for organic food exceeded US$100 billion annually by the late 2010s.

Health, taste and environment

Scientific reviews have generally found small or inconsistent nutritional differences between organic and conventional food, though organic produce tends to carry lower pesticide residues. Organic farming typically supports greater on-farm biodiversity and soil health but often yields less per hectare, so its overall environmental footprint per unit of food is debated. Organic products command a price premium, reflecting higher production costs and consumer willingness to pay.
