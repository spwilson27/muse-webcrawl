Louis Anquetin

Louis Anquetin (26 January 1861 – 19 August 1932) was a French artist associated with the Post-Impressionist movement. Together with Émile Bernard, he was a founder of Cloisonnism, a style characterised by flat areas of colour separated by bold black outlines, inspired by stained glass and Japanese woodblock prints.

Life and work

Born in Étrépagny, Anquetin moved to Paris in 1882 and studied at the ateliers of Léon Bonnat and Fernand Cormon, where he befriended Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec and Vincent van Gogh. In 1887 he and Bernard developed Cloisonnism, and Anquetin's painting Avenue de Clichy, cinq heures du soir (1887) was an early exemplar. His Woman at the Moulin Rouge and other scenes of Parisian nightlife showed the influence of Japanese prints and the flattened compositions of Degas.

Later career

In the 1890s Anquetin turned away from avant-garde styles toward a study of the Old Masters, advocating a return to traditional craftsmanship and classical technique; this change of direction cost him his standing with former associates and his reputation faded. He also designed posters and, with Toulouse-Lautrec, theatrical works. Since the late twentieth century his early Cloisonnist paintings have been reassessed as significant contributions to Post-Impressionism. See also Cloisonnism.
