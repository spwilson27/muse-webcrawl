The Pig War was a bloodless confrontation in 1859 between the United States and the British Empire over the boundary between the Colony of Vancouver Island and the United States in the San Juan Islands. It is so called because it was triggered by the shooting of a pig — the only casualty of the confrontation.

Background

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 set the boundary at the 49th parallel and then along "the middle of the channel" separating Vancouver Island from the mainland, but left ambiguous which channel — Haro Strait or Rosario Strait — was intended, and thus who owned the San Juan Islands between them.

The incident

On 15 June 1859, American farmer Lyman Cutlar, who had settled on San Juan Island, shot a large black pig belonging to Irishman Charles Griffin, an employee of the Hudson's Bay Company, after the pig repeatedly rooted in his potato garden. When British authorities threatened to arrest him, Cutlar appealed to the American authorities; Brigadier General William S. Harney dispatched troops under Captain George Pickett to occupy the island. The British responded with warships, and forces on both sides built up to hundreds of men and several ships of war, but orders from both governments forbade firing the first shot.

Resolution

President James Buchanan sent General Winfield Scott to negotiate, and the two sides agreed to a joint military occupation of the island — American Camp on the south and English Camp on the north — which lasted twelve years. In 1871–72 the boundary question was submitted to arbitration by German Emperor Wilhelm I, who ruled in favour of the United States; British forces withdrew in November 1872.

Legacy

The joint occupation camps are preserved today as the San Juan Island National Historical Park, where both the Union Jack and the Stars and Stripes are flown in commemoration of the peaceful resolution.
