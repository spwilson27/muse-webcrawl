Purebred


A purebred Arabian horse, a breed maintained through closed stud books.

A purebred is an animal of a recognized breed whose parents are both members of the same breed, typically with an ancestry recorded in a stud book or registry over several generations. Purebred status is certified by breed associations and pedigree papers. Purebreds are sometimes said to "breed true": the offspring of two purebred parents of the same breed will consistently express the breed's characteristic features.

Breeding practice

Purebred breeding relies on selective breeding within a closed gene pool: breed registries generally refuse to register animals with ancestors outside the breed book. This stabilizes desired traits—such as the speed of the Thoroughbred horse, the milk yield of the Holstein cow, or the appearance standards of kennel-club dog breeds—but it can also concentrate harmful recessive genes and reduce genetic diversity, contributing to inherited disorders in some breeds.

Purebred versus pedigree and crossbred

The term is distinct from pedigreed, which refers to recorded ancestry, and is the opposite of a crossbred (the offspring of parents of two different breeds) or a mixed-breed animal. In horticulture, the analogous concept is a cultivar maintained true to type. Breeders sometimes use "grading up"—repeated crossing to a purebred sire—until stock meets a registry's threshold for purebred registration.

Health and controversy

Concern over inbreeding depression and breed-specific diseases, notably in pedigree dogs, has led to debate about closed registries and calls for outcrossing in some breeds. Documentaries and veterinary studies have highlighted conditions propagated by extreme selection for appearance, prompting some kennel clubs to revise breed standards and health requirements.
