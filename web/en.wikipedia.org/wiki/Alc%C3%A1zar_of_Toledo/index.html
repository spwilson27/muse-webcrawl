Alcázar of Toledo

The Alcázar of Toledo is a stone fortification located in the highest part of Toledo, Spain. Once used as a Roman palace in the 3rd century and later restored under Charles I (Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor) and his son Philip II in the 1540s, it stands prominently above the city and the Tagus River.

History

The site has been fortified since Roman times. A Muslim fortress stood here during the period of al-Andalus, and after Alfonso VI of León and Castile captured Toledo in 1085, the fortress became a Christian stronghold. El Cid is said to have held the fortress. In the 16th century, Charles V rebuilt it as a royal palace, employing architects including Alonso de Covarrubias and Juan de Herrera, with its characteristic courtyard and Imperial staircase.

Spanish Civil War

The Alcázar is famous for the siege of July–September 1936, at the start of the Spanish Civil War, when Nationalist forces under Colonel José Moscardó held the fortress against Republican forces. The dramatic siege became a celebrated episode in Nationalist propaganda, including the famous (and possibly embellished) story of Moscardó's telephone conversation with his captured son. General Franco diverted his advance on Madrid to relieve the Alcázar, a decision of lasting strategic debate. The building was severely damaged during the siege.

Today

Rebuilt after the war, the Alcázar now houses the Museum of the Army (Museo del Ejército), which moved here from Madrid and opened in 2010, and the regional Library of Castile-La Mancha. Its four towers and commanding silhouette remain the symbol of Toledo.
