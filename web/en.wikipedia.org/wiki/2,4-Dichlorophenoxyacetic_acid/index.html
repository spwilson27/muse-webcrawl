2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (usually called 2,4-D) is an organic compound that is one of the most widely used herbicides in the world. It is a selective, systemic herbicide that kills broadleaf weeds without harming most grasses, and works by mimicking the plant growth hormone auxin, causing uncontrolled growth that kills the plant. It is used in agriculture on cereal crops, pastures and turf, and in lawn and garden weedkillers.

History

2,4-D was developed during the Second World War from competing British and American research into plant growth regulators, with Robert Pokorny reporting its synthesis in 1941. Released commercially in 1945–46, it is often called the first successful selective herbicide and helped launch the modern chemical weed-control industry. During the Vietnam War it was one of the two active herbicide ingredients in Agent Orange, the other being 2,4,5-T; the defoliant's notorious health effects were traced mainly to TCDD dioxin contamination of the 2,4,5-T component.

Use and mode of action

2,4-D is applied as various salts and esters in formulations for wheat, maize, rice, sugarcane, lawns, golf courses and roadsides. Absorbed through leaves and roots, it accumulates in growing points and deranges cell division and elongation. Herbicide-resistant genetically modified crops tolerant to 2,4-D (often paired with glyphosate tolerance) have been commercialised since the 2010s.

Safety and controversy

Regulators differ on 2,4-D's hazards. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified it in 2015 as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B), while the US EPA and European assessments, reviewing industry and independent studies, have concluded it is not classifiable as a human carcinogen at exposure levels anticipated from approved uses. It is moderately toxic to aquatic organisms and has been restricted or banned for some uses in several jurisdictions.
