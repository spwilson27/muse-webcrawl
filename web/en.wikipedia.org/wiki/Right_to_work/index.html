Right to work

Right to work
Senses1. Human right to employment (UDHR Art. 23)
2. U.S. laws prohibiting compulsory union fees ("right-to-work laws")
Key instrumentsUniversal Declaration of Human Rights; International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, Art. 6

The right to work is the concept that people have a human right to work, or to engage in productive employment, and should not be prevented from doing so. The phrase is used in two principal senses. In international human-rights law, it denotes the right of everyone to the opportunity to gain a living by work freely chosen or accepted, recognised by Article 23 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 6 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. In the politics of the United States, right-to-work laws instead refer to statutes that prohibit agreements between employers and labour unions requiring workers to pay union fees (agency or "fair share" fees) as a condition of employment.

The human right to work

The idea that the state should guarantee employment gained prominence in the 19th century — Louis Blanc argued for it during France's 1848 revolution — and the short-lived French National Workshops were an early attempt to implement it. In the 20th century the right to work entered constitutional law (for example, the Weimar and several later European constitutions, and socialist constitutions guaranteeing full employment) and international law. The ICESCR obliges states to work toward full employment through technical and vocational guidance, training programmes, and policies of steady economic development. The right to work is related to, but distinct from, the right to just and favourable conditions of work and freedom from forced labour; it does not generally mean that the state must directly employ every jobseeker.

Right-to-work laws in the United States

Section 14(b) of the Taft–Hartley Act (1947) permits U.S. states to ban union-security agreements. States that do so are called "right-to-work states": in them, an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement cannot be required to join the union or pay fees for representation, even though the union remains legally obliged to represent all employees in the bargaining unit (the "free rider" issue). As of the mid-2020s, 26 states have such laws; Michigan repealed its law in 2023, becoming the first state to do so in decades. For public-sector workers nationwide, the Supreme Court's decision in Janus v. AFSCME (2018) held compulsory agency fees unconstitutional under the First Amendment, effectively extending right-to-work principles to all public employees. Supporters argue the laws protect individual freedom of association and attract business; opponents argue they weaken unions and lower wages and benefits for all workers.
