Tribunal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tribunal, in the general sense, is any institution with the authority to judge, adjudicate on, or determine claims or disputes. In modern administrative law it denotes specialised decision-making bodies outside the ordinary court hierarchy — employment tribunals, immigration and asylum chambers, tax and social-security tribunals, professional disciplinary panels — designed for speed, expertise, informality and lower cost in high-volume fields.

Design and oversight

Modern tribunal systems (the UK's unified structure under the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 is a model) group jurisdictions into chambers with legally qualified judges and expert lay members, rights of appeal to upper tribunals and the courts, and procedural rules less formal than courts'. The label "tribunal" is also used for international and exceptional courts — the Nuremberg International Military Tribunal, the ad-hoc UN tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and military commissions — and for investigative or truth-finding bodies (tribunals of inquiry). Critics examine tension between tribunal informality and due process, and the constitutional status of bodies adjudicating rights outside the constitutional judiciary.
