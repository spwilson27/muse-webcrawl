Hula (/ˈhuːlə/) is a Hawaiian dance form accompanied by chant (oli) or song (mele). It was developed in the Hawaiian Islands by the Native Hawaiians who originally settled there. The hula dramatizes or portrays the words of the oli or mele in movements that enact the story, place and genealogy being told; the dance itself is inseparable from the text, which is the primary vehicle of meaning.

There are two main categories: ancient hula, hula kahiko, performed in traditional costume with chant and traditional instruments, and modern hula, hula ʻauana, developed in the 19th and 20th centuries under Western influence, accompanied by song and Western-influenced instruments such as the guitar, ʻukulele and double bass.

Origins and mythology

Hawaiian tradition attributes the origin of hula to the goddess Laka, its patroness, to whom altars (kuahu) in hula schools are dedicated; legends also credit the goddess Kapo and the voyager Laʻamaikahiki with bringing dance from Kahiki (Tahiti). Hula was performed in temples (heiau) as sacred ritual, at court for aliʻi (chiefs), and for narration of cosmogony — the great creation chant Kumulipo is a hula text.

Suppression and revival

After the abolition of the kapu system (1819) and the arrival of Calvinist missionaries (1820), public hula was condemned as immoral; Queen Regent Kaʻahumanu banned public performance in 1830, though it survived privately and in rural areas. King Kalākaua (r. 1874–1891) deliberately revived hula at court — "Hula is the language of the heart, therefore the heartbeat of the Hawaiian people" — and Kamehameha V and later patrons restored public performance. Dance was taught within hālau hula (schools) under a kumu hula (master teacher), with strict protocols of training, ʻūniki (graduation) and spiritual discipline.

Costume and instruments

Kahiko costume traditionally includes kapa (barkcloth) or ti-leaf skirts (pāʻū), kupeʻe (ankle and wrist ornaments) and lei of native materials; ʻauana dancers wear fabric skirts or cellophane and floral lei. Instruments include the ipu (gourd drum), pahu (sharkskin drum), ʻiliʻili (water-worn stone castanets), pūʻili (split bamboo rattles), ʻulīʻulī (feathered gourd rattles) and kālaʻau (rhythm sticks).

Modern hula

The Merrie Monarch Festival in Hilo, founded in 1963 and holding competitions since 1971, is the premier showcase of hula kahiko and ʻauana, credited with driving the Hawaiian Renaissance interest in traditional form. Hula is now taught worldwide, with large schools in Japan and the US mainland, and hālau compete in events at home and abroad. In 2025-era popular culture, hula remains a defining symbol of Native Hawaiian identity — and a subject of debate about cultural appropriation in tourism entertainment.
