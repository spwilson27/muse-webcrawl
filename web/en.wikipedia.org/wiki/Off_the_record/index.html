Off the record

Off the record is a term used in journalism and related fields for information provided by a source that is not intended for publication or attribution. Information given "off the record" may guide a journalist's further research but, under the conventional understanding, may not be quoted or reported directly. The term is also used more loosely in business, law, and everyday conversation for remarks not meant to be documented or repeated.

Journalistic usage

Journalistic practice distinguishes several levels of attribution:

  • On the record — the information may be published and attributed to the source by name.
  • On background (not for attribution) — the information may be published but attributed only generally, e.g. "a senior official said". The related "deep background" convention, associated with the Watergate reporting of Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, permits use of the information without any attribution at all.
  • Off the record — the information is for the journalist's knowledge only and is not to be published.

Definitions vary between countries, news organisations, and political cultures, so journalists are advised to clarify terms with sources before an interview. A statement cannot normally be made off the record retroactively once given on the record.

Other usages

In legal proceedings, remarks made "off the record" are excluded from the official transcript. In diplomacy and politics, backchannel and off-the-record briefings allow officials to explore positions without public commitment. Disputes over the term — for example when a politician's off-the-record comment is reported — are a recurring source of controversy.
