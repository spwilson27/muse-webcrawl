Eugene Victor Debs (November 5, 1855 – October 20, 1926) was an American socialist, political activist and trade union leader, one of the founding members of the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW) and five times the candidate of the Socialist Party of America for President of the United States. Through his campaigns — culminating in 6.0 percent of the popular vote in 1912 and nearly a million votes in 1920, cast while he was imprisoned — Debs became the best-known socialist figure in American history.

Early life and union career

Born in Terre Haute, Indiana, to Alsatian immigrant parents, Debs left school at fourteen to work on the railroads. He rose through the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, becoming editor of its magazine and then its grand secretary. Concluding that craft unions were too narrow, he founded the American Railway Union (ARU) in 1893, one of the first industrial unions in the United States, which won a major strike against the Great Northern Railway in 1894.

Pullman Strike and imprisonment

Later in 1894 the ARU supported the Pullman Strike with a nationwide boycott of trains handling Pullman cars. The federal government broke the strike with an injunction and troops; Debs was jailed for six months at Woodstock for contempt of court. In prison he read Marx and other socialist literature and emerged a committed socialist, having entered, as he later put it, as a trade unionist. The ARU did not survive the defeat.

Socialist leader

Debs helped found the Social Democratic Party and then the Socialist Party of America (1901), and ran for president in 1900, 1904, 1908, 1912 and 1920. His 1912 vote of about 900,000 (6.0 percent) remains the highest share ever won by a Socialist Party candidate. A celebrated orator, he campaigned tirelessly for public ownership, labour rights, women's suffrage and against militarism.

Anti-war stance and second imprisonment

Debs denounced American entry into the First World War as a capitalist war. In a speech at Canton, Ohio, on 16 June 1918 he urged resistance to conscription; he was prosecuted under the Espionage Act, convicted, and sentenced to ten years in federal prison. In 1920 he ran for president from the Atlanta penitentiary as "Convict No. 9653" and won over 900,000 votes. President Warren G. Harding commuted his sentence and released him on Christmas Day 1921. His citizenship, stripped at conviction, was restored posthumously in 1976.

Death and legacy

Debs's health was broken by imprisonment. He died in 1926 at Elmhurst, Illinois. His home in Terre Haute is a National Historic Landmark, and he remains the emblematic figure of American democratic socialism — praised by later labour and progressive movements as a moral witness against war and inequality.
