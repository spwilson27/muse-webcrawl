Social movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A social movement is a loosely organized effort by a large group of people to achieve a particular goal, typically a social or political one — carrying out, resisting or undoing a social change. Movements are distinguished from crowds and organisations by sustained campaigns of claim-making through public performances (meetings, marches, petitions, media events), networks of groups and activists, and shared collective identities and frames.

Fields of study

The classic movements were labour and nationalist; the 20th century added civil rights, women's, anti-colonial, student, peace and environmental movements; the "new social movements" literature (Touraine, Melucci) read post-1968 mobilisations as struggles over identity and cultural codes rather than material redistribution alone. Theory has converged on contentious politics: movements exploit political opportunities, mobilise resources and networks, and frame grievances ("injustice frames", diagnostic and prognostic claims) — cycles of protest diffusing tactics across borders (1968; the 2011 wave from Tahrir to Occupy; Fridays for Future; #MeToo's hashtag-era form). Digital media lowered organising costs and raised new vulnerabilities (leaderless movements' tactical freeze, surveillance, platform dependence). Movements are democracy's research-and-development wing and its pressure valve; they are also, occasionally, its arsonists — the literature's neutrality about ends being itself a contested stance.
