Energy transition

Wind and solar are the fastest-growing energy sources

The energy transition is the ongoing global shift of the energy system away from fossil fuels toward low- and zero-carbon sources, driven by climate change mitigation, falling technology costs, air-pollution concerns and energy security. It encompasses the rapid growth of renewable energy (especially solar and wind), the electrification of transport, heating and industry, improvements in energy efficiency, and the decline of coal, with natural gas's role contested between "bridge fuel" and lock-in risk.

History

Energy systems have transitioned before — from biomass to coal in the 19th century and the rise of oil and gas in the 20th — but those transitions were additions layered over growing demand rather than deliberate replacements. The modern transition is a policy-driven project, framed by the Paris Agreement's goal of limiting warming to well below 2 °C, and by net-zero pledges adopted by most major economies. The German Energiewende is often cited as a pioneering national programme.

State of progress

Solar photovoltaics have become the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world, and annual renewable capacity additions have set repeated records — exceeding 500 GW globally in 2023. Electric vehicles passed a fifth of new car sales in several major markets. Nevertheless, fossil fuels still supply roughly 80 percent of total primary energy, and emissions have remained near record levels, making the required pace of change historically unprecedented. Key challenges include grid integration and storage, permitting, mineral supply chains, financing in developing countries, and managing the social effects on fossil-fuel workers and regions (the "just transition").

Scenarios

The International Energy Agency's Net Zero Emissions by 2050 scenario and IPCC pathways describe the required trajectory: peaking fossil demand this decade, tripling renewable capacity by 2030 (a goal adopted at COP28 in 2023), and deep cuts in methane emissions, alongside contested roles for nuclear power, hydrogen, and carbon capture and storage.
