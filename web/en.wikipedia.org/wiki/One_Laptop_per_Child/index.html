One Laptop per Child

One Laptop per Child (OLPC) was a non-profit initiative founded in 2005 with the goal of transforming education for children in the developing world by providing low-cost, rugged, connected laptops to governments for distribution to schoolchildren.

The XO laptop

The project's machine, the XO-1, was designed by Yves Béhar's fuseproject and developed with the MIT Media Lab. It featured a distinctive green-and-white rubberized case, a dual-mode display readable in sunlight, very low power consumption, mesh networking capability, and a hand-crank or solar charging option in early concepts. It ran the Sugar learning environment on Linux rather than a conventional operating system.

People and deployment

OLPC was chaired by Nicholas Negroponte, co-founder of the MIT Media Lab, and drew on Seymour Papert's constructionist learning philosophy. Large deployments took place in countries including Uruguay (Plan Ceibal, the first nationwide one-to-one program), Peru, Rwanda, and Paraguay, with millions of laptops distributed in total. Production partners included Quanta Computer.

Criticism and legacy

The project was criticized for cost overruns relative to the "$100 laptop" ambition, for limited evidence of learning outcomes, and for competing with commercial low-cost computers. Nevertheless, it influenced the netbook market and one-to-one computing programs worldwide. The OLPC foundation wound down its original operations in the mid-2010s, while Sugar Labs and national programs continued aspects of the work.
