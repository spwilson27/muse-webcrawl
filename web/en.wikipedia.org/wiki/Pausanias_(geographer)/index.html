Pausanias (Ancient Greek: Παυσανίας; c. 110 – c. 180 AD) was a Greek traveller and geographer of the second century AD. He is famous for his work Description of Greece (Ἑλλάδος Περιήγησις, Hellados Periegesis), a lengthy work that describes ancient Greece from his own observations. The work provides invaluable information to modern historians, archaeologists, and art historians about the topography, monuments, art, religion, and mythology of Greece in the Roman period, and has repeatedly guided excavators to buried sites.

Life

Almost nothing is known of Pausanias's life. He was probably born in Lydia in Asia Minor and travelled extensively: besides mainland Greece, his writings show knowledge of Asia Minor, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Italy, including Rome. He wrote during the reigns of the Antonine emperors, and the Description appears to have been composed over a long period, roughly between the 150s and the 170s AD.

Description of Greece

The Description of Greece is organised in ten books, moving region by region — Attica, Corinth, Laconia, Messenia, Elis (with Olympia), Achaea, Arcadia, Boeotia, and Phocis (with Delphi). In each district Pausanias combines topography with myth and history, describing temples, statues, paintings, cult practices, and local legends, often with careful, eyewitness detail. His account of Olympia guided the German excavations that uncovered the sanctuary from 1875, and his identification of artworks and tombs — such as the tombs at Mycenae — has repeatedly proved substantially accurate.

Reception

Pausanias's work was little cited in antiquity and narrowly survived the Middle Ages, descending from a single manuscript tradition. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries scholars such as James Frazer, who produced a celebrated six-volume translation and commentary (1898), came to recognise the essential reliability of his testimony. Today the Description is a foundational source for the study of Greek religion, art, and landscape.
