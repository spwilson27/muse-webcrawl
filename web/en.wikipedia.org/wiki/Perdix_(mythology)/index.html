Perdix (Ancient Greek: Πέρδιξ, "partridge") was, in Greek mythology, the nephew and apprentice of the craftsman Daedalus of Athens. A boy of remarkable inventive talent, Perdix is credited in the myth with the invention of the saw and the compass. His uncle, fearing that the pupil would surpass the master, threw him from the Acropolis, but the goddess Athena, patroness of crafts, caught him in mid-fall and transformed him into a partridge.

Myth

Perdix was the son of Polycaste, the sister of Daedalus. As a youth he was placed in his uncle's workshop as an apprentice. According to Ovid's Metamorphoses, the boy found the spine of a fish (in some versions the jawbone of a serpent) on the seashore and, imitating its teeth in iron, invented the saw. He also joined two pieces of iron at one end with a rivet so that, spreading them apart, he could describe a circle — the first compass, or pair of dividers.

Daedalus, envious of his nephew's accomplishments and afraid of being eclipsed, pushed the boy from the top of the Athenian Acropolis. But Athena, who favours ingenuity, caught Perdix as he fell and changed him into the bird that bears his name, the partridge. Ovid adds that the partridge, remembering its fall, never flies high and builds its nest in hedges rather than lofty places.

Aftermath

The murder was said to be the crime for which Daedalus was tried before the Areopagus and exiled from Athens, eventually fleeing to Crete, where he built the Labyrinth for King Minos. The partridge reappears at the end of the Daedalus story: when Icarus fell into the sea, a partridge was seen to clap its wings in delight — the transformed Perdix rejoicing at the death of his cousin, the son of his murderer.
