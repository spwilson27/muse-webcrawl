Gokstad ship

The Gokstad ship is a 9th-century Viking ship found in a large burial mound at Gokstad farm in Sandefjord, Vestfold, Norway. Excavated in 1880, it is one of the best-preserved Viking Age vessels and, together with the grave goods buried with it, provides much of what is known about Norse shipbuilding and ship burial. The ship is displayed at the Viking Ship House (now part of the Museum of the Viking Age) at Bygdøy, Oslo.

Construction

The ship is built of oak in the clinker (overlapping strake) style, with sixteen strakes per side, a single mast carrying a square sail of about 110 square metres, and a side rudder. Along the gunwale hung 32 round shields, painted alternately yellow and black. Despite its size it is a relatively light, flexible vessel capable of roughly eleven knots under sail.

The burial

The ship was buried around 901 in a mound some 44 metres across and 5 metres high, its prow pointing seaward. Inside a wooden burial chamber lay the skeleton of a powerfully built man of about 40–50 years, possibly — though unproven — identified in tradition with Olaf Geirstad-Alf. Grave goods included three small boats, beds, a sledge, horses, dogs and gaming pieces; much portable wealth had been removed by grave robbers in antiquity. Twelve horses and six dogs were buried with the ship.

Replicas

A full-scale replica, Draken Harald Hårfagre and several others, have drawn on the Gokstad design. In 1893 a Gokstad replica named Viking crossed the Atlantic from Bergen to the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, demonstrating the type's seaworthiness.
