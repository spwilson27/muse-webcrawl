Ash'ari

Ash'arism (Arabic: الأشعرية, al-Ashʿariyya) is a school of Sunni Islamic theology founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (873–935 CE) in Basra. It became, alongside Maturidism, one of the two dominant orthodox schools of kalām (speculative theology), and was historically the most widespread theological tradition within Sunni Islam.

History

Al-Ash'ari was originally a follower of the rationalist Mu'tazila school but broke with it around 912 CE, advocating a middle path between Mu'tazilite rationalism and Hanbali literalism. His works, including Kitāb al-Lumaʿ ("The Book of Luminous Highlights"), defended the use of dialectical reasoning while affirming the traditional attributes of God. Later figures such as al-Baqillani, al-Juwayni, and above all al-Ghazali developed and spread the school, and it was adopted widely in Shafi'i and Maliki legal circles.

Doctrine

Ash'aris hold that God's attributes mentioned in the Qur'an are real but unlike created attributes, to be affirmed "without asking how" (bilā kayf). The school teaches occasionalism — that God creates every event directly — and the doctrine of kasb (acquisition), holding that humans acquire acts created by God, preserving both divine omnipotence and human accountability. On the createdness of the Qur'an, Ash'aris distinguish God's eternal speech from its physical recitation.

Legacy

Ash'arism dominated much of the Sunni world for centuries through institutions such as al-Azhar in Cairo, though in the modern period it has been challenged by Salafi movements and Hanbali literalist theology.
