Health effects of tobacco

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The health effects of tobacco are the physiological and pathological consequences of tobacco use, principally cigarette smoking. Tobacco use is the leading preventable cause of death worldwide: the World Health Organization estimates that tobacco kills more than 8 million people each year, including around 1.3 million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke. About half of people who smoke and do not quit will die of a tobacco-related disease, losing on average at least ten years of life.

Cancer

Tobacco smoke contains more than 7,000 chemicals, of which at least 70 are known carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, aromatic amines and tobacco-specific nitrosamines. Smoking is the dominant cause of lung cancer and also causes cancers of the mouth, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, bladder, pancreas, kidney, liver, stomach, cervix, colon and blood (acute myeloid leukaemia). Smokeless tobacco also causes oral, oesophageal and pancreatic cancers.

Cardiovascular and respiratory disease

Smoking damages blood vessels, promotes atherosclerosis and thrombosis, and greatly increases the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke and peripheral artery disease. It is the principal cause of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including chronic bronchitis and emphysema, and worsens asthma and respiratory infections.

Other effects

Tobacco use contributes to type 2 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, reduced fertility, erectile dysfunction, adverse pregnancy outcomes (miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth and low birth weight), sudden infant death syndrome, age-related macular degeneration, cataracts, periodontal disease and delayed wound healing. Nicotine is highly addictive, acting on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the brain's reward pathways.

Second-hand and third-hand smoke

Involuntary exposure to tobacco smoke increases non-smokers' risk of lung cancer and heart disease and harms children, raising rates of respiratory infections, ear disease and asthma attacks. There is no risk-free level of exposure to second-hand smoke.

Benefits of cessation

Quitting smoking reduces risks at any age: excess cardiovascular risk falls substantially within a few years, and quitting before about age 40 avoids roughly 90% of the excess mortality of continued smoking. Most countries regulate tobacco through taxation, advertising bans, smoke-free laws, packaging warnings and cessation services under the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control.
