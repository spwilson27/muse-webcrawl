Motion estimation

Motion estimation is the process of determining the motion of image content between two or more frames of a video sequence, typically expressed as two-dimensional motion vectors that describe how blocks of pixels (or individual pixels) have moved from one frame to the next. It underpins video compression, and is also used in frame-rate conversion, video stabilisation, object tracking, and computer vision.

Role in video compression

Successive video frames are usually very similar. Modern codecs (MPEG-2, H.264/AVC, HEVC, AV1) exploit this redundancy through inter prediction: for each block in the current frame, the encoder searches a reference frame for the best-matching block and transmits only the motion vector plus the small residual difference. The search is the most computationally expensive part of encoding. Strategies include:

  • Full search (exhaustive) — tests every candidate position within the search window; optimal by the chosen distortion measure but costly.
  • Fast search patterns — diamond, hexagon, and three-step searches that test a small set of candidate points and iteratively refine.
  • Hierarchical and predictive methods — coarse-to-fine searches and the use of neighbouring or co-located vectors as predictors.

Match quality is measured by the sum of absolute differences (SAD) or squared differences (SSD) between blocks, often with a rate term added so that cheap vectors are preferred. Sub-pixel accuracy — interpolating reference frames to half- and quarter-pixel positions — further reduces the residual.

Dense motion estimation

In computer vision, motion estimation often means computing a motion vector for every pixel, a field known as optical flow. Classical methods such as Lucas–Kanade (local window methods) and Horn–Schunck (global smoothness methods) formalise assumptions of brightness constancy and spatial coherence. Deep-learning approaches such as FlowNet and RAFT now dominate optical-flow benchmarks.

Applications

Beyond compression, motion estimation enables motion-compensated deinterlacing, frame interpolation for slow motion and refresh-rate conversion, electronic image stabilisation, video surveillance tracking, autonomous-vehicle scene analysis, and medical image registration.
