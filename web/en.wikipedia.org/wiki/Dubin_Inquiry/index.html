Dubin Inquiry

The Dubin Inquiry, formally the Commission of Inquiry into the Use of Drugs and Banned Practices Intended to Increase Athletic Performance, was a Canadian government inquiry established in 1988 after sprinter Ben Johnson tested positive for the anabolic steroid stanozolol at the 1988 Summer Olympics in Seoul and was stripped of his gold medal in the 100 metres. The inquiry was chaired by Ontario Chief Justice Charles Dubin.

Background

Johnson's positive test on 24 September 1988, days after he had won the Olympic 100 metres final in a world record 9.79 seconds, caused a national scandal in Canada. The federal government, under Minister of State for Fitness and Amateur Sport Jean Charest, ordered a public inquiry into drug use in Canadian sport. Hearings began in January 1989 in Toronto and lasted some 91 days, with testimony from 119 witnesses.

Testimony

The inquiry heard extensive evidence about the prevalence of performance-enhancing drugs in athletics. Johnson admitted to long-term steroid use, as did his coach Charlie Francis and physician Jamie Astaphan; fellow sprinters including Angella Issajenko also testified about systematic doping within Francis's training group. Testimony extended beyond track and field to weightlifting, football, and other sports, and examined the role of Sport Canada and the sports federations in funding and oversight.

Report and recommendations

Dubin's report, released in June 1990, concluded that the failure of many sports governing bodies to control doping amounted to a moral failure of Canadian sport, and famously attributed the crisis to a "loss of innocence" in athletics. The report made 70 recommendations, including independent drug testing, an arms-length anti-doping agency, and withholding government funding from organizations that failed to enforce anti-doping rules. The inquiry led directly to the creation of the Canadian Centre for Drug-free Sport in 1991, a predecessor of the Canadian Centre for Ethics in Sport (CCES), and influenced anti-doping policy internationally in the run-up to the creation of the World Anti-Doping Agency in 1999.
