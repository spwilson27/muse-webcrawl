The Fenian raids were a series of incursions carried out by the Fenian Brotherhood, an Irish republican organization based in the United States, against military fortifications, customs houses, and other targets in British North America (now Canada) between 1866 and 1871. The Fenians — many of them veterans of the American Civil War — hoped to seize Canadian territory and hold it as leverage to win Ireland's independence from Britain.

Background

The Fenian Brotherhood was founded in New York in 1858 as the American counterpart of the Irish Republican Brotherhood. After the Civil War ended in 1865, thousands of Irish-American soldiers demobilized with military experience and sympathy for the cause. One Fenian faction, led by William R. Roberts, favored attacking Canada as a pressure point against Britain. The United States government, initially tolerant, eventually moved to suppress the raids under its neutrality laws, though enforcement was uneven.

The raids of 1866

Campobello Island

In April 1866 a Fenian force under John O'Mahony attempted to seize Campobello Island, New Brunswick, but dispersed when British warships and American authorities intervened.

Battle of Ridgeway

The most serious incursion began on the night of 31 May – 1 June 1866, when about 800 Fenians under John O'Neill crossed the Niagara River near Buffalo and occupied Fort Erie. On 2 June they met a column of Canadian militia — largely inexperienced volunteers, including the Queen's Own Rifles of Toronto — at the Battle of Ridgeway. The Canadians were driven back with nine killed, in part after a mistaken alarm about cavalry caused a disastrous attempt to form square. The Fenians, short of supplies and facing converging British and Canadian forces and American interdiction of reinforcements, withdrew across the river that night. A second landing near Pigeon Hill, Quebec, on 7 June also fizzled out.

Later raids

In 1870 O'Neill led another attempt into Quebec, defeated at the Battle of Eccles Hill (25 May 1870) by Canadian militia and home guards who, forewarned, held prepared positions. A final raid in 1871 targeted Manitoba near the Red River settlement but was broken up by American troops. The raids thereafter ceased as the movement fractured and U.S. enforcement hardened.

Consequences

Although militarily unsuccessful, the raids had significant political effects. They discredited the idea that Canada could be defended colony by colony, strengthened the case for uniting the British North American colonies, and are generally considered a contributing factor in Canadian Confederation in 1867. The raids also prompted lasting improvements to the Canadian militia and spurred the award of the Canada General Service Medal to veterans.
