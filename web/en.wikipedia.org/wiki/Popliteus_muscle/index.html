Popliteus muscle

The popliteus muscle is a small, thin, triangular muscle located at the back of the knee joint, forming the floor of the popliteal fossa. It is an important stabiliser of the knee, responsible for "unlocking" the joint at the start of flexion and for controlling rotation of the tibia on the femur.

Structure

The popliteus arises by a strong tendon from the lateral condyle of the femur and the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus. The tendon passes downward and medially, beneath the lateral collateral ligament and the tendon of biceps femoris, and expands into a fleshy belly that inserts on the posterior surface of the tibia above the soleal line. Its tendon lies within the capsule of the knee joint, making the muscle intracapsular but extrasynovial.

Nerve supply

The popliteus is supplied by the popliteal branches of the tibial nerve, from the spinal segments L4 to S1.

Function

The popliteus laterally rotates the femur on the tibia (or, when the foot is planted, medially rotates the tibia) to unlock the fully extended knee so that flexion can begin. It also helps to stabilise the knee during standing and walking, draws the lateral meniscus posteriorly during flexion, and resists excessive external rotation of the tibia. For these reasons it is sometimes described as the "key to the knee".

Clinical significance

Injury to the popliteus, including tendinopathy and tears of its tendon, can cause posterolateral knee pain and instability. It is a component of the posterolateral corner of the knee, injuries to which are often associated with damage to the cruciate ligaments.
