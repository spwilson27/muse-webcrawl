The Antarctic Muon And Neutrino Detector Array (AMANDA) was a neutrino telescope located beneath the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station in Antarctica. It consisted of strings of optical modules — photomultiplier tubes housed in glass spheres — frozen into the polar ice cap at depths of about 1,500 to 2,000 metres, where the ice is exceptionally transparent. When a high-energy neutrino occasionally interacted in or near the array, the resulting muon produced Cherenkov light that the modules recorded, allowing the neutrino's direction and energy to be reconstructed.

History and operation

Deployment began in the mid-1990s, first at shallower depths where air bubbles in the ice scattered light more than expected, then deeper where the ice proved suitable. The completed detector comprised 677 optical modules on 19 strings. AMANDA operated from 1996 to 2009, producing limits and searches for astrophysical neutrino sources, dark matter annihilation in the Sun, and other phenomena, and demonstrating that Antarctic ice was an excellent detector medium.

Legacy

AMANDA served as the prototype and proof of concept for the much larger IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a cubic-kilometre array completed in 2010, into which AMANDA was incorporated as a denser sub-array before being decommissioned. IceCube went on to detect the first high-energy astrophysical neutrinos in 2013.
