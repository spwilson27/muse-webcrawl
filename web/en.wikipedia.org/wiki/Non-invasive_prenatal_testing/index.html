Non-invasive prenatal testing

Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), also called non-invasive prenatal screening or cell-free DNA screening, is a method of screening for certain chromosomal abnormalities in a fetus using a blood sample from the pregnant woman. It analyses cell-free DNA shed from the placenta into the maternal circulation, and can be performed from about the 10th week of pregnancy.

Performance

NIPT detects Down syndrome (trisomy 21) with sensitivity and specificity above 99 per cent in validation studies — far better than earlier maternal serum screening — and also screens for Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18), Patau syndrome (trisomy 13) and some sex chromosome abnormalities. It remains a screening test, not a diagnosis: positive results are usually confirmed by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, and accuracy is lower in low-risk populations and for rarer microdeletion panels.

History and issues

The presence of fetal DNA in maternal blood was demonstrated by Dennis Lo and colleagues in 1997; commercial tests launched in 2011. Widespread use has sharply reduced invasive procedures (and their miscarriage risk), while raising ethical debate about screening scope, incidental maternal findings such as occult malignancy, and use for fetal sex selection.
