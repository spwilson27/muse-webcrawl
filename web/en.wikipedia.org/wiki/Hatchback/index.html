Hatchback

A hatchback is a car body style with a rear door — the hatch — that swings upward to provide access to a cargo area shared with the passenger compartment. Hatchbacks may feature fold-down second-row seating, in which the interior can be reconfigured to prioritise passenger or cargo volume. The body style has been marketed worldwide on cars ranging from superminis to executive cars, and is the dominant form in much of Europe.

Characteristics

Hatchbacks are typically described as a two-box design: one box for the engine and one for the combined passenger and cargo volume, in contrast to the three-box layout of a sedan. Because the hatch opening includes the rear window, hatchbacks are conventionally counted as three-door or five-door models, the hatch itself being counted as a door. Their steeply raked or near-vertical rear ends distinguish them from station wagons, which have a longer roofline over an expanded cargo bay.

History

Early examples predate the Second World War, but the modern hatchback emerged in the 1960s and 1970s: the Renault 4 (1961) and Renault 16 (1965) pioneered the format in Europe, the Austin Maxi (1969) applied it in Britain, and cars such as the Volkswagen Golf (1974), Honda Civic and AMC Gremlin spread it worldwide. "Hot hatches" — high-performance versions such as the Golf GTI (1976) — became a genre of their own. In the United States the hatchback's popularity waned in the 1990s but revived with compact crossovers and five-door models in the 2000s.

Comparison with related styles

A liftback is a hatchback with a more gently sloping rear, resembling a fastback sedan; marketing terminology varies by maker and market. SUVs and crossovers frequently use the same upward-opening rear door, but their raised ride height and body forms place them in separate classes.
