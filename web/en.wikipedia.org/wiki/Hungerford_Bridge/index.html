The Hungerford Bridge crosses the River Thames in London, between Waterloo Bridge and Westminster Bridge. It is a steel truss railway bridge — sometimes known as the Charing Cross Bridge — flanked by two cable-stayed pedestrian and cycle bridges, the Golden Jubilee Bridges. It carries trains of the South Eastern Main Line into Charing Cross station.

The railway bridge, flanked by the two Golden Jubilee footbridges.

History

The first Hungerford Bridge, a suspension footbridge designed by Isambard Kingdom Brunel, opened in 1845 and led to the Hungerford Market on the north bank. When the market site was acquired for Charing Cross station, the bridge was demolished; its chains were reused in the Clifton Suspension Bridge at Bristol. The replacement railway bridge, designed by Sir John Hawkshaw, opened in 1864 with nine tracks, supported on the piers of Brunel's bridge. A walkway on the downstream side was long notorious for its narrowness and poor condition.

Golden Jubilee Bridges

In 2002 two new cable-stayed footbridges, designed by architects Lifschutz Davidson with engineers WSP, were built on either side of the railway bridge and named the Golden Jubilee Bridges for the Golden Jubilee of Elizabeth II that year. Their pylons lean outward to avoid the railway bridge foundations and a Bakerloo line tunnel below the riverbed. The bridges won several design awards and greatly improved the pedestrian link between the South Bank and the Embankment.
