Langerhans cell histiocytosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical details
Specialty:Haematology, oncology
Symptoms:Bone lesions, skin rash, diabetes insipidus, organ involvement (variable)
Frequency:about 1–2 per 100,000; most common in children aged 1–3
ICD-10:C96.0–C96.6

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare disorder characterised by the abnormal clonal proliferation of Langerhans cells — dendritic antigen-presenting cells normally found in the skin — together with an inflammatory infiltrate. It can affect a single bone or skin site or become a disseminated, life-threatening multi-system disease. Historically it was described under names including histiocytosis X, eosinophilic granuloma, Hand–Schüller–Christian disease and Letterer–Siwe disease, now understood as forms of the same condition.

Signs and pathology

Bone involvement is most common, producing painful lytic lesions, especially in the skull, ribs and long bones. Skin disease may resemble stubborn seborrhoeic dermatitis in infants. Infiltration of the pituitary stalk frequently causes diabetes insipidus, and extensive disease can involve the liver, spleen, lungs and bone marrow. Diagnosis rests on biopsy: Langerhans cells express CD1a and langerin (CD207) and contain characteristic Birbeck granules on electron microscopy. A majority of cases carry the BRAF-V600E mutation, and most others have activating mutations elsewhere in the MAPK pathway, establishing LCH as a myeloid neoplasm with inflammatory features.

Treatment

Single lesions may regress spontaneously or after curettage or intralesional steroid. Multi-system disease is treated with chemotherapy, classically vinblastine with prednisone, with cladribine or cytarabine for refractory cases. Targeted BRAF and MEK inhibitors have shown high response rates in mutation-positive relapsed disease. Prognosis depends chiefly on involvement of "risk organs" (liver, spleen and bone marrow) and early treatment response.
