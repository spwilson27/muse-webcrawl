Compact fluorescent lamp

A compact fluorescent lamp (CFL), also called a compact fluorescent light or energy-saving light, is a fluorescent lamp designed to replace incandescent light bulbs; some types fit into light fixtures formerly used for incandescent lamps. The lamps use a tube that is curved or folded to fit into the space of an incandescent bulb, and a compact electronic ballast in the base.

Principle

An electric current in the gas excites mercury vapour, producing ultraviolet light that causes a phosphor coating inside the tube to fluoresce and emit visible light. Compared with incandescent lamps of similar output, CFLs use roughly one-quarter to one-third the power and last several times longer.

History

Invented in forms by Edward Hammer at General Electric in the 1970s in response to the energy crisis, CFLs became widely affordable in the 1990s and 2000s and were promoted by energy-efficiency regulations phasing out incandescent bulbs. Since the 2010s they have increasingly been displaced by LED lamps, which are more efficient and mercury-free.

Issues

CFLs contain a small amount of mercury, requiring careful disposal and cleanup if broken. Early models were criticised for slow warm-up, poor colour rendering, incompatibility with dimmers, and reduced life when switched frequently.
