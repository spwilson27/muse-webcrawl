Francophonie

The term Francophonie (often rendered in English as "the Francophonie" or "the French-speaking world") designates the community of peoples and countries that use French as a first, official or widely spoken language. By extension it also refers to the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF), the international organisation of French-speaking states founded in 1970, which has 88 member states and governments and represents over 300 million French speakers worldwide.

The French-speaking world: French is an official language across five continents

The concept

The word was coined in the 1880s by the French geographer Onésime Reclus to describe the global spread of the French language through colonisation. It was revived after decolonisation by leaders such as Léopold Sédar Senghor of Senegal, Habib Bourguiba of Tunisia and Hamani Diori of Niger, who imagined a community of independent states united by a shared language and cultural exchange.

The organisation

The Agence de coopération culturelle et technique, founded at Niamey in 1970, evolved into the OIF. It holds summits of heads of state and government every two years, promotes the French language and cultural and linguistic diversity, and works in education, sustainable development and the defence of democracy and human rights. Its Secretary-General since 2019 is the Rwandan diplomat Louise Mushikiwabo.

French in the world

French is an official language in 29 countries, is spoken on all inhabited continents, and is one of the six official languages of the United Nations. The largest French-speaking populations are in France, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Canada, Cameroon, Belgium, Ivory Coast and Haiti. Demographic projections suggest Africa will account for the great majority of the world's French speakers in the twenty-first century.
