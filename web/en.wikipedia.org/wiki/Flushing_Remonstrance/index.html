Flushing Remonstrance
DateDecember 27, 1657
LocationFlushing, New Netherland
Signatories30 residents of Flushing

The Flushing Remonstrance was a 1657 petition to Peter Stuyvesant, the Director General of the Dutch colony of New Netherland, in which several citizens (the petition was signed by 30 individuals) requested an exemption to his ban on Quaker worship. It is considered a precursor to the United States Constitution's provision on freedom of religion in the Bill of Rights.

The Remonstrance was motivated by the suffering of Quakers at the hands of the official Dutch Reformed church in the colony; the English settlers of Flushing objected to the persecution of a religious minority by their government. Although the signers were not Quakers themselves, they argued on grounds of conscience and of the town's charter of 1645, which had promised "liberty of conscience."

Background

Flushing was founded in 1645 by English settlers under a patent from the Dutch West India Company, granted by Director Willem Kieft, which gave the inhabitants liberty of conscience. Stuyvesant, director general from 1647, enforced religious conformity with the Dutch Reformed Church and issued ordinances against unauthorized religious meetings. In 1656 he prohibited gatherings of Lutherans, Quakers, and other dissenters, imposing heavy fines on those who harbored them. Quakers arriving in the colony in 1657 were imprisoned, fined, or banished; Robert Hodgson was tortured and sentenced to hard labour for preaching in the colony, and John Bowne would later be arrested in 1662 for holding Quaker meetings in his house at Flushing.

In response to an ordinance against entertaining Quakers, a group of townspeople gathered at the house of Michael Milner on December 27, 1657, and adopted the remonstrance, which was drafted and signed by town clerk Edward Hart and presented to Stuyvesant by Hart, Tobias Feake (the town schout), and others.

Text

The document argued that the signers could not in conscience enforce persecution against people of other faiths:

"The law of love, peace and liberty in the states extending to Jews, Turks and Egyptians, as they are considered sons of Adam, which is the glory of the outward state of Holland, so love, peace and liberty, extending to all in Christ Jesus, condemns hatred, war and bondage..."

"Therefore if any of these said persons come in love unto us, we cannot in conscience lay violent hands upon them, but give them free egress and regress unto our Town, and houses, as God shall persuade our consciences, for we are bound by the law of God and man to do good unto all men and evil to no man."

Aftermath

Stuyvesant responded by arresting the signatories and town officials: Feake was imprisoned and fined, Hart was banished from office, and the town was forced to retract. Persecution of Quakers continued until John Bowne's appeal to the Dutch West India Company resulted in a 1663 letter ordering Stuyvesant to allow "everyone to have his own belief," invoking the religious toleration practised in the Netherlands itself.

Legacy

The Flushing Remonstrance is commemorated as an early American statement of religious freedom and freedom of conscience. The original document is held by the New York State Archives. Its 350th anniversary in 2007 was marked by commemorations in Flushing, Queens, and the document is often read alongside Bowne's appeal as a founding text of American religious liberty.
