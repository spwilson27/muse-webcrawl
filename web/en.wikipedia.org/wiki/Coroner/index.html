Coroner

A coroner is a government or judicial official who is empowered to conduct or order an inquest into the manner or cause of death, and to investigate or confirm the identity of an unknown person who has been found dead within the coroner's jurisdiction. In medieval times, English coroners were crown officials who held financial powers and conducted some judicial investigations in order to counterbalance the power of sheriffs or bailiffs.

Depending on the jurisdiction, the coroner may adjudge the cause of death personally, or may act as the presiding officer of a special court (a "coroner's jury"). The term coroner derives from the same source as the word crown, and denotes an officer of the Crown.

History

The office of coroner originates in medieval England. The office was probably established during the reign of Richard I; the Articles of Eyre of 1194 required that in every county three knights and one clerk be elected as custodes placitorum coronae (keepers of the pleas of the Crown), from which the word "coroner" derives. The coroner's early duties were primarily fiscal: recording forfeitures, treasure trove, and fines owed to the Crown, alongside inquiring into unnatural deaths.

Duties

The coroner investigates deaths that are sudden, violent, suspicious, unexplained, or occur in custody or state detention. Duties typically include confirming identity, determining the cause and manner of death, ordering post-mortem examinations (autopsies), issuing death certificates, and holding inquests. A coroner's inquest is a fact-finding inquiry, not a trial, and generally may not assign criminal blame.

Coroner vs. medical examiner

In the United States, death investigation systems vary by state and county. Coroners are often elected officials and in some jurisdictions are not required to be physicians, whereas medical examiners are appointed and are almost always licensed forensic pathologists. Many jurisdictions have replaced the coroner system with medical examiner systems, though elected coroners remain common, particularly in rural counties.

By country

England and Wales

Coroners are independent judicial officers, usually lawyers or doctors of at least five years' standing, appointed by local authorities. They investigate violent or unnatural deaths, deaths of unknown cause, and deaths in custody. Reforms following the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 created the office of Chief Coroner for England and Wales.

Canada and Australia

In Canada, coroners are appointed by provincial or territorial governments; in some provinces they are physicians, in others lawyers or other professionals. Australian coroners are magistrates who preside over coroners' courts and may make recommendations aimed at preventing similar deaths.
