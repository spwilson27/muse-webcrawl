The African Methodist Episcopal Church, usually called the AME Church, is a Methodist denomination based in the United States. It adheres to Wesleyan-Arminian theology and uses an episcopal polity. Founded in 1816 by Richard Allen in Philadelphia, it is the first independent Protestant denomination founded by Black people in the United States, and it counts about 2.5 million members in 39 countries.

History

The church grew out of the Free African Society founded by Allen and Absalom Jones in 1787, and from Allen's Bethel congregation, which formed after Black worshippers were subjected to segregation at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church — most famously when Allen and Jones were pulled from their knees at prayer for worshipping in a white-only section. In 1816, Black Methodist congregations from several cities met in Philadelphia to establish the AME Church as an independent denomination, electing Allen its first bishop.

The denomination grew rapidly in the free states before the Civil War and expanded dramatically in the South during Reconstruction, founding schools and colleges such as Wilberforce University and Morris Brown College. AME churches and clergy have been central to the abolitionist movement, the civil rights movement, and African-American political life.

Beliefs and structure

The AME Church follows the doctrines of John Wesley, summarized in the denomination's Articles of Religion, and is governed by a General Conference, a Council of Bishops, and bishops presiding over twenty episcopal districts in North America, the Caribbean, and Africa. Its motto is "God Our Father, Christ Our Redeemer, the Holy Spirit Our Comforter, Humankind Our Family."
