Solar tracker

A solar tracker is a device that orients a payload toward the Sun. Payloads are usually solar panels, parabolic troughs, Fresnel reflectors, lenses, or the mirrors of a heliostat. By keeping photovoltaic panels perpendicular to the Sun's rays, tracking can increase electricity production by roughly 25–35% compared with fixed-tilt arrays, depending on latitude and tracker type.

Types

Single-axis trackers

Single-axis trackers rotate about one axis, typically aligned north–south, following the Sun from east to west during the day. Horizontal single-axis trackers are the most common configuration in large utility-scale photovoltaic plants. Vertical and tilted single-axis designs are used at higher latitudes.

Dual-axis trackers

Dual-axis trackers rotate about both horizontal and vertical axes, keeping the panel exactly pointed at the Sun at all times. They capture the maximum possible energy but are more expensive and mechanically complex, and are mainly used in concentrating photovoltaics and concentrated solar power.

Drive mechanisms

Trackers may be driven by electric motors under computer control, using astronomical algorithms or light sensors to determine the Sun's position. Passive trackers use a low-boiling-point fluid whose vapour pressure shifts the centre of gravity as one side is heated. Counterweight and gravity-driven designs also exist.

Applications

Solar trackers are widely used in utility-scale solar power plants, in heliostat fields that focus sunlight onto a central tower, and in parabolic trough collectors. Trade-offs include higher capital and maintenance costs, greater land use for row spacing, and vulnerability to wind loading, which modern systems mitigate with stow positions in high winds.
