English Dissenters

English Dissenters or English Separatists were Protestant Christians who separated from the Church of England in the 17th and 18th centuries. They opposed state interference in religious matters and were in some cases persecuted by the established church and the state. The term includes Presbyterians, Independents (Congregationalists), Baptists and Quakers, among others.

A dissenting chapel; dissenters worshipped outside the established church.

History

Dissent had roots in the Puritan movement of the Elizabethan era, but the decisive break came after the Restoration of Charles II. The Act of Uniformity 1662 required clergy to assent to the Book of Common Prayer and episcopal ordination; about 2,000 ministers who refused were ejected in the "Great Ejection". The Clarendon Code imposed further penalties, restricting dissenting worship and excluding nonconformists from public office.

Toleration and influence

The Toleration Act 1689 allowed freedom of worship for Protestant dissenters who took oaths of allegiance, though civil disabilities persisted until the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828. Barred from Oxford and Cambridge, dissenters founded their own academies, which pioneered modern curricula in science and commerce, and dissenting families became prominent in industry, banking and social reform.
