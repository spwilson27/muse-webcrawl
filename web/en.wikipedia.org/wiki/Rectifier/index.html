Rectifier

A rectifier is an electrical device that converts alternating current (AC), which periodically reverses direction, to direct current (DC), which flows in only one direction. The reverse operation, converting DC to AC, is performed by an inverter. The process is known as rectification, since it "straightens" the direction of current.

Types

Rectifiers take several physical forms, including vacuum tube diodes, wet chemical cells, mercury-arc valves, stacks of copper and selenium oxide plates, semiconductor diodes, silicon-controlled rectifiers and other silicon-based semiconductors. A half-wave rectifier passes only one half of the AC waveform, whereas a full-wave rectifier passes both halves; the most common full-wave circuit is the bridge rectifier, which uses four diodes in a bridge configuration.

Applications

Rectifiers are components of the power supplies of virtually all electronic equipment, converting mains AC to the DC voltages the equipment requires. They are also used in battery chargers, DC power transmission, welding, and the detection of amplitude-modulated radio signals, where the rectifier serves as a simple demodulator.

History

Before the development of silicon semiconductor rectifiers, vacuum tube thermionic diodes and copper oxide- or selenium-based metal rectifier stacks were used. The introduction of semiconductor electronics after 1945 made compact, efficient rectifiers widely available.
