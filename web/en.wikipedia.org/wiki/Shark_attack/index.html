Shark attack

A shark attack is an attack on a human by a shark. Worldwide, roughly 70–100 unprovoked attacks are recorded in a typical recent year, with a small number of fatalities — usually fewer than ten — making fatal encounters extraordinarily rare relative to the number of people who enter the ocean. Data are compiled by the International Shark Attack File (ISAF), maintained by the Florida Museum of Natural History since 1958.

Species involved

Only a minority of the more than 500 shark species have been implicated in attacks on humans. The three species most often responsible for serious or fatal unprovoked bites are the great white shark, the tiger shark and the bull shark — large predators that inhabit coastal waters where people swim and surf. Surfers and board riders account for the largest share of victims in ISAF statistics.

Types and causes

Researchers distinguish "hit-and-run" bites, often attributed to mistaken identity in surf zones; "bump-and-bite" attacks, in which the shark circles and bumps the victim first; and rare "sneak" attacks without warning. Provoked attacks occur when people harass, feed or attempt to handle sharks. Proposed explanations include mistaken identity — a swimmer's silhouette resembling a seal — territorial behaviour, and investigative biting, since sharks explore objects with their mouths.

Prevention

Risk reduction includes avoiding swimming at dawn and dusk, staying in groups, avoiding murky water and river mouths, and not wearing shiny jewellery. Management measures range from shark nets and drum lines (controversial for their bycatch) to non-lethal approaches such as drone surveillance, personal electric deterrents and SMART drumlines that release tagged sharks. Famous incidents shaping public perception include the 1916 Jersey Shore attacks and the 1975 film Jaws, whose author Peter Benchley later became a shark-conservation advocate.
