Otto Paul Hermann Diels (23 January 1876 – 7 March 1954) was a German chemist. He is best known for the diene synthesis he reported with his student Kurt Alder in 1928 — the reaction now universally called the Diels–Alder reaction — for which the two shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1950.

Early life and career

Diels was born in Hamburg, the son of Hermann Diels, a noted classical philologist at the University of Berlin. He studied chemistry at Berlin under Emil Fischer, among others, receiving his doctorate in 1899 and joining the university's chemical institute, where he rose to associate professor. In 1916 he moved to the University of Kiel as full professor and director of its Chemical Institute, remaining there until his retirement in 1945.

Research

Diels's early work included the discovery in 1906 of carbon suboxide (C₃O₂), a malonic anhydride he prepared and characterised. He also developed selenium dehydrogenation as a tool for determining the structures of steroids and other natural products, work that helped establish the carbon skeleton of cholesterol.

His lasting fame rests on the 1928 paper with Alder describing the addition of a conjugated diene to a substituted alkene (the "dienophile") to give a cyclohexene ring system. The Diels–Alder reaction became one of the most powerful bond-forming reactions in organic synthesis, used in constructing complex molecules from cortisone to modern pharmaceuticals and materials. Diels and Alder shared the Nobel Prize "for their discovery and development of the diene synthesis".

Personal life

Diels lost two sons in the Second World War, and his Kiel institute was damaged by bombing; he rebuilt his research group in provisional quarters after 1945. He died in Kiel in 1954. His textbook Einführung in die organische Chemie introduced generations of German students to organic chemistry.
