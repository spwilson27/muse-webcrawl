An augmented triad is a chord composed of two superimposed major thirds. It consists of a root, a major third, and an augmented fifth: for example, the C augmented triad contains the notes C, E, and G♯. The chord is symmetric, dividing the octave into three equal major thirds, and consequently there are only four distinct augmented triads in twelve-tone equal temperament.

Properties

Because the augmented triad is built from stacked major thirds, each of its notes can function as the root, making the chord inherently ambiguous in tonal contexts. Unlike the major and minor triads, it does not occur diatonically in the major scale; it arises naturally in the harmonic minor and melodic minor scales (as the III chord) and in the whole-tone scale, of which it is a characteristic sonority.

Usage

In common-practice harmony the augmented triad most often appears as a chromatic passing or dominant-function chord, frequently created by raising the fifth of a tonic or dominant chord. Schubert, Chopin, Liszt, and Wagner used it for expressive chromatic colour; Franz Liszt's Faust Symphony opens with a famous series of augmented triads. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, composers including Debussy and Scriabin used the chord within whole-tone and other symmetrical contexts. In popular music it appears as a colouristic device, for instance in the opening of the Beach Boys' and Chuck Berry-influenced rock repertoire and in jazz harmony as an altered dominant colour.

Inversions

The augmented triad has three positions (root position and two inversions), but because of its symmetry each inversion has the same intervallic structure of two major thirds, so position is determined by context and notation rather than sound alone.
