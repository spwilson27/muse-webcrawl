Nitrate

Nitrate is a polyatomic ion with the chemical formula NO3−. Salts containing this ion are called nitrates. Nitrates are common components of fertilizers and explosives. Almost all inorganic nitrates are soluble in water. An example of an insoluble nitrate is bismuth oxynitrate.

Structure and properties

The nitrate ion consists of one nitrogen atom bonded to three oxygen atoms in a trigonal planar arrangement, with the negative charge delocalised over the oxygens by resonance; the N–O bond order is 4/3. The nitrogen atom is in the +5 oxidation state. Nitrate is the conjugate base of nitric acid and is a mild oxidising agent, becoming more powerful at high temperatures or in molten salts.

Occurrence and production

Nitrates occur naturally where nitrogen fixation and nitrification deposit them — historically, large beds of sodium nitrate (saltpetre, "Chile saltpetre") were mined in the Atacama Desert. Industrially, nitrate salts are made from nitric acid, produced by the Ostwald process from ammonia. In the nitrogen cycle, nitrite is oxidised to nitrate by soil bacteria such as Nitrobacter, and plants absorb nitrate as a key nutrient.

Uses

The chief use of nitrates is in agriculture as nitrogen fertilisers, including ammonium nitrate, potassium nitrate and calcium nitrate. Potassium nitrate is a traditional ingredient of gunpowder and fireworks, and nitrate esters such as nitroglycerin and nitrocellulose are important explosives and propellants. Sodium nitrite and nitrate are used to cure meats, though their use is regulated because nitrosamine formation is a health concern.

Health and environment

Excess nitrate in drinking water can cause methemoglobinemia ("blue baby syndrome") in infants, and nitrate runoff from fertilised fields contributes to eutrophication of waterways. Drinking-water standards commonly limit nitrate to about 10 mg/L measured as nitrogen (US EPA) or 50 mg/L as nitrate (WHO guideline).
