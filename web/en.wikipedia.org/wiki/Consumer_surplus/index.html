Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus (or consumers' surplus) is an economic measure of consumer benefit. It is the difference between the maximum price a consumer is willing to pay for a good and the price actually paid. If a consumer would pay more than the current price, they obtain a surplus from the purchase. In aggregate, consumer surplus is the area under the demand curve and above the market price, bounded by the quantity consumed.

History

The concept was developed by the French engineer-economist Jules Dupuit in the 1840s in measuring the utility of public works, and was refined by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890), who popularised the term and its graphical representation.

Calculation

For an individual, consumer surplus is the sum, over each unit purchased, of the difference between the buyer's reservation price and the price paid. In market analysis it is measured as the area of the (roughly triangular) region below the demand curve and above the price line. Consumer surplus together with producer surplus (the analogous gain to sellers) makes up total economic surplus, or total welfare; interventions such as taxes and price ceilings redistribute or destroy surplus, creating deadweight loss.

Assumptions and criticism

The Marshallian measure treats the marginal utility of money as approximately constant. John Hicks developed the compensating variation and equivalent variation as theoretically more exact money measures of welfare change, which coincide with consumer surplus when income effects are absent. Critics note that measured surplus depends on how the demand curve is estimated, and that it does not by itself address distributional questions.

Applications

  • Evaluating the welfare effects of taxation, subsidies, tariffs and price controls.
  • Cost–benefit analysis of public projects, in the tradition of Dupuit.
  • Analysing monopoly: a monopolist transfers part of consumer surplus to itself and destroys another part as deadweight loss.
