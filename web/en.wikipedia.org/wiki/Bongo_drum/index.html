Bongo drum

Bongos (Spanish: bongó) are an Afro-Cuban percussion instrument consisting of a pair of small open-bottomed hand drums of different sizes, joined together. The smaller drum is called macho ("male") and the larger hembra ("female"). The player, a bongosero, holds them between the knees or on a stand and strikes the heads with fingers and palms. Bongos are a foundational instrument of Cuban son, salsa and Latin jazz.

History

The bongó originated in eastern Cuba around the turn of the 20th century, in the region of Oriente where the son cubano was born, drawing on African drumming traditions and Spanish musical forms. Early bongos had tacked-on heads heated over a flame to tune them; modern instruments use lug tuning and sometimes synthetic shells. The instrument spread with son to Havana in the 1920s and then to the United States, where bongos became prominent in Latin jazz, mambo and, in the 1950s, beatnik popular culture.

Technique and role

The bongosero maintains the steady martillo ("hammer") pattern throughout a song, varying it with finger strokes, palm slaps and rim shots, and improvises more freely during solos — often switching to a hand bell (campana) during the climactic montuno sections of salsa arrangements. Famous bongoseros include José Mangual Sr., Ray Barretto (before switching to congas), Johnny "Dandy" Rodríguez and Roberto Roena.

Modern bongos are usually made of wood, fibreglass or metal, with heads of rawhide or synthetic film; typical head diameters are about 7 and 8.5 inches.
