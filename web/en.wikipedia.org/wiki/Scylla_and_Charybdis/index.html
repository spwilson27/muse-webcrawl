Scylla and Charybdis

Scylla and Charybdis
SourceHomer, Odyssey Book 12
TypeSea monsters of Greek mythology
Traditional locationStrait of Messina, between Sicily and Calabria

Scylla and Charybdis are two sea monsters of Greek mythology situated on opposite sides of a narrow strait, so close to each other that sailors avoiding one inevitably came into the grasp of the other. They appear in Book 12 of Homer's Odyssey, and the pair gave rise to the idiom "between Scylla and Charybdis" — to be caught between two equally dangerous alternatives, similar to "between a rock and a hard place".

In the Odyssey

On Circe's advice, Odysseus steered closer to Scylla, a six-headed monster dwelling in a cliff-side cave who snatched six of his men — one for each head — rather than risk the whole ship in Charybdis, a monster beneath a fig tree on the opposite shore who thrice a day sucked down and vomited the sea in a vast whirlpool. After the wreck of his ship, Odysseus survived a second encounter by clinging to the fig tree above Charybdis until his improvised raft was disgorged.

Location

From antiquity the monsters were identified with the Strait of Messina between Sicily and the Italian mainland (Calabria), where a genuine if modest whirlpool and dangerous rocks exist; the Sicilian headland of Scilla preserves Scylla's name. Later authors including Virgil, Ovid and Apollonius of Rhodes retold the encounter, and Jason's Argo was said to have passed safely with the help of the Nereid Thetis.

Legacy

The phrase became a standard metaphor in literature and political commentary for a dilemma with no safe option. In 2014 astronomers named features informally, and the monsters remain staples of retellings of the Odyssey from Joyce's Ulysses — whose "Scylla and Charybdis" episode stages a literary debate — onward.
