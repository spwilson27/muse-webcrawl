Chunking (psychology)

Chunking
FieldCognitive psychology
ConceptGrouping individual items into larger meaningful units ("chunks")
Key paperMiller (1956), "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"
AffectsWorking/short-term memory capacity

Chunking is a cognitive process by which individual pieces of information are bound together into a meaningful whole, or "chunk." By reorganising material into familiar units — for example remembering the letters F-B-I as one item rather than three — a person can hold far more information in short-term memory than the raw item limit would allow. Chunking is a key mechanism in expert memory and skilled performance.

Background

The term is most associated with George A. Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two," which argued that immediate memory spans about seven items — but that the items themselves can be recoded into chunks of arbitrary size. Thus 149217761941 can be remembered as three dates rather than twelve digits.

Chunking and expertise

Studies of chess masters showed they reconstruct mid-game positions from memory far better than novices — but perform no better on random positions — indicating they perceive board patterns as meaningful chunks from thousands of stored configurations. Similar effects appear in music, sport and language learning: expertise is partly the acquisition of larger, richer chunks in a domain. Chunking also underlies mnemonics, telephone-number formatting and the design of user interfaces.
