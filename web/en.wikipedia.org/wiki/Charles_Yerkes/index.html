Charles Tyson Yerkes Jr. (25 June 1837 – 29 December 1905) was an American financier of the Gilded Age, a central figure in the development of the street railway systems of Chicago and of the London Underground. His unscrupulous business methods made him a byword for the "robber baron" era, and he served prison time for embezzlement before rebuilding his fortune in transit franchises.

Early career and prison

Born into a Quaker family in Philadelphia, Yerkes worked in banking and brokerage. Acting as an agent for the city treasurer, he speculated with municipal funds and lost heavily in the crash following the Great Chicago Fire of 1871; convicted of embezzlement, he served seven months of a 33-month sentence before being pardoned.

Chicago

Moving to Chicago in 1881, Yerkes assembled control of most of the city's street railways and cable car lines, using bribery of aldermen (the "boodle council") to secure long franchises. His attempts at a fifty-year franchise extension failed amid public scandal, and he sold out in 1899 for a large profit.

London

In London from 1900, Yerkes's Underground Electric Railways Company of London bought and electrified the District Railway and built three deep-level tube lines — the Baker Street and Waterloo, Piccadilly, and Hampstead railways — which form the core of today's Bakerloo, Piccadilly and Northern lines. He died in 1905, a year before they opened.

Philanthropy and legacy

Yerkes funded the Yerkes Observatory of the University of Chicago, opened in 1897 and home to the largest refracting telescope ever used for astronomy. Theodore Dreiser's novels The Financier, The Titan and The Stoic fictionalise his life as Frank Cowperwood.
