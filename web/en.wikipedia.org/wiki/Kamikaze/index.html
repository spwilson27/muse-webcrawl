Kamikaze (神風, "divine wind") were Japanese suicide-attack pilots of the Second World War, officially the tokubetsu kōgekitai ("special attack units"), who deliberately crashed bomb-laden aircraft into Allied ships. The name evoked the typhoons that destroyed Mongol invasion fleets in 1274 and 1281.

Origins

Vice Admiral Takijirō Ōnishi organised the first official units in the Philippines in October 1944, as conventional Japanese air power collapsed against overwhelming Allied material superiority. Attacks began during the Battle of Leyte Gulf, sinking the escort carrier USS St. Lo. Army units, and related weapons such as the Ohka piloted bomb, kaiten human torpedoes, and shinyō suicide boats, followed.

Scale and effect

Mass attacks (kikusui) accompanied the Battle of Okinawa in 1945. Roughly 3,800 kamikaze pilots died overall; their attacks sank several dozen ships — none larger than escort carriers — and damaged hundreds, causing heavy Allied casualties but failing to alter the war's outcome. Many pilots were very young volunteers or conscripted students, whose final letters remain a poignant record.

Legacy

The kamikaze became a powerful and contested symbol in postwar Japan and a generic term in English for any suicide attack. Historians debate motivations — patriotism, peer pressure, and coercion — and the strategy's military rationality.
