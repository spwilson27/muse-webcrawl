Genome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A genome is the complete set of genetic material of an organism — all of its DNA (or RNA, in RNA viruses), including its genes and the non-coding sequences between and within them. The word blends gene and chromosome. Genomes are the object of genomics, the field that reads, compares and interprets them wholesale.

The Human genome spans about 3 billion base pairs across 23 chromosome pairs, with roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes — a small fraction of the total; much of the rest is regulatory sequence, introns, repetitive elements and the decayed remains of ancient viruses. Genome size varies wildly and does not track complexity (the 'C-value paradox'): some salamanders and lilies carry genomes dozens of times larger than ours.

Sequencing

The first genome sequenced was a small virus (1977, Sanger); the Human Genome Project (1990–2003) produced a reference human sequence at a cost of billions, and modern machines read a genome in hours for under a thousand dollars. Pangenome projects now capture human diversity beyond a single reference.

Uses

Genomes underpin diagnostics for inherited disease, cancer profiling, ancestry and forensics, pathogen surveillance (viral genomes tracked in the COVID-19 pandemic), conservation genetics and breeding. Comparing genomes quantifies evolutionary relationships precisely.
