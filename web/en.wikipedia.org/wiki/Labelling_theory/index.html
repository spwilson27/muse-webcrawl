Labelling theory

Labelling theory is a theory in sociology and criminology which holds that deviance is not inherent in an act, but instead emerges from the social reaction to it: behaviour becomes deviant when others successfully label it, and the people who perform it, as deviant. Developed within the symbolic interactionist tradition by scholars including Frank Tannenbaum, Edwin Lemert and Howard S. Becker, the theory shifted attention from the presumed causes of rule-breaking to the processes of rule-making, enforcement and stigmatisation.

Origins

Tannenbaum's concept of the "dramatization of evil" (1938) argued that tagging a young person as delinquent helps make him one. Lemert (1951) distinguished primary deviance — initial, often trivial rule-breaking with little effect on identity — from secondary deviance, which occurs when a person reorganises his life and self-concept around the deviant label following social reaction. Becker's Outsiders (1963) supplied the theory's classic statement: "deviance is not a quality of the act the person commits, but rather a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions to an 'offender'." Becker also highlighted "moral entrepreneurs" who create and enforce rules, and the selective character of enforcement.

Key concepts

  • Stigma — drawing on Erving Goffman's work, the spoiled identity that a label such as "ex-convict" or "mentally ill" imposes.
  • Deviancy amplification — Leslie Wilkins' model of a feedback spiral in which reaction to deviance increases it.
  • Master status — a label that overrides all other statuses in how a person is treated.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy — the labelled person internalising the label (related to Thomas Scheff's application of labelling to mental illness and to "looking-glass self" ideas).

Criticisms and legacy

Critics charged that labelling theory neglected the origins of primary deviance, understated serious harm, and romanticised offenders; conflict and positivist criminologists offered rival emphases. Nonetheless it reshaped policy debates — supporting decriminalisation, diversion and deinstitutionalisation — and its core insight survives in modified form in later work on stigma, reintegrative shaming (John Braithwaite) and restorative justice.
