Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenomics, often abbreviated as PGx, is the study of the role of the genome in drug response. Its name combines pharmacology and genomics. Pharmacogenomics analyses how the genetic makeup of an individual affects their response to drugs, with the goal of developing effective, safe medications that can be prescribed based on a person's genetics. The related term pharmacogenetics has historically referred to the study of single-gene influences on drug response, while pharmacogenomics encompasses genome-wide effects; the terms are now often used interchangeably.

Principles

Genetic variants can alter drug response by affecting drug-metabolizing enzymes, drug transporters, and drug targets. Well-known examples include variants of the CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 cytochrome P450 enzymes, which determine how quickly patients metabolize many antidepressants, opioids such as codeine, and the antiplatelet drug clopidogrel. Variants in TPMT affect tolerance of thiopurine drugs, VKORC1 and CYP2C9 variants influence warfarin dosing, and HLA-B*57:01 screening prevents hypersensitivity to the antiretroviral abacavir.

Clinical applications

Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration include pharmacogenetic information in the labeling of well over a hundred drugs. Pre-emptive genotyping panels are increasingly offered by hospitals so that results are available in the medical record before a drug is prescribed. In oncology, tumor genomic profiling guides targeted therapies such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer and tyrosine kinase inhibitors for cancers bearing specific mutations.

Challenges

Barriers to wider adoption include the cost and reimbursement of testing, interpretation of variants of uncertain significance, differences in variant frequencies across ancestries, and the need for clinician education and decision-support tools. Ethical questions around incidental findings and genetic privacy also arise.
