Anglo-Saxon runes

The Anglo-Saxon runes, also known as the futhorc (from the first six letters ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚩ ᚱ ᚳ, fuþorc), are runes used by the early Anglo-Saxons in England and Frisia from roughly the 5th century onward. They are an extended form of the Elder Futhark, expanded to accommodate the sound changes of Old English and Old Frisian.

History

The futhorc grew from the 24-character Elder Futhark, expanding to between 28 and 33 characters as new runes were created for sounds such as /æ/ (æsc ᚳ is not; æsc is ᚳ—rather the new runes included ᚳ cen repurposed, and new letters ᚷ gyfu, ᚸ gar, and others). Inscriptions appear on coins, weapons, jewellery, memorial stones, and monuments such as the Ruthwell Cross and the Franks Casket. The Old English Rune Poem preserves the names and traditional meanings of 29 runes.

Use and decline

Runes were used for writing Old English alongside the Latin alphabet, with wynn (ᚹ) and thorn (ᚦ) passing into manuscript writing. After the Norman Conquest, runic writing fell out of general use, though inscriptions continued sporadically and antiquarians revived interest in the early modern period. The Unicode block "Runic" includes the futhorc letters.
