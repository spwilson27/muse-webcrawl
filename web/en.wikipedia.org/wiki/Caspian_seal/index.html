The Caspian seal (Pusa caspica) is one of the smallest members of the earless seal family and the only seal found in the landlocked Caspian Sea. It is endemic to that sea, distributed from the winter ice fields of the shallow north to the deeper waters of the south, migrating seasonally with the ice and with prey fish.

Description and life history

Adults reach about 1.4 m in length with a coat patterned in dark spots on a grey-brown background. Females give birth to a single pup, usually in late January or February, on the northern ice, where the pup nurses for about a month. Caspian seals feed mainly on small fish such as kilka (Caspian sprat), gobies and crustaceans.

Threats and conservation

The population crashed from an estimated one million at the start of the 20th century to roughly 100,000 or fewer, owing to intensive commercial hunting, habitat degradation, pollution, disease (including mass die-offs linked to canine distemper virus), invasive species and, increasingly, the decline of the Caspian's winter ice with climate change and falling sea levels. The species has been listed as Endangered by the IUCN since 2008, and all five Caspian littoral states have moved to restrict hunting.
