In Islam, du'a (Arabic: دعاء, plural: ad'iyah) is a prayer of invocation, supplication, or request directed to God. Unlike the formal ritual prayer (salah), which follows prescribed movements and formulas at set times, du'a is a personal, often spontaneous appeal in which a believer asks Allah for help, guidance, forgiveness, or blessings for themselves or others. It is regarded as one of the most direct expressions of worship; a well-known hadith states, "Du'a is worship itself," and another calls it "the weapon of the believer."

Practice

Du'a may be offered at any time and in any language, though many Muslims learn traditional Arabic supplications from the Qur'an and the Sunnah. Common etiquette (adab) includes facing the qibla, raising the open palms toward the face, beginning with praise of God and blessings on the Prophet Muhammad, repenting of sins, asking with humility and certainty of response, and ending by wiping the hands over the face. Certain times are considered especially propitious: the last third of the night, while fasting, when breaking the fast, between the adhan and iqama, during prostration, on the Day of Arafah, and on Laylat al-Qadr in Ramadan.

Types

Du'a al-mas'ala is the supplication of request, asking God for one's needs in this world and the next; du'a al-'ibada is supplication through worship itself—acts of devotion viewed as implicit appeals for divine reward. Famous transmitted supplications include the Du'a Kumayl, attributed to Ali ibn Abi Talib and taught to his companion Kumayl ibn Ziyad, which is recited especially in Shia tradition on Thursday nights, and Du'a al-Jawshan al-Kabir. Collections such as Hisn al-Muslim ("Fortress of the Muslim") gather short daily supplications for waking, eating, travelling, and other occasions.

Theology

The Qur'an commands, "Call upon Me; I will respond to you" (40:60). Theologians discuss why some supplications appear unanswered, traditionally answering that God may grant the request, avert an equivalent harm, or store the reward for the hereafter. Asking intermediaries for du'a—such as requesting the prayers of the righteous—is widespread, while directing requests to the dead or to saints is a point of contention between schools of thought, with Salafi scholars classifying it as shirk and many Sufi and traditionalist traditions permitting intercessory prayer (tawassul).
