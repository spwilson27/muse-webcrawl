Angiotensin-converting enzyme

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)
GeneACE, chromosome 17q23.3
EC number3.4.15.1
ReactionAngiotensin I → Angiotensin II
Also degradesBradykinin

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) is a central component of the renin–angiotensin system, which regulates blood pressure and fluid balance. ACE is a zinc-dependent dipeptidyl carboxypeptidase found on the surface of endothelial cells, especially in the lungs and kidneys. It converts the hormone angiotensin I into the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II, and inactivates the vasodilator bradykinin.

Function

Renin, released by the kidney, cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I. ACE then removes two C-terminal residues to yield angiotensin II, which constricts blood vessels, stimulates aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex, promotes sodium and water retention, and increases thirst and antidiuretic hormone release — all raising blood pressure. Serum ACE levels are sometimes measured clinically, as they are often elevated in sarcoidosis.

ACE inhibitors

Drugs that inhibit ACE — including captopril (the first, developed from peptides in the venom of the jararaca snake), enalapril, lisinopril and ramipril — are mainstays of treatment for hypertension, heart failure, diabetic kidney disease and after myocardial infarction. Their characteristic side effects include a dry cough and, rarely, angioedema, attributed to accumulation of bradykinin.

ACE2

A homologue, ACE2, converts angiotensin II to the vasodilator angiotensin (1–7) and acts as a counterweight within the system. ACE2 is also the cellular receptor used by the coronaviruses SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 to enter human cells.
