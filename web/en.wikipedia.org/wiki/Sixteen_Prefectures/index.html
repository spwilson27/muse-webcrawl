Sixteen Prefectures

The Sixteen Prefectures of Yan and Yun (燕雲十六州) were a group of prefectures in northern China — roughly corresponding to modern Beijing, Tianjin, northern Hebei, and northern Shanxi — that were ceded in 938 by Shi Jingtang, founder of the Later Jin dynasty, to the Khitan-led Liao dynasty in exchange for military support. The cession had enduring strategic consequences for subsequent Chinese dynasties.

Background

Shi Jingtang rebelled against the Later Tang in 936 and sought Khitan help, promising the Liao emperor territory and annual payments. After his victory he formally ceded the sixteen prefectures in 938. The region included the passes and high ground along the Great Wall line, depriving later Chinese states of their natural northern defensive barrier.

Significance

The loss of the Sixteen Prefectures left the North China Plain exposed to cavalry advances from the steppe. The Song dynasty, which reunified most of China from 960, repeatedly attempted to recover the prefectures; failed campaigns in 979 and 986 ended in major defeats. The Chanyuan Treaty of 1005 stabilised the border on the basis of continued Liao possession. The prefectures became a lasting symbol of territorial loss and irredentism in Chinese historical memory. In 1123, under the Alliance Conducted at Sea, the Song briefly recovered some of the prefectures from the collapsing Liao, paying a large indemnity, but the Jurchen Jin dynasty conquered the region — and then all of northern China — shortly afterward.

List

The sixteen prefectures were You, Ji, Ying, Mo, Zhuo, Tan, Shun, Gui, Ru, Wu, Yun, Ying, Huan, Shuo, Wei, and Yu.
