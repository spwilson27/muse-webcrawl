Islam and war

The relationship between Islam and war is addressed in the Qur'an, the practice and sayings of Muhammad (the Hadith), and a developed body of classical Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) known as siyar, the Islamic law of nations. Classical jurists articulated criteria for the legitimate resort to war (jus ad bellum) and for conduct within it (jus in bello) — including defensive justification, legitimate authority, prohibitions on killing noncombatants and rules on treaties and prisoners — that scholars frequently compare with the Western just war theory. The concept of jihad, literally "striving", encompasses spiritual and moral struggle as well as, in its juridical usage, armed struggle under defined conditions.

Scriptural foundations

Qur'anic verses on fighting are read by Muslim scholars in the context of the early community's circumstances in Mecca and Medina. Verses such as 2:190 — "Fight in the way of God those who fight you, but do not transgress; God does not love the transgressors" — and 22:39, granting permission to fight to those who have been wronged, are taken to ground war in defence against aggression and persecution. Verses including 8:61 ("if they incline to peace, incline thou to it") and 2:256 ("no compulsion in religion") are cited for limits on war and the rejection of forced conversion. Muhammad's conduct — the Constitution of Medina, the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah (628), and instructions attributed to him and to the first caliph Abu Bakr forbidding the killing of women, children, the elderly and monks, the mutilation of the dead, and the destruction of crops and trees — forms the second major source of the tradition.

Classical jurisprudence

From the 8th century, jurists such as Muhammad al-Shaybani (whose Kitab al-Siyar al-Kabir is a foundational text of international law) systematised rules covering: declaration and authority for war; the invitation to Islam or treaty before hostilities against distant peoples; safe-conduct (aman) for envoys and merchants; the protected (dhimmi) status of Jews, Christians and others under Muslim rule; division of spoils; ransoming and treatment of prisoners; and the binding force of treaties. The medieval world was conceptually divided into dar al-Islam (abode of Islam) and dar al-harb (abode of war), a juristic device rather than a Qur'anic term, softened in practice by extensive trade, diplomacy and long truces.

History and modern debates

Historically, Muslim states waged wars of expansion in the early caliphates and defensive and dynastic wars thereafter, much as other pre-modern empires did, while also developing diplomacy, truces and coexistence (as in al-Andalus and the Ottoman millet system). In the modern period, mainstream Muslim scholars and bodies — for example in the 2004 Amman Message and the 2014 open "Letter to Baghdadi" signed by over a hundred scholars — have condemned terrorism and the killing of civilians as violations of Islamic law, while militant groups invoke competing interpretations of jihad. Academic debate continues over how classical doctrines map onto modern international humanitarian law, with many scholars noting substantial convergences on noncombatant immunity and treaty fidelity.
