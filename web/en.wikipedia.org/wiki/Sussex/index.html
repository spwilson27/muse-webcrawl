Sussex

Region: South East England
Historic county: now East Sussex, West Sussex, and Brighton and Hove
Area: ~3,800 km²
Population: ~1.7 million
Motto: "We wunt be druv"

Sussex is a historic county in South East England, bounded by Surrey to the north, Kent to the east, Hampshire to the west, and the English Channel to the south. Since 1888 it has been administered as the separate counties of East Sussex and West Sussex, a division with roots in the county's ancient eastern and western halves.

History

Sussex was one of the seven kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England: the Kingdom of the South Saxons, founded, according to tradition, by Ælle in 477 AD. The Norman Conquest began on its coast in 1066, where William of Normandy defeated Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings. The county's Weald was a centre of the English iron industry from Roman times through the Tudor period, and its coastal towns — the Cinque Ports of Hastings, Rye, and Winchelsea among them — were major medieval ports. In the 18th and 19th centuries, Brighton grew from a fishing village into Britain's most fashionable seaside resort.

Geography

The South Downs, a ridge of chalk hills now a national park, run the length of the county, ending at the white cliffs of Beachy Head and the Seven Sisters. Between the Downs and the coast are the marshes of Pevensey and the lower river valleys of the Arun, Adur, Ouse, and Cuckmere. The densely wooded High Weald in the north is an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty.

Culture

Sussex has its own traditional dialect, county day (16 June, St Richard's Day), and the defiant unofficial motto "We wunt be druv" — "we won't be driven". Bonfire Night celebrations in Lewes are among the largest in Britain.
