Victor Franz Hess (24 June 1883 – 17 December 1964) was an Austrian-American physicist and Nobel laureate in Physics who discovered cosmic rays. Electroscopes discharged faster than known radioactivity could explain; assuming the ionising radiation came from the ground, it should fade with altitude. In a series of manned balloon flights in 1911–12, reaching 5,350 metres, Hess found the opposite: ionisation decreased slightly at first, then rose strongly with height — the radiation came from space (it did not diminish during a solar eclipse, ruling out the Sun as its sole source).

Hess's 'radiation from above' (Höhenstrahlung) was confirmed by Werner Kolhörster and, in America, by Robert Millikan, who coined 'cosmic rays'. The discovery opened the window through which the positron, muon, pion and kaon were first seen, and Hess shared the 1936 Nobel Prize with Anderson. An opponent of Nazism, Hess left Austria after the Anschluss (he was dismissed for his wife's Jewish ancestry and his associations) and spent his later career at Fordham University in New York, working on radiation safety — he advised on measurements after the first atomic tests.

Method

Hess flew with three electrometers of his own meticulous construction, sealing them against pressure and temperature effects that had misled earlier attempts (which reached lower altitudes and ambiguous conclusions). His 1912 conclusion — 'a radiation of very high penetrating power enters our atmosphere from above' — is one of experimental physics' cleanest discoveries.
