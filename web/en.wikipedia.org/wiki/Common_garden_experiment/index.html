Common garden experiment

In a common garden, organisms from different environments are grown under identical conditions.

A common garden experiment, or common garden test, is an experimental design in ecology and evolutionary biology in which organisms from different populations or environments are grown together under the same, shared conditions. Because the environment is held constant, any differences that persist between groups can be attributed to genetic differences rather than to environmental effects — a classic method for separating nature from nurture in wild populations.

Rationale

Populations of a species living in different places often differ in traits such as size, growth rate, or flowering time. Such differences may reflect phenotypic plasticity — a direct response to local conditions — or local adaptation, genetic divergence shaped by natural selection. Growing representatives of each population in a common environment removes the environmental component: traits that converge are plastic; traits that remain distinct have a genetic basis.

Design and use

Common gardens are widely used in forestry and plant evolutionary ecology, where seeds from many provenances are planted in replicated plots and measured over years. The landmark experiments of Clausen, Keck, and Hiesey in the 1940s, transplanting plants such as Achillea across California's altitudinal gradient, became foundational studies of ecotypic differentiation. The method is also applied to animals, for example rearing fish or insects from different populations under identical laboratory conditions, and complements the reciprocal transplant experiment, in which organisms are exchanged between their home environments.

Limitations

Results can still be confounded by maternal effects — the environment of the parent influencing offspring — which researchers control by raising a generation in the garden before measuring. Conditions in a single garden also represent only one environment, so genotype-by-environment interactions may require multiple gardens at different sites.
