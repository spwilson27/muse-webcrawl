La Niña (Spanish: [la ˈniɲa], "the girl") is the colder phase of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a recurring climate pattern involving changes in the temperature of waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. During La Niña, sea surface temperatures there fall 0.5 °C or more below average for at least several months, trade winds strengthen, and upwelling of cold water intensifies off South America.

Mechanism

In the neutral state, easterly trade winds push warm surface water westward, piling it up near Indonesia while cold water upwells in the east. La Niña amplifies this pattern: stronger trades cool the eastern Pacific further, steepen the thermocline, and shift the Walker circulation. Meteorologists typically declare a La Niña when the Oceanic Niño Index (three-month mean SST anomaly in the Niño 3.4 region) is at or below −0.5 °C for five consecutive overlapping seasons, together with matching atmospheric changes.

Effects

La Niña's impacts are broadly the mirror image of El Niño's. It tends to bring wetter-than-average conditions to eastern Australia, Indonesia, and southern Africa; drought to the southwestern United States and parts of South America; colder winters to the Pacific Northwest and northern North America; and an above-average Atlantic hurricane season, because reduced wind shear over the Caribbean and tropical Atlantic lets storms strengthen. Notable recent events include the strong 2010–12 La Niña associated with severe flooding in Queensland, and the rare "triple-dip" La Niña of 2020–2023.
