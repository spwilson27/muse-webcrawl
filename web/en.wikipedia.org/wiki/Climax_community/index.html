Climax community

In scientific ecology, a climax community or climatic climax community is the final stage of ecological succession in an area: a biological community of plants, animals, and fungi which, through succession, has developed the steady state characteristic of its climate and geography. Because each area was thought to progress toward a single inevitable endpoint determined by climate, the theory was known as the "monoclimax" theory, and was most fully developed by the American ecologist Frederic Clements in the early twentieth century.

Theory

Clements compared succession to the development of an organism: a "sere" passes through predictable stages (pioneer, intermediate, and so on) until reaching the mature climax, which then perpetuates itself indefinitely unless disturbed. Within a climatic region the climax was expected to be uniform — for example, deciduous forest across the eastern United States — regardless of local soil differences.

Criticism and modern view

The monoclimax theory was challenged from the start. Henry Gleason proposed the "individualistic concept," arguing that communities are not super-organisms but the overlapping results of species each responding to the environment. A. G. Tansley introduced the idea of multiple stable endpoints ("polyclimax") determined by soil, topography, fire, and grazing as well as climate. Modern ecology generally regards climax as a useful but simplified concept: real communities are dynamic, shifting mosaics influenced by disturbance regimes, and under ongoing climate change no community can be assumed to be in permanent equilibrium. Related concepts include the potential natural vegetation used in European phytosociology, and "disclimax" communities maintained by recurring disturbance such as fire or grazing.
