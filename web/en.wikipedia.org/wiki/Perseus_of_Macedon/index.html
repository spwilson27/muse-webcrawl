Perseus (Greek: Περσεύς, c. 212 – c. 166 BC) was the last king of the Antigonid dynasty of Macedon, ruling from 179 BC until his defeat by Rome in 168 BC. His reign ended with the Third Macedonian War and the abolition of the Macedonian monarchy, a turning point in Rome's domination of the Hellenistic world.

Early life and accession

Perseus was the elder son of Philip V. Ancient sources report that he intrigued against his younger half-brother Demetrius, who was popular at Rome, and induced his father to have him executed in 180 BC. Perseus succeeded Philip in 179 BC and inherited a kingdom whose resources his father had rebuilt after the defeat in the Second Macedonian War.

Reign

Perseus renewed alliances in Greece, married Laodice, daughter of Seleucus IV, and cultivated popularity among the Greek states, alarming Rome and its ally Eumenes II of Pergamon. Roman complaints about Macedonian expansion in Thrace and influence in Greece led to war in 171 BC.

Third Macedonian War

After early successes, including a cavalry victory near Callinicus in 171 BC, Perseus was decisively defeated by the consul Lucius Aemilius Paullus at the Battle of Pydna on 22 June 168 BC, where the Macedonian phalanx was destroyed by the more flexible Roman legions. Perseus fled, was captured, and was displayed in Paullus's triumph at Rome. He spent his remaining years in captivity at Alba Fucens, dying around 166 BC. Macedon was divided into four client republics, ending over a century of Antigonid rule.
