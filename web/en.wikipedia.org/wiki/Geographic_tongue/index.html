Geographic tongue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geographic tongue, also known as benign migratory glossitis, is a harmless inflammatory condition affecting the surface of the tongue. It is characterized by patches of depapillation — loss of the small filiform papillae — surrounded by slightly raised whitish borders, giving the tongue an appearance reminiscent of a map, hence the name. The patches change in size, shape, and location over time, sometimes healing in one area and appearing in another.

Signs and symptoms

Geographic tongue affects roughly 1–3 percent of the general population and can occur at any age. In most people it causes no symptoms and is found incidentally during dental examinations. Some people report sensitivity or a burning sensation with spicy, acidic, or salty foods. The condition is frequently seen together with fissured tongue.

Causes

The cause is unknown. Geographic tongue runs in families, suggesting a genetic component, and it has been associated with psoriasis — histologically the two conditions look similar — as well as with atopy, stress, and hormonal factors. It is not contagious and is not caused by infection or cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually made on the characteristic clinical appearance alone; biopsy is rarely necessary. No treatment is required for asymptomatic cases. For symptomatic cases, measures may include avoiding irritating foods, topical anesthetics or corticosteroids, and reassurance that the condition is benign and self-limiting, though it may persist or recur over many years.
