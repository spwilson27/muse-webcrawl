Espagnole sauce (French: [ɛs.pa.ɲɔl], "Spanish sauce") is a mildly flavoured brown sauce, and one of the five mother sauces of French cooking codified by Auguste Escoffier. Its opposite number among the mother sauces is the pale velouté. The name has nothing to do with Spain as such in the modern recipe: theories range from the Spanish tomatoes included in early versions to a pun on the brown colouring of Spanish leather or wood.

Preparation

The basic method of making espagnole is to prepare a very dark brown roux, to which brown veal stock is added, along with browned bones, pieces of beef, vegetables and various seasonings. This blend is allowed to slowly reduce while being frequently skimmed, and finally tomato paste is added. The sauce is the foundation of demi-glace — espagnole reduced with an equal part of brown stock — from which a large family of derivative sauces (bordelaise, chasseur, Robert, Madeira, and others) is built.

History

Brown sauces of this type were systematised by Antonin Carême in the early 19th century and simplified by Escoffier in Le Guide Culinaire (1903). Because of its long cooking time and reliance on veal stock, espagnole is rarely made from scratch in modern restaurants, where demi-glace bases are more common.
