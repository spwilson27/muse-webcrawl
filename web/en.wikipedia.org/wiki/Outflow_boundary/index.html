Outflow boundary

Outflow boundary

TypeMesoscale meteorological boundary
Also calledGust front
Produced byRain-cooled air descending from thunderstorms

An outflow boundary, also known as a gust front, is a storm-scale or mesoscale boundary separating rain-cooled air produced by a thunderstorm from the warmer surrounding air. Similar in effect to a shallow cold front, it is the leading edge of a density current of dense, evaporatively chilled air that spreads out beneath and away from the parent storm, sometimes travelling tens or hundreds of kilometres and lasting for many hours after the storm itself has dissipated.

Formation

Within a mature thunderstorm, rain falling into unsaturated air evaporates and cools it, producing a downdraft. On reaching the ground this cool air spreads radially outward, undercutting the warm ambient air. The depth of the cold pool is typically one to two kilometres, and its leading edge advances at speeds of roughly 10–25 metres per second depending on the pool's depth and temperature deficit.

Weather effects

Passage of a gust front is marked by a sudden wind shift and gustiness, a sharp temperature fall, and a rise in pressure. The lifting of warm air over the advancing cold pool may be visualised by a shelf or roll cloud (arcus), and dust raised by the outflow can produce an approaching wall of haze. Because the boundary lifts warm, moist air, it frequently triggers new thunderstorms along its length; where outflow boundaries from different storms collide, the enhanced convergence can focus explosive storm development, including, occasionally, supercells and tornadoes.

Detection

Outflow boundaries appear on weather radar as thin lines of weak reflectivity (fine lines) in clear air, caused by insects and refractive-index gradients concentrated along the boundary, and as propagating arcs on visible satellite imagery. Surface mesonet stations record their passage as characteristic wind and temperature traces. Recognising lingering boundaries is an important input to short-term forecasting (nowcasting), as storms forming on them can produce flash flooding when successive cells train over the same area.
