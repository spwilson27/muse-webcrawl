Jiddu Krishnamurti (11 May 1895 – 17 February 1986) was an Indian philosopher, speaker, and writer. In his early life, he was groomed by the Theosophical Society to be the next World Teacher, a messianic figure its leaders expected to advance human spiritual evolution. In 1929, however, Krishnamurti renounced the role, dissolved the Order of the Star founded around him, and returned the money and property donated for his work, declaring that "truth is a pathless land" and that no organisation, creed, or guru can lead another to truth.

For the remaining six decades of his life he travelled the world speaking on the need for a revolution in the human mind — on self-knowledge, attention, fear, freedom, and the ending of psychological conflict — insisting he maintained no authority and that listeners must observe themselves without dependence on teachers, including himself. Foundations bearing his name in India, the United Kingdom, and the United States preserve his talks and run schools based on his educational ideas.

Early life and Theosophy

Krishnamurti was born in Madanapalle in present-day Andhra Pradesh into a Telugu-speaking Brahmin family; his father worked for the Theosophical Society at Adyar, Madras. In 1909 the theosophist C. W. Leadbeater encountered the boy on the beach at Adyar and, claiming clairvoyant insight into his aura, proclaimed him a vehicle for the coming World Teacher (Maitreya). Taken under the guardianship of Annie Besant, president of the Society, Krishnamurti was educated in India and England; the Order of the Star in the East (1911, later Order of the Star) was founded to prepare the world for his teaching. His brother Nitya's early death in 1925 precipitated a crisis of faith and a transformative experience of grief and awakening that he described in his writings.

Dissolution of the Order of the Star

On 3 August 1929, at the opening of the Star Camp at Ommen in the Netherlands, Krishnamurti dissolved the Order before thousands of members, returning its assets. In the speech he stated: "I maintain that Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect." The act severed his ties with the Theosophical Society's expectations, though he remained grateful to Besant. He spent the rest of his life as an independent speaker.

Teaching

Krishnamurti emphasised total attention and self-observation: the observer is the observed; thought, memory, and images condition perception; freedom lies in choiceless awareness of fear, desire, sorrow, and the self as they arise, rather than in methods, systems, or gradual self-improvement. He rejected organised religion and spiritual authority and held dialogues with figures including the physicist David Bohm, with whom he explored the limits of thought (published as The Ending of Time, 1985), the biologist Rupert Sheldrake, and Buddhist and Hindu scholars. His recorded talks and dialogues fill dozens of books and archives of audio and video.

Schools and foundations

Schools inspired by his view of education — the whole development of the human being rather than academic achievement alone — include Rishi Valley School and Rajghat Besant School in India, Brockwood Park School in England, and Oak Grove School in Ojai, California. The Krishnamurti Foundations in India, the UK, the US, and Spain maintain his archives and publish his work in many languages.

Later life and death

Krishnamurti continued annual speaking tours between India, Europe, and California into his ninetieth year. He died of pancreatic cancer at his home in Ojai on 17 February 1986. Biographies, most comprehensively by Mary Lutyens (with whom he had a lifelong association), and later controversial accounts concerning his private relationships, document a life that remained subject to public fascination and debate.
