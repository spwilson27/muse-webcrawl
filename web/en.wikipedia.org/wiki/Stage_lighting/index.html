Stage lighting

Stage lighting is the craft of lighting in theatre, dance, opera, concerts and other live performance. Beyond simply making performers visible, stage lighting shapes mood, focuses the audience's attention, models form through light and shadow, and signals time, place and atmosphere. Several instruments are typically combined with gels (colour filters), gobos (pattern templates) and control systems to achieve the designer's intentions.

History

Early theatres performed outdoors in daylight. Indoor performance brought candles and oil lamps, followed in the nineteenth century by gaslight — which allowed centralized control from a "gas table" — and by limelight, an intense spotlight produced by heating quicklime. Electric lighting transformed the art from the 1880s onward; visionaries such as Adolphe Appia and Edward Gordon Craig reconceived light as a sculptural, expressive medium, and designers such as Jean Rosenthal and Tharon Musser professionalized lighting design in the twentieth century. The invention of the computerized memory console and, later, automated (moving) fixtures and LED instruments, greatly expanded flexibility.

Instruments

Common fixtures include ellipsoidal reflector spotlights (profiles) for sharp, shaped beams; Fresnel lanterns for soft-edged washes; PAR cans for intense, simple beams; cyclorama lights for backdrops; followspots operated manually to track a performer; and automated moving lights that pan, tilt, change colour and project patterns under console control. Lighting is planned on a light plot and executed through dimmers and control protocols, today most commonly DMX512.

Functions and design

The classic functions of stage lighting are visibility, selective focus, modelling (revelation of form), mood and composition. Designers choose angle, colour, intensity, movement and distribution for each cue; front light flatters but flattens, side light sculpts dancers' bodies, backlight separates figures from the background, and saturated colour washes establish emotional tone. The lighting designer collaborates with the director and the set and costume designers, and the design is realized by master electricians and board operators.
