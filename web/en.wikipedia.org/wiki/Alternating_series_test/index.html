Alternating series test

The alternating series test, also called the Leibniz test or Leibniz criterion, is a method in mathematical analysis for proving that an alternating series converges. It applies to series of the form Σ (−1)n bn (or with the signs shifted), where all bn are positive.

Statement

If the sequence bn decreases monotonically to a limit of zero, then the alternating series converges. The standard proof observes that the even partial sums form an increasing sequence and the odd partial sums a decreasing one, both bounded, so they converge to a common limit. A useful consequence is the alternating series estimation theorem: the error in stopping at any term is no larger than the magnitude of the first omitted term, and has its sign.

Examples and limits

The test proves convergence of the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, whose sum is ln 2; that series converges only conditionally, since the harmonic series itself diverges. The monotonicity condition matters: alternating series whose terms tend to zero non-monotonically can diverge. The test gives no information when its hypotheses fail and says nothing about absolute convergence.
