Mary Mallon (September 23, 1869 – November 11, 1938), commonly known as Typhoid Mary, was an Irish-born American cook who is believed to have infected between 51 and 122 people with typhoid fever, three of whom are known to have died. She was the first person in the United States identified as an asymptomatic carrier of Salmonella typhi. She worked as a cook for wealthy families in the New York City area, spreading the disease through the food she prepared.

Identification

In 1906, sanitary engineer George Soper was hired to investigate a typhoid outbreak at a rented summer home in Oyster Bay, Long Island. Tracing the household staff, Soper identified Mallon as the probable source and discovered that outbreaks had followed her through several previous employers. When Soper approached her, Mallon refused to provide samples and resisted examination. In 1907, the New York City health department had her arrested and forcibly quarantined on North Brother Island; testing showed that her gallbladder harboured typhoid bacteria.

Release and second quarantine

Mallon was released in 1910 on condition that she stop working as a cook. She initially complied but later returned to cooking under assumed names, including "Mary Brown". In 1915 she was linked to an outbreak at Sloane Hospital for Women in Manhattan that infected 25 people and killed two. She was arrested again and returned to quarantine on North Brother Island, where she spent the remaining 23 years of her life, dying in 1938 of pneumonia. Her case raised enduring questions about public health powers, civil liberties, and the rights of asymptomatic carriers.

Legacy

"Typhoid Mary" has become a colloquial term for anyone who, knowingly or unknowingly, spreads disease or something undesirable. Her story is frequently discussed in the context of medical ethics and public health law.
