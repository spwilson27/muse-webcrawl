Declaration of the Clergy of France

[Image: Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, drafter of the Declaration]
Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, bishop of Meaux, who drafted the Declaration

The Declaration of the Clergy of France was a document adopted on 19 March 1682 by an assembly of the French clergy, following a dispute between King Louis XIV and Pope Innocent XI over the régale, the king's right to revenues and patronage of vacant bishoprics. Drafted principally by Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, the Declaration set out the principles of Gallicanism in four articles that limited papal authority in France.

The Four Articles

  1. St Peter and his successors have power only in spiritual matters; kings are not subject to ecclesiastical authority in temporal affairs, cannot be deposed by the pope, and their subjects cannot be released from their obedience.
  2. The plenitude of papal power is limited by the decrees of the Council of Constance, which affirmed the authority of general councils.
  3. The exercise of papal power must respect the canons and the ancient customs and liberties of the Gallican Church.
  4. Decisions of the pope in matters of faith are not irreformable unless confirmed by the consent of the whole Church.

Consequences

The Declaration was registered by the Parlement of Paris and ordered to be taught in French seminaries. Innocent XI refused to confirm bishops who had participated in the assembly, leaving many French sees vacant and deepening the quarrel. In 1693, under Innocent XII, a compromise was reached: the bishops-elect wrote letters of apology and Louis XIV withdrew enforcement of the Declaration, though it was never formally repudiated and remained the charter of Gallican doctrine. It was invoked throughout the eighteenth century and influenced the Civil Constitution of the Clergy of 1790.
