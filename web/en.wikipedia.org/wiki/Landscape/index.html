A landscape is the visible features of an area of land, its landforms, and how they are integrated with natural or man-made features. A landscape includes the physical elements of geophysically defined landforms such as (ice-capped) mountains, hills, water bodies such as rivers, lakes, ponds and the sea, living elements of land cover including indigenous vegetation, human elements including different forms of land use, buildings, and structures, and transitory elements such as lighting and weather conditions.

Definitions and usage

The word comes from the Dutch landschap, originally denoting a painting of scenery and then the scenery itself; it entered English in the late 16th century. Combining both their physical origins and the cultural overlay of human presence, landscapes are studied in geography, ecology, and the arts. Geographers distinguish the natural landscape, largely unaffected by people, from the cultural landscape, shaped by human activity over time — a concept developed by Carl Sauer and the Berkeley School. The European Landscape Convention (2000) defines landscape as "an area, as perceived by people, whose character is the result of the action and interaction of natural and/or human factors."

Landscape in science

Landscape ecology studies how spatial patterns — patches, corridors, and mosaics of habitat — affect ecological processes. Geomorphology studies the formation and evolution of landforms. In evolutionary biology and optimization, the metaphor of a "fitness landscape" describes how outcomes vary across a space of possibilities.

Landscape in art

Landscape became an independent genre of painting in the Netherlands in the 17th century and in China centuries earlier as shan shui ("mountain-water") painting. Landscape also denotes a page or image orientation wider than it is tall, as opposed to portrait orientation.
