Counterculture of the 1960s

The counterculture of the 1960s was an anti-establishment cultural phenomenon that spread across much of the Western world between the mid-1960s and the mid-1970s. The movements — involving the baby boom generation's youth — rejected the social norms of the 1950s, opposed the Vietnam War, and championed civil rights, sexual liberation, environmentalism, and the exploration of consciousness through psychedelic drugs and Eastern spirituality.

The counterculture at a late-1960s music festival.

Origins

The counterculture grew from the Beat Generation of the 1950s, the civil rights movement, and the Free Speech Movement at Berkeley in 1964. Key gathering points included the Haight-Ashbury district of San Francisco, culminating in the "Summer of Love" of 1967, when some 100,000 young people converged on the city.

Key elements

  • Music — psychedelic and folk rock by acts such as the Grateful Dead, Jefferson Airplane, The Doors, Bob Dylan, and The Beatles; festivals like Monterey Pop (1967) and Woodstock (1969).
  • Opposition to the Vietnam War — mass protests, draft resistance, and the 1968 demonstrations at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago.
  • Psychedelics — experimentation with LSD, promoted by Timothy Leary's slogan "turn on, tune in, drop out."
  • Communal living and alternative media — communes, underground newspapers, and head shops.
  • New social movements — second-wave feminism, gay liberation (Stonewall, 1969), and the environmental movement.

Spread and decline

Countercultural movements appeared in Britain, France (May 1968), West Germany, Mexico, and elsewhere. By the mid-1970s the movement faded — through the end of the Vietnam draft, disillusionment after events like the Altamont concert and the Manson murders, and absorption of its styles into mainstream commercial culture — while leaving lasting effects on attitudes toward sex, dress, food, technology, and civil rights.
