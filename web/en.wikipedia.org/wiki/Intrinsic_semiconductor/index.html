Intrinsic semiconductor


Thermal excitation creates equal numbers of electrons and holes.

An intrinsic semiconductor, also called an undoped or i-type semiconductor, is a pure semiconductor without any significant dopant species present. Its electrical behaviour is determined by the properties of the pure material itself rather than by impurities.

Carrier behaviour

At absolute zero an intrinsic semiconductor behaves as an insulator. As temperature rises, thermal energy promotes electrons from the valence band across the band gap into the conduction band, leaving behind holes; the numbers of conduction electrons and holes are exactly equal, so the Fermi level lies near the middle of the band gap. The intrinsic carrier concentration depends strongly on temperature and on the size of the band gap — for silicon at room temperature it is roughly 1 × 1010 per cubic centimetre.

Contrast with extrinsic semiconductors

Deliberate doping produces extrinsic (n-type or p-type) semiconductors in which one carrier type predominates and conductivity is far higher and less temperature-sensitive at operating temperatures. Intrinsic material still matters in practice: near-intrinsic regions are used in PIN diodes and some power and detector devices.
