Horizontal stabilizer


The horizontal stabilizer of an airliner tail

A horizontal stabilizer is a fixed or movable aerodynamic surface on the tail of an aircraft that provides longitudinal (pitch) stability. Together with the vertical stabilizer it forms the tailplane or empennage. In most conventional aircraft the horizontal stabilizer is mounted at the rear of the fuselage and carries the elevators, the movable surfaces that control pitch.

Function

The horizontal stabilizer acts like a small wing. Because it sits far behind the centre of gravity, the lift (usually directed downward in conventional designs) that it generates counteracts pitching moments from the main wing, keeping the aircraft stable in pitch. Changing the elevator deflection alters the tail's lift and thus rotates the aircraft nose-up or nose-down. Many airliners use a trimmable horizontal stabilizer whose entire incidence angle can be adjusted in flight to set pitch trim over a wide range of speeds and centre-of-gravity positions.

Configurations

Horizontal stabilizers may be mounted low on the fuselage, partway up the fin (cruciform), or at the top of the vertical stabilizer (a T-tail, as on many regional jets). An all-moving tailplane, or stabilator, combines the stabilizer and elevator into a single surface and is common on supersonic military aircraft. Canard designs instead place the horizontal surface ahead of the main wing. Failures and runaway trim of horizontal stabilizers have figured in several notable accidents, including those involving the stabilizer jackscrew and, in the case of the Boeing 737 MAX, the MCAS flight control system.
