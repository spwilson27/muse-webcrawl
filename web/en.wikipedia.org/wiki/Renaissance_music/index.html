Renaissance music is traditionally understood to be the music of the Renaissance era in Western and Central Europe, spanning roughly 1400 to 1600. This was a period of growing richness in polyphonic style, the spread of music printing, and an increasing concern that music should express the texts it set.

Characteristics

Renaissance polyphony is characterised by smooth, flowing melodic lines, a preference for consonance, and the technique of imitation, in which voices enter in turn with the same melodic idea. The range of voices widened with the addition of a true bass part. Harmony was still governed by the church modes, though cadential practice increasingly foreshadowed the major–minor tonality of the Baroque. Composers paid growing attention to text setting, word painting, and (under Counter-Reformation pressure) textual intelligibility.

Sacred music

The Mass and the motet dominated sacred composition. Guillaume Du Fay, Johannes Ockeghem, Josquin des Prez, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, Orlande de Lassus, Tomás Luis de Victoria and William Byrd were leading figures. Techniques such as the cantus firmus Mass, the parody (imitation) Mass and musica ficta shaped the repertory. The Franco-Flemish school supplied choir composers to courts and cathedrals across Europe.

Secular music

Important secular genres included the madrigal in Italy and England, the French chanson, the German Lied, and the Spanish villancico. The madrigal in particular became a vehicle for expressive text depiction. Instrumental music also flourished: dances for lute, keyboard music, and consort music for viols and recorders, with composers such as John Dowland celebrated for lute songs and pavanes.

Developments

Ottaviano Petrucci's invention of music printing with movable type (the Harmonice Musices Odhecaton, 1501) made music far more widely available. The Reformation and Counter-Reformation each left marks: Luther's chorales, the English anthem, and the Council of Trent's debates over polyphony all shaped composition. Around 1600, the rise of monody, the basso continuo and opera marked the transition to the Baroque.
