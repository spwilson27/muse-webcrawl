International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia

The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) was a United Nations court established in 1993 to prosecute serious violations of international humanitarian law committed during the Yugoslav Wars, including genocide, crimes against humanity, grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions, and violations of the laws or customs of war.

Mandate and work

Created by Security Council Resolution 827 while the war in Bosnia was still raging, the ICTY was the first international war-crimes tribunal since Nuremberg and Tokyo. It indicted 161 people from all parties to the conflicts. Its trials prosecuted heads of state and senior commanders, most prominently Slobodan Milošević, president of Serbia and Yugoslavia (who died in custody in 2006 before judgment), Bosnian Serb leader Radovan Karadžić (sentenced to life imprisonment), and general Ratko Mladić (life imprisonment) for the Srebrenica genocide and other crimes.

Jurisprudence and legacy

The Tribunal produced landmark rulings: the Tadić appeals judgment defined armed conflict and expanded jurisdiction over internal wars; the Krstić case established the legal characterisation of the Srebrenica massacre as genocide; and the Čelebići and Furundžija judgments advanced the law on rape and sexual violence in war. The ICTY indicted its final fugitives by 2011, completed its work on 31 December 2017, and transferred remaining functions — appeals, retrials, and supervision of sentences — to the MICT. It is widely credited with reviving international criminal law and paving the way for the permanent International Criminal Court.

Criticism

The Tribunal faced accusations of selective justice and anti-Serb bias, especially after acquittals of Croatian and Kosovar accused, as well as criticism of its cost (over $2 billion) and distance from affected communities, partly addressed by outreach programmes in the region.
