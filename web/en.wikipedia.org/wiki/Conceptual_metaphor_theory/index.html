Conceptual metaphor theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conceptual metaphor theory, or cognitive metaphor theory, is a framework in cognitive linguistics holding that metaphor is not merely a decorative feature of language but a fundamental mechanism of thought: people understand one conceptual domain (the target) in terms of another (the source). The theory was set out by George Lakoff and Mark Johnson in Metaphors We Live By (1980).

Mappings

A conceptual metaphor is a systematic set of correspondences (a mapping) between source and target domains, conventionally written in small capitals, e.g. ARGUMENT IS WAR ("he attacked my position", "defend a claim"), TIME IS MONEY ("spend time", "waste an hour"), LIFE IS A JOURNEY, and THEORIES ARE BUILDINGS ("a solid foundation", "the theory collapsed"). The linguistic expressions are surface evidence for the underlying mapping. Metaphors are typically unidirectional: abstract targets are structured by more concrete, embodied sources.

Primary metaphors and embodiment

Joseph Grady's theory of primary metaphors proposes that complex metaphors are assembled from primitive ones arising directly from embodied experience in childhood — e.g. AFFECTION IS WARMTH (from being held), KNOWING IS SEEING, MORE IS UP (from pouring liquid into a container). On this view, metaphorical thought is grounded in recurring correlations in sensorimotor experience.

Invariance and metonymy

The invariance principle holds that mappings preserve the cognitive topology (image-schema structure) of the source domain consistent with the target. Conceptual metaphor theory is often paired with conceptual metonymy (part-for-whole and related mappings within one domain) and contrasted with — or extended by — conceptual blending theory.

Criticism and evidence

Critics have raised concerns about circularity (inferring metaphors from language, then using them to explain language), the difficulty of falsifying specific mappings, and cross-cultural variation. Supporters cite converging evidence from gesture studies, psycholinguistic experiments on processing time, sign languages, and the systematicity of polysemy across languages.
