GSK3

Glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK-3 or GSK3) is a serine/threonine protein kinase that mediates the addition of phosphate molecules onto serine and threonine amino acid residues. First discovered in 1980 as a regulatory kinase for its namesake, glycogen synthase, GSK-3 has since been found to be a kinase for over one hundred different proteins in a variety of different pathways.

Isoforms

In mammals, GSK-3 is encoded by two genes, GSK3A (GSK-3α) and GSK3B (GSK-3β). GSK-3β in particular has been extensively studied in connection with type 2 diabetes, Alzheimer's disease, inflammation, cancer, and bipolar disorder. Unlike most kinases, GSK-3 is constitutively active in resting cells and is primarily regulated by inhibition — for example by phosphorylation of an N-terminal serine (Ser9 in GSK-3β) downstream of insulin and growth-factor signalling via Akt.

Function

GSK-3 is a central component of the Wnt signalling pathway: in the absence of Wnt, GSK-3 within the "destruction complex" phosphorylates β-catenin, targeting it for degradation; Wnt signalling inhibits this process, allowing β-catenin to accumulate. GSK-3 typically phosphorylates substrates that have been "primed" by prior phosphorylation four residues downstream. It also phosphorylates the microtubule-associated protein tau, linking it to the neurofibrillary tangles of Alzheimer's disease.

Inhibition

Lithium, used for decades in bipolar disorder, is a direct inhibitor of GSK-3, an action proposed to contribute to its therapeutic effect. Numerous pharmaceutical GSK-3 inhibitors have been investigated for diabetes, neurodegeneration, and cancer.
