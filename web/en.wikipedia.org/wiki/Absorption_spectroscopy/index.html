Absorption spectroscopy

Absorption spectroscopy is a spectroscopic technique that measures the absorption of radiation as a function of frequency or wavelength, due to its interaction with a sample. The sample selectively absorbs energy at frequencies matching transitions between its quantum mechanical states, producing a characteristic spectrum used to identify and quantify substances.

Principles

When radiation passes through a sample, photons whose energy matches the gap between a ground state and an excited state are absorbed. The transmitted intensity follows the Beer–Lambert law, in which absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing species and the path length. Absorption spectra are often plotted as absorbance or transmittance against wavelength.

Types

Common forms include ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy of electronic transitions, infrared spectroscopy of molecular vibrations (including FTIR), atomic absorption spectroscopy for elemental analysis, nuclear magnetic resonance (technically an absorption method), and X-ray absorption spectroscopy. In astronomy, absorption lines in stellar spectra reveal the composition, temperature and motion of stars and interstellar gas; the dark Fraunhofer lines in the solar spectrum are a classic example.

Applications

Applications range from chemical analysis, environmental monitoring and pharmaceutical quality control to remote sensing of planetary atmospheres and the detection of exoplanet atmospheres by transit spectroscopy.
