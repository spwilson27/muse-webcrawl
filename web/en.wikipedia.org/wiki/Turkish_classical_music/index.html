Turkish classical music (Turkish: Türk sanat müziği, "Turkish art music") is the courtly, urban art-music tradition that developed in the Ottoman Empire, centred on Istanbul. It is monophonic and modal, organised around melodic modes called makam and rhythmic cycles called usul, and it embraces a large repertory of instrumental and vocal compositions refined over several centuries.

History

The tradition drew on Byzantine, Arabic, and Persian musical practices, crystallising at the Ottoman court from the 16th century onward. Dimitrie Cantemir, a Moldavian prince at the Istanbul court around 1700, devised a notation system and wrote a foundational treatise. Composers such as Buhurizade Mustafa Itri and, in the 19th century, Hacı Arif Bey shaped the repertory of peşrevs, bestes, and şarkıs. In the early Turkish Republic the genre was briefly discouraged in favour of Westernisation, but it survived through radio broadcasts and conservatoires and remains a living tradition.

Theory

The makam system defines scales built from intervals measured in commas, with characteristic melodic progressions (seyir) that distinguish modes beyond their pitches alone. Rhythmic cycles (usul) range from short 2-beat patterns to monumental cycles of more than 100 beats used in ceremonial works. Performance is led by instruments such as the ud (short-necked lute), tanbur (long-necked lute), ney (end-blown flute), kanun (zither), and kemençe (spike fiddle).

Forms

Concerts traditionally present a fasıl, a suite of pieces in a single makam alternating instrumental works — the peşrev and saz semaisi — with vocal compositions such as the beste, ağır semai, and the lighter şarkı song form, linked by improvised taksim solos.
