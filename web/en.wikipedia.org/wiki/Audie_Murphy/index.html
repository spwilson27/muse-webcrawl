Audie Leon Murphy (20 June 1925 – 28 May 1971) was an American soldier, actor, and songwriter. He was one of the most decorated American combat soldiers of World War II, receiving every military combat award for valor available from the United States Army, as well as French and Belgian awards for heroism. Murphy received the Medal of Honor for valor that he demonstrated at the age of 19 for single-handedly holding off a company of German soldiers for an hour at the Colmar Pocket in France in January 1945, then leading a successful counterattack while wounded and out of ammunition.

Early life

Murphy was born on 20 June 1925 in Kingston, Texas, to sharecropper parents Emmett Berry Murphy and Josie Bell Murphy (née Killian), in a family of twelve children of Irish descent. The Murphys moved to Farmersville, and later to Greenville. Abandoned by his father, Murphy's mother died when he was sixteen, and he was forced to help raise his younger siblings, dropping out of school in the fifth grade to work picking cotton and other farm jobs.

World War II service

After the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, Murphy tried to enlist but was turned away by the Army, Navy, and Marine Corps because he was underweight and underage. After falsifying his birth certificate with his sister's help to appear a year older, he enlisted in the Army on 30 June 1942. He served with Company B, 15th Infantry Regiment, 3rd Infantry Division, seeing action in Tunisia, Sicily, Italy, France, and Germany.

Medal of Honor action

On 26 January 1945, near Holtzwihr, France, Murphy's company was attacked by six German tanks and waves of infantry. Murphy ordered his men to withdraw to prepared positions in a woods, while he remained forward at his command post and continued to give fire directions to artillery by telephone. When the enemy tanks and infantry closed to within ten yards, he climbed onto a burning American tank destroyer and fired its .50-caliber machine gun at the German troops, killing or wounding some 50 of them despite being wounded in the leg. He held his position for nearly an hour, stopping the German advance, and then organized a counterattack that drove the enemy from Holtzwihr.

Postwar career

After the war, Murphy pursued an acting career, appearing in more than 40 feature films, mostly Westerns. He played himself in the 1955 film To Hell and Back, based on his 1949 autobiography of the same name. He was also a successful country music songwriter, and his songs were recorded by artists including Dean Martin and Eddy Arnold. Murphy suffered from what would now be diagnosed as post-traumatic stress disorder, and he slept with a loaded handgun under his pillow; he spoke out publicly about his condition to draw attention to the problems of returning veterans.

Death and legacy

On 28 May 1971, Murphy was killed when the private plane in which he was a passenger crashed into Brush Mountain, near Catawba, Virginia, in poor weather. He was interred with military honors at Arlington National Cemetery. His gravesite is one of the most visited at the cemetery, second only to that of President John F. Kennedy.
