Complex logarithm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a complex logarithm is an inverse function of the complex exponential function, analogous to the natural logarithm ln x for real numbers. For a nonzero complex number z written in polar form z = reiθ, a logarithm of z is

log z = ln r + i(θ + 2πk),

where k is any integer. Because the angle θ is determined only up to multiples of 2π, the complex logarithm is a multivalued function: every nonzero complex number has infinitely many logarithms.

Principal value

To obtain an ordinary (single-valued) function, one restricts the imaginary part to a fixed interval of length 2π, conventionally (−π, π]. The resulting principal value, denoted Log z, uses the principal argument Arg z ∈ (−π, π]:

Log z = ln |z| + i Arg z.

Log is holomorphic on the complex plane minus the non-positive real axis (the standard branch cut), where it is discontinuous: approaching a negative real number from above and below gives imaginary parts approaching π and −π respectively. More generally, any holomorphic branch of the logarithm can be defined on a domain that does not wind around the origin, and such a branch exists whenever the domain of the function omits a ray (or any curve) from 0 to infinity.

Properties

The familiar identity log(zw) = log z + log w holds only modulo 2πi for the multivalued logarithm, and can fail outright for the principal value. The complex logarithm is used to define complex powers, zw = ew Log z, and appears throughout complex analysis, for example in evaluating integrals, conformal mapping and the theory of the Riemann surface of the logarithm, on which the function becomes single-valued by "unwrapping" the plane into an infinite helicoid of sheets.

Inverse relations

For every nonzero z, eLog z = z, but Log(ew) equals w only when the imaginary part of w lies in the principal range. The derivative of the principal branch is d/dz Log z = 1/z, matching the real case.
