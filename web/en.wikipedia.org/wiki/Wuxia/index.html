Wuxia

Wuxia (武俠, literally "martial heroes") is a genre of Chinese fiction concerning the adventures of martial artists in ancient China. Although wuxia is a genre in modern popular culture — in literature, films, television, comics (manhua), and video games — its roots lie in the youxia (wandering knight-errant) tradition of early Chinese literature more than two millennia old. Wuxia heroes typically belong to the jianghu, a fictionalised milieu of martial arts sects, clans, and wanderers parallel to but outside the authority of the imperial court, and they are bound by the code of xia: chivalry, altruism, and the righting of wrongs with the sword.

History

The historian Sima Qian devoted a chapter of the Records of the Grand Historian (c. 100 BCE) to the youxia, wandering swordsmen who kept their word and aided the oppressed. Tang dynasty chuanqi tales introduced supernatural martial feats, and Ming dynasty novels such as Water Margin popularised bands of outlaw heroes. In the early twentieth century the genre flourished in Republican-era magazines; after it was suppressed in mainland China following 1949, wuxia writing thrived in Hong Kong and Taiwan, where Liang Yusheng, Jin Yong (Louis Cha), and Gu Long modernised it into its classic form. Jin Yong's novels — including The Legend of the Condor Heroes and The Smiling, Proud Wanderer — have sold over 100 million copies.

Conventions

Wuxia heroes cultivate martial arts (wugong) through internal energy (neigong) and qi manipulation, achieving feats such as qinggong — lightness skill that lets them leap across rooftops and skim water. Stories feature secret manuals, rival sects (such as Shaolin, Wudang, and Emei), poison and antidote plots, and romances amid clan feuds and quests for supremacy in the jianghu. Unlike the later xianxia genre, wuxia is usually set in a recognisably historical, non-mythological China and its heroes remain mortal.

Film and television

Wuxia cinema began with silent films such as Burning of the Red Lotus Temple (1928). Directors including King Hu (Come Drink with Me, 1966; A Touch of Zen, 1971) and Chang Cheh elevated the genre, and Ang Lee's Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000), adapted from Wang Dulu's novel, brought wuxia to a global audience, winning the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. Zhang Yimou's Hero (2002) and House of Flying Daggers (2004) followed.
