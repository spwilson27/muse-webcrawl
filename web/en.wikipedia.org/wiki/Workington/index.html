Workington is a coastal town and civil parish in the Cumberland district of Cumbria, England. The town is at the mouth of the River Derwent on the Irish Sea coast. It had a population of about 25,000 at the 2011 census. Historically in Cumberland, Workington was a busy coal-mining and steel-making town, and its port remains an important part of the local economy.

History

The area around Workington has been settled since Roman times; a Roman fort stood nearby at Burrow Walls, and the town's name is thought to derive from Old English, meaning the settlement of Weorc's people. Workington developed as a small fishing and trading port, but its fortunes changed with the exploitation of local coal measures from the 17th century onwards. Under the patronage of the Curwen family, who lived at Workington Hall, coal was shipped in large quantities to Ireland, and the town grew rapidly during the Industrial Revolution.

Iron and steel making became dominant in the 19th and 20th centuries. The Moss Bay works produced steel rails exported around the world. The decline of heavy industry in the later 20th century hit the town hard, with the closure of the steelworks and the last deep coal mine, Haig Colliery at nearby Whitehaven, marking the end of an era. In November 2009 Workington was severely affected by flooding when the River Derwent burst its banks; several bridges collapsed or were condemned, including the Northside Bridge, whose collapse killed police constable Bill Barker, who was directing traffic when the structure gave way.

Geography

Workington lies on the west Cumbrian coastal plain, roughly 32 miles (51 km) south-west of Carlisle and about 8 miles (13 km) north of Whitehaven. The River Derwent flows through the town to its estuary at the harbour. The Lake District fells rise to the east.

Economy

The Port of Workington is one of the larger ports on England's north-west coast, handling cargoes including bulk goods and materials for local industry. Major employers in the area include the Sellafield nuclear site further down the coast, to which many residents commute, as well as manufacturing, retail and public services.

Transport

Workington railway station is on the Cumbrian Coast Line, with services to Carlisle and Barrow-in-Furness. A temporary station, Workington North, was built in a week after the 2009 floods severed road links across the Derwent. The A66 trunk road connects the town eastwards to Penrith and the M6 motorway.

Sport

The town is home to Workington Town, a rugby league club founded in 1945 that plays at Derwent Park and won the Challenge Cup in 1952, and to Workington A.F.C., nicknamed the Reds, an association football club based at Borough Park. Speedway was also staged at Derwent Park by the Workington Comets.

Notable people

The Curwen family produced several MPs for the town, including John Christian Curwen, an agricultural reformer. The town was also associated with the Fletcher Christian family; Christian, leader of the mutiny on HMS Bounty, was born at nearby Eaglesfield.
