Resampling (statistics)

Resampling methods in statistics are procedures that use a single observed sample to draw additional conclusions about a population by repeatedly drawing new samples — either from the data themselves or from a model — and recomputing a statistic of interest. They allow estimation of the sampling distribution of almost any statistic using random sampling methods, often without requiring strong parametric assumptions.

Main techniques

  • Bootstrapping — sampling with replacement from the observed data to estimate the distribution of a statistic, its standard error, bias and confidence intervals.
  • Jackknife — systematically recomputing an estimate by leaving out one observation at a time, used to estimate bias and variance.
  • Permutation tests — exact or Monte Carlo tests that recompute a test statistic over all (or random) rearrangements of the data under the null hypothesis.
  • Cross-validation — partitioning data into subsets to assess how a predictive model generalises, rotating which subset is used for validation.
  • Monte Carlo simulation — generating samples from a specified distribution to approximate distributions of statistics.

Advantages and limitations

Resampling methods make few distributional assumptions and can be applied to statistics (such as the median or a correlation) whose sampling distributions are hard to derive analytically. Their main limitations are computational cost and the fact that the original sample must adequately represent the population; they cannot repair a biased or unrepresentative sample.
