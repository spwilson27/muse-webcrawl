British ensigns are flags flown by British ships, each consisting of a field of red, blue or white with the Union Flag in the canton. The system descends from the colour-coded squadrons of the 17th-century Royal Navy. Today the three plain ensigns have distinct legal uses, and many "defaced" ensigns — bearing a badge or emblem in the fly — are used by government departments, colonies, territories and clubs.

The three ensigns

The Red Ensign (nicknamed the "Red Duster") is the civil ensign, flown by British merchant ships and private vessels. The Blue Ensign is flown by government-owned non-naval vessels, by ships commanded by retired Royal Navy officers holding warrants, and serves as the basis for many colonial and departmental ensigns. The White Ensign, with the red cross of St George superimposed on the field, is reserved to the Royal Navy and the Royal Yacht Squadron.

History

In the 17th century the Royal Navy was organised into red, white and blue squadrons, each flying the corresponding ensign, with seniority in that order (red most senior). The White Ensign was initially distinguished by the cross. In 1864 an Admiralty reform assigned the ensigns their modern roles: white to the Royal Navy, red to the merchant service, and blue to government and reserve vessels. Colonial governments were authorised to deface the Blue or Red Ensign with local arms, giving rise to the familiar flags of many British colonies, several of which survive as the flags of overseas territories and, in modified form, of countries such as Australia and New Zealand.

Usage today

British-registered vessels must fly the Red Ensign or a permitted defaced variant. Defaced ensigns are used by organisations including the Royal National Lifeboat Institution, Trinity House, HM Coastguard and certain privileged yacht clubs, some of which fly special defaced red or blue ensigns or the plain Blue Ensign by warrant. The RAF and civil aviation have separate ensigns (sky blue and dark blue respectively, with roundel devices).
