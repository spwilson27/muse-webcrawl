Eid al-Adha

Eid al-Adha
Official nameʿĪd al-ʾAḍḥā — "Festival of the Sacrifice"
TypeIslamic festival
Date10 Dhu al-Hijjah (last month of the Islamic calendar), lasting 3–4 days
ObservancesPrayer, animal sacrifice (qurbani), charity, feasting, visiting
Related toHajj; Eid al-Fitr

Eid al-Adha (Arabic: عيد الأضحى, "Festival of the Sacrifice") is one of the two major festivals of Islam, celebrated on the 10th day of Dhu al-Hijjah, the final month of the Islamic lunar calendar, and coinciding with the culmination of the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca. It honours the prophet Ibrahim's (Abraham's) willingness to sacrifice his son in obedience to God's command — in Islamic tradition the son is usually identified as Ismaʿil (Ishmael) — before God provided a ram to sacrifice in his place.

Observances

The festival begins with a special congregational prayer (salat al-Eid) held in mosques and open grounds after sunrise, preceded by the recitation of the takbir. Muslims dress in their finest clothes, visit family and friends, exchange the greeting "Eid Mubarak" ("Blessed Eid"), and give gifts and money to children.

The sacrifice

Those who can afford it sacrifice a livestock animal — a sheep, goat, cow or camel — in commemoration of Ibrahim's sacrifice. The meat is traditionally divided into three parts: one for the family, one for relatives and friends, and one for the poor, making the festival a major occasion of charitable giving in spirit as well as practice. Pilgrims performing Hajj carry out the sacrifice as part of the rites, following the Day of Arafah (9 Dhu al-Hijjah), when pilgrims gather on the plain of Arafat.

Duration and public holidays

Eid al-Adha is a public holiday across the Muslim world, lasting from one day to more than a week depending on the country, and is widely observed by diaspora communities elsewhere. Because it is dated by the lunar calendar, it falls about 11 days earlier each solar year.

Names

The festival is known by many regional names, including Kurban Bayramı (Turkish), Eid-e Qorban (Persian), Bakra Eid or Bari Eid (South Asia, "Goat Eid"/"Greater Eid" — the "greater" of the two Eids) and Tabaski in West Africa.
