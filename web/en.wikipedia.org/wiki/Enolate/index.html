Enolate

An enolate is an anion formed by deprotonation of the α-carbon of a carbonyl compound — an aldehyde, ketone, ester or related species. The negative charge is delocalised between the α-carbon and the carbonyl oxygen, so enolates behave as ambident nucleophiles, reacting at carbon or at oxygen depending on conditions. Enolates are the anionic counterparts of enols and are among the most important intermediates in carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions in organic synthesis.

Formation

Because the α-hydrogens of ketones and aldehydes are weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 19–20 in water), enolates can be generated with strong, non-nucleophilic bases such as lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) in THF at low temperature, giving the kinetic enolate (from the less substituted α-position). Weaker bases such as alkoxides or hydroxide give low equilibrium concentrations of the more substituted thermodynamic enolate, sufficient for reactions such as the aldol condensation. Esters, amides and nitriles form analogous anions; malonates and β-keto esters, with pKa near 11–13, are deprotonated by mild bases.

Geometry and reactivity

Enolates adopt (E) or (Z) geometry about their C=C bond, and this geometry controls the stereochemical outcome of subsequent reactions — notably in the Zimmerman–Traxler model of the aldol reaction, where (Z)-enolates give syn products and (E)-enolates anti products. Preformed enolates, including silyl enol ethers and boron enolates, are widely used for controlled, stereoselective synthesis.

Reactions

Key enolate reactions include alkylation with alkyl halides, the aldol reaction and condensation, the Claisen and Dieckmann condensations of esters, Michael (conjugate) addition, the Mannich reaction (via iminium ions), and α-halogenation. Alkylation typically occurs at carbon with "hard" conditions favouring some O-alkylation; control of regiochemistry and stereochemistry in enolate chemistry is a central theme of modern synthesis.
