Edwardian era

The Edwardian era in the United Kingdom is the period covering the reign of King Edward VII (1901–1910), and is sometimes extended to include the years up to the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. It followed the Victorian era and preceded the Georgian era of George V.

Society and politics

The era is often remembered nostalgically as a golden summer afternoon of garden parties and imperial confidence before the catastrophe of 1914, though it was also a time of sharp social tension: a powerful labour movement and new trade unionism, militant suffragette campaigns for women's votes, the constitutional crisis over Lloyd George's "People's Budget" of 1909 (which led to the Parliament Act 1911 curbing the House of Lords), agitation over Irish Home Rule, and strikes and industrial unrest.

Liberal governments from 1906 introduced foundational welfare reforms: old-age pensions (1908), labour exchanges, and the National Insurance Act 1911 — the beginnings of the British welfare state.

Culture

Edwardian culture saw the flourishing of writers such as H. G. Wells, John Galsworthy, Arnold Bennett, E. M. Forster and Rudyard Kipling; the music of Elgar; popular music hall; and the spread of new technologies — motor cars, aeroplanes, cinema, telephones and electric light. Fashion relaxed from Victorian stiffness, while the arts moved towards modernism.
