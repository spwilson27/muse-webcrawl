Sin tax

A sin tax (also known as a sumptuary tax) is an excise tax specifically levied on certain goods deemed harmful to society and individuals, for example alcohol and tobacco, drugs, candies, soft drinks, fast food, coffee, sugar, gambling, and pornography. In contrast to Pigovian taxes, which are aimed at internalising the externalities imposed by certain goods, sin taxes are often used purely as a source of revenue, and are often popular because they affect only those who use the taxed products or engage in the taxed behaviour.

Justification and criticism

Supporters argue that sin taxes discourage harmful consumption, raise revenue for public health programmes, and help offset the social costs of the taxed behaviour, such as healthcare spending attributable to smoking or alcohol abuse. Tobacco taxes are widely regarded by health economists as among the most effective measures for reducing smoking.

Critics respond that such taxes are regressive, falling disproportionately on low-income consumers; that they can encourage smuggling and illicit markets when set too high; and that governments may become dependent on the revenue, creating a conflict with the stated health purpose. Behavioural economists also debate whether such taxes overcorrect, since some of the harm is borne by the consumer themselves rather than third parties.

Examples

Examples include cigarette and alcohol excise duties in many countries, sugar-sweetened beverage taxes such as the United Kingdom's Soft Drinks Industry Levy (2018), and taxes on gambling winnings and cannabis in jurisdictions where it is legal.
