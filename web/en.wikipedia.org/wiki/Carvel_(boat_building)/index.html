Carvel built, or carvel construction, is a method of boat building in which the hull planks are laid edge to edge and fastened to a rigid frame, producing a smooth hull surface. It contrasts with clinker (lapstrake) construction, in which the planks overlap one another.

Method

In traditional carvel building, frames (ribs) are erected first on the keel, and planks are steamed, bent and fastened to them so that each plank's edge meets its neighbour flush. The seams are then caulked — driven with oakum (tarred fibre) and sealed with pitch or, in modern practice, flexible sealant — to make the hull watertight. Because the planking does not overlap, a carvel hull presents a smooth surface to the water, reducing drag, and the structure can be scaled up to very large vessels; it also lends itself to diagonal and double-diagonal planking and to cold-moulded and strip-planked variants in modern wooden boatbuilding.

History

Edge-to-edge planking is ancient — Egyptian and Mediterranean ships of antiquity were built shell-first with mortise-and-tenon joined planks — but frame-first carvel construction developed in the Mediterranean in the later Middle Ages and spread to northern Europe in the 15th and 16th centuries. It made possible the great ocean-going ships of the age of sail, such as the carrack and the galleon (the word "carvel" is related to "caravel", the Portuguese ship type), displacing the clinker-built tradition of the Viking and Hanseatic worlds for large vessels, though clinker survived for small craft.
