Notre-Dame school

The Notre-Dame school refers to a group of composers working at or near the Cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris from about 1160 to 1250, during the period known as the ars antiqua. They are credited with the first major repertory of Western polyphonic music written by identifiable composers and preserved in substantial manuscripts.

Composers

The principal source for the school is the treatise of an English student known as Anonymous IV, who names two masters: Léonin (Magister Leoninus), credited with the Magnus liber organi, a great book of two-voice organum for the liturgical year; and Pérotin (Perotinus), who revised the book and composed organum in three and four voices, including the celebrated four-voice works Viderunt omnes and Sederunt principes, probably performed at Notre-Dame around 1198–1199.

Music and notation

The school's genres included organum, conductus and the early motet. Its composers developed modal (rhythmic) notation, the first system in Western music to specify rhythm systematically by patterned ligatures. Manuscripts preserving the repertory include the Florence and Wolfenbüttel codices. The innovations of the Notre-Dame school laid the foundations for the motet and for measured polyphony in the centuries that followed.
