Igapó

Igapó forest flooded by blackwater along the Rio Negro.

Igapó (from Old Tupi: "root forest") is a word used in Brazil for a type of permanently or seasonally flooded forest that grows along blackwater rivers in the Amazon biome. These forests and similar swamp forests are seasonally inundated with freshwater, the trees standing in water for months each year as river levels rise by many metres.

Characteristics

Because blackwater rivers such as the Rio Negro are acidic and nutrient-poor, leached of minerals from sandy soils, igapó forests are less species-rich than the whitewater floodplain forests known as várzea, but they host a distinctive, highly adapted flora. Trees tolerate prolonged submersion of their roots; their seeds are often dispersed by fruit-eating fish (such as the tambaqui) that swim into the forest during the flood season and feed on fallen fruit — a relationship called ichthyochory.

Ecology and fauna

During high water, igapó becomes an aquatic habitat used by fish for feeding and breeding, river dolphins, caimans and aquatic birds; during the dry season, the exposed forest floor supports terrestrial animals. The forests occur along the Rio Negro and its tributaries, the Jaú, and other clear- and blackwater systems. Large protected tracts exist in Jaú National Park and the Anavilhanas archipelago.

Threats

Igapó forests are threatened by hydroelectric dams that alter flood pulses, by gold mining that introduces mercury into blackwater systems, and by climate-driven changes in flood timing and depth that the slow-growing trees cannot track.
