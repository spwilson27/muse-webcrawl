Medicinal chemistry

Medicinal chemistry combines synthesis with biological evaluation of drug candidates

Medicinal chemistry is a scientific discipline at the intersection of chemistry and pharmacology involved with designing, developing, and synthesising pharmaceutical drugs. It combines elements of organic chemistry with computational chemistry, pharmacology, and the biological sciences to discover molecules that can be developed into medicines and to optimise their properties.

Drug discovery process

Medicinal chemists work in multidisciplinary teams. After a biological target is validated, screening of compound libraries identifies "hits"; chemical modification of these gives "lead" compounds, which are iteratively optimised for potency and selectivity against the target while also balancing absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties. Successful optimisation yields a development candidate that proceeds to preclinical and clinical testing.

Key concepts include structure–activity relationships (SAR), which map how chemical structure changes affect biological activity; pharmacophores, the spatial arrangement of features a molecule needs for activity; Lipinski's rule of five, guidelines for oral bioavailability; and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modelling. Modern practice also employs fragment-based drug discovery, DNA-encoded libraries, structure-based design using X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, and machine learning.

History

The discipline grew from 19th-century work isolating active principles from plants — morphine, quinine, and salicylic acid among them — and the synthetic dye industry. Paul Ehrlich's concept of the "magic bullet" and his development of arsphenamine (Salvarsan) for syphilis are often taken as founding moments. The 20th century brought sulfonamides, penicillin and its semi-synthetic descendants, and the rational design of drugs such as cimetidine and the statins.

Education and profession

Medicinal chemists typically train in organic or pharmaceutical chemistry. The field is practised mainly in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries and in academic drug discovery centres, and is represented by journals such as the Journal of Medicinal Chemistry.
