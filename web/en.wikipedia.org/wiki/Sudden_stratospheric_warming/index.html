Sudden stratospheric warming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schematic of a sudden stratospheric warming disrupting the polar vortex.

A sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) is an event in which polar stratospheric temperatures rise by several tens of kelvins (up to increases of about 50 °C) over the course of a few days. The warming is preceded by a slowdown and then reversal of the westerly winds of the winter polar vortex. Sudden stratospheric warmings were first observed in 1952 by Richard Scherhag using radiosonde measurements over Berlin.

Classification

Major warmings occur when the zonal-mean zonal winds at 60°N and 10 hPa reverse from westerly to easterly and the polar vortex splits or is displaced; in the Northern Hemisphere major events occur on average about six times per decade. Minor warmings involve strong temperature rises and a weakening of the vortex without a full wind reversal. Final warmings mark the seasonal transition to the summer circulation.

Causes

SSWs are driven by upward-propagating planetary-scale Rossby waves generated in the troposphere by mountains and land–sea contrasts. When these waves break in the stratosphere, they deposit westward momentum, decelerating the polar night jet and causing the polar cap air to descend and warm adiabatically.

Effects on surface weather

Following a major warming, anomalies typically propagate downward over several weeks, often producing a negative phase of the Arctic Oscillation and Northern Annular Mode. This is associated with blocking high pressure over the Arctic and high latitudes, and an increased likelihood of cold-air outbreaks over northern Europe, Asia, and eastern North America. A prominent example was the February 2018 event that preceded the "Beast from the East" cold spell in Europe.
