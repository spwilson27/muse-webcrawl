Flinders Petrie

Sir William Matthew Flinders Petrie (3 June 1853 – 28 July 1942) was an English Egyptologist and a pioneer of systematic methodology in archaeology and the preservation of artefacts. He excavated at many of the most important archaeological sites in Egypt, such as Abydos and Amarna, and is often regarded as the founder of modern scientific archaeology.

Flinders Petrie in later life

Early life and the pyramid survey

Petrie was educated at home and developed a passion for surveying and measurement. In 1880 he travelled to Egypt to survey the Great Pyramid of Giza, producing measurements so accurate that they corrected decades of speculation about the pyramid's dimensions and demolishing fringe theories about its construction.

Excavations

Working first for the Egypt Exploration Fund and later independently, Petrie excavated dozens of sites over more than four decades in Egypt. He developed sequence dating (seriation), a method of chronological dating based on the styles of pottery found in graves, first applied at Diospolis Parva. His discoveries included the Predynastic cemeteries of Naqada, the workers' town at Lahun, the Merneptah Stele (the earliest known mention of Israel), and, with his wife Hilda, work at Abydos and Amarna.

Later career

Petrie became the first Edwards Professor of Egyptian Archaeology and Philology at University College London in 1892. In 1923 he was knighted. From 1926 he worked mainly in Palestine, excavating at sites including Tell el-Ajjul. He died in Jerusalem in 1942 and donated his head to the Royal College of Surgeons in London, in keeping with his scientific interests; it was later buried in Jerusalem.

Legacy

Petrie's insistence on meticulous recording, total collection of finds, and rapid publication transformed field archaeology. The Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology in London, housing some 80,000 objects, bears his name.
