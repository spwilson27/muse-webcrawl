Neal Leon Cassady (8 February 1926 – 4 February 1968) was a major figure of the Beat Generation of the 1950s and the psychedelic and countercultural movements of the 1960s. Though he published little in his lifetime, his manic energy, rapid-fire conversation and legendary letters inspired his friends' writing: he is the model for Dean Moriarty, the hero of Jack Kerouac's On the Road (1957).

Early life

Cassady was born in Salt Lake City and raised largely in Denver, much of it alongside his alcoholic father in skid-row hotels, interspersed with reform school and petty crime — he was arrested repeatedly for car theft as a teenager. Largely self-educated in jail and public libraries, he arrived in New York in 1946, where he met Kerouac, Allen Ginsberg and William S. Burroughs, marrying the 16-year-old LuAnne Henderson. His 1947–48 cross-country journeys with Kerouac, celebrated in On the Road, became the defining myth of the Beats.

The Joan Anderson letter

Cassady's December 1950 letter to Kerouac — the "Joan Anderson letter", some 13,000 words recounting his love affairs in a breathless, confessional rush — convinced Kerouac to abandon conventional plotting for "spontaneous prose", directly shaping the composition of On the Road in April 1951. Long believed lost, the letter resurfaced in 2011. Ginsberg called Cassady's style the "secret of his influence".

Later life and the Pranksters

Cassady married Carolyn Robinson in 1948 (while still entangled with LuAnne), raised a family in California and worked as a Southern Pacific railroad brakeman — while also conducting the affair with Kerouac fictionalised in their novels. In 1958 he was imprisoned in San Quentin for two years over a small marijuana transaction. On release he joined Ken Kesey's Merry Pranksters, driving their psychedelic bus Furthur on the 1964 cross-country trip chronicled in Tom Wolfe's The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test, linking the Beats to the hippie era as "Cowboy Neal at the wheel".

Death and posthumous work

After years of amphetamine use and hard travelling, Cassady collapsed beside railroad tracks near San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, after a wedding party in February 1968, dying of exposure and organ failure days short of his 42nd birthday. His autobiographical novel The First Third was published posthumously in 1971, as were collections of his letters; Carolyn Cassady's memoir Off the Road (1990) gave her account of their unconventional household.
