Homeotherm

A homeotherm is an organism that maintains its body at a metabolically favourable temperature, largely independent of the temperature of its environment — a property called homeothermy. Homeothermy is one aspect of thermoregulation, and is contrasted with poikilothermy, in which internal temperature varies with ambient conditions. Most homeotherms are also endotherms, generating internal heat metabolically; the familiar terms "warm-blooded" and "cold-blooded" loosely correspond to these categories.

Mechanisms

Birds and mammals are the principal homeotherms, maintaining core temperatures typically between about 36 °C and 42 °C. They do so through metabolic heat production (including shivering and non-shivering thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue), insulation by fur, feathers or blubber, control of blood flow to the skin, and evaporative cooling by sweating or panting. Behavioural adjustments such as seeking shade or basking supplement physiological control.

Advantages and costs

Stable internal temperature keeps enzyme systems operating near their optimum and permits sustained activity in a wide range of climates, including at night and in winter. The cost is high energy expenditure: an endotherm may require roughly ten times the food intake of a similar-sized ectotherm, making homeothermy viable only where food supplies permit.

Exceptions and intermediates

Homeothermy is not absolute. Many mammals and some birds allow body temperature to fall during sleep, torpor or hibernation (heterothermy); the naked mole-rat is an almost poikilothermic mammal. Conversely, some non-avian animals — certain large dinosaurs are debated, leatherback turtles, tuna and lamnid sharks, and some insects in flight — maintain elevated or regional temperatures (regional endothermy), showing that the homeotherm–poikilotherm distinction is a continuum.
