The Tripiṭaka (Sanskrit) or Tipiṭaka (Pāli), "Three Baskets", is the traditional term for the Buddhist canon — the collections of scriptures regarded as authoritative records of the teaching of the Buddha. The three baskets are the Vinaya Piṭaka (monastic discipline), the Sutta Piṭaka (discourses of the Buddha and his close disciples), and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka (systematic philosophy and psychology).

Canons

Several canons survive. The Pāli Canon of the Theravāda school, preserved in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, is the only early canon to survive complete in an Indian language; its discourses include the Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṃyutta and Aṅguttara Nikāyas and the beloved verse collection Dhammapada. The Chinese canon (the Dàzàngjīng) preserves Āgama parallels plus the Mahāyāna sūtras and commentaries; the Tibetan canon is divided into the Kangyur (Buddha's word) and Tengyur (treatises). The Korean woodblock edition, the Tripiṭaka Koreana carved in the 13th century, is among the most complete and is stored at Haeinsa Temple.

History

The teachings were transmitted orally for centuries after the Buddha's death, recited communally at councils beginning, according to tradition, at Rājagṛha shortly after his passing. The Pāli Canon was first written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE. Modern scholarship treats the canons as layered records: broadly parallel discourse collections across schools point to a shared early stratum, while each canon also contains later material reflecting its tradition's development.
