Gallic invasion of the Balkans

The Gallic invasion of the Balkans was a mass migration of Celtic tribes into southeastern Europe in the early 3rd century BC. Driven by overpopulation, pressure from Germanic peoples, and the lure of wealth in the Hellenistic kingdoms, Celtic groups crossed the Danube and moved south through Illyria, Thrace and Macedon, culminating in the attack on Delphi in 279 BC and the settlement of the Galatians in central Anatolia.

Background

By the 4th century BC, Celtic peoples of the La Tène culture had expanded across much of Europe. Groups known to the Greeks as Galatai settled in the middle Danube valley, where tribes such as the Scordisci established fortified centres. The death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and the wars of his successors (the Diadochi) left the Balkan kingdoms politically fragmented and tempting targets for raiders.

The invasions

In 281 BC a Celtic force under a chieftain named Cambaules raided Thrace. The main invasion came in 279 BC, when three large tribal groupings moved south. One army under Bolgios defeated and killed the Macedonian king Ptolemy Keraunos at the Battle of Macedon, briefly leaving the kingdom in chaos. A second army under Brennus and Acichorius advanced into central Greece, forcing the pass at Thermopylae and marching on the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi, where, according to Greek tradition, the invaders were repulsed by storms, rockfalls and a counterattack — an event the Greeks celebrated as miraculous. A third group under Leonorius and Lutarius crossed into Asia Minor.

Aftermath

Many of the invaders who had entered Anatolia settled in the highlands of Phrygia, where their territory became known as Galatia; the Galatians became a lasting political power, serving as mercenaries and later as a Roman client region, famously addressed in the Epistle to the Galatians. In Europe, Celtic settlers founded Tylis in Thrace and consolidated the Scordisci around Singidunum (modern Belgrade). The invasion reshaped the geopolitics of the Hellenistic world, and the Aetolian League's role in defending Delphi greatly enhanced its prestige.

Sources

Principal ancient accounts include Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Justin's epitome of Pompeius Trogus, and Strabo.
