Hyperfocus is an intense, narrow form of concentration on a single task or topic.

Hyperfocus is an intense form of mental concentration or visualisation that focuses consciousness on a subject, topic, or task. In an individual who experiences hyperfocus, their attention may concentrate so completely on the activity that they become unaware of their surroundings, losing track of time and neglecting bodily needs such as hunger.

Hyperfocus is most commonly discussed in relation to attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), where it appears paradoxical alongside the distractibility characteristic of the condition. It is also reported in autism and other conditions. In the ADHD literature, hyperfocus is often described not as an excess of attention but as a difficulty regulating or shifting attention: the person can sustain attention powerfully on things that interest them, but struggles to direct it at will.

Characteristics

During hyperfocus a person may work on a project for many hours without noticing fatigue, may fail to respond when spoken to, and may become irritable or disoriented when interrupted. Tasks commonly associated with hyperfocus include creative work, video games, research into a special interest, programming, and reading. After an episode ends, the person may experience exhaustion and difficulty reconstructing how much time has passed.

Relationship to flow

Hyperfocus resembles the psychological concept of flow, described by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, in which a person is fully immersed in an activity with energised focus and enjoyment. The two differ in that flow is generally considered a positive, voluntary state associated with a balance of challenge and skill, whereas hyperfocus can be involuntary, can fix on activities of low importance, and may persist to the detriment of other obligations.

Hyperfocus versus perseveration

Hyperfocus is sometimes distinguished from perseveration, the repetition of a response or activity despite its cessation being appropriate. Perseveration is associated with frontal lobe injury and certain neurological conditions; hyperfocus, by contrast, is typically a sustained but coherent engagement with a topic.

In ADHD

Although hyperfocus is not listed among the diagnostic criteria for ADHD in the DSM-5, many clinicians and adults with ADHD report it as a prominent feature. It has been proposed that both inattention and hyperfocus in ADHD arise from a common deficit in executive regulation of attention: attention behaves as if it is controlled by the interest of the task rather than by conscious intention. Some researchers describe this as an interest-based nervous system, in contrast to an importance-based one.
