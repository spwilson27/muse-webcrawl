Lulav

Lulav (Hebrew: לולב) is a closed frond of the date palm tree. It is one of the Four Species (arba'ah minim — ארבעת המינים) used during the Jewish holiday of Sukkot. The other species are the hadass (myrtle), aravah (willow), and etrog (citron). When bound together, the lulav, hadass, and aravah are commonly referred to collectively as "the lulav".

Biblical source

The Torah commands in Leviticus 23:40: "And you shall take for yourselves on the first day the fruit of beautiful trees, branches of palm trees, the boughs of leafy trees, and willows of the brook; and you shall rejoice before the LORD your God for seven days." Rabbinic tradition identifies the "branches of palm trees" (kappot temarim) with the lulav.

Halachic requirements

The lulav must be at least three handbreadths (approximately 32 cm) long, straight, with its leaves (the spine, or shidrah) intact and the topmost leaf (the tiyomet) unbroken. It is bound together with three myrtle branches on the right, the palm branch in the centre, and two willow branches on the left, traditionally using rings woven from palm leaves.

The blessing and waving

Each day of Sukkot (except Shabbat in many communities), the Four Species are held and waved in six directions — east, south, west, north, up, and down — during the recitation of Hallel, symbolising that God is everywhere. The blessing "...who has sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us concerning the taking of the lulav" is recited.

Symbolism

According to a well-known midrash, the Four Species represent different types of Jews: the lulav, from a tree whose fruit has taste but no smell, symbolises those who possess knowledge of Torah but not good deeds — or, in another reading, the spine represents the upright person. Together the species are held as one, teaching the unity of the people.
