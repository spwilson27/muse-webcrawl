Black British people are a multi-ethnic group of British people of either African or Afro-Caribbean descent. The term Black British developed in the 1950s, referring to people of the former British West Indies who immigrated after the Second World War — the "Windrush generation" — and their descendants, alongside a growing population of African origin. In the 2021 census for England and Wales, around 2.4 million people (about 4 per cent of the population) identified within the Black, Black British, Caribbean or African ethnic group categories.

History

Black people have been present in Britain since Roman times, and communities existed in port cities such as London, Liverpool, and Cardiff from the era of the transatlantic slave trade, which Britain abolished in 1807 (slavery itself was abolished across most of the Empire in 1833). Black servicemen and students formed settled communities in the early twentieth century. Large-scale migration began with the arrival of the Empire Windrush at Tilbury in 1948, bringing Caribbean workers recruited to fill post-war labour shortages. From the 1960s onward, migration from Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and other African countries grew, and in recent decades African-origin Britons have outnumbered those of Caribbean origin.

Culture

Black British culture has profoundly influenced British music — from calypso and reggae through jungle, UK garage, and grime — as well as literature, theatre, sport, and food. The Notting Hill Carnival, founded by Claudia Jones and the Caribbean community in 1964, is Europe's largest street festival. Institutions such as the Black Cultural Archives (Brixton) preserve and celebrate this heritage, and Black History Month has been marked in the UK each October since 1987.

Issues

Black Britons have experienced persistent inequalities in employment, housing, education, policing, and health outcomes, and campaigns against racial discrimination — from the 1963 Bristol Bus Boycott to the Stephen Lawrence inquiry (1999) and the Windrush scandal (2018), in which long-resident Caribbean Britons were wrongly detained and deported — have been defining episodes in modern British race relations.
