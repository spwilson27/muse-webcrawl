Audio codec

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Encoding and decoding of a digital audio signal

An audio codec is a device or computer program capable of encoding or decoding a digital data stream of audio. In software, an audio codec is a computer program implementing an algorithm that compresses and decompresses digital audio data according to a given audio coding format. The objective of the algorithm is to represent the high-fidelity audio signal with a minimum number of bits while retaining quality.

Types

Lossless codecs

Lossless codecs compress audio data without discarding any information, so the decoded signal is bit-identical to the original. Examples include FLAC, Apple Lossless (ALAC), and Monkey's Audio. Lossless compression typically achieves ratios of about 2:1 on music.

Lossy codecs

Lossy codecs discard information deemed perceptually irrelevant, using psychoacoustic models to achieve much higher compression ratios. Widely used lossy formats include MP3, AAC, Vorbis, and Opus. Opus, standardized by the Internet Engineering Task Force, is designed for both speech and music at low latency and is widely used in voice-over-IP and streaming applications.

Compression techniques

Most audio codecs exploit characteristics of human hearing, such as the masking effect, in which a loud sound renders nearby frequencies inaudible. Transform coding—commonly using the modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT)—converts the signal into frequency bands that can be quantized with precision allocated according to perceptual importance. Speech codecs such as AMR and Speex use different techniques, modelling the human vocal tract through linear predictive coding.

Hardware codecs

In hardware, an audio codec is a single device that encodes analogue audio as digital signals and decodes digital back into analogue. The analogue-to-digital and digital-to-analogue conversion stages exist as separate chips in most audio devices, but the term is commonly applied to integrated circuits containing both functions, found in sound cards, smartphones, and embedded systems.
