Robert Hooke (28 July 1635 – 3 March 1703) was an English polymath who was active as a physicist (then called a natural philosopher), astronomer, geologist, meteorologist, and architect. He is credited as one of the first scientists to investigate living things at microscopic scale in 1665, publishing the landmark book Micrographia, and coining the word "cell" for the structures he observed in cork.

Career

Hooke studied at Oxford, where he became assistant to Robert Boyle and helped construct the air pump used in Boyle's gas-law experiments. In 1662 he was appointed the first Curator of Experiments of the newly founded Royal Society, a post he held for decades, demonstrating new experiments at its weekly meetings. He also served as Gresham Professor of Geometry and, after the Great Fire of London (1666), as a city surveyor alongside Christopher Wren, helping to design buildings including the Monument and Bethlem Royal Hospital.

Science

Hooke formulated the law of elasticity that bears his name — "ut tensio, sic vis" (as the extension, so the force) — first published as an anagram in 1676 and openly in 1678. In Micrographia (1665) he presented exquisite engravings of objects seen through his compound microscope, including the famous flea and the cellular structure of cork. He anticipated aspects of the theory of gravitation and corresponded with Isaac Newton about planetary motion, later bitterly disputing priority for the inverse-square law; he also feuded with Christiaan Huygens over the balance spring for watches. Hooke proposed an early wave theory of light and made pioneering observations in palaeontology, recognising fossils as remains of once-living organisms.

Legacy

Hooke died in London in 1703. No authenticated portrait survives, and his reputation was long overshadowed by Newton's, but modern scholarship has restored him as one of the most inventive experimentalists of the Scientific Revolution — sometimes called "England's Leonardo".
