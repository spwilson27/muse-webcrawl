Filioque

The Filioque (Latin for "and from the Son") is a Latin phrase added to the Nicene Creed of Western Christianity, asserting that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The original Greek text of the creed, agreed at the First Council of Constantinople in 381, states that the Spirit proceeds "from the Father" alone. The addition became a major point of theological and ecclesiastical contention and one of the principal causes of the East–West Schism of 1054; it remains a significant difference between Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic doctrine.

History

The clause first appeared in the West at the Third Council of Toledo (589) in Visigothic Spain, where it served to combat Arianism by emphasising the full divinity and equality of the Son. Its use spread through the Frankish church and was championed at Charlemagne's court, though Pope Leo III, while accepting the doctrine, refused to alter the creed and had the original text inscribed on silver shields in Rome. Rome itself adopted the interpolated creed in the liturgy only in 1014, under Pope Benedict VIII.

In the 9th century, Patriarch Photios I of Constantinople condemned the addition as heretical, arguing that it disturbed the monarchy of the Father as the single source within the Trinity and that no local church could unilaterally change an ecumenical creed. The dispute contributed to the mutual excommunications of 1054 and was reaffirmed as a dividing issue at the reunion councils of Lyons (1274) and Florence (1438–39), both of which the East ultimately repudiated.

Theological positions

Western (Latin) theology defends the Filioque on the basis of passages such as John 15:26's counterpart texts where Christ "sends" the Spirit, the consubstantial unity of Father and Son, and authorities such as Augustine of Hippo, teaching that the Spirit proceeds from the Father "principally" and from the Son by the Father's gift. The Latin Church distinguishes the eternal procession from the Spirit's temporal mission.

Eastern Orthodox theology maintains that the Spirit proceeds hypostatically from the Father alone, the sole cause (monarchia) in the Godhead, while acknowledging that the Spirit is sent through the Son and shines forth eternally through him. Orthodox theologians argue the addition implies two sources in the Trinity.

Modern discussion

Ecumenical dialogues in the 20th and 21st centuries have narrowed the gap. A 1995 clarification from the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity affirmed the Greek tradition's legitimacy and Rome's practice of reciting the creed without the addition in Greek liturgy. Some Eastern Catholic churches omit the clause. The issue, together with papal primacy, remains a principal obstacle to full communion.
