Rape of the Sabine women

The Rape of the Sabine women is an episode in the legendary history of Rome, recounted by Livy, Plutarch, and others. "Rape" here translates the Latin raptio, meaning abduction: in the story, Romulus and the first Romans, short of women, invited the neighbouring Sabines to a festival of Neptune Equester (the Consualia) and seized the Sabine women to take as wives.

The legend

The outraged Sabines eventually made war on Rome under their king Titus Tatius. As battle was joined, the Sabine women — now wives and mothers of Romans — famously rushed between the armies and persuaded both sides to make peace, arguing that the war would make them widows and orphans at once. The Romans and Sabines united into one community, ruled jointly by Romulus and Tatius until the latter's death.

In art

The episode has been a major subject of Renaissance and Baroque art, including celebrated versions by Nicolas Poussin and Jacques-Louis David (The Intervention of the Sabine Women, which depicts the women's peacemaking), and a famous sculpture of the abduction by Giambologna in the Loggia dei Lanzi, Florence.
