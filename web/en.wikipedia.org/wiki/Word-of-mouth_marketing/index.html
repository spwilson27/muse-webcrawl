Word-of-mouth marketing (WOMM, WOM marketing, or word-of-mouth advertising) differs from naturally occurring word of mouth in that it is actively influenced or encouraged by organisations (e.g., 'seeding' a message in networks, rewarding regular consumers to engage in WOM, or employing "buzz agents"). Studies show that word-of-mouth referrals are among the most trusted forms of promotion, since consumers tend to credit friends and family over advertising.

While it is difficult to truly control WOM, research on how consumers talk, and the ways companies can intervene, has led to a discipline in which marketers attempt to trigger and accelerate interpersonal communication about products and services.

History

George Silverman is credited with pioneering organised word-of-mouth marketing in the 1970s, when he created "teleconferenced peer influence groups" to engage physicians in dialogue about new pharmaceutical products. In the 2000s the rise of online communities prompted marketers to study digital word of mouth (eWOM) in reviews, forums, and social media. The Word of Mouth Marketing Association (WOMMA) was founded in 2004 to promote ethical practice in the field.

Forms

  • Buzz marketing — generating excitement and anticipation, often through surprising or entertaining events.
  • Viral marketing — creating messages designed to be shared rapidly online.
  • Referral programmes — rewarding existing customers for recommending new ones.
  • Influencer seeding — providing products to influential consumers in the hope they will discuss them.
  • Community and cause marketing — supporting groups and causes around which conversation forms.

Theories

Researchers explain WOM through social exchange and self-presentation motives: people share experiences to help others, signal identity, or discharge strong emotions after unusually good or bad service. The Net Promoter Score framework, introduced by Fred Reichheld in 2003, links willingness to recommend with business growth. Empirical studies consistently find negative word of mouth more common and more impactful than positive.

Ethics and regulation

Because WOM derives credibility from perceived independence, undisclosed paid advocacy is controversial. Regulators such as the US Federal Trade Commission require disclosure of material connections between endorsers and advertisers, including in social media posts.
