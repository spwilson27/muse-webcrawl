Puddling (metallurgy)

Puddling
InventorHenry Cort
Patented1784
InputPig iron
OutputWrought iron
Superseded byBessemer process / steelmaking

Puddling is the process of converting pig iron into wrought iron by subjecting it to heat and frequent stirring in a reverberatory furnace, in the presence of oxidising substances. Developed by Henry Cort in 1784, it was the first method that allowed wrought iron to be produced on a large scale, and it was central to the Industrial Revolution.

Process

In the puddling furnace, the flames and hot gases passed over the hearth rather than through the fuel bed, so the iron never came into direct contact with the coal and its sulphur impurities. The puddler charged the furnace with pig iron, which melted and was stirred — "rabbled" — with long iron rods through a working door. As air and the iron-oxide lining oxidised the carbon in the melt, the melting point rose and the iron became pasty, gathering into balls of about 30–40 kg. The puddler worked these into a single "puddle ball", removed it glowing from the furnace, and it was shingled under a hammer to expel slag and then rolled into bars.

History

Cort's patents of 1783 (grooved rolling) and 1784 (puddling) transformed British ironmaking. The process was dangerous, exhausting work: puddlers were highly paid but had short working lives. The puddling furnace was gradually mechanised in the later 19th century and was displaced for most purposes by the Bessemer process and open-hearth steelmaking, though puddled wrought iron continued to be made into the 20th century for applications such as chains and anchors.
