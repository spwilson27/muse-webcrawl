Korotkoff sounds

Listening for Korotkoff sounds during blood pressure measurement

Korotkoff sounds are the sounds that medical personnel listen for when measuring blood pressure using a non-invasive procedure, in which a sphygmomanometer cuff is inflated around the upper arm and a stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery. They are named after the Russian physician Nikolai Korotkov, who described them in 1905.

Mechanism

When the cuff is fully inflated, no blood flows and no sound is heard. As cuff pressure is slowly released, blood begins to spurt through the partially compressed artery, producing turbulent flow and audible tapping sounds. The pressure at which the first sound appears corresponds to systolic pressure; the pressure at which the sounds finally disappear corresponds to diastolic pressure.

Phases

Five phases are conventionally described. Phase I is the appearance of faint, clear tapping sounds. Phase II involves softer murmurs or swishing. In Phase III the sounds become crisper and louder. Phase IV is marked by muffling, and Phase V by the complete disappearance of sound. The sounds arise from arterial wall vibration and turbulent blood flow distal to the cuff.

Clinical use

The auscultatory method based on Korotkoff sounds long served as the reference standard for office blood pressure measurement, although automated oscillometric devices have largely replaced manual measurement in many settings. Phenomena such as the auscultatory gap can cause errors if the technique is not performed carefully.
