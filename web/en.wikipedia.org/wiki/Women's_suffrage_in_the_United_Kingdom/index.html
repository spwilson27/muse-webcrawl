Women's suffrage in the United Kingdom

Women's suffrage in the United Kingdom was a movement to give women the right to vote in parliamentary elections. After decades of organised campaigning by constitutional "suffragists" and, from 1903, militant "suffragettes", the Representation of the People Act 1918 enfranchised women over 30 who met a property qualification (some 8.4 million women), and the Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928 put women on equal terms with men at 21.

Origins

John Stuart Mill presented the first mass women's suffrage petition to Parliament in 1866 and proposed a women's suffrage amendment to the 1867 Reform Bill. Local societies merged in 1897 into the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS), led by Millicent Fawcett, which campaigned by persuasion, petitions and support for sympathetic MPs. Private members' bills repeatedly won second readings but were blocked by governments and by the House of Lords.

The suffragettes

In 1903 Emmeline Pankhurst and her daughters Christabel and Sylvia founded the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) in Manchester under the motto "Deeds, not words". After Christabel Pankhurst and Annie Kenney were arrested for heckling a Liberal meeting in 1905, the WSPU adopted escalating militancy: mass marches, chaining to railings, window-smashing, and from 1912 arson and bombing attacks on empty buildings. Imprisoned suffragettes hunger-struck and were forcibly fed; the "Cat and Mouse Act" (1913) allowed release and re-arrest of weakened prisoners. In June 1913 Emily Davison died after stepping in front of the King's horse at the Derby — the movement's most famous martyr.

War and victory

Both the WSPU and NUWSS suspended militancy in August 1914 and threw themselves into war work, while the franchise question was reopened by the need to enfranchise serving soldiers. The Speaker's Conference of 1916–17 recommended a limited women's franchise; the Representation of the People Act 1918 gave the vote to women over 30 meeting a property qualification, and to virtually all men over 21. In the same year the Parliament (Qualification of Women) Act allowed women to sit as MPs; the first woman elected, Constance Markievicz (1918), did not take her seat, and the first to sit was Nancy Astor (1919). Equal franchise at 21 followed in 1928 under Stanley Baldwin's Conservative government, creating the so-called "flapper vote" at the 1929 election.

Legacy

The campaign is commemorated by the statue of Millicent Fawcett unveiled in Parliament Square in 2018 — the first statue of a woman there — and by Emmeline Pankhurst's statue in Manchester. Historians continue to debate the relative contributions of militancy, constitutional campaigning and women's war work to the 1918 victory.
