Venomous snake

Venomous snakes are species of the suborder Serpentes that are capable of producing venom, which they use for killing prey, for defence, and to assist with digestion of their prey. The venom is typically delivered by fangs — specialised teeth connected to venom glands. Of the roughly 3,900 known snake species, about 600 are venomous, and around 200 are considered medically significant to humans.

Families

Four groups are generally recognised. The Elapidae include cobras, mambas, kraits, coral snakes, taipans, and sea snakes, with short, fixed front fangs. The Viperidae — vipers and pit vipers such as rattlesnakes — have long, hinged fangs that fold against the roof of the mouth. The Atractaspididae include burrowing asps with unusual side-stabbing fangs, and many species in the vast family Colubridae are rear-fanged or mildly venomous, with only a few, such as the boomslang, dangerous to humans.

Venom

Snake venoms are complex mixtures of proteins and enzymes, including neurotoxins, hemotoxins, cytotoxins, and cardiotoxins. Venom composition varies by species and even within a species by geography and age. Snakebite is a significant public-health problem in tropical regions; the World Health Organization lists snakebite envenoming as a neglected tropical disease, with millions of bites and tens of thousands of deaths each year, treated primarily with antivenom.
