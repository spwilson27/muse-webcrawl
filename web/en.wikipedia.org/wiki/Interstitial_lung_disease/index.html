Interstitial lung disease

Interstitial lung disease (ILD), or diffuse parenchymal lung disease, is a group of more than 200 respiratory diseases affecting the interstitium — the tissue and space around the alveoli (air sacs) of the lungs. These disorders are characterised by inflammation and/or fibrosis (scarring) of the lung tissue, which stiffens the lungs and impairs gas exchange.

Signs and symptoms

The typical presentation is progressive breathlessness (dyspnoea), first on exertion, and a dry cough. Examination may reveal fine inspiratory crackles and, in advanced disease, clubbing of the fingers. Many patients are initially misdiagnosed with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or heart failure.

Causes and classification

ILDs are classified into those with identifiable causes and idiopathic forms. Known causes include occupational and environmental exposures (asbestos, silica, coal dust, bird and mould antigens causing hypersensitivity pneumonitis), drugs, radiation, and connective-tissue diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis. The most common idiopathic form is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF); others include nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organising pneumonia, sarcoidosis (often classified separately) and desquamative interstitial pneumonia, which is strongly associated with smoking.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines clinical history, high-resolution computed tomography, pulmonary function tests (typically a restrictive pattern with reduced gas transfer), and sometimes surgical lung biopsy, reviewed in a multidisciplinary meeting. Treatment depends on the subtype: removal of exposures, corticosteroids or immunosuppressants for inflammatory forms, and antifibrotic drugs such as nintedanib and pirfenidone for IPF and other progressive fibrosing phenotypes. Lung transplantation is an option for suitable patients with advanced disease.
