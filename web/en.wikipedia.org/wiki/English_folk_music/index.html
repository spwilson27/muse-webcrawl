English folk music

English folk music comprises the traditional music of England, transmitted orally over centuries and encompassing folk songs and ballads, dance tunes for morris dancing, country dances and jigs, sea shanties, and ceremonial and seasonal music. It has ancient roots, absorbed strong influences from the broadside ballad trade, industrial song and music hall, and has undergone two great revivals — the collecting movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries led by Cecil Sharp, and the post-1950s folk revival that fed into British folk rock.

Morris dancing, accompanied by fiddle, melodeon or pipe and tabor, is a signature English folk tradition.

Traditional forms

The core repertoire consists of narrative ballads — many catalogued among the Child Ballads collected by Francis James Child (published 1882–98), such as "Tam Lin", "Barbara Allen" and "Lord Randall" — together with lyric songs of love, work, drinking and the supernatural, often sung unaccompanied by a solo singer. Dance traditions include morris dancing (documented from the fifteenth century), rapper and longsword dances of the north-east and Yorkshire, and the social dances of Playford's The English Dancing Master (1651). Sailors' shanties, hornpipes, wassailing songs and May Day customs such as the Padstow 'Obby 'Oss ceremony form further strands.

The first revival

Fearing the tradition was dying with industrialisation and the First World War, collectors including Cecil Sharp (from 1903), Ralph Vaughan Williams, Percy Grainger and Maud Karpeles visited rural singers and notated or recorded thousands of songs and tunes; Sharp founded the English Folk Dance Society in 1911. Vaughan Williams and Gustav Holst wove folk melodies into classical compositions, embedding folk song in the national musical identity, while Sharp's Appalachian expeditions (1916–18) revealed English ballads preserved in the mountains of Virginia and North Carolina.

The second revival and beyond

After 1945, figures such as A. L. Lloyd, Ewan MacColl and Peggy Seeger rebuilt a performing folk culture around clubs, Topic Records and the BBC's radio ballads, mixing traditional material with industrial and political song. In the late 1960s Fairport Convention and Steeleye Span electrified the repertoire, creating British folk rock on albums such as Liege & Lief (1969). Later generations — Martin and Eliza Carthy, Kate Rusby, Seth Lakeman, Sam Lee — have kept the tradition a living one, supported by the English Folk Dance and Song Society at Cecil Sharp House in London.
