The Middle Stone Age (or MSA) is a period of African prehistory between the Earlier Stone Age and the Later Stone Age, generally considered to have begun around 280,000 years ago and ended between about 50,000 and 25,000 years ago, varying by region. It is roughly contemporary with the Middle Paleolithic of Eurasia but is associated with the emergence and early history of anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) in Africa.

Characteristics

MSA industries are dominated by prepared-core (Levallois) and blade technologies producing points, scrapers and backed pieces, often regionally distinctive — including the bifacial Still Bay points and the backed segments of the Howiesons Poort industry in southern Africa, the tanged Aterian tools of North Africa and early microliths. Many assemblages show careful selection and heat treatment of fine-grained stone such as silcrete.

Behavioural modernity

Sites such as Blombos Cave and Pinnacle Point in South Africa have yielded some of the earliest evidence for symbolic behaviour: engraved ochre, shell bead ornaments, and systematic exploitation of marine resources, dating to roughly 100,000–70,000 years ago. Such finds have reshaped debates over the origins of behavioural modernity, suggesting a gradual African accumulation of innovations rather than a single "human revolution", though the record is patchy and episodic.

End and dispersal

The MSA grades into the Later Stone Age at different times across the continent. During the later MSA, modern human populations expanded within Africa and, in the major dispersals after about 60,000 years ago, out of Africa into Eurasia.
