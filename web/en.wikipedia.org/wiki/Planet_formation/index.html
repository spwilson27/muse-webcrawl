Planet formation

Planet formation
SettingProtoplanetary disk around a young star
Main theoriesCore accretion; disk (gravitational) instability
TimescaleMillions to tens of millions of years
Key stagesDust growth → planetesimals → embryos → planets

Planet formation is the process by which planets grow from the gas and dust of a protoplanetary disk surrounding a newborn star. In the most widely accepted account, the nebular hypothesis, a molecular cloud collapses under gravity to form a star, and the leftover material flattens into a rotating disk in which planets assemble over millions of years. Observations of disks around young stars — such as the rings and gaps imaged by the ALMA observatory in the disk of HL Tauri — show planet formation in progress across the galaxy.

From dust to planetesimals

The process begins with sub-micrometre dust grains colliding and sticking to form pebbles. Growth beyond metre sizes faces barriers: boulders drift rapidly inward through gas drag (the "metre-size barrier") and tend to shatter on collision. The streaming instability solves part of the problem: drag between gas and dense clumps of pebbles concentrates solids until they collapse gravitationally into planetesimals tens to hundreds of kilometres across, skipping gradual growth. Pebble accretion then lets larger seeds grow quickly by sweeping up drifting pebbles.

Core accretion

In the core-accretion model, planetesimals collide and merge into planetary embryos, which grow into cores of several Earth masses. Beyond the snow line, where water and other volatiles are frozen, solids are several times more abundant, so cores grow big enough (roughly 10 Earth masses) to accrete massive hydrogen–helium envelopes — becoming gas giants like Jupiter and Saturn — before the disk gas disperses within a few million years. Inner cores that never reach runaway gas accretion remain rocky terrestrial planets.

Disk instability and later evolution

In the alternative disk-instability model, parts of a massive, cold disk fragment directly under self-gravity into gas-giant clumps, possibly explaining very massive planets on wide orbits. After formation, planets exchange angular momentum with the disk and each other: many migrate inward, producing "hot Jupiters", and systems may pass through late instabilities that scatter planets and bombard the survivors — as the Nice model proposes for the early Solar System, whose Late Heavy Bombardment and its configuration of giant planets it seeks to explain.
