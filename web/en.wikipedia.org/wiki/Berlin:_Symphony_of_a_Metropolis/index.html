Berlin: Symphony of a Metropolis (German: Berlin: Die Sinfonie der Großstadt, "Berlin: The Symphony of the Great City") is a 1927 German silent documentary film directed by Walter Ruttmann. It portrays a single day in the life of Berlin from before dawn to midnight, and is the most celebrated example of the "city symphony" genre and of absolute film techniques applied to documentary material.

Structure and content

The film is organised in five acts, like movements of a symphony. It opens with a train journey into the sleeping city at dawn, the gradual waking of streets and factories, the rush of the working day — trams, crowds, machines, typists, stock-exchange screens — then leisure, sport, shopping, nightlife and cabaret, before the city rests again. There is no plot or commentary; meaning arises from montage, rhythm and visual rhymes between shots, for example between factory pistons and dancing legs.

Production

Ruttmann, a painter and pioneer of abstract animation (Lichtspiel: Opus I, 1921), worked from an idea by Carl Mayer; Karl Freund, the great cameraman of Metropolis and The Last Laugh, photographed the city over more than a year, sometimes with concealed cameras to capture unposed street life. Edmund Meisel composed an orchestral score precisely synchronised with the cutting, of which only fragments survive; many modern scores have since been written for the film.

Reception and influence

Contemporary reactions were mixed: critics praised its virtuosity but some, including Siegfried Kracauer, later charged it with aestheticising the city while ignoring its social divisions. The film defined the city-symphony genre alongside Dziga Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929) and remains a fixture of film studies as a portrait of Weimar Berlin and as an experiment in rhythmic editing. Ruttmann returned to the form with the sound film Melody of the World (1929).
