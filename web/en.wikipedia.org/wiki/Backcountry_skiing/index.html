Backcountry skiing

Backcountry skiing (North America), also called off-piste skiing (Europe) or ski touring in some contexts, is skiing in unmarked or unpatrolled areas outside the boundaries of a ski resort, typically in remote mountain terrain. Unlike resort skiing, backcountry skiers earn their descents by climbing under their own power, usually with climbing skins attached to their skis.

Equipment

Backcountry skiers typically use alpine touring (AT) bindings that release the heel for climbing and lock it for descent, removable adhesive climbing skins, and adjustable poles. Because there is no ski patrol, skiers carry avalanche safety equipment: a transceiver (beacon), shovel, and probe, and increasingly avalanche airbag packs.

Risks

The principal hazard is the avalanche: most fatal accidents involve skiers triggering slabs on steep slopes. Travellers are advised to obtain avalanche training, check regional avalanche forecasts, assess snowpack stability, and choose terrain appropriate to conditions. Other hazards include tree wells, crevasses on glaciers, falls, and rapidly changing mountain weather.

Popularity

Backcountry skiing has grown rapidly since the 2000s with improvements in lightweight touring equipment. Related disciplines include ski mountaineering, splitboarding (the snowboard equivalent), and sidecountry skiing, which uses resort lifts to access adjacent ungroomed terrain.
