Burnt offering (Judaism)

The burnt offering (Hebrew: עֹלָה, ʿolah, "that which ascends"; Greek: ὁλοκαύτωμα, holokautōma) was a form of sacrifice in ancient Israelite religion in which a whole animal was consumed by fire on the altar of the Tabernacle, and later the Temple in Jerusalem. It is the first offering described in detail in the Book of Leviticus (chapter 1) and gives its name, via the Greek, to the modern term "holocaust".

Procedure

The offerer brought an unblemished male animal — a bull, a sheep or goat, or, for the poor, a turtledove or pigeon — laid a hand on its head, and slaughtered it at the entrance of the sanctuary. Priests sprinkled the blood around the altar, flayed and cut up the animal, washed the entrails and legs, and burned the entire carcass on the altar fire, where it was wholly consumed, producing a "pleasing odour" to God. Only the hide was retained, becoming the property of the officiating priest (Leviticus 7:8). Nothing was eaten by the offerer — unlike peace offerings — making the burnt offering a total gift.

Occasions

The tamid, a lamb offered morning and evening, was a continual burnt offering offered daily on behalf of the whole community; additional burnt offerings were prescribed for Sabbaths, new moons, festivals and the Day of Atonement, and individuals brought them voluntarily, for vows, and after certain purifications. Burnt offerings ceased with the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE; rabbinic Judaism substituted prayer for sacrifice, though the liturgy continues to recall and study the sacrificial laws, and Samaritans still offer the Passover sacrifice (a different, though related, rite) on Mount Gerizim.
