Otto Viktor Karl Liman von Sanders (17 February 1855 – 22 August 1929) was a German general who served as adviser and military commander in the Ottoman Empire during the First World War. He is best known for commanding the Ottoman Fifth Army in the successful defence of the Gallipoli peninsula in 1915–16 against the Allied landings.

Appointment to Turkey

Liman von Sanders arrived in Constantinople in December 1913 at the head of a German military mission to reform the Ottoman army — an appointment that provoked a diplomatic crisis with Russia, which feared German control of the Straits. He was made inspector-general of the Ottoman army and an Ottoman field marshal. His relations with War Minister Enver Pasha were consistently strained.

Gallipoli

In March 1915, as the Allies prepared to land, Liman was given the new Fifth Army — some six divisions — to defend both shores of the Dardanelles. Expecting the main blow at Bulair or on the Asiatic shore, he kept his reserves mobile and his forces thin on the peninsula itself; when the landings came on 25 April at Cape Helles and Anzac Cove, Ottoman troops, driven on by subordinates such as Mustafa Kemal, contained them. Liman's refusal to withdraw the Asian-side divisions saved the August crisis, when the Landing at Suvla Bay and the attack on Chunuk Bair nearly broke his line. The Allied evacuation by January 1916 confirmed the Ottoman victory, for which Liman received much of the strategic credit, though he acknowledged the Turkish soldier as its true author.

Palestine and later life

In 1918 Liman succeeded Erich von Falkenhayn in command of the Yıldırım Army Group in Palestine. He was defeated by General Allenby at the Battle of Megiddo in September 1918 and narrowly escaped capture at Nazareth. After the Armistice he was briefly detained by the British at Malta on suspicion of war crimes but released without trial. He retired to Munich, published his memoir Five Years in Turkey, and died in 1929.
