Prudence (Latin: prudentia, contracted from providentia, "seeing ahead") is the ability to govern and discipline oneself by the use of reason. It is classically counted as one of the four cardinal virtues, alongside justice, temperance and fortitude, and is often ranked first among them because it directs the others: a person cannot act justly or bravely in practice without judging rightly what a situation requires. In ordinary modern usage the word has narrowed toward caution and carefulness, especially about risk and money, but in the philosophical and theological tradition it denotes practical wisdom about ends as well as means.

In ancient philosophy

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics treats phronesis (practical wisdom) as an intellectual virtue: the disposition to deliberate well about what is good and advantageous for living well as a whole, and to act accordingly. Unlike theoretical wisdom (sophia), phronesis concerns the variable world of human action. Plato had already listed prudence among the cardinal virtues in the Republic, associating it with the ruling class of the ideal city.

In medieval theology

Thomas Aquinas, drawing on Aristotle, Cicero and the Church Fathers, called prudence the "charioteer of the virtues" (auriga virtutum): right reason in action (recta ratio agibilium). He analysed prudence into component parts, including memory of the past, understanding of the present, foresight of the future, circumspection and caution. In Catholic moral theology prudence remains the first cardinal virtue, the faculty by which the other virtues are applied to concrete circumstances.

Allegorical representation

In Renaissance iconography Prudence appears as a woman holding a mirror (self-knowledge) and a serpent (recalling Christ's injunction to be "wise as serpents"), sometimes with three faces looking to past, present and future. Titian's Allegory of Prudence (c. 1550–1565) is the best-known painted treatment, pairing three human heads with those of a wolf, lion and dog.

Modern senses

Outside moral philosophy, "prudence" survives in specialised vocabularies: in finance and law the "prudent person rule" governs fiduciaries investing the assets of others; in accounting, prudence (or conservatism) counsels recognising losses promptly and gains only when realised; and jurisprudence (juris prudentia) preserves the root association with reasoned judgement. Critics have occasionally warned that prudence degrades into mere timidity — a confusion classical authors explicitly rejected, since prudence may equally counsel bold action when the situation demands it.
