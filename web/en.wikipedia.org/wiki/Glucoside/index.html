Glucoside

Glucoside
ClassGlycoside
Sugar componentGlucose
BondGlycosidic bond to an aglycone

A glucoside is a glycoside that is chemically derived from glucose. Glucosides are common in plants, but rare in animals. In a glucoside, a glucose molecule is linked through its anomeric carbon by a glycosidic bond to a non-sugar group called the aglycone (or genin). Hydrolysis — by enzymes such as glucosidases, or by acid — splits the molecule back into glucose and the aglycone.

Examples

Many important plant products are glucosides. They include the cyanogenic glucosides such as amygdalin (in bitter almonds and stone-fruit kernels) and linamarin (in cassava), which release hydrogen cyanide on hydrolysis; the cardiac glucoside-like compounds of foxglove (more precisely cardenolide glycosides); salicin from willow bark, the precursor of aspirin chemistry; the vanilla flavouring glucovanillin; steviol glucosides responsible for the sweetness of stevia; and numerous flavonoid and anthocyanin pigments, most of which occur in plants as glucosides. Glucosinolates in brassicas are sulfur-containing glucosides that give mustard, horseradish and cabbage their pungency.

Classification

Glucosides may be classified by the atom linking sugar to aglycone (O-, C-, N- or S-glucosides) and by the stereochemistry of the glycosidic bond (α- or β-glucosides); most natural plant glucosides are β-O-glucosides.
