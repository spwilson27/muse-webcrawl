Belfries of Belgium and France

UNESCO World Heritage SiteInscribed 1999, extended 2005
Number of belfries56 (33 in Belgium, 23 in France)
CriteriaCultural: (ii), (iv)
RegionFlanders, Wallonia and northern France

The Belfries of Belgium and France are a group of 56 historic buildings designated by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, in recognition of the civic (rather than ecclesiastical or seigneurial) architecture of the belfries of the former County of Flanders and neighbouring regions. The belfry was a symbol of the independence and prosperity of the great communes of the Low Countries and northern France in the Middle Ages.

Background

From the 11th century onwards, the towns of Flanders won charters of liberties from their counts. A belfry was built to house the town's bells — rung to mark the hours, sound alarms and summon citizens — and often to store the archives, charters and treasury of the commune, and to hold a watch over the surrounding plain. Rising above cloth halls and market squares, the towers of Bruges, Ghent, Ypres, Mons, Tournai, Arras, Dunkirk and many others became emblems of municipal pride.

Inscription

Thirty-two Belgian belfries were inscribed in 1999 as the "Belfries of Flanders and Wallonia". In 2005 the listing was extended to include the belfry of Gembloux in Belgium and 23 belfries of the Nord-Pas-de-Calais and Picardy regions of France, and renamed "Belfries of Belgium and France". The tallest is that of the cathedral-like town hall belfry in Lille; famous examples include the 83-metre Belfry of Bruges, the Belfry of Ghent with its gilded dragon, and the belfry of the Cloth Hall in Ypres, destroyed in the First World War and faithfully rebuilt.

Significance

UNESCO recognised the belfries as an exceptional testimony to the emergence of municipal power in late medieval Europe: an urban architecture created by wealthy merchant cities, expressing civic freedom in stone. Many still house working carillons, and their bells continue to mark the life of their towns.
