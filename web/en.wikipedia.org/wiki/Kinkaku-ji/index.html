Kinkaku-ji (金閣寺, "Temple of the Golden Pavilion"), formally named Rokuon-ji, is a Zen Buddhist temple in northern Kyoto, Japan, whose top two storeys are completely covered in gold leaf. It is one of the most famous buildings in Japan and the emblem of the opulent Kitayama culture of the Muromachi period.

History

The site was the villa of the courtier Saionji Kintsune before the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu acquired it in 1397 and built his retirement palace, the Kitayama-dai, around the gilded pavilion. Each of the pavilion's three floors exemplifies a different style: the ground floor in the palace style (shinden-zukuri), the second in the samurai-house style (buke-zukuri), and the third, topped by a golden phoenix, in the Zen temple style (karayo). After Yoshimitsu's death in 1408 the complex was converted, per his will, into a Zen temple under the priest Musō Soseki.

Arson and rebuilding

The pavilion survived the Ōnin War and six centuries, but on 2 July 1950 it was burned to the ground by a novice monk, Hayashi Yōken, in an act that shocked Japan and inspired Yukio Mishima's novel The Temple of the Golden Pavilion (1956). The present building is a faithful reconstruction completed in 1955, regilded in a major restoration of 1987 with gold leaf five times thicker than before. The temple garden, centred on the Kyōko-chi (Mirror Pond), is designated a Special National Landscape, and Kinkaku-ji is one of the seventeen Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto on the UNESCO World Heritage list.
