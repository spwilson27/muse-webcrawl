Geolibertarianism

Geolibertarianism is a political and economic ideology that combines libertarianism with Georgism. Its adherents hold that natural resources — in particular land — belong equally to all members of the community, while each individual owns the fruits of their own labour and the capital they create. The term was coined by the economist and activist Dan Sullivan.

Doctrine

Geolibertarians argue that self-ownership and the homestead principle apply fully to labour products, but that land and natural resources are not produced by anyone and are fixed in supply, so exclusive possession of them must be compensated to the community. The standard remedy is the collection of the economic rent of land and natural resources — for example through a land value tax along the lines proposed by Henry George in Progress and Poverty (1879) — with the revenue used either to fund minimal public services or to pay an equal citizen's dividend to everyone. In this system, taxes on labour, trade and buildings would be abolished, aligning geolibertarianism with other libertarians against most forms of taxation while rejecting absolute private property in land itself.

Advocates and influence

The philosophy descends from the classical liberal and Georgist traditions through figures such as Albert Jay Nock and has been developed by modern writers including economist Fred Foldvary, who used the related term "geo-libertarianism". It overlaps with left-libertarian theories of equal world ownership and with "geonomics". Geolibertarian ideas have influenced some advocates of resource dividends, such as the Alaska Permanent Fund model, although that institution is not itself libertarian.

Criticism

Anarcho-capitalist critics such as Murray Rothbard argued that land rent collection implies a landlord-like state and contradicts full self-ownership, while Georgist critics sometimes fault geolibertarians for giving priority to market mechanisms over social provision.
