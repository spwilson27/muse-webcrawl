Samuel Adams (1722–1803) was an American statesman, political philosopher and one of the Founding Fathers of the United States. A leader of the movement that became the American Revolution, he was a key organiser of resistance to British taxation in Boston, helped found the Sons of Liberty, and was a delegate to the Continental Congress and a signer of the Declaration of Independence.

Early life

Adams was born in Boston to a prosperous family; his father, Samuel Adams Sr., was a brewer, deacon and political activist. Educated at Harvard, the younger Adams failed in business and as a tax collector — he fell into arrears that dogged his career — but found his vocation in political agitation and newspaper writing, arguing that British policies violated the rights of the colonists as Englishmen.

Revolutionary leader

Adams led opposition to the Sugar Act (1764) and Stamp Act (1765), drafting Boston's instructions against them, and helped build the network of Committees of Correspondence that united the colonies. After the Boston Massacre (1770) he pressed for the removal of British troops, and he was a central figure in the events leading to the Boston Tea Party (1773). The British Coercive Acts and the battles of Lexington and Concord (1775, famously signalled to riders including Paul Revere through networks Adams helped create) made war inevitable. In the Continental Congress he pushed for independence and later served on wartime committees.

Later career

Adams helped draft the Massachusetts Constitution of 1780 and served as president of the Massachusetts Senate. Initially suspicious of the federal Constitution, he supported ratification in 1788 after assurances of amendments protecting rights. He served as lieutenant governor under John Hancock and as governor of Massachusetts from 1794 to 1797.

Legacy

Adams is remembered as one of the Revolution's chief propagandists and organisers, dubbed the "last of the Puritans" for his strict Calvinist morality. His cousin John Adams called him a man of "zeal" indispensable to the cause. A famous Boston lager brand, introduced in 1984, bears his name, referencing his family's brewing trade.
