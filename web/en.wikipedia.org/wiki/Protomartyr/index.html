A protomartyr (from Greek πρῶτος prōtos, "first", and μάρτυς martys, "witness") is the first martyr of a faith, region, country or religious order — the first person known to have died for that cause. The term is most commonly used in Christianity but is also applied analogously in other traditions and to secular movements.

In Christianity

Saint Stephen is honoured as the protomartyr of the Christian Church: according to the Acts of the Apostles he was stoned to death in Jerusalem after testifying before the Sanhedrin, with the future apostle Paul (then Saul) consenting to his death. His feast is celebrated on 26 December in the West and 27 December in the East. The Holy Innocents — the children killed by Herod in the Gospel of Matthew — are sometimes called protomartyrs as well, regarded as martyrs "in fact though not in will".

National and regional protomartyrs

Many countries venerate a protomartyr of their own Christianization:

  • Saint Alban — protomartyr of Britain, executed at Verulamium (St Albans) in the 3rd or 4th century.
  • Saint Denis — protomartyr of France, first bishop of Paris.
  • Saint Wenceslaus — often called protomartyr of Bohemia.
  • Saints Boris and Gleb — first martyrs of Kievan Rus', venerated as passion-bearers.
  • Saints Peter and Paul are honoured jointly as protomartyrs of Rome and founders of the Roman church.

The first missionaries killed in a region are also frequently termed protomartyrs, as in the protomartyrs of China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam and the Americas.

Other uses

In the Baháʼí Faith, the Báb is regarded as a martyr, and Mírzá Mihdí is sometimes called its first martyr. The term is also used figuratively in secular history for the first person to die for a cause, such as the first fallen of a revolution or protest movement.
