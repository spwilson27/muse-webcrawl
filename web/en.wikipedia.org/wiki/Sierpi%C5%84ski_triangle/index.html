Sierpiński triangle

The Sierpiński triangle (also spelled Sierpinski), also called the Sierpiński gasket or sierpinsky sieve, is a fractal attractive fixed set with the overall shape of an equilateral triangle, subdivided recursively into smaller equilateral triangles. Originally constructed as a curve, this is one of the basic examples of self-similar sets — that is, it is a mathematically generated pattern that is reproducible at any magnification or reduction. It is named after the Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński, who described it in 1915, though the pattern appears in much older art, such as 13th-century Cosmatesque mosaics in Italian churches.

Construction

The triangle can be constructed by starting with an equilateral triangle, repeatedly removing the central inverted triangle formed by joining the midpoints of the sides. After n steps there are 3n small triangles of side (1/2)n; in the limit the remaining set has zero area and Hausdorff dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585. Equivalently, it is the attractor of an iterated function system of three contractions toward the vertices, and it appears as the outcome of the "chaos game", in which a point repeatedly jumps halfway toward a randomly chosen vertex. The Sierpiński triangle is also exactly the set of entries of Pascal's triangle that are odd (mod 2), and it appears in the evolution of elementary cellular automaton Rule 90.

Properties and variants

Three-dimensional analogues include the Sierpiński tetrahedron (or tetrix). The Sierpiński carpet and Menger sponge are related square- and cube-based constructions. Because the set is self-similar with three copies scaled by one-half, it is a canonical teaching example of a fractal whose dimension is not an integer. Shapes resembling it occur in antenna design (fractal antennas) and in decorative art across cultures.
