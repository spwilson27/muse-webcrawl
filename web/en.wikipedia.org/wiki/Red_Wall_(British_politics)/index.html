The Red Wall denotes long-held Labour seats across post-industrial northern England and the Midlands.

In British politics, the Red Wall is a term for a belt of parliamentary constituencies in the North of England, the Midlands, and North Wales that had traditionally returned Labour MPs for many decades — in some cases since the early twentieth century — but which began voting for the Conservative Party in significant numbers at the 2019 general election. The phrase was coined by the pollster James Kanagasooriam, whose modelling before the 2019 election suggested that Labour's grip on its post-industrial heartlands was far weaker than commonly assumed.

2019 general election

At the 2019 election, held in the shadow of Brexit, the Conservatives under Boris Johnson won dozens of Red Wall seats, many in heavily Leave-voting, working-class towns. Labour lost seats it had held for generations, including Sedgefield (Tony Blair's former constituency), Bolsover, Blyth Valley, Bishop Auckland, and Great Grimsby. Analysts attributed the shift to Brexit alignment, the unpopularity of Labour's leadership, and longer-term cultural and demographic change in former industrial communities.

Subsequent elections

Holding the Red Wall became central to Conservative strategy after 2019, expressed in the "levelling up" agenda. By-elections gave mixed signals: the Conservatives gained Hartlepool in 2021 but lost ground thereafter. At the 2024 general election Labour regained most Red Wall seats as the Conservative vote collapsed, though the insurgent party Reform UK finished second in many of them, leaving the region's politics more competitive and volatile than the old one-party pattern.
