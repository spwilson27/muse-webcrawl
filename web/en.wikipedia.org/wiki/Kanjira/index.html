Kanjira

The kanjira, khanjira, or ganjira is a South Indian frame drum, a member of the tambourine family, used as a supporting instrument in Carnatic music concerts. Small but capable of remarkable rhythmic complexity, it has become a concert instrument in its own right and has crossed into fusion and Western percussion.

Construction

The kanjira consists of a circular wooden frame, traditionally made of jackfruit wood, about 17–23 cm in diameter and 5–10 cm deep. One side is covered with a drumhead traditionally made from monitor lizard skin (now usually replaced by goat skin, as the monitor lizard is protected), and the frame carries a single slit fitted with three or four small brass jingle discs. The head is not tunable in the usual sense; players lower the pitch by sprinkling water on the skin and raise it by warming it.

Playing technique

The drum is held in one hand while the other strikes the head with the fingers and palm, producing bass tones, open sounds, and crisp slaps. Unlike most tambourines it is played principally for its drum pitches rather than its jingles. Because the pitch can be bent by pressure from the holding hand, skilled players can mimic the gamaka (ornamentation) of melodic instruments.

History

The kanjira was long used in folk music and bhajan devotional singing before entering the Carnatic concert stage in the early 20th century, largely through the efforts of Manpoondia Pillai, regarded as the father of the modern instrument. In the modern concert it serves as upapakkavadyam (secondary percussion) alongside the mridangam, though virtuosos such as G. Harishankar elevated it to soloistic status. It features prominently in the world fusion group Shakti under V. Selvaganesh, whose father Vikku Vinayakram popularised South Indian percussion in the West.
