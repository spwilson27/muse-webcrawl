A naval mine is a self-contained explosive device placed in water to damage or destroy surface ships or submarines. Unlike depth charges, mines are deposited and left to wait until they are triggered by the approach of, or contact with, any vessel or a particular vessel type. Naval mines can be used offensively, to hamper enemy shipping movements or lock vessels into a harbour; or defensively, to protect friendly vessels and create "safe" zones.

Types

Mines are classified by position in the water (moored, bottom, drifting) and by triggering mechanism. Contact mines detonate when touched, historically via Hertz horns that complete an electrical circuit. Influence mines respond to a ship's magnetic field, sound or pressure signature, or combinations of these; modern designs can count ships and distinguish targets. Limpet mines are attached directly to a hull by divers or delivery vehicles.

History

Precursors were described in 14th-century China and devised by inventors such as Samuel Colt and Alfred Nobel. Mines saw extensive use in the American Civil War (where they were called "torpedoes"), the Russo-Japanese War, and both World Wars, when hundreds of thousands were laid in barrages such as the North Sea Mine Barrage. They remain cheap and effective weapons: several U.S. warships, including USS Samuel B. Roberts in 1988 and USS Princeton and USS Tripoli in 1991, were damaged by mines.

Countermeasures

Minesweepers clear mines mechanically or by simulating ship signatures; modern minehunters use sonar and remotely operated vehicles to locate and destroy individual mines. The Hague Convention of 1907 regulates mine laying, requiring that free-floating mines become harmless and that danger zones be announced after hostilities.
