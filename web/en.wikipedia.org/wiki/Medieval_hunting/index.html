Medieval hunting

Medieval hunting, during the Middle Ages in Europe, was a crucial activity — a training ground for warfare, a source of meat, hides, and other materials, and above all a defining ritual of aristocratic identity. Hunting rights were jealously guarded: royal forests and chases were protected by forest law, and poaching was harshly punished.

Forms of the hunt

The prestige form was the hunt par force des chiens, in which a single quarry — usually a red deer stag — was pursued on horseback with hounds through a ritualized sequence of stages, from the gathering and harboring of the deer to the unmaking (ritual butchery) and distribution of the venison. Other methods included hunting with bows from stands, hawking and falconry (especially favored by noblewomen), and the pursuit of boar, bear, wolf, hare, and game birds. Elaborate technical vocabulary and etiquette were codified in treatises such as Gaston III of Foix's Livre de chasse (c. 1387–1389) and its English adaptation, Edward of York's The Master of Game.

Social role

Hunts tested horsemanship, courage, and knowledge of ceremony, and served as occasions for diplomacy and display. By the later Middle Ages, deer parks enclosed for hunting dotted the landscapes of England and France.
