Venetian Ghetto

The Venetian Ghetto was the area of Venice in which Jews were forced to live by the Venetian Republic. Established on 29 March 1516 in the sestiere of Cannaregio, it is regarded as the first ghetto in Europe, and the English word ghetto derives from its name — possibly from the Venetian ghèto, a foundry, since the site had formerly housed copper foundries.

History

Jews confined to the Ghetto Nuovo were locked in at night and paid for the Christian guards who patrolled it; they were restricted in the trades they could practise, working chiefly as moneylenders, physicians and merchants of second-hand goods. Population pressure in the small area produced some of the tallest residential buildings in Venice, up to seven or eight storeys. Successive enclosures — the Ghetto Vecchio (1541) and Ghetto Nuovissimo (1633) — housed distinct communities: German and Italian Jews, Levantine Jews, and Spanish and Portuguese Jews, each building its own synagogue (scuola), five of which survive.

The gates were opened when Napoleon conquered Venice in 1797, and full emancipation followed with Italian unification, though Jews remained associated with the quarter. Around 200 Jewish residents were deported during the Holocaust; a memorial stands in the main campo today. The ghetto remains the centre of Jewish life in Venice, with synagogues, a museum and kosher shops.
