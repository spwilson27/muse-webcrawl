Komatiite

Komatiite is a type of ultramafic volcanic rock that originated from some of the hottest magmas known on Earth. Komatiites are defined by a very high magnesium content — typically more than 18 percent MgO — and correspondingly low silica, potassium, and aluminium. They are named after the Komati River in South Africa, near the Barberton greenstone belt where they were first described in 1969 by Richard and Morris Viljoen.

Properties

Komatiitic magma had an exceptionally low viscosity and erupted at temperatures estimated between about 1,400 °C and 1,600 °C — several hundred degrees hotter than modern basaltic lava. Such fluid lava could flow turbulently and erode channels into the ground it crossed, much like water. As the flows cooled, long, blade-like crystals of olivine grew in a crisscross pattern known as spinifex texture, named for its resemblance to an Australian spiky grass.

Occurrence and age

Nearly all komatiites are Archaean in age, older than about 2.5 billion years, because producing such hot magma required a much hotter early mantle. Classic localities include the Barberton greenstone belt of South Africa, the Abitibi belt of Canada, and the greenstone belts around Kambalda in Western Australia. The ~90-million-year-old komatiites of Gorgona Island, Colombia, are a rare younger exception.

Economic importance

Komatiite flows host major deposits of nickel sulfide ore — formed where the hot lava melted sulfur-bearing sediments along its channel — notably at Kambalda and Mount Keith in Australia and in the Abitibi belt of Canada. They are therefore an important target in mineral exploration as well as a key source of evidence about the thermal history of the early Earth.
