Institutional critique

Institutional critique is an artistic practice, emerging from conceptual art in the late 1960s, in which artists investigate and challenge the institutions of art — museums, galleries, markets, and the wider cultural structures that frame, distribute, and assign value to art. Rather than treating the museum as a neutral container, institutional critique exposes how display conventions, funding sources, architecture, and collecting policies shape what art is seen and how it is understood.

First generation

Marcel Broodthaers' fictional museum, the Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles (1968–1972), mimicked museum structures to parody their authority. Daniel Buren's striped works called attention to the sites of display and the conventions that frame art. Hans Haacke's systems works, such as his real-time exposure of museum trustees' business connections, provoked censorship — his 1971 Guggenheim exhibition was cancelled — and Michael Asher made architectural interventions, such as removing gallery partitions, that revealed the gallery's physical and ideological structures.

Second generation

In the 1980s and 1990s, artists such as Andrea Fraser, Renée Green, and Fred Wilson expanded the critique to issues of representation, race, gender, and colonialism within collections. Fraser's museum tours, performed in the persona of a docent, satirized institutional speech, while Wilson's Mining the Museum (1992) rearranged a historical society's own collection to highlight the histories — slavery and dispossession — its displays had omitted.

Criticism and legacy

Institutional critique has itself been criticized for being absorbed by the institutions it opposes: museums now commission critical works and display them as attractions. Fraser's 2005 essay "From the Critique of Institutions to an Institution of Critique" argued that critique has become professionalized within the art world, a theme debated by later theorists such as Gerald Raunig and Hito Steyerl. The practice nevertheless shaped how exhibitions acknowledge funding, provenance, and bias, and it paved the way for today's institutional reforms around restitution and inclusion.
