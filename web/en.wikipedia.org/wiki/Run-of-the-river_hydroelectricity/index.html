Run-of-the-river hydroelectricity

Run-of-the-river hydroelectricity (ROR) is a type of hydroelectric generation in which electricity is generated from the natural flow and elevation drop of a river, with little or no water storage. Unlike conventional hydroelectric dams, run-of-the-river plants have no large reservoir; a low weir or barrage may divert part of the flow through an intake, channel or penstock to turbines, after which the water is returned to the river.

Characteristics

Because output depends on the river's natural flow, generation varies seasonally and from year to year, and cannot be dispatched on demand as readily as reservoir hydro. In compensation, ROR schemes flood little land, displace few people and have a smaller footprint than large storage dams. Most run-of-the-river plants are small (under 30 MW), although large schemes exist on big rivers — for example the Beauharnois and La Grande complexes in Canada use elements of the approach.

Environmental considerations

Although generally considered less damaging than reservoir projects, run-of-the-river schemes can still fragment aquatic habitats, obstruct fish passage and alter flow regimes downstream of the diversion reach. Modern designs incorporate fish ladders, minimum environmental flows and sediment-management measures.
