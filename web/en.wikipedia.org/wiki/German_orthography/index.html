German orthography

German orthography is the system of spelling conventions for the German language, codified for official and school use in the German-speaking countries and published in the Duden reference works. Its most conspicuous features are the capitalization of all nouns, the letters Ä, Ö, Ü and ß, and a largely phonemic spelling in which letter combinations such as sch, ei, ie, eu and doubled consonants have consistent values.

Principles

German spelling follows the phonemic principle (write as you speak) moderated by the stem principle (Stammprinzip), which keeps the spelling of a word's stem constant across inflections — hence Hand with a d although it is pronounced [hant], by analogy with the plural Hände. Vowel length is signalled by doubling the vowel (See), by a following silent h (gehen), by ie for long /iː/, or by a single following consonant; short vowels are marked by doubled consonants (kommen, Wasser). Long compounds are written as a single word, as in Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft.

History

Written German was standardized gradually, aided by Luther's Bible translation, the chancery languages, and 18th- and 19th-century grammarians such as Gottsched and Adelung. Konrad Duden published his Complete Orthographic Dictionary in 1880; the Second Orthographic Conference of 1901 produced rules adopted across the German Empire and Austria in 1902–03. In 1941 the Nazi government decreed the replacement of Fraktur blackletter by Latin type, which incidentally settled the printed form of the alphabet. ß was dropped from Swiss standard usage during the 20th century.

The 1996 spelling reform

After decades of preparation, the German-speaking states agreed in 1996 on a reform (Rechtschreibreform) intended to simplify rules — notably extending the stem principle (e.g. Stängel from Stange), regularizing ß/ss distribution by vowel length (dass, but Maß), loosening compound and hyphenation rules, and adjusting comma rules. The reform was politically controversial, opposed by many writers and some newspapers, and was revised by the newly created Council for German Orthography in 2004 and 2006; in 2017 the capital letter ẞ was officially admitted as an alternative to SS in uppercase writing.
