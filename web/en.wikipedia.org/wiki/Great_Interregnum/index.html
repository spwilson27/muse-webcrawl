Great Interregnum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Interregnum (German: Großes Interregnum) was a period in the history of the Holy Roman Empire extending from the deposition of Frederick II by Pope Innocent IV in 1245 (or Frederick's death in 1250) to the election of Rudolf of Habsburg in 1273. During these decades there was no universally recognised emperor, and imperial authority fragmented among rival kings, territorial princes, and city leagues.

Course

After 1245 the papacy sponsored anti-kings, Henry Raspe and then William of Holland, against the Hohenstaufen. Following William's death in 1256, a disputed double election in 1257 produced two foreign candidates: Richard of Cornwall, brother of Henry III of England, and Alfonso X of Castile. Richard was crowned at Aachen and visited Germany several times; Alfonso never came. Neither could exercise effective rule, and the imperial office effectively lapsed. In the vacuum, the prince-electors consolidated their constitutional position — the traditional college of seven electors crystallised in this period — while imperial lands and rights were pawned, usurped, or absorbed into princely territories, and cities formed defensive leagues such as the Rhenish League of 1254.

End and significance

After Richard's death in 1272, the electors, urged on by Pope Gregory X, who wanted a functioning emperor to support a crusade, passed over the powerful Ottokar II of Bohemia and in October 1273 elected the comparatively minor Swabian count Rudolf of Habsburg. Rudolf's recovery of imperial domains (revindication) and his defeat of Ottokar at the Battle on the Marchfeld in 1278 began the restoration of central authority and founded the Habsburgs' Austrian power base. Historians regard the Interregnum as a decisive stage in Germany's development toward territorial particularism, although revisionist scholarship cautions that the period was not one of simple anarchy — regional governance, courts, and peace associations continued to function.
