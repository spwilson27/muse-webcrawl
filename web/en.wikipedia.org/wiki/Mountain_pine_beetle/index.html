Mountain pine beetle

Mountain pine beetle
Scientific nameDendroctonus ponderosae Hopkins, 1902
Order / familyColeoptera / Curculionidae (bark beetles)
RangeWestern North America, from Mexico to central British Columbia
HostsPine species, especially lodgepole, ponderosa, whitebark and limber pine

The mountain pine beetle (Dendroctonus ponderosae) is a species of bark beetle native to the forests of western North America. Though normally present at low levels, it periodically erupts into vast outbreaks that kill pine trees over millions of hectares, making it one of the most destructive forest insects on the continent.

Life cycle

Adult beetles, black and roughly the size of a grain of rice (about 5 mm), emerge in summer and fly to new host trees. Females bore through the bark and construct egg galleries in the phloem, releasing aggregation pheromones that draw mass attacks able to overwhelm a tree's resin defences. The beetles also carry blue-stain fungi, which colonise the sapwood, block water transport and help kill the tree. Larvae feed under the bark through the winter and emerge as adults the following summer, usually completing one generation per year. Needles of killed trees turn red the year after attack — the conspicuous "red attack" stage visible across whole landscapes.

Outbreaks

The outbreak that began in British Columbia in the 1990s was the largest in recorded history, infesting more than 18 million hectares of lodgepole pine and killing over half the province's merchantable pine volume; it spread across the Rockies into Alberta. In the western United States, outbreaks devastated high-elevation whitebark pine and lodgepole forests from Colorado to Montana. Warmer winters — which kill fewer overwintering larvae — and large expanses of mature, even-aged pine have been identified as major drivers, linking outbreak scale to climate change.

Effects and management

Massive tree mortality affects timber supply, wildlife habitat, watershed hydrology and wildfire behaviour, and converts forests from carbon sinks into carbon sources. Management options are limited at landscape scale; they include sanitation logging, thinning to reduce stand susceptibility, prescribed burning, and protecting individual high-value trees with anti-aggregation pheromones such as verbenone.
