Abu Sufyan ibn Harb

Ṣakhr ibn Ḥarb ibn Umayya (c. 565 – 653), better known by his kunya Abu Sufyan, was a prominent leader of the Quraysh tribe of Mecca and one of the chief opponents of the Islamic prophet Muhammad in the early years of Islam. After the Muslim conquest of Mecca in 630 he converted to Islam and became a Companion. He was the father of Mu'awiya I, founder of the Umayyad Caliphate, and of Ramla bint Abi Sufyan (Umm Habiba), a wife of Muhammad.

Opposition to Muhammad

Abu Sufyan was a wealthy merchant and one of Mecca's leading men. A caravan he was leading from Syria was the target of the Muslim expedition that led to the Battle of Badr (624), where the Quraysh were defeated. He subsequently commanded Meccan forces at the Battle of Uhud (625), which avenged Badr, and led the confederate army at the Battle of the Trench (627), which failed to take Medina. He was also a principal negotiator of the Treaty of al-Hudaybiya (628) and travelled to Medina to attempt its renewal.

Conversion and later life

On the eve of the Muslim conquest of Mecca (630), Abu Sufyan converted to Islam — according to tradition after meeting Muhammad's uncle al-Abbas — and negotiated the city's largely bloodless surrender. He fought on the Muslim side at Hunayn and Taif, where he lost an eye, and later served in the conquest of Syria, losing his other eye, by tradition, at the Battle of Yarmouk (636), where his sons Yazid and Mu'awiya held commands. He died in Medina around 653 during the caliphate of Uthman.

Legacy

Abu Sufyan's descendants, the Sufyanid branch of the Umayyad family, ruled the caliphate through his son Mu'awiya I (r. 661–680) and grandson Yazid I. Assessments of Abu Sufyan differ sharply between Sunni tradition, which honours him as a Companion, and Shia tradition, which remembers him chiefly as an enemy of Muhammad and of Ali's family.
