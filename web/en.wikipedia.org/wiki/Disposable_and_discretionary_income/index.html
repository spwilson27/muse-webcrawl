Disposable and discretionary income

Disposable income is the total personal income minus current income taxes. In national accounts definitions, personal income minus personal current taxes equals disposable personal income. Subtracting personal outlays for necessities (such as food, shelter, and clothing) yields discretionary income, the income available for spending on non-essential goods, investing, or saving.

Disposable income

Disposable income can be thought of as the money a household has left to spend and save after income taxes have been accounted for. It is closely monitored by economists as an indicator of overall economic health, since consumer spending forms a large share of economic activity in many countries.

Discretionary income

Discretionary income is what remains of disposable income after paying for necessities and fixed obligations. Some definitions also subtract required payments such as student loan repayments; for example, the United States Department of Education uses discretionary income in calculating payments under income-driven student loan repayment plans.

Marginal propensity to consume

The share of additional disposable income that a household spends rather than saves is called the marginal propensity to consume. Households with little discretionary income tend to spend a higher share of any increase in disposable income.
