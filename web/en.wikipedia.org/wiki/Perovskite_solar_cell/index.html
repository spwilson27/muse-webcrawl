Perovskite solar cell

A perovskite solar cell (PSC) is a type of solar cell that includes a perovskite-structured compound — most commonly a hybrid organic–inorganic lead or tin halide material — as the light-harvesting active layer. Perovskite materials, such as methylammonium lead halides and formamidinium lead halides, are cheap to produce and simple to manufacture.

Development

Solar-cell efficiencies of devices using perovskites increased from 3.8% in 2009 to over 25% within a decade — the fastest advance of any photovoltaic technology — and perovskite-on-silicon tandem cells have surpassed 33%. The materials have a tuneable bandgap, strong absorption, and long charge-carrier diffusion lengths, and can be deposited by solution processing at low temperature.

Device structure

A typical cell sandwiches the perovskite absorber between an electron-transport layer (often tin oxide or titanium dioxide) and a hole-transport layer (such as Spiro-OMeTAD or self-assembled monolayers), with transparent and metal electrodes completing the stack. Architectures are classed as n-i-p or inverted p-i-n.

Challenges

Commercialisation is limited chiefly by stability: perovskites degrade under moisture, heat, light, and oxygen, and ion migration causes hysteresis and performance loss. Encapsulation, compositional engineering, and 2D/3D interface treatments have greatly improved lifetimes. The presence of soluble lead compounds raises environmental and regulatory questions, motivating recycling schemes and lead-free (tin-based) research, though tin devices remain less efficient and less stable.

Commercialisation

Companies such as Oxford PV have begun pilot production of perovskite–silicon tandem modules, and perovskite photovoltaics are a leading candidate for lightweight, flexible, and building-integrated solar power.
