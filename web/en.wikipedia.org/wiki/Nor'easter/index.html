Nor'easter

A nor'easter (also northeaster; see below) is a large-scale extratropical cyclone in the western North Atlantic Ocean. The name derives from the strong northeasterly winds that blow in from the ocean over the northeastern United States, particularly New England and the Mid-Atlantic states. Nor'easters are most common and most powerful between October and April, although they can occur at any time of year.

Nor'easter
Type: Extratropical cyclone
Regions affected: Eastern Seaboard of the United States and Canada
Season: Primarily October–April

Formation

Nor'easters develop as low-pressure systems along the East Coast, often forming off the Carolinas or near the Gulf of Mexico and tracking northeastward along the coast. Cold continental air from Canada meets warm, moist air over the Gulf Stream, and the contrast fuels rapid deepening — sometimes "bombogenesis" — as the storm approaches New England.

Impacts

Nor'easters can produce heavy rain or snow, gale-force winds, rough seas, coastal flooding, and beach erosion. In winter they may stall into blizzards; famous examples include the Great Blizzard of 1888, the Ash Wednesday Storm of 1962, the Blizzard of 1978, the "Storm of the Century" of 1993, and the January 2015 blizzard.

Terminology

The term reflects the prevailing wind direction during the storm in coastal areas: winds rotate counterclockwise around the low, driving air ashore from the northeast. In maritime New England speech, "nor'easter" became the standard contraction of "northeaster."
