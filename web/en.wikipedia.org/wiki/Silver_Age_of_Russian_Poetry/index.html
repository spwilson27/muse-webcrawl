Silver Age of Russian Poetry

The Silver Age of Russian Poetry is the term traditionally applied to the extraordinarily fruitful period in the history of Russian poetry spanning roughly the last decade of the 19th century and the first two or three decades of the 20th century, conventionally dated from about 1890 to the early 1920s (some scholars extend it to 1930). It succeeded the "Golden Age" of Pushkin and Lermontov in the first third of the 19th century and was a period of innovation and experimentation in which a remarkable generation of poets transformed Russian verse.

Movements

Symbolism

The first major movement of the Silver Age was Symbolism, influenced by French models and by the philosophy of Vladimir Solovyov. The older generation included Valery Bryusov, Konstantin Balmont, Dmitri Merezhkovsky and Zinaida Gippius; the younger generation, led by Alexander Blok and Andrei Bely, developed a mystical, apocalyptic strain that viewed art as a means of perceiving higher spiritual realities.

Acmeism

In reaction against Symbolist vagueness, the Acmeists — Nikolai Gumilev, Anna Akhmatova and Osip Mandelstam among them — championed clarity, concreteness and the crafted word, celebrating the material world and cultural memory. Their "Poets' Guild" (founded 1911) and Gumilev's 1913 manifesto marked the school's emergence.

Futurism

The Russian Futurists, including Vladimir Mayakovsky, Velimir Khlebnikov, Alexei Kruchenykh and David Burliuk, demanded a radical break with the past, inventing new words (the transrational language zaum) and shocking bourgeois taste, as in their 1912 manifesto "A Slap in the Face of Public Taste".

Other currents

The period also included the peasant poets (Sergei Yesenin, Nikolai Klyuev), the Imaginists, and independent figures such as Marina Tsvetaeva, Boris Pasternak and Maximilian Voloshin, whose work defies easy classification.

End of the Silver Age

The First World War, the revolutions of 1917 and the Civil War shattered the milieu that had sustained the Silver Age. Blok died in 1921, the year Gumilev was executed; Khlebnikov died in 1922. Many poets emigrated; those who remained faced increasing ideological control under the Soviet regime. By the late 1920s, with the imposition of Socialist Realism and the deaths or silencing of its leading voices, the Silver Age is generally considered closed.

Legacy

The Silver Age is now regarded as one of the supreme achievements of Russian culture. Its poets — read clandestinely during much of the Soviet period — were restored to prominence after the 1960s and today occupy a central place in the Russian literary canon.
