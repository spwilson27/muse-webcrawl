Bayonet

A bayonet (from French baïonnette, after the town of Bayonne) is a knife, dagger, sword or spike-shaped weapon designed to fit on the end of the muzzle of a rifle, carbine or similar firearm, allowing it to be used as a spear-like weapon. From the early 17th to the early 20th century it was a primary infantry weapon for close combat, and it remains in service worldwide, in modern form, as a multipurpose knife and close-quarters weapon.

History

The first "plug" bayonets, wedged into the musket barrel, appeared in France in the early 1600s but prevented firing while fixed. The socket bayonet, fitting around the barrel with a ring, solved the problem and was widely adopted in the 1690s, allowing infantry to dispense with pikemen entirely. The 18th and 19th centuries saw long sword bayonets and brutal bayonet charges; yet analyses of wounds — for example in the American Civil War — consistently showed bayonets caused well under one per cent of battlefield casualties, their real value being psychological.

Modern use

Twentieth-century bayonets shortened into knife bayonets doubling as fighting and utility knives, such as the US M7 and M9 and the British L3A1. Bayonet charges remained occasionally significant — British troops used them in the Falklands War (1982) and in Iraq and Afghanistan in the 2000s. Most modern armies still issue bayonets and train with them, valuing their role in close combat, prisoner control, and as a general-purpose tool.
