Fraternities and sororities

Fraternities and sororities, also referred to collectively as Greek life, are social organisations at colleges and universities, primarily in North America. A fraternity is generally an organisation formed by and for men, and a sorority one formed by and for women, though some are co-educational. They are typically identified by two or three Greek letters, and share features such as secret rituals, a system of chapters at multiple institutions, organised recruitment ("rush"), pledging and initiation, communal housing, philanthropy, and alumni networks that extend beyond graduation.

History

The first Greek-letter society, Phi Beta Kappa, was founded at the College of William & Mary in 1776 as a literary and social society (it later became an academic honour society). The modern social fraternity system began with the "Union Triad" at Union College in the 1820s — Kappa Alpha Society (1825), Sigma Phi (1827), and Delta Phi (1827). The first women's organisations, later called sororities — a term coined in 1882 — appeared in the 1850s, notably at Wesleyan College in Macon, Georgia. In the twentieth century, culturally based organisations flourished, including the historically African American fraternities and sororities of the National Pan-Hellenic Council (the "Divine Nine," founded from 1906 onward) and Latino and Asian-interest Greek organisations.

Structure and activities

Local chapters are chartered by (inter)national organisations, which set standards and are grouped into umbrella councils such as the North American Interfraternity Conference, the National Panhellenic Conference, the National Pan-Hellenic Council, and multicultural Greek councils. Chapters emphasise scholarship, leadership, community service, and philanthropy, and many maintain chapter houses near campus. Membership selection, initiation rituals, and internal governance are traditionally secret.

Criticism

Greek life has been criticised for hazing — sometimes with fatal outcomes, which has led to anti-hazing laws in most U.S. states — as well as for alcohol abuse, exclusivity, and associations with sexual misconduct. Supporters respond that members on average perform substantial community service, develop leadership skills, and report strong alumni engagement and charitable giving. Debates over the role of fraternities and sororities continue on many campuses, and a few institutions have abolished them altogether.
