Reform Acts

The Reform Acts (or Reform Bills before passage) are legislation enacted in the United Kingdom in the 19th and 20th centuries to reform the electoral system, principally by extending the franchise and redistributing seats in the House of Commons. Between them, the acts of 1832, 1867 and 1884 (with the Representation of the People Acts of 1918 and 1928 completing the process) transformed Britain from a system in which a small, property-owning minority elected Parliament — many of them in "rotten boroughs" controlled by patrons — into a mass democracy.

Reform Act 1832

The Great Reform Act, steered through Parliament by Earl Grey's Whig government after the "Days of May" crisis in which the Lords' resistance provoked riots and the threat of mass creation of peers, abolished 56 rotten boroughs, reduced representation of 30 more, and gave seats to the new industrial cities such as Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds and Sheffield. In England and Wales the electorate rose from roughly 400,000 to about 650,000 — one adult man in five — by standardising the borough franchise on £10 householders. Companion acts covered Scotland and Ireland.

Reform Act 1867

Benjamin Disraeli's Conservative government, aided by Liberal divisions, passed a second act that enfranchised most urban working-class men: all male householders in boroughs (subject to a year's residence and rate-paying), plus lodgers paying £10 a year. The electorate of England and Wales nearly doubled to about 2.5 million. A limited redistribution accompanied it, and the Scottish and Irish acts of 1868 followed.

Reform Act 1884 and redistribution

Gladstone's Third Reform Act extended the 1867 borough franchise (household suffrage) to the counties, adding roughly two million voters, some 40% of adult men still excluded. The accompanying Redistribution of Seats Act 1885, negotiated between Gladstone and Lord Salisbury, created largely equal single-member constituencies and redrew the electoral map into a recognisably modern pattern. Further milestones included the secret ballot (1872), corrupt practices legislation (1883), payment of MPs (1911), and the abolition of plural voting and university seats in 1948.

Representation of the People Acts

The Representation of the People Act 1918 enfranchised virtually all men over 21 (and servicemen at 19) and women over 30 meeting a property qualification — tripling the electorate to about 21 million. The Equal Franchise Act 1928 put women on equal terms at 21. The voting age was lowered to 18 by the Representation of the People Act 1969.

Legacy

The Reform Acts were milestones in the development of British parliamentary democracy and in the rise of disciplined national parties, requiring mass organisation between elections. The language of "reform" campaigns of 1830–32, echoed by the Chartists who demanded still more, remains a reference point in debates about the franchise and the House of Lords.
