Yoshua Bengio (born 5 March 1964) is a Canadian computer scientist and a pioneer of deep learning. A professor at the Université de Montréal and founder of the Montreal Institute for Learning Algorithms (Mila), he shared the 2018 ACM A.M. Turing Award with Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun for conceptual and engineering breakthroughs that made deep neural networks a critical component of computing. The three are often called the "Godfathers of AI."

Career and research

Bengio earned his bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees at McGill University, completing his PhD in 1991, and did postdoctoral work at MIT and Bell Labs before joining the Université de Montréal in 1993. When neural network research was out of fashion in the 1990s and early 2000s, he persisted in developing it, publishing influential work on neural probabilistic language models (2003), which introduced word embeddings learned by a neural network; on representation learning; and on the training difficulties of deep architectures, including greedy layer-wise pretraining. He co-founded the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) and helped build the Theano software library. His doctoral students included Ian Goodfellow, inventor of generative adversarial networks.

Mila and public engagement

Under Bengio's direction, Mila grew into one of the world's largest academic research institutes in machine learning, anchoring Montreal's AI ecosystem. In later years Bengio became a prominent voice on the risks of advanced AI, warning about loss of control, misuse, and concentration of power, and advocating national and international governance; in 2024 he led the first International Scientific Report on the Safety of Advanced AI at the request of governments, and he has supported pauses and regulation for frontier systems while continuing research on safer, Bayesian approaches to machine intelligence.

Honours

Besides the Turing Award, Bengio is a Fellow of the Royal Society, an Officer of the Order of Canada, a chevalier of the French Legion of Honour, and among the most-cited computer scientists in the world.
