A light cruiser is a type of small to medium-sized warship. The term is a shortening of "light armoured cruiser", describing a ship fitted with a protective belt of armour; under the naval treaties of the interwar period the category was defined by gun calibre — cruisers with guns of 6.1 inches (155 mm) or less were "light", those with larger guns "heavy".

History

Light cruisers evolved from the protected and scout cruisers of the late 19th century. The Royal Navy's Town class and the Arethusa class set the pattern before the First World War, in which light cruisers scouted for the battle fleet, led destroyer flotillas and fought actions such as Heligoland Bight and Jutland. The Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 and the London Naval Treaty of 1930 codified the light/heavy distinction and capped tonnage.

Second World War

Light cruisers were built in large numbers during the 1930s rearmament: the American Brooklyn class, the British Town (Southampton) class and the Japanese Mogami class (initially) carried up to fifteen 6-inch guns. They performed fleet screening, shore bombardment, anti-aircraft escort — the specialised Atlanta class — and commerce protection.

Postwar

Missiles displaced guns after 1945, and surviving gun-armed light cruisers lingered in some navies into the 1970s and 1980s. The Peruvian Almirante Grau, a former Dutch ship, was the last in service when decommissioned in 2017. Modern usage rarely applies the term; today's cruisers are guided-missile ships.
