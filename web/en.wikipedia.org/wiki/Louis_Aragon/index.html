Louis Aragon (3 October 1897 – 24 December 1982) was a French poet, novelist, and journalist, a founding figure of Dada and Surrealism who became one of the leading intellectuals of the French Communist Party. His love poetry to his wife Elsa Triolet, his Resistance verse, and his long sequence of novels made him one of the most widely read French writers of the 20th century.

Dada and Surrealism

Aragon served as a medical auxiliary in the First World War and afterwards joined André Breton and Philippe Soupault in founding the review Littérature and the Paris Dada movement. A central surrealist, he wrote the prose masterpiece Le Paysan de Paris (1926, "Paris Peasant") and helped define the movement before breaking with Breton in the early 1930s as he committed himself to communism.

Communism and Resistance

Aragon joined the French Communist Party in 1927 and, after meeting the Russian-born writer Elsa Triolet in 1928 (they married in 1939), became the party's most prominent literary figure, editing the newspaper Ce soir and defending socialist realism. Mobilised in 1939, he was decorated for bravery; during the Occupation he was a leading voice of the literary Resistance, publishing clandestine poetry — Le Crève-cœur (1941) and Les Yeux d'Elsa (1942) — whose love lyrics to Elsa doubled as patriotic appeals.

Later work

His novel cycles Le Monde réel and Les Communistes traced French society toward the war years. A member of the PCF central committee, he remained broadly loyal to the party while growing more critical of Soviet repression after 1956; his late autobiographical poem Le Roman inachevé (1956) and the novel La Semaine sainte (1958) are often judged his finest postwar works. Many of his poems, set to music, entered French popular song.
