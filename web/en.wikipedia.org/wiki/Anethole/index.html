Anethole is an organic compound, a phenylpropene derivative, that is the principal flavour and fragrance component of anise, fennel, and star anise. It gives these plants — and drinks flavoured with them, such as ouzo, pastis, rakı, and absinthe — their characteristic sweet, liquorice-like taste and smell. Anethole is about thirteen times sweeter than sugar but is used as a flavouring rather than a sweetener.

Properties and occurrence

The naturally occurring and commercially important form is trans-anethole, obtained by steam distillation of star anise or fennel oil, in which it can constitute 80–90% of the oil. The cis isomer is more toxic and has a harsher flavour. Anethole is only slightly soluble in water but freely soluble in ethanol; this difference produces the famous "ouzo effect": when water is added to an anise spirit, the anethole spontaneously forms a stable milky micro-emulsion (louche) without any added emulsifier.

Uses

Anethole is widely used to flavour alcoholic drinks, confectionery, baked goods, chewing gum, and oral-hygiene products, and as a fragrance in soaps and cosmetics. Industrially it has served as a starting material in the synthesis of other flavour compounds and, historically, of photographic chemicals; its structural resemblance to catecholamine neurotransmitters has also made it a watched precursor in illicit drug manufacture in some jurisdictions.

Safety

At flavouring doses anethole is generally recognised as safe, but in concentrated form it is a skin sensitiser, and high doses in animal studies have shown hepatotoxic effects; the related compound estragole, which co-occurs in fennel, has attracted more toxicological concern. Anethole also exhibits mild antimicrobial and insecticidal activity.
