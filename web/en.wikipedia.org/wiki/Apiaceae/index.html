Apiaceae (/ˌeɪpiˈeɪsiːˌiː, -siˌaɪ/), also called Umbelliferae, is a family of mostly aromatic flowering plants named after the type genus Apium. It is the 16th-largest family of flowering plants, with more than 3,800 species in about 446 genera, including such well-known and economically important plants as ajwain, angelica, anise, asafoetida, caraway, carrot, celery, chervil, coriander, cumin, dill, fennel, lovage, cow parsley, parsnip, parsley, and sea holly, as well as the highly poisonous plants such as poison hemlock, water hemlock, spotted cowbane, fool's parsley, and various species of water dropwort.

Description

Most Apiaceae are annual, biennial or perennial herbs (frequently with the leaves aggregated toward the base), though a minority are woody shrubs or trees such as Bupleurum fruticosum. Their leaves are of variable size and alternately arranged, or with the upper leaves becoming nearly opposite. In some taxa, the texture is leathery or even rigid, but usually they are partly or wholly divided.

The inflorescence is usually a compound umbel — an umbrella-shaped cluster in which individual flower stalks (rays) radiate from a common point — from which the family's older name, Umbelliferae, derives. The flowers are typically small, actinomorphic, and bisexual, with five petals and five stamens. The fruit is a schizocarp consisting of two fused carpels that typically separates into two mericarps at maturity.

Taxonomy

John Lindley first described the family in 1836. The name "Apiaceae" was conserved and is based on the type genus Apium, described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The family is divided into four subfamilies: Apioideae (the largest, with roughly 90% of the genera), Azorelloideae, Mackinlayoideae, and Saniculoideae. Apiaceae is closely related to the families Araliaceae and Pittosporaceae within the order Apiales.

Distribution and ecology

The family is distributed nearly worldwide, but is most diverse in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, particularly in the Mediterranean basin, Central Asia, and western North America. Many species are important food plants and spices, while others produce potent toxins: poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), famous as the poison used to execute Socrates, contains the alkaloid coniine, and water hemlock (Cicuta) is among the most violently toxic plants in North America.

Uses

Many members are cultivated as food crops, including the carrot (Daucus carota), parsnip (Pastinaca sativa), parsley (Petroselinum crispum), and celery (Apium graveolens). Others are grown as culinary herbs and spices: coriander, cumin, fennel, dill, caraway, anise, and asafoetida. The family's aromatic character comes largely from essential oils stored in specialized ducts (vittae) in the fruits and foliage. Some species, such as Queen Anne's lace, are grown as ornamentals.
