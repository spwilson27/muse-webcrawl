Epoxy

Epoxy refers to a class of thermosetting polymers formed by the reaction of an epoxide resin with a hardener (curing agent). Epoxy resins contain reactive epoxide groups — three-membered rings of two carbon atoms and one oxygen. When mixed with amine, anhydride or other hardeners, these rings open and cross-link into a rigid, insoluble network notable for strong adhesion, chemical and heat resistance, and good electrical insulation.

Chemistry

The most common resin is diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (DGEBA), produced from bisphenol A and epichlorohydrin; variants include novolac epoxies for higher temperature resistance and glycidyl-amine resins used in aerospace composites. Curing may occur at room temperature or be accelerated with heat. Fillers, tougheners and diluents adjust viscosity, flexibility and cost. Because the cured material shrinks little, epoxies make accurate castings and strong bonds.

Applications

  • Adhesives — two-part "epoxy glue" for structural and household bonding of metals, wood, glass and plastics.
  • Coatings — corrosion-resistant paints, floor coatings and can linings.
  • Composites — the matrix resin for carbon-fibre and fibreglass in aircraft, wind-turbine blades, boats and sports equipment.
  • Electronics — encapsulants ("potting") and the FR-4 laminate of printed circuit boards.
  • Construction — grouts, repair mortars and chemical anchors.

History and health

Epoxy chemistry dates to the 1930s, with Paul Schlack in Germany and Sylvan Greenlee in the United States developing resins commercially in the 1940s. Uncured resins and amine hardeners can be skin sensitisers, and bisphenol A–based epoxies have drawn scrutiny where they contact food; cured epoxy is comparatively inert.
