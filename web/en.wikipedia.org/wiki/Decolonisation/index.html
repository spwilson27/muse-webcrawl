Decolonisation

Decolonisation
DefinitionProcess by which colonies become independent of their colonising country
Peak period1945–1975 (Africa and Asia)
Key eventsIndian independence 1947; "Year of Africa" 1960; end of Portuguese empire 1975

Decolonisation is the undoing of colonialism, the process whereby a nation establishes and maintains its domination of foreign territories. The concept covers the achievement of independence by former colonies, the dismantling of colonial administrations, and — in wider usage — the intellectual and cultural work of freeing societies from colonial modes of thought. The United Nations has made decolonisation a central mission since 1945.

Waves of decolonisation

The Americas decolonised first: the Thirteen Colonies became the United States in 1776–1783, Haiti won independence from France in 1804, and most of Spanish America broke away in the 1810s–1820s. A second great wave followed the Second World War as weakened European powers faced organised nationalist movements: India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesia in 1949, Ghana in 1957, and seventeen African states in 1960 alone. Portugal held out until its Carnation Revolution in 1974, whereupon Angola and Mozambique became independent in 1975.

Methods and outcomes

Independence was achieved through negotiation in many colonies and through armed struggle in others, including Algeria, Vietnam, Kenya, and the Portuguese territories. Partition (India, Palestine, Cyprus, Korea) left lasting conflicts. Many new states joined the Non-Aligned Movement, seeking a path between the Cold War blocs, while critics argued that political independence without economic change amounted to neocolonialism.

The United Nations

In 1960 the General Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, and in 1961 created a Special Committee on Decolonization. A small number of territories — including Western Sahara — remain on the UN list of Non-Self-Governing Territories.
