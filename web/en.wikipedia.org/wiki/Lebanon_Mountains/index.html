The Mount Lebanon Range (Arabic: جبل لبنان, Jabal Lubnān) is a mountain range in Lebanon, running roughly parallel to the Mediterranean coast for about 170 kilometres from north to south. It averages about 25 km in width and reaches its highest point at Qurnat as Sawda' (3,088 m), the highest peak in Lebanon and the Levant. The name "Lebanon" itself derives from a Semitic root meaning "white", referring to the range's snow-covered summits.

Geography

The range separates the narrow coastal plain from the Beqaa Valley to the east, beyond which rises the Anti-Lebanon range on the Syrian border. Deep river gorges — including those of the Litani, Ibrahim and Kadisha rivers — cut through the western slopes. Snow falls on the high peaks in winter and supports several ski resorts, including the Cedars and Mzaar Kfardebian. Settlements climb the western slopes, whose terraced hillsides grow olives, grapes and fruit trees.

Geology and ecology

The range is composed mainly of limestone, sandstone and dolomite formed in the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods and uplifted by the tectonics of the Dead Sea Transform. It is noted for its ancient stands of cedar of Lebanon (Cedrus libani), preserved in reserves such as the Cedars of God (a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and the Al Shouf Cedar Nature Reserve. The mountains host diverse wildlife including striped hyenas, wolves, and numerous endemic plants.

History

Mount Lebanon has long served as a refuge for religious minorities, including Maronite Christians and Druze, and in the Ottoman period enjoyed periods of autonomy under the Ma'n and Shihab emirates and, from 1861, the Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate. The modern Mount Lebanon Governorate covers only part of the historic region.
