Permanent supportive housing

Permanent supportive housing (PSH) is a model of housing that combines long-term affordable housing with voluntary support services — such as case management, mental health and substance-use treatment, and help with daily living — for people who have experienced chronic homelessness, often in combination with disability, mental illness, or addiction. Unlike emergency shelters or transitional housing, permanent supportive housing has no limit on the length of stay and tenants hold leases with normal tenancy rights.

Model

PSH is closely associated with the Housing First philosophy, which holds that stable housing should be provided without preconditions such as sobriety or treatment compliance, and that services are more effective once a person is housed. Units may be in dedicated buildings or scattered across ordinary apartment buildings, with services delivered on site or by visiting teams, including assertive community treatment teams for people with the highest needs.

Evidence

Research on permanent supportive housing consistently finds high rates of housing retention — most tenants remain housed after a year — and reductions in use of expensive crisis services such as emergency rooms, hospitals, and jails. Studies of "high-utiliser" populations have found that the savings from avoided crisis services can offset much or all of the programme's cost, although results vary by population and locality. The model was scaled up in the United States with federal funding from the 1990s onward, notably through the McKinney–Vento Homeless Assistance Grants, and similar approaches have been adopted in Canada, Finland, and other countries.
