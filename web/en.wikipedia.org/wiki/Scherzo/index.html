Scherzo

A scherzo (UK: /ˈskɛətsoʊ/, US: /ˈskɛrtsoʊ/; Italian: [ˈskertso]; plural scherzos or scherzi) is a short composition — sometimes a movement within a larger work such as a symphony or sonata — characterized by a lively, playful character. The Italian word literally means "joke" or "sport".

Development

The scherzo developed out of the minuet and trio, which it gradually replaced as the standard dance-like movement of the Classical symphony. Haydn's late string quartets already called certain fast triple-metre movements scherzo, but it was Beethoven who transformed the movement: in the symphonies and quartets the scherzo became faster, metrically unstable and frequently humorous or grotesque, with sudden accents and dynamic contrasts replacing courtly grace. The typical form remains ternary — scherzo, a contrasting trio section, and a repeat of the scherzo — usually in 3/4 time at a brisk tempo.

Independent scherzi

In the Romantic period the scherzo also became an independent piece. Chopin's four piano scherzos are large, dramatic single movements that stretch the "joke" far beyond its origins; Mendelssohn favoured light, fleet scherzos (as in the Midsummer Night's Dream music), while Brahms, Bruckner and Mahler gave the symphonic scherzo enormous structural weight. The term is also applied to movements marked scherzando or to self-contained orchestral pieces such as Dukas's The Sorcerer's Apprentice, subtitled a scherzo after Goethe.
