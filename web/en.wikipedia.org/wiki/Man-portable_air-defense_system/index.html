Man-portable air-defense system

Man-portable air-defense systems (MANPADS or MPADS) are shoulder-launched surface-to-air missiles designed to be carried and fired by a single soldier. They are typically guided weapons using infrared homing seekers that lock onto an aircraft's heat signature, and are effective against low-flying helicopters, transport aircraft and ground-attack jets.

Development

The first MANPADS entered service in the late 1960s: the American FIM-43 Redeye and the Soviet 9K32 Strela-2 (SA-7 Grail). Successive generations improved seeker sensitivity and resistance to countermeasures:

  • First generation — rear-aspect-only infrared seekers (Redeye, Strela-2).
  • Second generation — cooled, all-aspect seekers, e.g. the FIM-92 Stinger and Strela-3.
  • Third and fourth generations — dual-colour and imaging infrared seekers with strong flare rejection, e.g. the Russian 9K38 Igla and 9K333 Verba, the French Mistral, the Swedish RBS 70 (laser beam-riding) and the British Starstreak (laser-guided, high velocity).

Combat use

MANPADS have been used in nearly every conflict since the Vietnam War. Stinger missiles supplied to Afghan mujahideen in the 1980s are widely credited with increasing Soviet helicopter losses. More recently, MANPADS supplied to Ukraine from 2022 were used extensively against Russian aircraft.

Proliferation concerns

Because they are small, concealable and capable of downing airliners during takeoff and landing, MANPADS are a major counter-proliferation concern. Tens of thousands are held in national stockpiles, and leakage to insurgent and terrorist groups has prompted export-control regimes such as the Wassenaar Arrangement's MANPADS elements and buy-back programmes following the collapse of states such as Libya in 2011.
