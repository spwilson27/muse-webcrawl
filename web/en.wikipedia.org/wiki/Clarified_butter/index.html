Clarified butter is milk fat rendered from butter to separate the milk solids and water from the butterfat. It is typically produced by melting butter and allowing the components to separate by density: water evaporates, some solids float to the surface and are skimmed off, and the remaining milk solids sink to the bottom and are left behind when the butterfat is poured off. The resulting fat has a higher smoke point (about 252 °C / 486 °F) than ordinary butter (about 163 °C / 325 °F) and a much longer shelf life.

Preparation

Butter is gently melted over low heat until the water boils off and the milk proteins separate into a surface foam and sediment at the bottom. The clear golden fat between them is carefully strained or decanted. Because clarifying removes nearly all lactose and casein, clarified butter is often tolerated by people sensitive to those components.

Culinary uses

Clarified butter is prized for high-heat cooking such as searing and sautéing, and for sauces — it is the fat used in hollandaise and béarnaise and as a drawn-butter dip for lobster and other seafood. In French cuisine, gently browned clarified butter becomes beurre noisette ("hazelnut butter").

Regional varieties

In South Asia, ghee is simmered longer until the solids brown lightly, giving a nutty flavor; it is central to Indian cooking and Ayurvedic tradition. Middle Eastern samneh, Ethiopian spiced niter kibbeh, and Egyptian samna are similar long-cooked clarified butters. Because it keeps for months without refrigeration in cool conditions, clarified butter has long been important in hot climates.
