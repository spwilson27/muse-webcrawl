The Unicorn Tapestries

Datec. 1495–1505
MediumWool, silk, silver and gold thread
PanelsSeven
LocationThe Cloisters, Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

The Unicorn Tapestries are a series of seven tapestries, woven in the Southern Netherlands around 1495–1505, among the most celebrated works of late medieval art. They depict a group of noblemen and hunters pursuing a unicorn through a flower-strewn landscape, capturing and killing it, and — in the final, most famous panel — the unicorn alive again, resting within a fenced enclosure.

The panels

The sequence runs: The Hunters Enter the Woods; The Unicorn Purifies Water (or The Unicorn at the Fountain); The Unicorn Crosses a Stream; The Unicorn Defends Itself; The Unicorn Is Captured (surviving only as two fragments); The Hunters Return to the Castle (the unicorn slain and brought to the lord and lady); and The Unicorn in Captivity, in which the beast, collared to a pomegranate tree, appears content and possibly resurrected. The backgrounds are in the millefleurs style, with over a hundred identifiable plant species.

History and interpretation

The tapestries bear the cipher "A.E.", whose owner remains unidentified; they may have been made for Anne of Brittany. Long in the possession of the La Rochefoucauld family, they were bought by John D. Rockefeller Jr., who donated them to the Metropolitan Museum in 1937 for display at The Cloisters. Scholars read them variously as an allegory of Christ's incarnation and passion, of courtly love and marriage, or of a lover's pursuit — readings the sequence sustains simultaneously.
