Current-mode logic

Current-mode logic (CML), also called source-coupled logic (SCL), is a differential digital logic family in which a constant bias current is steered between two branches of a differential pair according to the input signal. It is closely related to emitter-coupled logic (ECL) and is widely used in high-speed integrated circuits.

Operation

A CML gate consists of a differential pair of transistors, load resistors, and a constant-current source. The input signal determines which branch carries the current; the resulting voltage drop across the load resistors produces a small differential output swing, typically around 250–400 mV. Because the total current drawn from the supply is constant, switching generates little supply noise, and because transistors never saturate, very high switching speeds are possible.

Applications

CML is the standard logic style for high-speed serial data interfaces, including SerDes transceivers, clock distribution networks, and physical layers for standards running at multiple gigabits per second. Its low voltage swing reduces dynamic power at high frequencies compared with full-swing CMOS, although the static bias current means it consumes power even when idle — a trade-off acceptable in performance-critical links.

Comparison with other logic families

  • CMOS — zero static power and full voltage swing, but higher switching noise and limited speed in simple gates.
  • ECL — the bipolar predecessor of CML, with similar differential operation.
  • LVDS — a signalling standard that also uses a steered current source, applying the same principle off-chip.
