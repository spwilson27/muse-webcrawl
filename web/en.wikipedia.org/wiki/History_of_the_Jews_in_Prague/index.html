History of the Jews in Prague

The history of the Jews in Prague stretches back to the tenth century, making the city home to one of Europe's oldest continuous Jewish communities. Prague's Jewish Town — later named Josefov — produced landmarks such as the Old New Synagogue (c. 1270), the Old Jewish Cemetery, and figures including Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel (the Maharal), associated with the legend of the Golem. The community flourished under Rudolf II, suffered expulsions and pogroms, was emancipated in the nineteenth century, and was nearly annihilated in the Holocaust; today a small but active community maintains the Jewish Quarter as a major heritage site.

The Old New Synagogue (1270), Europe's oldest active synagogue.

Early settlement and the medieval ghetto

Jewish merchants are recorded in Prague by the tenth century; the traveller Ibrahim ibn Yaqub mentioned the city in 965. The community settled around Prague Castle and later the Old Town. The Crusader era brought violence (1096), and in 1389 an Easter pogrom killed some 3,000 Jews. Confined to a walled ghetto, Prague's Jews developed distinctive institutions and crafts; the Old Jewish Cemetery, used from the fifteenth century to 1787, accumulated some 12,000 gravestones.

The Rudolfinian golden age

Under Rudolf II (r. 1576–1612) Prague Jewry prospered. The wealthy court Jew Mordecai Maisel financed synagogues, a town hall and charitable foundations, while Rabbi Loew, chief rabbi from 1597, led the community and met the emperor in 1592. Yom-Tov Lipmann Heller and the astronomer-rabbi David Gans (a correspondent of Tycho Brahe and Kepler) exemplify the era's scholarship. The ghetto reached perhaps 11,000 inhabitants, among the largest in Europe.

Expulsion, return and emancipation

Maria Theresa ordered the expulsion of Prague's Jews in 1744 (effective 1745), accusing them of collaboration with Prussian invaders; the order was rescinded in 1748. Joseph II's Toleration Edicts (1781–82) eased restrictions. Emancipation proceeded after 1848; the ghetto was dissolved, renamed Josefov, and formally incorporated into Prague in 1850. Jews became prominent in Czech and German cultural and commercial life — Franz Kafka, Max Brod and Franz Werfel were Prague Jews — though the 1897 Hilsner affair showed lingering antisemitism.

The Holocaust and after

Of roughly 56,000 Jews in Prague in 1939, the great majority were deported via Theresienstadt to extermination camps; only a few thousand survived. The Nazi plan to make Prague's Jewish Museum a "museum of an extinct race" paradoxically preserved its collections. Under communism the community was suppressed; after 1989 it revived, and the Jewish Museum in Prague now cares for the quarter's synagogues, cemetery and collections.
