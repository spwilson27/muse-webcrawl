Death row

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Death row is the area of a prison housing inmates sentenced to death, and by extension the condition of awaiting execution. In the United States, condemned prisoners are held in special units (in Texas at the Polunsky Unit, in California historically at San Quentin, in the federal system at Terre Haute), spend years or decades litigating appeals and habeas corpus petitions while confined — typically in solitary or near-solitary conditions for 22–23 hours a day — a regime critics call the “death row phenomenon” when prolonged suffering is alleged to be cruel and unusual.

The phenomenon has legal force elsewhere: the European Court of Human Rights in Soering v. United Kingdom (1989) blocked extradition to face Virginia's death row, and the Privy Council in Pratt v. Attorney General for Jamaica (1993) held that execution after more than five years on death row would be inhuman punishment, leading to mass commutations in the Caribbean. The US death-row population peaked at roughly 3,600 around 2000 and has since declined to roughly 2,000–2,500, as death sentences have fallen; exonerations (more than 190 in the modern US era) figure centrally in policy debate.
