DNA helicases are a class of enzymes and motor proteins that separate the two strands of a DNA double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the bases. Driven by ATP hydrolysis, they move directionally along one strand — either 3′→5′ or 5′→3′ — unwinding the duplex ahead of them. They are essential for DNA replication, repair, recombination and some transcription.

Structure and mechanism

Many helicases form ring-shaped hexamers that encircle a single strand of DNA and translocate along it, excluding the complementary strand; others act as monomers or dimers that wedge the strands apart. They are classified into superfamilies (SF1–SF6) based on conserved sequence motifs, including the Walker A and Walker B boxes involved in ATP binding and hydrolysis.

In replication

At the replication fork, the replicative helicase unwinds the parental DNA so polymerases can copy each strand. In bacteria this is the DnaB helicase, loaded at the origin by DnaC; in eukaryotes and archaea it is the MCM2–7 complex, which with Cdc45 and GINS forms the active CMG helicase. Helicases work with topoisomerases, which relieve the torsional strain built up ahead of the fork.

Disease

Mutations in human helicases cause several genetic disorders: WRN mutations cause Werner syndrome (premature ageing), BLM mutations cause Bloom syndrome, and defects in XPD/ERCC2, a helicase involved in nucleotide excision repair, cause xeroderma pigmentosum and related conditions. The RecQ family of helicases is especially important for genome stability.
