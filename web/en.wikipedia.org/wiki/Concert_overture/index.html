FormSingle-movement orchestral work
Era19th century onwards

A concert overture is a free-standing, single-movement orchestral work, usually in sonata form, composed to be performed at a concert rather than as an introduction to an opera or play. The genre emerged in the early nineteenth century as overtures written for the theatre began to be played, and enjoyed, on their own.

Development

Felix Mendelssohn was a pioneer: his overture to A Midsummer Night's Dream (1826), written at seventeen, could accompany the play but was also performed independently, and The Hebrides (1830, "Fingal's Cave") evoked a seascape without any dramatic text. Such pieces often carry an extramusical idea — a landscape, a literary work, a national celebration — without the detailed programme of the later symphonic poem.

Notable examples

  • Beethoven's Coriolan and Egmont overtures, originally for plays, became concert staples
  • Berlioz: Le Carnaval romain
  • Brahms: Academic Festival Overture and Tragic Overture
  • Tchaikovsky: Romeo and Juliet (described as an overture-fantasy) and the 1812 Overture
  • Dvořák's concert overtures and Elgar's Cockaigne and In the South

The concert overture blurred into the symphonic poem but remained a distinct, compact concert opener through the twentieth century and beyond.
