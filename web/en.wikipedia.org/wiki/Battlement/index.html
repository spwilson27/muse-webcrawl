Battlement

A battlement, in defensive architecture such as that of city walls or castles, comprises a parapet (a defensive low wall between chest-height and head-height) in which gaps or indentations, known as crenels or embrasures, are formed between raised sections called merlons. From behind the merlons defenders could shelter between shots while firing through the crenels. Battlements on walls have protected walkways (wall-walks) accessed from interior staircases.

VariantsRectangular, swallowtail (Ghibelline), stepped, and others

History

Crenellation is ancient: battlements appear in Assyrian reliefs, on Egyptian fortresses and on the towers of Roman and Byzantine walls. In medieval Europe the form became ubiquitous on castles and town walls, and in England a royal "licence to crenellate" was formally required to fortify a manor house. The shape of merlons sometimes signalled allegiance — in Italy, rectangular merlons were associated with the Guelphs and swallow-tailed merlons with the Ghibellines.

Design

The merlon shields the defender; the crenel lets him shoot arrows, bolts or later firearms. Battlements were often combined with other features such as machicolations (floor openings for dropping missiles), arrow slits, wooden hoardings, and overhanging parapets. As gunpowder artillery made high thin walls obsolete, battlements became largely decorative, surviving in Gothic Revival and castellated country-house architecture.
