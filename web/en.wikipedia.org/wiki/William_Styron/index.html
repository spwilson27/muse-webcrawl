William Clark Styron Jr. (June 11, 1925 – November 1, 2006) was an American novelist and essayist. His major works include Lie Down in Darkness (1951), The Confessions of Nat Turner (1967), which won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, and Sophie's Choice (1979). His memoir of depression, Darkness Visible (1990), is considered a landmark account of mental illness.

Early life

Styron was born in Newport News, Virginia, the son of a shipyard engineer. He attended Davidson College and Duke University, served in the Marine Corps near the end of World War II, and after the war studied under William Blackburn at Duke. In Paris in the early 1950s he helped found The Paris Review.

Major works

Lie Down in Darkness, published when Styron was 26, tells of a dysfunctional Virginia family. Set This House on Fire (1960) followed. The Confessions of Nat Turner imagined the inner life of the leader of the 1831 slave rebellion; it won the Pulitzer Prize but drew criticism from some Black writers for its portrayal. Sophie's Choice (1979) interweaves the narrator Stingo's coming of age in Brooklyn with the story of Sophie Zawistowska, a Polish survivor of Auschwitz forced into an impossible choice; it was adapted into an acclaimed 1982 film.

Darkness Visible

In the mid-1980s Styron suffered a severe depressive episode. His account, expanded from an essay into the memoir Darkness Visible: A Memoir of Madness (1990), described the illness with stark honesty and reached a wide audience of sufferers and their families.

Later life and death

Styron lived for decades in Roxbury, Connecticut, and Martha's Vineyard. He died of pneumonia in 2006. His unfinished novel about the Marine Corps, The Way of the Warrior, was published posthumously in excerpted form.
