The Ubaid period (c. 6500–3800 BC) is a prehistoric period of Mesopotamia. The name derives from Tell al-'Ubaid, where the earliest large excavation of Ubaid period material was conducted by Henry Hall, and later by Leonard Woolley in the 1920s.

In the south of Mesopotamia, the period is the earliest known period on the alluvial plain, although it is likely that earlier periods exist obscured under the alluvium. In the south it has a very long duration between about 6500 and 3800 BC, when it is replaced by the Uruk period.

History of research

The term "Ubaid period" was coined at a conference in Baghdad in 1930, where the Jemdet Nasr and Uruk periods were also defined. The first excavations at Tell al-'Ubaid were conducted on behalf of the British Museum and the University of Pennsylvania. Woolley's excavations at Ur, especially of the "Flood Pit", brought Ubaid material to wide attention.

Characteristics

The Ubaid period is divided into phases, often numbered 0 through 5. Ubaid 0, sometimes called the Oueili phase, is attested at Tell el-'Oueili in the far south. Ubaid pottery is distinctive: a buff or greenish ware painted in dark brown or black, with geometric designs, produced on a slow wheel (tournette) and fired in kilns. Over time, pottery became progressively more mass-produced and less finely decorated.

Settlements of the period included large tripartite houses with a central hall flanked by smaller rooms, and great temple buildings on platforms, most famously the sequence of temples at Eridu, which grew progressively larger over successive rebuildings. Southern Ubaid communities practised irrigation agriculture, growing emmer wheat, barley, flax, and dates, and herding cattle, sheep, and goats. Clay sickles, hoe blades, and spindle whorls are common finds.

Expansion

During the later Ubaid phases, Ubaid material culture spread widely across the Near East, reaching northern Mesopotamia (where the Halaf culture was gradually replaced, notably at sites such as Tepe Gawra and Tell Arpachiyah), western Iran, the Arabian Gulf coast, and southeastern Anatolia. This broad "Ubaid interaction sphere" is one of the earliest examples of a shared material culture spanning such a large region, and it laid the groundwork for the greater urbanisation of the Uruk period that followed.

Society

Ubaid society appears to have been relatively egalitarian, though growing differences in house size and the existence of elaborate temple institutions suggest emerging social stratification. Stamp seals, used to mark ownership of goods, and clay tokens, possibly precursors of cuneiform writing, are found at Ubaid sites.
