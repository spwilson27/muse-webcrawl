Jagir

A jagir was a feudal land grant in the Indian subcontinent, under which the revenues of a tract of land were assigned by the ruler to a jagirdar in return for service, typically military or administrative. The system was developed under the Delhi Sultanate and systematized by the Mughal Empire, and forms of it persisted under the Marathas, the Sikhs, and in the princely states under British rule.

The system

Under the Mughal mansabdari system, officers (mansabdars) were usually paid not in cash but with jagirs whose assessed revenue matched their salary. The jagirdar did not own the land; he held only the right to collect its revenue, and assignments were frequently transferred to prevent officials from building local power bases. Peasants continued to cultivate the land under their customary arrangements with zamindars and village bodies.

Types of jagir included tankha jagirs (given in lieu of pay), mashrut jagirs (conditional on service), and watan jagirs (hereditary holdings of local chiefs). By the late Mughal period, a shortage of available jagirs relative to the number of claimants contributed to political instability.

Abolition

After Indian independence, jagirdari and similar intermediary tenures were abolished by state land-reform legislation in the 1950s, as part of zamindari abolition, with compensation paid to the dispossessed holders.
