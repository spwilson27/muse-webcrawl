Chagres River

The Chagres River (Spanish: Río Chagres) is the largest river in the Panama Canal watershed. Rising in the rainforests of central Panama, it flows northwest to the Caribbean Sea. The river was dammed twice during the construction of the canal to form Gatun Lake and Lake Alajuela, which supply the water that operates the canal's locks and much of Panama City's drinking water.

Course

The Chagres originates in the mountains of what is now Chagres National Park and descends through steep, densely forested terrain. Its upper basin receives some of the heaviest rainfall in the Americas, and the river is notorious for violent, rapid flooding — in flood it could rise many metres in hours, a behaviour that alternately frustrated and enabled the canal builders.

History

Spanish explorers reached the Chagres in the early sixteenth century; Vasco Núñez de Balboa's party encountered it in 1513. For centuries the lower river, navigated by bongo and cayuco to the transshipment point at Cruces, formed the middle leg of the Camino de Cruces, the mule-and-river route by which Peruvian silver crossed the isthmus. The pirate Henry Morgan ascended the river in 1671 on his way to sack Panama City. During the California Gold Rush, forty-niners crossed the isthmus partly by boat on the Chagres.

Role in the Panama Canal

The American canal plan harnessed rather than avoided the river: the Gatun Dam, completed in 1913, was then the largest earth dam in the world, and it drowned the lower Chagres valley to create Gatun Lake, through which ships transit much of the canal's length. The Madden Dam (1935) formed Lake Alajuela on the upper river to store additional water and control floods. Because every lockage releases enormous volumes of fresh water to the sea, the Chagres watershed's rainfall and forest cover are critical to the canal's operation, as drought-driven draft restrictions in recent years have demonstrated.
