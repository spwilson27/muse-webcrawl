AlphaGo Zero is a version of DeepMind's Go software AlphaGo that achieved superhuman performance in the game of Go while learning entirely from self-play, without any data from human games. Announced in a paper in Nature in October 2017, AlphaGo Zero surpassed the strength of all previous AlphaGo versions within about 40 days of training, becoming the strongest Go player in history.

Architecture and training

Unlike earlier versions, which were first trained on databases of expert human games, AlphaGo Zero began with a neural network that knew nothing about Go beyond its rules. A single residual neural network with a dual "policy" head (suggesting moves) and "value" head (evaluating positions) was improved by reinforcement learning: the system played games against itself, using Monte Carlo tree search guided by the network to generate improved move choices, which then became training targets.

Training used 4 tensor processing units (TPUs) for self-play generation in the final evaluation configuration — far less hardware than earlier versions. After three days of training and 4.9 million self-play games, AlphaGo Zero defeated the version of AlphaGo that had beaten Lee Sedol by 100 games to 0. After about 21 days it reached the level of AlphaGo Master, which had beaten the world's top players online and defeated Ke Jie in 2017. After 40 days it surpassed all previous versions, achieving an Elo rating above 5,000.

Discoveries

Starting from random play, AlphaGo Zero independently rediscovered standard joseki (corner sequences) known to human players for centuries, and then developed novel variations that overturned conventional wisdom — for example an early 3–3 point invasion under a hoshi stone, subsequently adopted by professional players worldwide.

Significance

DeepMind presented AlphaGo Zero as evidence that algorithms can acquire mastery in a complex domain from first principles using tabula rasa learning. Its generalised successor, AlphaZero, applied the same approach to chess and shogi. Commentators noted that removing the human-data bottleneck made the technique relevant to scientific problems where expert data are scarce.
