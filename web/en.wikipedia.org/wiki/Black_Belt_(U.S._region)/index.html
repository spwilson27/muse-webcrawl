The Black Belt is a region of the Southern United States. The term originally described a crescent-shaped band of dark, fertile prairie soil running through central Alabama and Mississippi, and it later took on a demographic meaning, referring to the large African American population whose ancestors were enslaved on the region's cotton plantations. In the broadest usage, the Black Belt arcs through some 200 counties from Virginia to Texas, sharing plantation history, rural poverty, and majority or plurality Black populations.

History

The chalk-derived soils of the Alabama Black Belt prairie were exceptionally suited to cotton, and after the removal of the Creek and Choctaw peoples in the 1830s, planters moving west with enslaved labourers made the region the heart of the antebellum cotton kingdom; counties such as Dallas and Wilcox had enslaved majorities of 70–80%. Booker T. Washington, who built Tuskegee Institute in the Alabama Black Belt, popularized the demographic sense of the term in Up from Slavery (1901).

Twentieth century and today

The region was a stronghold of Jim Crow and a crucible of the civil rights movement—Montgomery, Selma, and Birmingham lie within or at its edges. Mechanization of agriculture and the Great Migration thinned its population, and today the Black Belt is known for persistent rural poverty, health disparities, and infrastructure problems alongside a rich cultural and historical heritage; Selma's Edmund Pettus Bridge and the voting-rights struggle draw continuing national attention, and the region reliably elects African American representatives, including Alabama's majority-Black congressional district.
