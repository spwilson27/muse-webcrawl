Sheol (Hebrew: שְׁאוֹל, Šəʾōl) is the place of darkness in the Hebrew Bible to which the dead go. In the ancient Israelite conception, Sheol lay beneath the earth, and all the dead — righteous and wicked alike — descended to it, existing there as weak shades (rephaim) cut off from the praise of God and the life of the living.

In the Hebrew Bible

Sheol is mentioned 66 times in the Hebrew Bible. Jacob expects to "go down to Sheol" mourning for Joseph; Korah and his company are swallowed alive into it; and the Psalms speak of God ransoming souls from its power. It is described as a land of darkness, silence and forgetfulness, personified in poetry as an insatiable devourer with a mouth and appetite. Only a few exceptional texts hint at escape from its finality, such as the ascents of Enoch and Elijah, and much later passages (Daniel 12) that introduce resurrection.

Development

During the Second Temple period, under influences including Persian and Hellenistic thought, Jewish ideas about Sheol diversified. Works such as 1 Enoch describe compartments within the realm of the dead where the righteous and wicked await judgement separately, and the Greek Septuagint translated Sheol as Hades. Pharisaic and later rabbinic Judaism developed doctrines of resurrection and the world to come, while in the New Testament Hades functions as the temporary abode of the dead before the final judgement.
