International English

International English is the concept of using the English language as a global means of communication in numerous dialects, and the movement towards an international standard for the language. It is sometimes referred to as Global English, World English, Common English, or General English. The term is especially associated with the use of English as a lingua franca in business, aviation, science, diplomacy, and the internet.

English is a global lingua franca, though regional standards vary.

Historical context

English spread worldwide through the British Empire and, in the 20th century, through the economic and cultural influence of the United States. Today, non-native speakers greatly outnumber native speakers—estimates often cite a ratio of about three or four to one—so most English communication takes place between people for whom it is a second language.

The concept

Scholar Braj Kachru's "Three Circles" model divides English use into the Inner Circle (native-speaker countries such as the UK, US, Australia), the Outer Circle (countries where English has an established institutional role, such as India, Nigeria, Singapore), and the Expanding Circle (countries where English is learned as a foreign language, such as China and Brazil). Advocates of an international standard argue for maximizing clarity and avoiding idiom and region-specific usage, while critics argue that no single standard can or should be imposed on such a pluricentric language.

Controlled varieties

Several simplified or controlled forms have been developed for international use, including Basic English (C. K. Ogden, 1930), Simplified Technical English for aerospace documentation, Seaspeak and the IMO Standard Marine Communication Phrases for shipping, and ICAO phraseology for aviation, where English proficiency is formally required of pilots and controllers.

English as a lingua franca research

Applied linguists such as Jennifer Jenkins and Barbara Seidlhofer have studied English as a lingua franca (ELF) as a phenomenon in its own right, documenting features of successful non-native communication and challenging the assumption that native-speaker norms should be the target of teaching worldwide.
