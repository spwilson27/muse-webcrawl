Two-hit hypothesis

The two-hit hypothesis, also known as the Knudson hypothesis, is a model of carcinogenesis proposed by Alfred G. Knudson in 1971. It states that cancer results when both alleles of a tumour suppressor gene in a single cell are inactivated by mutation — the "two hits". The hypothesis was formulated to explain the difference between the hereditary and sporadic forms of retinoblastoma, a rare cancer of the retina in children.

In hereditary cases one "hit" is inherited in the germline, so only one further somatic mutation is needed.

Background

Knudson performed a statistical analysis of cases of retinoblastoma, comparing the age of onset in children in whom both eyes were affected (bilateral cases, usually hereditary) with those in whom only one eye was affected (unilateral cases, usually sporadic). He found that bilateral cases appeared earlier and followed kinetics consistent with a single additional random event, while unilateral cases followed kinetics consistent with two random events. He inferred that a hereditary case begins with one defective copy of a gene already present in every cell of the body; a tumour arises when a second, somatic mutation disables the remaining functional copy in a retinal cell. In sporadic cases, both mutations must occur somatically in the same cell, which is rarer and happens later.

The RB1 gene

The gene responsible, RB1, was cloned in 1986 and became the first tumour suppressor gene identified. It encodes the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), a key regulator of the cell cycle that restrains entry into S phase. Loss of both functional copies removes this brake and contributes to uncontrolled proliferation. The second hit often occurs through loss of heterozygosity, for example by mitotic recombination, deletion, or chromosome loss.

Generalisation and limits

The two-hit model became a paradigm for tumour suppressor genes generally, including TP53 and APC, and it contrasts with oncogenes, in which a single activating mutation can be sufficient. Later research complicated the picture: multistep carcinogenesis typically requires mutations in several genes, some tumour suppressors show haploinsufficiency (one hit partly compromising function), and epigenetic silencing can serve as a "hit" in place of mutation. Nonetheless, the Knudson hypothesis remains a foundational concept in cancer genetics.
