The lentiform nucleus (or lenticular nucleus) is a lens-shaped mass of gray matter within each cerebral hemisphere of the brain. It forms part of the basal ganglia and consists of two components: the putamen, which lies laterally, and the globus pallidus, which lies medially and is itself divided into external and internal segments. Together with the caudate nucleus, the lentiform nucleus forms the corpus striatum (more specifically, the putamen and caudate together are called the striatum or neostriatum).

Anatomy

In coronal section the lentiform nucleus appears as a wedge or lens shape, bounded laterally by the external capsule and medially by the internal capsule, whose fibers separate it from the thalamus and the head of the caudate nucleus. It lies beneath the insular cortex, from which it is separated by the external capsule, the claustrum, and the extreme capsule.

Function

As part of the basal ganglia circuitry, the lentiform nucleus participates in the regulation of voluntary movement, procedural learning, and habit formation. The putamen is especially involved in motor control, receiving input from the motor and somatosensory cortex and projecting through the globus pallidus and thalamus back to the cortex. The globus pallidus internus is one of the principal output nuclei of the basal ganglia.

Clinical significance

The lenticulostriate arteries, small penetrating branches of the middle cerebral artery that supply the lentiform nucleus, are a common site of lacunar strokes and of hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage. Dysfunction of the basal ganglia circuits involving the lentiform nucleus is implicated in movement disorders such as Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease; copper deposition in the lentiform nucleus in Wilson's disease gives rise to the condition's older name, hepatolenticular degeneration.
