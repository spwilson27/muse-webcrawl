Peak coal

ConceptPeak in coal production/consumption after which output declines
Key countriesChina, India, United States, Australia, Indonesia
Global demand peakOften dated to around 2013–2014 in energy use (IEA estimates)

Global coal production and consumption grew rapidly in the early 21st century before flattening as climate policy and cheap renewables slowed demand.

Peak coal is the point in time when the maximum rate of global coal production, or more commonly coal consumption, is reached, after which production or use enters a terminal decline. The concept adapts M. King Hubbert's peak theory, originally developed for oil, to coal. Analysts debate whether the world has already passed a demand peak: global coal consumption reached record levels in the early 2010s and, after a plateau, rose again to new highs in the early 2020s driven by China and India, though coal use in Europe and North America has fallen steeply since its regional peaks.

History

Coal fuelled the Industrial Revolution and remained the world's largest source of electricity into the 21st century. United States coal production peaked in 2008 at about 1.17 billion short tons and has since roughly halved as natural gas and renewables displaced coal in power generation. The United Kingdom's coal output peaked as early as 1913. China's coal consumption, which accounts for more than half of the world total, appeared to peak around 2013–2014 before stabilising.

Causes of decline

Drivers of peak coal demand include climate-change policy and carbon pricing, air-pollution controls, the falling cost of wind and solar power, competition from natural gas, and financial institutions restricting funding for new coal projects. Countervailing forces include rising electricity demand in developing economies, energy-security concerns, and coal's role in steelmaking (metallurgical coal), for which alternatives are less mature.
