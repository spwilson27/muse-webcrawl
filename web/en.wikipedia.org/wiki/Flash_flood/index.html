Flash flood

A flash flood is a rapid flooding of low-lying areas: washes, rivers, dry lakes and depressions. It may be caused by heavy rain associated with a severe thunderstorm, hurricane, or tropical storm, or by meltwater from ice or snow flowing over ice sheets or snowfields.

Causes

Flash floods may be caused by intense rainfall over a short period, dam or levee failures, ice or debris jams, or the release of water held in natural or man-made reservoirs. They are distinguished from regular floods by having a timescale of fewer than six hours between rainfall and the onset of flooding.

Hazards

Flash floods are the most dangerous kind of flood because they combine destructive power with incredible speed. Water moving at high velocity can tear out trees, destroy buildings and bridges, and scour new channels. The United States National Weather Service gives the advice "Turn Around, Don't Drown" for flash floods, since as little as six inches of moving water can knock a person off their feet and two feet of water can float a vehicle.

Warning systems

Meteorological agencies issue flash flood watches and warnings based on radar estimates, rain gauges, and soil saturation. Areas with steep terrain, urban drainage problems, or recently burned wildfire scars are at particular risk.
