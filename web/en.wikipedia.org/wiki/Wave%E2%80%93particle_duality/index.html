Wave–particle duality

Wave–particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that quantum entities exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. It is a central, if paradoxical, feature of the theory, illustrated by experiments such as the double-slit experiment, in which particles such as electrons produce an interference pattern characteristic of waves.

History

The debate over the nature of light reaches back to Newton, who favoured a corpuscular view, and Huygens, who proposed a wave theory. In the nineteenth century Young's interference experiments and Maxwell's equations seemed to settle the matter in favour of waves. However, Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect showed that light also behaves as quantised packets of energy — photons. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that matter itself has wave properties, predicting a wavelength λ = h/p for particles, a prediction confirmed by electron diffraction experiments within a few years.

Modern view

In contemporary quantum mechanics, duality is understood through the wave function, whose squared amplitude gives probabilities for particle-like detection events. Niels Bohr's principle of complementarity held that wave and particle descriptions are both necessary but mutually exclusive aspects of a single phenomenon. The duality has been demonstrated not only for photons and electrons but also for atoms and large molecules.
