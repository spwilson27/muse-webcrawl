Völsunga saga

The Völsunga saga (Old Norse: Vǫlsunga saga) is a legendary saga — a fornaldarsaga — written in Icelandic prose in the late 13th century, around 1270. It tells the story of the rise and fall of the Völsung clan, and is the most famous of the Icelandic legendary sagas. Much of its material overlaps with heroic poems preserved in the Poetic Edda, and it is the principal prose source for the story of the hero Sigurd (Siegfried).

Contents

The saga traces the family from its divine origin: the god Odin is the ancestor of Völsung, whose children include Sigmund, bearer of the sword Gram, which Odin himself thrust into the tree Barnstokkr. The central section concerns Sigmund's son Sigurd: his upbringing by the smith Regin, his slaying of the dragon Fáfnir and taking of the cursed hoard of Andvari, his awakening of the valkyrie Brynhildr (Sigrdrífa), and his betrayal, his marriage to Gudrun of the Gjukungs, and his murder. The latter chapters follow Gudrun's revenge on her second husband Atli (Attila the Hun) and the deaths of her brothers Gunnar and Högni, and end with the doomed vengeance of Gudrun's daughter Svanhildr.

Sources and influence

The saga's compiler drew on older eddic poems, some now lost, combining them into a continuous prose narrative. Its story corresponds broadly to the German Nibelungenlied, though the Icelandic version preserves more archaic mythic material. The best-known manuscript is Ny kgl. Saml. 1824 b 4to, in the Royal Library, Copenhagen. The saga profoundly influenced Richard Wagner's Ring cycle, William Morris's poem The Story of Sigurd the Volsung, and the work of J. R. R. Tolkien, who wrote his own retelling, The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún.
