Kazakh famine of 1930–1933

Kazakh famine of 1930–1933
Location: Kazakh ASSR, Soviet Union
Deaths: estimated 1.3–1.5 million (up to ~2.3 million in some estimates)
Cause: forced collectivisation and sedentarisation
Also known as: Asharshylyk ("famine" in Kazakh)

The Kazakh famine of 1930–1933, known in Kazakh as Asharshylyk, was a catastrophic famine in the Soviet Kazakh republic caused by the forced collectivisation of agriculture and the compulsory sedentarisation of nomadic and semi-nomadic Kazakh herders under Filipp Goloshchyokin's "Little October" campaign. Between roughly 1.3 and 1.5 million people died — estimates range higher — representing a greater proportional loss than in any other Soviet republic, including Ukraine during the Holodomor.

Causes

From 1928 the Soviet authorities confiscated livestock from Kazakh households and compelled nomads into collective farms. The herds collapsed — Kazakhstan lost the overwhelming majority of its livestock, with figures often cited around 90 per cent — destroying the pastoral economy on which the population depended. Grain requisitioning continued even as starvation spread, and hundreds of thousands fled, many to China, Mongolia and neighbouring republics.

Consequences

Kazakhs fell from about 60 per cent of the republic's population in 1926 to under 40 per cent by 1939, becoming a minority in their own republic — a demographic position reversed only after independence in 1991. The famine was long unmentionable in the USSR; since independence Kazakhstan commemorates the victims on 31 May, and historians continue to debate its classification, with some describing it as genocide.
