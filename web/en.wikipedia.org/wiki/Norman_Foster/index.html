Norman Foster

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: 30 St Mary Axe — 'the Gherkin' — rising over the City of London, its diagrid skin spiralling to the rounded point]

Norman Robert Foster, Baron Foster of Thames Bank (born 1 June 1935) is an English architect and designer, founder of Foster + Partners and the leading figure of British high-tech architecture. His work — glass, steel, and systems thinking at every scale — includes the Willis Faber building in Ipswich (1975), the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank headquarters in Hong Kong (1985), London Stansted Airport (1991), 30 St Mary Axe ('the Gherkin', 2003), the Millennium Bridge and the Great Court of the British Museum (2000), the Reichstag's new dome in Berlin (1999) and Apple Park in California (2017).

He won the Pritzker Prize in 1999 and the Stirling Prize twice; born in Stockport to a working-class family, he studied at Manchester and Yale (where he met Richard Rogers), founded Team 4 with Rogers and their wives in 1963, and built Foster Associates from the high-tech landmark of the Willis building onward.

High-tech and systems

Foster's creed — the building as an optimisable system, structure expressed, services integrated, daylight and air engineered — descended from Buckminster Fuller (whose geodesic thinking he championed) and produced the HSBC headquarters' suspended floors and the Reichstag dome's mirrored cone bringing light and public into parliament: high-tech grown civic and symbolic. His practice, among the world's largest, runs from furniture and the Nomos table to airports (Beijing's Terminal 3, then the world's largest) and masterplans.

Later career

A peer since 1999 (he resigned from active Lords participation in a 2010s tax-residence controversy), knighted and an OM, Foster in his late eighties remains his firm's presiding intelligence, with the Norman Foster Foundation in Madrid promoting his gospel of design, data and sustainability. The Gherkin-era skyline of London — much of it his or his imitators' — is his monument.
