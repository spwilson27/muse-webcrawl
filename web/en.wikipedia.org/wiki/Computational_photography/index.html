Computational photography

Computational photography refers to digital image capture and processing techniques that use digital computation instead of optical processes. Computational photography can improve the capabilities of a camera, or introduce features that were not possible at all with film-based photography, or reduce the cost or size of camera elements. Examples of computational photography include in-camera computation of digital panoramas, high-dynamic-range images, and light field cameras. Light field imaging is prominently exemplified by Lytro.

History

The term was popularised by computer graphics and vision researchers at Stanford and elsewhere in the 2000s; Marc Levoy's course and research programme at Stanford treated the camera as a computational platform, exploring features such as synthetic aperture focusing and high dynamic range from bracketed exposures. Computational photography builds on earlier work in image processing, computer vision, and light-field rendering (Levoy and Hanrahan, 1996).

Techniques

  • High dynamic range (HDR) – merging multiple exposures of a scene into a single image with greater dynamic range than a single capture.
  • Image stitching and panoramas – aligning and blending overlapping frames.
  • Burst photography and multi-frame fusion – combining many quickly captured frames to reduce noise, enable digital zoom, and synthesise shallow depth of field (portrait modes); central to smartphone cameras such as Google's Pixel (HDR+, Night Sight) and Apple's iPhone (Deep Fusion, Photonic Engine).
  • Light-field photography – recording the direction of light rays so focus and viewpoint can be altered after capture.
  • Coded aperture and flutter shutter – modifying optics/capture so that deblurring or depth estimation becomes well-posed in computation.
  • Focus stacking, super-resolution, and removal of unwanted elements via inpainting and, increasingly, machine learning models.

Influence

Because smartphone cameras are constrained by small sensors and lenses, computational techniques have become the main axis of progress in consumer photography since the 2010s. Neural-network-based processing, including learned demosaicing, denoising, and semantic segmentation for portrait effects, has blurred the boundary between photography and computer graphics, raising ongoing debates about authenticity in photojournalism and evidence.
