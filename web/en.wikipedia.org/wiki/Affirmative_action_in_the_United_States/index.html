Affirmative action in the United States

In the United States, affirmative action consists of policies, programmes and practices in employment, education, and contracting that take race, sex, national origin, or other protected characteristics into account in an effort to promote access to opportunities for historically underrepresented or disadvantaged groups. The term first entered federal policy in President John F. Kennedy's Executive Order 10925 (1961), requiring federal contractors to "take affirmative action" to ensure applicants were treated without regard to race. President Lyndon B. Johnson's Executive Order 11246 (1965) institutionalised enforcement, and the "Philadelphia Plan" of 1969 added goals and timetables for minority employment in construction.

In higher education

Colleges and universities adopted race-conscious admissions to increase diversity and remediate past discrimination. The Supreme Court first upheld the practice in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978), which banned strict quotas but allowed race as one "plus factor", and reaffirmed the diversity rationale in Grutter v. Bollinger (2003), while striking a mechanistic points system in Gratz v. Bollinger. Fisher v. University of Texas (2013, 2016) further tested and narrowly sustained holistic review. In June 2023, in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard and its companion UNC case, the Court held that the race-conscious admissions programmes violated the Equal Protection Clause, effectively ending affirmative action in college admissions nationwide.

Employment and contracting

Federal contractors remain subject to nondiscrimination rules and, historically, to OFCCP affirmative action programme requirements for women and minorities; set-asides and disparity studies have been litigated under strict scrutiny since City of Richmond v. Croson (1989) and Adarand Constructors v. Peña (1995). Several states — beginning with California's Proposition 209 (1996), followed by Washington, Michigan, and others — banned preferential treatment by public institutions; 2025 federal executive action further revoked the Johnson-era contractor framework.

Debate

Supporters argue affirmative action compensates for structural inequality and yields educational benefits of diversity; critics argue it can constitute reverse discrimination, mismatch beneficiaries with institutions, or stigmatise those it helps. Public opinion has generally been more favourable to outreach and class-based measures than to explicit racial preferences, and universities have responded to the 2023 ruling with expanded socioeconomic and geographic recruitment strategies.
