A Kodak Brownie-style box camera.

A box camera is a simple type of camera, the most common form being a rectangular box containing a fixed-focus lens, a shutter and a mechanism for holding and advancing roll film. Box cameras were the dominant amateur cameras from the late 1880s until the mid-20th century, and are generally classed as "snapshot" cameras for casual photography.

Design

The typical box camera has a fixed shutter speed (around 1/40–1/50 of a second), one or two small apertures selected by a sliding plate, a simple meniscus lens behind the shutter, and one or two brilliant viewfinders for composing in portrait and landscape orientation. There is no focusing control: the lens is set at its hyperfocal distance so everything beyond a few feet is acceptably sharp. Bodies were made of wood or cardboard covered in leatherette, later of pressed steel, aluminium and Bakelite.

History

George Eastman's original Kodak of 1888 — sold loaded with film under the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest" — established the type, but the design's mass era began with the Kodak Brownie of 1900, which sold for one dollar and put photography in the hands of children and working-class families. Competitors such as Agfa (whose "Box" cameras were ubiquitous in Germany) and Ansco produced millions of similar models. Models such as the Kodak Brownie Six-20 and the Agfa Synchro Box remained in production into the 1960s, when cartridge-loading Instamatic cameras displaced them.

Box cameras' limitations — slow lenses, fuzzy corners and fixed exposure — nonetheless gave them a distinctive soft aesthetic that remains popular with toy-camera and Lomography enthusiasts.
