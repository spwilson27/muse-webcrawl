Scientific management
FounderFrederick Winslow Taylor
Key textThe Principles of Scientific Management (1911)
Also known asTaylorism
Key methodsTime study, standardisation, piece-rate pay

Scientific management is a theory of management that analyses and synthesises workflows to improve economic efficiency, especially labour productivity. It was developed chiefly by the American engineer Frederick Winslow Taylor and set out in his book The Principles of Scientific Management (1911); for this reason it is often called Taylorism.

Principles

Taylor proposed four principles: (1) replace rule-of-thumb working methods with methods based on a scientific study of the tasks; (2) scientifically select, train, and develop each worker rather than leaving workers to train themselves; (3) cooperate with workers to ensure the scientifically developed methods are followed; and (4) divide work nearly equally between managers and workers, so that managers apply scientific management principles to planning while workers execute the tasks. Practical tools included time-and-motion study (refined by Frank and Lillian Gilbreth), standard tools and procedures, and differential piece-rate pay rewarding workers who met the standard output.

Spread and criticism

Scientific management spread rapidly in American and European industry in the 1910s and 1920s and influenced figures from Henry Ford to Lenin, who promoted its study in the Soviet Union. Critics — including trade unions and later human-relations theorists — attacked it for deskilling craft work, treating workers as machine parts, and provoking resistance and strikes; these criticisms helped spur the human relations movement in management thought.

Legacy

Although Taylorism as a label fell out of favour, its descendants include industrial engineering, operations management, work study, and modern process-improvement and algorithmic management practices.
