Transit-oriented development

Transit-oriented development (TOD) is a type of urban development that maximises the amount of residential, business and leisure space within walking distance of public transport. It promotes a symbiotic relationship between dense, compact urban form and high-quality transit service: a TOD neighbourhood typically has a centre with a train station, tram stop or bus station, surrounded by relatively high-density development, with progressively lower-density development spreading outward within roughly a half-mile (800 m) radius — a comfortable walking distance.


Mixed-use development centred on a transit station

Principles

The concept was articulated by architect and urban designer Peter Calthorpe in his 1993 book The Next American Metropolis. Core principles include mixed land uses, moderate-to-high density near stations, pedestrian-friendly street networks, reduced and managed parking, and public spaces oriented to the station. Supporters argue TOD reduces car dependence, traffic congestion, air pollution and household transport costs, and increases transit ridership and land values. Critics raise concerns about displacement and gentrification of existing residents near stations, leading many programmes to include affordable-housing requirements.

Examples

Noted examples include the "rail plus property" model of Hong Kong's MTR, Curitiba's bus rapid transit corridors in Brazil, the Finger Plan development of Copenhagen along suburban rail lines, and station-area redevelopment in cities such as Arlington County, Virginia, and Portland, Oregon. Analysts sometimes distinguish true TOD from "transit-adjacent development", which is built near transit but retains car-oriented design.
