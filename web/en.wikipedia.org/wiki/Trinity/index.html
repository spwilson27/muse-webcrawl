Trinity

The Trinity is the central doctrine of most Christian theology, holding that God is one being in three coeternal, consubstantial persons: God the Father, God the Son (Jesus Christ), and God the Holy Spirit.

Doctrine

The doctrine was articulated in the creeds of the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) in response to controversies such as Arianism. The Athanasian Creed summarises that the persons are distinct but not separate gods — one God in Trinity, and Trinity in Unity.

Biblical basis and debate

Support is drawn from the baptismal formula in Matthew 28:19 and other passages, though the word Trinity does not appear in scripture. Nontrinitarian traditions, including Unitarianism and Jehovah's Witnesses, reject the doctrine.

In other religions

Analogous triads exist elsewhere, but the Christian Trinity is distinct. See Nicene Creed and God in Christianity.
