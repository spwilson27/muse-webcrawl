The Church of Greece (Greek: Ἐκκλησία τῆς Ἑλλάδος, Ekklisía tis Elládos) is one of the autocephalous churches of the Eastern Orthodox communion. It comprises most of the territory of the modern Greek state south of the regions annexed after 1912 (whose dioceses remain under the jurisdiction of the Ecumenical Patriarchate while being administered by the Church of Greece). Its primate is the Archbishop of Athens and All Greece, since 2008 Ieronymos II. Eastern Orthodoxy is recognised in the Greek constitution as the "prevailing religion" of Greece.

History

Christianity in Greece dates to the missions of Paul the Apostle. During the Byzantine and Ottoman periods the Greek church was governed by the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople. Following Greek independence, the regency government of King Otto unilaterally declared the church in the kingdom autocephalous in 1833, a step regularised when Patriarch Anthimus IV granted a tomos of autocephaly in 1850, with conditions preserving canonical ties.

Organisation

The Church is governed by the Holy Synod of Bishops, presided over by the Archbishop of Athens. It comprises some 80 dioceses (metropolises) within Greece, along with monasteries including the monastic community of Mount Athos — which, though geographically in Greece, falls under the Ecumenical Patriarchate rather than the Church of Greece. Clergy salaries are paid by the Greek state, a recurrent subject of church–state debate.

Role in Greek society

The Church of Greece is closely identified with Greek national identity, having preserved Greek language and culture under Ottoman rule and supported the independence struggle. It remains influential in public life, while its welfare institutions operate extensive charitable networks, notably expanded during the Greek debt crisis.
