Collective agreement

A collective agreement, or collective bargaining agreement (CBA), is a written contract negotiated through collective bargaining between an employer (or employers' association) and a trade union representing employees. It regulates the terms and conditions of employment — wages, hours, benefits, leave, grievance procedures and workplace rules — for the workers in the bargaining unit, typically for a fixed term of one to five years.

Legal status

The legal force of collective agreements varies by jurisdiction. In the United States, CBAs are enforceable contracts under the National Labor Relations Act, and most contain a no-strike clause and binding grievance arbitration during their term. In much of continental Europe, agreements are negotiated at industry or sector level and may be extended by law to all employers in a sector, giving them very wide coverage; in the United Kingdom, collective agreements are generally presumed not to be legally binding between the parties unless stated in writing, though their terms are incorporated into individual employment contracts.

Content

Besides pay and hours, agreements commonly cover seniority and layoff rules, just-cause discipline standards, health and pension benefits, union security arrangements, and procedures for resolving disputes. In professional sports, league-wide CBAs between owners and players' associations govern salary caps, free agency and revenue sharing, and their expiry has repeatedly led to strikes and lockouts.
