Data center

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a long aisle of server racks in a hyperscale data centre with blue lighting]

A data center (American English) or data centre is a building, a dedicated space within a building, or a group of buildings used to house computer systems and associated components, such as telecommunications and storage systems. Data centres are the factories of the Internet: the physical plants where cloud computing, websites and AI models actually run.

Their design revolves around power and cooling: uninterruptible power supplies and diesel generators against outages, chillers and increasingly liquid cooling against the heat of computation, biometric security, fire suppression and redundant network connections — including to Internet exchanges, which is why data centres cluster in a few 'carrier hotel' districts and fibre crossroads. Ratings (the Uptime Institute's Tiers I–IV) grade their redundancy.

From the 2010s, 'hyperscale' campuses built by Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta grew to the size of industrial estates, consuming power on the scale of cities and driving debates about energy, water and land use; the AI boom of the 2020s intensified both the build-out and the scrutiny. Meanwhile the colocation business lets thousands of smaller firms rent cages in the same buildings — the unglamorous real estate on which the cloud floats.
