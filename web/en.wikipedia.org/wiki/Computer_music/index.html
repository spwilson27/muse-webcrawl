Computer music

Computer music is the application of computing technology in music composition, to help human composers create new music or to have computers independently create music, such as with algorithmic composition programs. It includes the theory and application of new and existing computer software technologies and basic aspects of music, such as sound synthesis, digital signal processing, sound design, sonic diffusion, acoustics, electrical engineering, and psychoacoustics. The field of computer music can trace its roots back to the origins of electronic music and the very first experiments with computer-generated sound in the 1950s.

History

The first computer to play music was Australia's CSIR Mk 1 (later CSIRAC) in 1951, programmed by Geoff Hill to play popular melodies. In 1957 Max Mathews at Bell Labs developed the MUSIC I program, beginning the influential MUSIC-N family of sound-synthesis languages; Mathews is often called the father of computer music. Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson's Illiac Suite (1957) was an early landmark of algorithmic composition, and Iannis Xenakis applied stochastic mathematics to composition from the early 1960s. Stanford's CCRMA and Paris's IRCAM (founded by Pierre Boulez, opened 1977) became leading research centres. Key technologies followed: FM synthesis (John Chowning, commercialised in the Yamaha DX7, 1983), MIDI (1983), sampling, granular synthesis, and physical modelling.

Practice today

Computer music now spans digital audio workstations (DAWs), live coding and laptop performance, interactive installations, game and film audio, and machine-learning-based composition and synthesis. Academic study continues through the International Computer Music Association (founded 1974) and its annual conference, and through journals such as Computer Music Journal (founded 1977). Software environments widely used in the field include Max/MSP, Pure Data, SuperCollider, and Csound.

Techniques

Core techniques include additive, subtractive, FM, and wavetable synthesis; sampling and granular processing; algorithmic and generative composition (Markov chains, rule systems, cellular automata, machine learning); spatialisation; and computer-assisted composition environments that help composers model harmony, rhythm, and form.
