Laryngitis

Laryngitis is inflammation of the larynx (voice box). Symptoms often include a hoarse voice and may include fever, cough, pain in the front of the neck, and trouble swallowing. Typically, these last under two weeks.

The larynx, whose inflammation causes laryngitis.

Laryngitis is categorised as acute if it lasts less than three weeks and chronic if symptoms last more than three weeks. Acute cases usually occur as part of a viral upper respiratory tract infection. Other infections and trauma such as from coughing are other causes. Chronic cases may occur due to smoking, tuberculosis, allergies, acid reflux, rheumatoid arthritis, or sarcoidosis.

Signs and symptoms

The primary symptom of laryngitis is a hoarse voice. Because laryngitis is often caused by an infection, other symptoms such as fever, cough, a sore throat, and a feeling of dryness or rawness in the throat may also be present. Changes in the voice can vary from a slight huskiness to a near-total loss of voice. In chronic laryngitis, symptoms persist for more than three weeks.

Causes

Acute

  • Viral: The majority of acute cases are caused by viruses such as rhinovirus, influenza virus, parainfluenza virus, and adenovirus — the same viruses responsible for the common cold.
  • Bacterial: Less commonly, bacteria such as Moraxella catarrhalis, Haemophilus influenzae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae may be responsible.
  • Traumatic: Overuse or misuse of the voice, such as shouting, prolonged singing, or excessive coughing, can inflame the vocal cords.

Chronic

  • Smoking and exposure to irritants such as dust and chemicals
  • Laryngopharyngeal reflux (acid reflux reaching the larynx)
  • Chronic sinusitis and postnasal drip
  • Excessive alcohol use
  • Allergies
  • Rarely, infections such as tuberculosis or fungal infections in immunocompromised people

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically based on the symptoms and history. If symptoms persist for more than three weeks, examination of the vocal cords with a laryngoscope is recommended to rule out more serious causes such as laryngeal cancer. Visual findings may include redness and swelling of the vocal cords and, in some cases, small nodules or polyps.

Treatment

Most cases of acute laryngitis improve without specific treatment. Resting the voice, drinking adequate fluids, and humidifying the air may help. Whispering should be avoided because it can strain the vocal cords more than speaking softly. If a bacterial cause is suspected, antibiotics may be prescribed. For chronic laryngitis, treatment focuses on the underlying cause — for example, stopping smoking, treating reflux, or voice therapy with a speech-language pathologist.

Prognosis

Acute laryngitis usually resolves within one to two weeks. Chronic laryngitis can persist for longer and requires identification and management of the contributing factors. Persistent hoarseness lasting more than three weeks warrants further investigation.
