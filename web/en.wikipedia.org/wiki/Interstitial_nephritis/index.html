Interstitial nephritis


Interstitial nephritis involves inflammation of the spaces between kidney tubules.

Interstitial nephritis, also called tubulointerstitial nephritis, is inflammation of the area of the kidney known as the renal interstitium, which consists of a collection of cells, extracellular matrix and fluid surrounding the renal tubules. It may be acute, developing over days to weeks and causing acute kidney injury, or chronic, leading gradually to scarring and chronic kidney disease.

Causes

The most common cause of acute interstitial nephritis is an allergic-type reaction to medications, including antibiotics such as beta-lactams, proton pump inhibitors, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and diuretics. Infections, autoimmune diseases such as Sjögren syndrome and sarcoidosis, and the syndrome of tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU) are other recognised causes.

Signs and symptoms

Patients may present with a rising creatinine, reduced urine output, fever, rash and eosinophilia — the classic triad appears in only a minority of cases — along with sterile pyuria, white cell casts and eosinophiluria. Kidney biopsy, showing an interstitial inflammatory infiltrate with oedema and tubulitis, is often needed for a definitive diagnosis.

Treatment

The cornerstone of management is stopping the offending drug or treating the underlying cause. Corticosteroids are frequently used for drug-induced disease, particularly when started early, although evidence for their benefit is largely observational. Severe cases may temporarily require dialysis.
