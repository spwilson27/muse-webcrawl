Gestalt therapy

Gestalt therapy is a form of psychotherapy that emphasises personal responsibility and focuses on the individual's experience in the present moment, the therapist–client relationship, and the environmental and social contexts of a person's life. It was developed by Fritz Perls, his wife Laura Perls, and Paul Goodman in the 1940s and 1950s, and set out in the 1951 book Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality.

Theory

Drawing on Gestalt psychology, phenomenology, existentialism, and field theory, Gestalt therapy treats the person as a whole formed in relation to an environment (the "organism/environment field"). Key ideas include awareness of the here and now, the figure–ground formation of needs, contact and its disturbances (such as introjection, projection, and retroflection), and "unfinished business" — unexpressed feelings that linger in the background. A famous, often-misread expression of its ethos is the "Gestalt prayer" written by Perls in 1969.

Methods

Techniques are experiential and experimental rather than interpretive. The best known is the "empty chair" technique, in which a client speaks to an imagined other (or to a disowned part of the self) seated opposite, then may switch chairs to reply. Others include exaggeration of gestures, dream work in which every element of the dream is treated as a projection of the dreamer, and guided awareness exercises. At the Esalen Institute in the 1960s Perls's confrontational demonstration style made Gestalt therapy emblematic of the human potential movement, though later practice is generally more dialogic and relational.
