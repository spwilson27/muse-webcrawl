The Tōkaidō (Japanese: 東海道, "Eastern Sea Road") was the most important of the Five Routes (Gokaidō) of the Edo period in Japan, connecting the shogunal capital Edo (modern Tokyo) with the imperial capital Kyoto along the Pacific coast of Honshu. It ran roughly 500 km and was served by 53 post stations (shukuba) where travellers could rest, eat and change horses.

History

The name derives from the older Tōkaidō circuit of provinces under the ritsuryō system. Under Tokugawa Ieyasu the road was formalised after 1601: post stations were established, milestones (ichirizuka) planted, and pine avenues planted along stretches of the route. The sankin-kōtai system, obliging daimyo to travel regularly to Edo, made the Tōkaidō the busiest road in Japan, used by processions, merchants, pilgrims and officials. Its western end in Kyoto was the Sanjō Ōhashi bridge; its eastern end, Nihonbashi in Edo.

Culture

The road inspired celebrated art and literature: Utagawa Hiroshige's print series The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1832–33), Jippensha Ikku's comic novel Tōkaidōchū Hizakurige ("Shank's Mare"), and Bashō's travel writing. In the modern era the corridor remains Japan's main transport artery: it is followed by National Route 1, the Tōkaidō Main Line and the Tōkaidō Shinkansen (opened 1964), and the name survives in the Tōkai region.
