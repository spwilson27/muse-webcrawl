Temporomandibular joint

The temporomandibular joints (TMJ) are the two joints connecting the jawbone to the skull. They are bilateral synovial joints between the mandibular condyle, the articular tubercle of the temporal bone in the mandibular fossa, and the articular disc interposed between them. The TMJ is one of the most complex and most frequently used joints in the human body.

Structure

Each TMJ is formed by the condyle of the mandible below and the mandibular fossa and articular eminence of the temporal bone above. A biconcave fibrocartilaginous articular disc divides the joint cavity into two compartments. The joint capsule and the lateral (temporomandibular) ligament stabilize the joint, with the sphenomandibular and stylomandibular ligaments as accessory ligaments.

Movements

The joint permits both hinge (rotation) movements and gliding (translation) movements, allowing depression and elevation of the mandible (opening and closing the mouth), protrusion and retrusion, and side-to-side lateral deviation used in grinding food. Rotation occurs mainly in the lower joint compartment and translation in the upper, a combination that distinguishes the TMJ from a simple hinge.

Clinical significance

Temporomandibular joint dysfunction (often abbreviated TMD or TMJD) encompasses pain and dysfunction of the jaw joint and the muscles of mastication, with symptoms including jaw pain, clicking or locking, headaches and limited opening. Causes are multifactorial and may include disc displacement, osteoarthritis, bruxism and stress-related muscle tension. Most cases are managed conservatively with self-care, occlusal splints, physiotherapy and medication; surgery is reserved for a minority of cases.
