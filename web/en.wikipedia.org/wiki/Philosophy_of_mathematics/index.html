The philosophy of mathematics is the branch of philosophy that investigates the nature, methods and epistemological status of mathematics. Its central questions include: Do numbers, sets and functions exist independently of the human mind? What makes mathematical statements true? Why is mathematics so effective in describing the physical world? And how can mathematical knowledge be certain when it is not empirical?

Major schools

  • Platonism (realism) holds that mathematical objects are abstract, non-spatiotemporal entities that exist independently of us and that mathematical statements describe them truly or falsely. Kurt Gödel defended a version of this view.
  • Logicism, developed by Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell, aimed to reduce all of mathematics to logic, showing arithmetical truths to be analytic.
  • Formalism, associated with David Hilbert, treats mathematics as the manipulation of symbols according to stated rules; Hilbert's programme sought a finitary consistency proof for mathematics, which Gödel's incompleteness theorems showed to be unattainable in its original form.
  • Intuitionism, founded by L. E. J. Brouwer, regards mathematics as a mental construction and rejects proofs that rely on the law of excluded middle for infinite domains.
  • Structuralism focuses on mathematical structures and the positions within them, rather than on individual objects.
  • Nominalism and fictionalism deny the existence of abstract objects, treating mathematics as a useful fiction or as talk about concrete inscriptions.

Key results

Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) — that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic contains true but unprovable statements, and cannot prove its own consistency — are the most consequential philosophical results of twentieth-century mathematics, reshaping debates between the schools. Other enduring topics include the applicability of mathematics (Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness"), the status of computer-assisted and non-surveyable proofs, and Benacerraf's dilemma about how we could have knowledge of abstract objects.
