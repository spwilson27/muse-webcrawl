Contact geometry

Contact geometry is the study of a geometric structure on smooth manifolds given by a hyperplane distribution in the tangent bundle that is "completely non-integrable". Equivalently, it is given by a 1-form whose kernel defines the distribution and which satisfies a maximal non-integrability condition. Contact geometry is the odd-dimensional counterpart of symplectic geometry, and it arises naturally in the study of differential equations, dynamical systems, and thermodynamics.

Contact structures

A contact structure on a (2n+1)-dimensional manifold is a hyperplane field ξ that can locally be written as the kernel of a 1-form α satisfying α ∧ (dα)n ≠ 0. The standard example on ℝ3 is given by α = dz − y dx. By the Darboux theorem (the contact analogue), all contact structures are locally equivalent, so the subject concerns global properties. A submanifold whose tangent spaces lie in ξ and which has maximal dimension n is called Legendrian.

History and applications

The subject's roots lie in the work of Sophus Lie on contact transformations in the 1870s and in geometrical optics and thermodynamics, where the first law can be expressed as a contact form. Modern contact topology developed rapidly from the 1980s, with results on tight and overtwisted contact structures by Eliashberg and others, and applications to Hamiltonian dynamics via the Weinstein conjecture (proved in dimension three by Taubes using Seiberg–Witten theory).
