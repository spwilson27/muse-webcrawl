Taxonomic rank

[Image: The principal ranks of biological classification]

In biological classification, taxonomic rank is the relative level of a group of organisms (a taxon) in an ancestral or hereditary hierarchy. The system of ranks descends from the work of Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century and remains the framework of the international nomenclature codes.

The species is conventionally treated as the fundamental rank, and the binomial name (genus + specific epithet) uniquely identifies it. Zoology additionally uses the ranks of tribe and cohort; botany recognises variety and form below species. Botanical and zoological family names are formed with standardised endings (e.g. -aceae for plant families, -idae for animal families).

Subdivisions and criticism

Intermediate ranks are created with prefixes such as super-, sub- and infra- (superfamily, suborder, infraclass), producing dozens of possible levels. Because ranks are not equivalent in age or diversity across groups — a beetle family may be far older than a mammal order — many systematists criticise ranked classification and advocate rank-free phylogenetic nomenclature, as in the PhyloCode, in which taxa are defined by ancestry alone. In practice, ranks remain widely used for communication, legislation and databases.
