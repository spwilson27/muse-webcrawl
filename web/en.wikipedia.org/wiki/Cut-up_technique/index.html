Cut-up technique

The cut-up technique (or découpé in French) is an aleatory literary technique in which a written text is physically cut up and rearranged to create a new text. The concept can be traced to the Dadaists of the 1920s — Tristan Tzara described making a poem by drawing cut-out words from a hat — but it was developed and popularised in the late 1950s and early 1960s by the painter and writer Brion Gysin and the novelist William S. Burroughs.

Development

Gysin, who had used the method in painting, accidentally rediscovered it in 1959 at the Beat Hotel in Paris while cutting through a stack of newspapers with a Stanley knife. He showed it to Burroughs, who applied it extensively in novels such as The Soft Machine, The Ticket That Exploded and Nova Express. Their joint book The Third Mind (1978) is a manifesto for the method. Burroughs argued that language is a mechanism of control — a "virus" — and that cutting text breaks habitual associations and reveals hidden meanings: "When you cut into the present the future leaks out."

Related forms and influence

A variant, the fold-in, folds a page of one text over another so the two are read together. The technique influenced experimental writing, and also music: David Bowie adopted cut-ups for lyric writing in the 1970s, and the approach is echoed in sampling and in the work of bands such as Genesis P-Orridge's Throbbing Gristle. It is related to collage, the "exquisite corpse" game of the Surrealists, and modern remix practices; software generators now perform cut-ups digitally.
