Meninges

The meninges (singular: meninx) are the three membranes that envelop the brain and spinal cord, collectively protecting the central nervous system. In mammals, the meninges are the dura mater, the arachnoid mater, and the pia mater. Cerebrospinal fluid is located in the subarachnoid space between the arachnoid mater and the pia mater. The primary function of the meninges is to protect the central nervous system.

Structure

Dura mater

The dura mater is a thick, dense, fibrous membrane forming the outermost layer. In the skull it has two layers: an outer periosteal layer attached to the inner surface of the cranium and an inner meningeal layer. It forms infoldings such as the falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli that compartmentalize the cranial cavity.

Arachnoid mater

The arachnoid mater is the middle layer, a delicate web-like membrane beneath which lies the subarachnoid space containing cerebrospinal fluid, blood vessels, and the arachnoid trabeculae. Arachnoid granulations project into the dural venous sinuses and allow cerebrospinal fluid to drain into the venous system.

Pia mater

The pia mater is the innermost layer, a thin membrane that adheres closely to the surface of the brain and spinal cord, following all their contours. Together the arachnoid and pia are called the leptomeninges.

Spaces

The epidural space lies between the dura and the surrounding bone (in the spine) or is a potential space in the cranium. The subdural space is a potential space between dura and arachnoid. The subarachnoid space is a real space filled with cerebrospinal fluid.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the meninges is called meningitis, most often caused by bacterial or viral infection. Bleeding into the meningeal spaces produces epidural or subdural hematomas. Tumors arising from the meninges are called meningiomas, most of which are benign.
