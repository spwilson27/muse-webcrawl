Roller chain

A roller chain is a type of chain drive most commonly used to transmit mechanical power on many kinds of domestic, industrial, and agricultural machinery, including conveyors, printing presses, automobiles, motorcycles, and bicycles. It consists of a series of short cylindrical rollers held together by side links, and is driven by a toothed wheel called a sprocket.

A roller chain meshing with a sprocket

Construction

A roller chain link is assembled from inner and outer plates joined by pins. A bushing is fixed between the inner plates, and a free-spinning roller surrounds each bushing. As the chain engages a sprocket, the rollers roll into the tooth spaces, reducing friction and wear relative to simpler chain designs. Chains are joined end-to-end with a connecting (master) link. Two common variants are the standard roller chain and the heavier engineering steel chain; double, triple, and wider multi-strand versions transmit higher power.

Sizes

Chain size is designated by standards such as ANSI and ISO (British Standard) series, expressed as multiples of an eighth of an inch of pitch — for example, a #40 chain has a pitch of 4/8 inch (12.7 mm). Bicycle and motorcycle chains follow their own common sizes, such as the 1/2 × 1/8 inch bicycle chain and the 520–530 motorcycle series.

History

Although chain drives are ancient — sketches appear in the notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci — the modern roller chain was patented in 1880 by Hans Renold, whose design underlies most roller chains in use today. Its adoption transformed bicycle design (the safety bicycle) and early automobiles and motorcycles.

Maintenance

Roller chains require periodic lubrication and tension adjustment. Wear manifests as elongation ("chain stretch") caused by pin and bushing wear rather than actual stretching of the metal; worn chains accelerate sprocket wear and are replaced as a unit with the sprockets in critical applications.
