Martin Van Buren (December 5, 1782 – July 24, 1862) was the eighth president of the United States, serving from 1837 to 1841. A founder of the Democratic Party, he had previously served as the eighth vice president (1833–1837) under Andrew Jackson, and prior to that as Secretary of State, minister to the United Kingdom, governor of New York, and a United States senator. He was the first president born a United States citizen (after independence) and the only president whose native language was not English — he grew up speaking Dutch in Kinderhook, New York.

Early life and career

Van Buren was born in Kinderhook to a Dutch-American family; his father kept a tavern. Apprenticed to a lawyer, he was admitted to the bar in 1803 and built a successful practice. Nicknamed the "Little Magician" and "Old Kinderhook" (a source of the expression "OK") for his political skill and slight stature, he became the dominant figure of New York's "Albany Regency" machine, served in the state senate, and entered the U.S. Senate in 1821. He helped assemble the Jacksonian coalition that became the Democratic Party, briefly served as governor of New York in 1829, and then joined Jackson's cabinet as Secretary of State, becoming Jackson's closest adviser and chosen successor.

Presidency (1837–1841)

Van Buren won the 1836 election but took office just as the Panic of 1837 triggered a severe depression. Committed to laissez-faire principles, he refused direct federal relief, instead pursuing the Independent Treasury system (established 1840) to separate federal funds from banks. His presidency also saw the forced removal of the Cherokee on the Trail of Tears under the Indian Removal policy he inherited and carried out, and tensions with Britain over the Caroline affair and the Maine–New Brunswick boundary (Aroostook War). He declined to annex Texas, fearing war with Mexico and sectional conflict over slavery. Blamed for the depression, he lost the 1840 election to Whig William Henry Harrison's "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too" campaign.

Later life

Van Buren sought the Democratic nomination again in 1844 but lost to James K. Polk after opposing immediate Texas annexation. In 1848 he ran for president as the candidate of the anti-slavery Free Soil Party, winning no electoral votes but drawing votes that helped elect Whig Zachary Taylor. He retired to his estate, Lindenwald, in Kinderhook, where he died in 1862.

Legacy

Historians credit Van Buren as a principal architect of the modern American party system and of the Democratic Party, while criticising his enforcement of Indian removal and his equivocal record on slavery. Lindenwald is now the Martin Van Buren National Historic Site.
