Inflammation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inflammation is the response of body tissues to harmful stimuli — pathogens, damaged cells or irritants — in which the immune system, blood vessels and signalling molecules act to remove the cause, clear dead tissue and begin repair. Its classic signs, described since Roman times, are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. Acute inflammation resolves in days; chronic inflammation persists and contributes to many long-term diseases.

Mechanism

Injured tissues and resident immune cells release mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins and cytokines. Local blood vessels dilate and become leaky, letting plasma proteins and white blood cells — first neutrophils, then macrophages — enter the tissue. The process walls off microbes, destroys them by phagocytosis, and sets up healing, including new blood vessel growth and scar formation.

Chronic inflammation and treatment

When the stimulus persists or control fails, inflammation becomes chronic, as in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, and low-grade chronic inflammation is implicated in atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes and some cancers. Treatments include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs such as Ibuprofen), corticosteroids, and targeted biologic drugs against cytokines such as TNF.
