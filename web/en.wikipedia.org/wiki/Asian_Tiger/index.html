Asian Tiger

The tiger economies are known for rapid industrialisation and high growth rates.

Asian Tiger most commonly refers to the Four Asian Tigers — the highly developed, high-growth economies of Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea and Taiwan. Between the early 1960s and the 1990s these economies sustained growth rates above 7 per cent per year through rapid industrialisation, export-oriented manufacturing and heavy investment in education and infrastructure, transforming themselves from low-income economies into global financial and technological centres.

The Four Tigers

  • Hong Kong — a free port and financial hub whose manufacturing sector later shifted largely to mainland China.
  • Singapore — a city-state that leveraged its strategic port location, multinational investment and state-led planning.
  • South Korea — home to giant conglomerates (chaebol) such as Samsung and Hyundai, and now a leader in electronics and shipbuilding.
  • Taiwan — a dominant force in semiconductors, anchored by companies such as TSMC.

Tiger Cub Economies

The term Tiger Cub Economies is applied to the following generation of fast-industrialising Southeast Asian economies — Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam — which sought to emulate the export-led model of the original four.

1997 Asian financial crisis

The tigers were shaken by the 1997 Asian financial crisis, which began with the collapse of the Thai baht and spread through the region. South Korea required an IMF rescue package, but the tiger economies restructured and largely recovered, emerging with stronger financial regulation and large foreign-exchange reserves.

Other uses

The term is also a literal zoological reference: the tiger (Panthera tigris) is native to Asia, and subspecies such as the Bengal, Siberian and South China tiger are sometimes informally called Asian tigers. In entomology, the "Asian tiger mosquito" is a common name for Aedes albopictus.
