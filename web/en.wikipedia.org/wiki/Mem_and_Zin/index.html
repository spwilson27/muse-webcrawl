Mem and Zin (Kurdish: Mem û Zîn) is a Kurdish classic love story written down by the poet Ahmad Khani in 1692. It is considered the national epic of Kurdish literature and the foundational work of Kurdish written literature in the Kurmanji dialect.

Story

The tale, based on an older oral legend, is set in the principality of Botan in Cizre. Mem, a young nobleman, and Zin, the sister of the prince (Mir) of Botan, fall in love at the Newroz festival, where they exchange rings disguised in each other's dress. Their union is opposed and obstructed by the prince's steward Bakr (Beko), whose intrigues have Mem imprisoned. Mem dies in prison; Zin, coming to his grave, dies of grief upon it. The betrayer Bakr is killed, and a thorn bush growing between the lovers' graves — nourished by Bakr's blood — symbolises the enmity that keeps them apart even in death.

Themes

Beneath the tragic romance, Khani's poem is read as an allegory of the Kurdish condition: the lovers' separation mirrors the division of Kurdistan, and Khani's famous preface laments the Kurds' disunity and subjugation to foreign rulers, calling for a Kurdish king and state. The poem also reflects Sufi themes of divine love and earthly passion.

Legacy

Mem û Zîn has been translated into many languages and adapted for the screen, notably in the 1991 film Mem û Zîn. The supposed tombs of Mem and Zin in Cizre are a place of pilgrimage. Ahmad Khani's own tomb there is also a revered site.
