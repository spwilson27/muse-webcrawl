Nuclear explosion

A nuclear explosion is an explosion that occurs as a result of the rapid release of energy from a high-speed nuclear reaction, either nuclear fission, nuclear fusion, or a combination of the two. Nuclear explosions release energy orders of magnitude greater than chemical explosions of comparable mass, and are characterised by intense light and heat, a powerful blast wave, ionising radiation and radioactive fallout.

Effects

The effects of a nuclear explosion depend on its yield, the height of burst and the surroundings. Roughly half the energy typically appears as blast and shock, about a third as thermal radiation, and the remainder as ionising radiation, both prompt and delayed in the form of fallout. An air burst maximises blast effects over a wide area, while a surface burst produces intense local fallout. Very large explosions develop the familiar mushroom cloud as the hot fireball rises and cools. Electromagnetic pulse effects can disrupt electrical and electronic systems.

History

The first nuclear explosion was the Trinity test conducted by the United States on 16 July 1945 in New Mexico, with a yield of about 20 kilotons. Nuclear weapons were used in warfare against Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. Thermonuclear (hydrogen) weapons, using fusion reactions staged from a fission primary, were first demonstrated in 1952. More than 2,000 nuclear test explosions were carried out worldwide before the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty of 1996 sought to end all such testing. Nuclear explosions have also been studied for proposed peaceful uses, such as excavation, under programmes like Operation Plowshare, though these were abandoned because of radioactive contamination.
