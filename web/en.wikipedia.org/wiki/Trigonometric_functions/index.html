In mathematics, the trigonometric functions (also called circular functions, angle functions, or goniometric functions) are real functions which relate an angle of a right-angled triangle to ratios of two side lengths. The most widely used are the sine, cosine, and tangent, together with their reciprocals the cosecant, secant, and cotangent. They are widely used in all sciences related to geometry and periodic phenomena, including navigation, solid mechanics, celestial mechanics, and signal processing.

The sine, cosine, and tangent functions

Definitions

In a right triangle with acute angle θ, sin θ is the ratio of the opposite side to the hypotenuse, cos θ the adjacent side to the hypotenuse, and tan θ the opposite to the adjacent. The unit circle definition generalises these to all real arguments: for a point at angle θ on a circle of radius 1 centred at the origin, cos θ and sin θ are its x- and y-coordinates. The functions are periodic — sine and cosine with period 2π — and satisfy the Pythagorean identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, as well as addition formulas such as sin(α + β) = sin α cos β + cos α sin β.

History

Trigonometry arose in Hellenistic astronomy: Hipparchus compiled chord tables in the 2nd century BC, and Ptolemy's Almagest preserved them. Indian mathematicians developed the sine function (jyā), transmitted through Arabic scholarship — where al-Khwārizmī and al-Battānī built systematic tables — to medieval Europe. The modern analytic treatment, defining the functions by power series and extending them to complex arguments, culminated in Euler's work, including Euler's formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ.

Applications

Trigonometric functions model periodic phenomena such as sound and light waves, alternating current, and seasonal cycles. Fourier analysis decomposes arbitrary periodic signals into sums of sines and cosines, and the functions are indispensable in surveying, navigation, geodesy, and computer graphics.
