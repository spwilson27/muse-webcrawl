Scientific law

A scientific law is a statement based on repeated experimental observations that describes some aspect of the universe. A law always applies under the same conditions and implies a causal relationship; examples include Newton's law of universal gravitation, the laws of thermodynamics, and Mendel's laws of inheritance.

Characteristics

Laws are typically expressed concisely, often mathematically, and summarize a large body of data rather than explaining why a phenomenon occurs — explanation is the role of scientific theory. Laws are accepted as true within their domain of validity but are not "proven" in the deductive sense; they can be refined or superseded, as Newtonian mechanics was generalized by relativity.

Laws versus theories

Contrary to popular usage, theories do not "become" laws with enough evidence; the two serve different functions in science. Philosophers of science debate whether laws describe genuine regularities in nature or are idealizations that hold only in models, and exceptions (e.g., "laws" of biology) complicate the picture.

Examples

Famous laws include Kepler's laws of planetary motion, Ohm's law, Boyle's law, the law of conservation of energy, and the Hubble–Lemaître law.
