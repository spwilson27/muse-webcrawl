Molecular vibration

Molecular vibration
FieldSpectroscopy, physical chemistry
TypesStretching, bending, wagging, rocking, twisting
Studied byInfrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy

A molecular vibration is a periodic motion of the atoms of a molecule relative to each other, such that the center of mass of the molecule does not move. Typical frequencies of molecular vibrations range from less than 10^12 to approximately 10^14 Hz. In general, a non-linear molecule with N atoms has 3N − 6 normal modes of vibration, while a linear molecule has 3N − 5, because rotation about the molecular axis does not displace atoms.

Normal modes

The normal modes of vibration include stretching, in which bond lengths change, and bending, in which bond angles change; bending motions are further classified as scissoring, rocking, wagging, and twisting. Each mode has a characteristic frequency determined by the masses of the atoms and the strength of the bonds.

The vibrations of carbon dioxide illustrate symmetry effects: its symmetric stretch is invisible in the infrared spectrum but Raman-active, while its asymmetric stretch and bending modes absorb infrared radiation — a property central to its role as a greenhouse gas.

Spectroscopy

Molecular vibrations are observed directly by infrared spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy, which together provide fingerprints used to identify compounds and determine molecular structure. Vibrational energy levels are quantised and, in the harmonic approximation, evenly spaced; real (anharmonic) oscillators permit overtone transitions.

Vibrational analysis also underlies calculations of thermodynamic properties such as heat capacity and entropy from molecular structure.
