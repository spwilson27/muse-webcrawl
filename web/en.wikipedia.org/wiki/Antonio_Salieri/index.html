Antonio Salieri (18 August 1750 – 7 May 1825) was an Italian classical composer, conductor and teacher. Born in Legnago, in the Republic of Venice, he was taken to Vienna as an orphaned teenager by Florian Gassmann and spent most of his life at the Habsburg court, becoming Imperial Kapellmeister in 1788 — a post he held for 36 years. He was a pivotal figure in the development of late-18th-century opera, composing more than forty works for stages in Vienna, Paris and Italy.

Career

Salieri's early opera Armida (1771) established him in Vienna. With Giuseppe Bonno's death and his own appointment, he became the dominant composer of Italian opera at court. His Europa riconosciuta inaugurated La Scala in Milan in 1778; in Paris, under Gluck's patronage, his Les Danaïdes (1784) and Tarare (1787, libretto by Beaumarchais) were major successes. Later operas include Axur, re d'Ormus and the comedy Falstaff (1799). As Kapellmeister he also composed and supervised sacred music and taught a generation of musicians: Beethoven, Schubert, Liszt (briefly), Hummel and Mozart's son Franz Xaver were among his pupils.

Relationship with Mozart

Salieri and Mozart were professional rivals for court favour, though they also collaborated — a joint cantata in 1785 — and Salieri conducted Mozart's works. After Mozart's death in 1791, rumours arose that Salieri had poisoned him; no evidence supports them, and historians regard them as baseless. The legend nonetheless inspired Pushkin's play Mozart and Salieri (1830), Rimsky-Korsakov's opera of the same name, and Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus (1979) and its celebrated 1984 film, which revived public interest in Salieri's music.

Legacy

Salieri spent his last years in declining health and died in Vienna in 1825. His operas fell from the repertory during the 19th century but have been increasingly revived and recorded since the late 20th century.
