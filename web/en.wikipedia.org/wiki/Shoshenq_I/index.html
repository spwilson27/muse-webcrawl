Shoshenq I (Egyptian: Ššnq), also known by his throne name Hedjkheperre Setepenre, was the founder of the Twenty-second Dynasty of ancient Egypt, reigning about 943–922 BCE. Of Libyan Meshwesh descent, he reunified Egypt after the fragmentation of the late New Kingdom and is generally identified with the biblical Pharaoh Shishak, who campaigned in Judah and Israel and plundered Jerusalem.

Rise to power

Shoshenq's family had risen through the military: his ancestors were "Great Chiefs of the Meshwesh" settled at Bubastis in the Delta, and his uncle Osorkon the Elder had briefly reigned as a king of the Twenty-first Dynasty. Shoshenq himself served as commander-in-chief under Psusennes II, whose daughter he married, and on that king's death he ascended the throne, consolidating control by appointing his sons to key posts — notably installing Iuput as High Priest of Amun at Thebes, uniting the religious south with the royal north.

Campaign in the Levant

Around 925 BCE Shoshenq invaded the kingdoms of Israel and Judah. The Hebrew Bible (1 Kings 14) records that Shishak took the treasures of the Temple and royal palace in Jerusalem in the fifth year of Rehoboam. Shoshenq's own record — a triumphal relief and list of conquered towns on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak — names over a hundred places in Israel, Judah and the Negev, and a fragment of a victory stele bearing his name was found at Megiddo. The campaign asserted Egyptian influence in the Levant for the first time in two centuries, though Egypt did not permanently occupy the region.

Building and death

Shoshenq undertook building works at Karnak (the Bubastite Portal and a forecourt), Memphis, Heliopolis and Bubastis. He died about 922 BCE and was succeeded by his son Osorkon I. His original tomb is unknown; a silver falcon-headed coffin inscribed for a Shoshenq was found in the royal necropolis at Tanis, associated with a later namesake king.

Identification with Shishak

The identification of Shoshenq I with the biblical Shishak, first proposed by Champollion after decipherment of the Karnak list, is accepted by most scholars and anchors the conventional chronology of the early Iron Age Levant; it has been debated in discussions of revised ("New Chronology") schemes, but the Karnak topographical list remains a key synchronism between Egyptian and biblical history.
