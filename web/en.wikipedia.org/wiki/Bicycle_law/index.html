Bicycle law

Bicycle law is the parts of law, in various legal systems, that relate to bicycling and the use of bicycles. Because cycling law usually forms part of traffic or vehicle codes, it governs the rights and duties of cyclists on public roads, bicycle equipment requirements, and liability when collisions occur.

History

Late nineteenth-century cyclists, organized in groups such as the League of American Wheelmen, campaigned for legal recognition of bicycles as vehicles entitled to use public roads, and for paved "good roads." Courts in the 1880s and 1890s generally held that cyclists had the same rights and duties as the drivers of carriages.

Common rules

In most jurisdictions cyclists must ride with traffic, obey traffic signals, signal turns, and use lights at night. Many legal systems allow or require riding in cycle lanes where provided; sidewalk riding is variably permitted. Helmet laws differ widely: some countries mandate helmets for all riders, others only for children, and others impose no requirement. Traffic safety aspects are treated in the article on bicycle safety.

International variation

The Netherlands and Denmark regulate cycling within comprehensive traffic codes supported by extensive infrastructure law. In the United States, bicycle law is primarily state law; nearly every state defines the bicycle as a vehicle (or grants its rider vehicle rights) and requires riding as far to the right as practicable, with exceptions. Some jurisdictions impose strict (presumed) liability on motorists in collisions with cyclists.

Liability and enforcement

Crashes raise questions under general tort law, including negligence and comparative fault. Dooring statutes in several states make it an offense to open a vehicle door into a cyclist's path. Advocates debate enforcement priorities, mandatory sidepath laws, and the "Idaho stop," which permits cyclists to treat stop signs as yield signs and is now law in several states.
