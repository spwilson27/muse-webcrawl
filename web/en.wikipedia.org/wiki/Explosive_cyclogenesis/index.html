Explosive cyclogenesis

Satellite view of a "bomb cyclone" off the US East Coast.

Explosive cyclogenesis (also referred to as a bomb cyclone, meteorological bomb, explosive development, or bombogenesis) is the rapid deepening of an extratropical cyclonic low-pressure area. The change in pressure needed to classify something as explosive cyclogenesis is latitude dependent. For example, at 60° latitude, explosive cyclogenesis occurs if the central pressure decreases by 24 millibars (hPa) or more in 24 hours — a rate defined by Sanders and Gyakum (1980), scaled by the sine of latitude relative to 60°.

Explosive cyclogenesis occurs mostly over water, predominantly in winter, and happens in both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. Well-known settings include the western Atlantic off the North American east coast (feeding nor'easters), the North Pacific, and the waters east of Japan.

Formation

Explosive deepening typically occurs where a strong upper-level trough and jet stream interact with a sharp low-level temperature gradient, such as along a warm ocean current like the Gulf Stream or Kuroshio. Intense latent heat release from condensation in the ascending warm air, together with strong baroclinic forcing, accelerates the pressure falls. The resulting storms can produce hurricane-force winds, heavy precipitation, and coastal flooding, even though they are cold-core extratropical systems rather than tropical cyclones.

Terminology in media

The term "bomb cyclone" gained wide popular use during the January 2018 North American blizzard, when media coverage of the storm's rapid intensification brought the meteorological term to a general audience.
