Clauser at Berkeley in the 1970s.

John Francis Clauser (born 1 December 1942) is an American theoretical and experimental physicist known for contributions to the foundations of quantum mechanics, in particular the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) inequality (1969) and the first experimental test of a Bell inequality.

As a graduate student at Columbia, Clauser encountered Bell's theorem and — against the era's indifference — set out to test it. With Stuart Freedman at Berkeley he performed the first Bell test in 1972: measurements on entangled photons from a calcium cascade violated the CHSH bound and agreed with quantum mechanics. Their result began the experimental programme continued by Alain Aspect and Anton Zeilinger, with whom Clauser shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, fifty years after the experiment.

Career

Clauser worked at Berkeley, Livermore and in industry (including aviation research and his own company) rather than in a conventional academic ladder — a path that delayed his recognition. The 2010 Wolf Prize (with Aspect and Zeilinger) and the 2022 Nobel closed the gap. In later years Clauser has been a public sceptic of some climate science conclusions, a stance criticised by many colleagues and noted in his biographies.
