Ant colony optimization algorithms

Ant colony optimization (ACO) is a class of metaheuristic algorithms for solving combinatorial optimization problems, inspired by the foraging behaviour of real ant colonies. First proposed by Marco Dorigo in the early 1990s, ACO algorithms use a population of simple agents — "artificial ants" — that construct solutions step by step, communicating indirectly through a shared memory of artificial pheromone values.

Biological inspiration

Many ant species deposit pheromone on the ground as they travel. When foraging, ants initially wander at random; when an ant finds food it returns to the nest laying a pheromone trail. Other ants tend to follow stronger trails, reinforcing them, while pheromone on unused paths evaporates. This positive feedback, called stigmergy, lets the colony converge on short paths between nest and food, as dramatically shown in the double-bridge experiment.

Algorithm

In ACO, the problem is modelled as a graph. Each ant builds a complete solution by repeatedly choosing the next component with a probability influenced by (a) the pheromone value on the candidate edge and (b) a heuristic desirability (such as the inverse of distance). After all ants have built solutions, pheromone evaporates by a fixed fraction and is then deposited in proportion to solution quality:

τ(i,j) ← (1 − ρ) · τ(i,j) + Σ Δτ(i,j)

where ρ is the evaporation rate and Δτ is larger for edges used in better solutions. The balance between pheromone and heuristic influence is controlled by parameters α and β.

Variants

The original Ant System (1991) was demonstrated on the travelling salesman problem. Later variants include Ant Colony System, which adds a local pheromone update and pseudo-random proportional choice; the MAX–MIN Ant System, which bounds pheromone values and lets only the best ant deposit; and rank-based systems in which ants deposit in proportion to their ranking.

Applications

ACO has been applied to vehicle routing, job-shop scheduling, network routing (notably the AntNet protocol), protein folding, sequential ordering, and assignment problems. It belongs to the broader family of swarm intelligence methods, alongside particle swarm optimization and artificial bee colony algorithms.
