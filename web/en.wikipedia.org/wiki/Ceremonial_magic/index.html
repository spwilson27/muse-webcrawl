Ceremonial magic

Ritual implements and a magic circle

Ceremonial magic (also called ritual magic, and in the early modern period sometimes "high magic") broadly encompasses a wide variety of long, elaborate, and complex rituals of magic with numerous adjunct paraphernalia. It is named as such because the works included are characterized by ceremony similar to semi-religious rites and a set of supplementary objects used to support the practitioner's will.

History

Its roots lie in the grimoire tradition of medieval and Renaissance Europe — works such as the Key of Solomon and the Lemegeton (Lesser Key of Solomon) — drawing on Jewish and Christian mysticism, Kabbalah, astrology, and alchemy. Renaissance scholars such as Marsilio Ficino, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa systematized learned magic in works like Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1533).

Modern revival

In the 19th century, Éliphas Lévi revived interest in ritual magic, and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (founded 1888) organized ceremonial magic into a graded system of study incorporating tarot, astrology, geomancy, and Enochian magic derived from the work of John Dee. Golden Dawn members included W. B. Yeats and Aleister Crowley, whose system of Thelema, and the practices of orders such as the A∴A∴ and Ordo Templi Orientis, carried ceremonial magic into the 20th century. Techniques commonly associated with it include banishing rituals such as the Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram, invocation and evocation of spirits, scrying, and consecration of ritual tools.
