Mirepoix (cuisine)

Mirepoix (French pronunciation: [miʁ.pwa]) is a mixture of diced vegetables cooked in fat (usually butter or oil), used as an aromatic flavour base for many dishes in French cuisine, including soups, stocks, stews, braises, and sauces. The classic combination consists of onion, carrot, and celery, typically in the proportion 2:1:1 by weight.

Name and history

The mixture is traditionally said to be named after Charles-Pierre-Gaston François de Lévis, duc de Lévis-Mirepoix (1699–1757), a French aristocrat and Marshal of France, whose cook is credited with the preparation. The term entered standard culinary use in the 19th century and was codified by chefs such as Auguste Escoffier.

Preparation

Mirepoix vegetables are diced to a size appropriate to the cooking time of the dish, then cooked slowly in fat without colouring ("sweated") for most preparations, or browned — a variant called mirepoix au gras may include ham or bacon, and a browned version (brunoise variations aside) is used for brown stocks and sauces. The vegetables are often strained out of the finished preparation, serving only to season it.

International equivalents

  • Soffritto (Italy): onion, carrot, celery, sometimes with garlic, parsley, or pancetta.
  • Sofrito (Spain and Latin America): typically onion, garlic, tomato, and peppers.
  • Suppengrün (Germany): leek, carrot, and celeriac.
  • Włoszczyzna (Poland): leek, carrot, parsley root, and celery root.
  • Holy trinity (Cajun and Creole cooking): onion, celery, and green bell pepper.
  • Refogado (Portugal): onion, garlic, and tomato.
