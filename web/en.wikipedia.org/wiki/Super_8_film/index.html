Super 8 film

Super 8 is a motion picture film format released by Eastman Kodak in 1965 as an improvement on the older Double 8 (Standard 8) format. The film is 8 millimetres wide but uses smaller perforations, allowing a roughly 50 per cent larger image area, and is supplied in a convenient drop-in cartridge holding 50 feet of film — about three minutes and twenty seconds at 18 frames per second. Super 8 became the dominant home-movie format of the 1960s and 1970s and remains in use by experimental filmmakers and music-video directors for its distinctive grain.

Introduced: 1965 (Kodak)
Gauge: 8 mm
Cartridge: 50 ft (15 m)

History

Kodak launched Super 8 in April 1965 with Kodachrome II film and a range of cameras and projectors; a magnetic sound stripe option followed, and sound cartridges arrived in 1973. Fujifilm's competing Single-8 system used the same gauge in a different cartridge. The format was displaced for home use by video in the 1980s, but Kodak continued manufacturing Super 8 film stocks, including Ektachrome colour reversal and Tri-X black-and-white, and introduced a new digital-assisted Super 8 camera concept in 2016. The 2011 feature film Super 8, directed by J. J. Abrams, is a tribute to the format's amateur-filmmaking era.

Use in professional production

Despite its amateur origins, Super 8 has been used in television, documentaries and feature-film sequences for its texture, in productions ranging from JFK to music videos and fashion films, and is supported by festivals such as Straight 8, where films are shot in-camera-edited on a single cartridge.
