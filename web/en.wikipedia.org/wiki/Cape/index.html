Cape

In geography, a cape is a headland of large size extending into a body of water, usually the sea. A cape usually represents a marked change in trend of the coastline, which makes it prone to natural forms of erosion, mainly tidal actions. This results in the cape being relatively short-lived in geological terms. Capes can be formed by glaciers, volcanoes, and changes in sea level.

Formation

Erosion plays a large role in cape formation. When a section of coastline is made of differing rock types, the softer rock erodes faster, leaving the more resistant rock projecting into the water. Tidal currents and longshore drift also deposit sediment, building up depositional capes of sand, such as Cape Cod in Massachusetts, which is largely a glacial and sedimentary feature.

Notable capes

Famous capes include the Cape of Good Hope in South Africa, Cape Horn at the southern tip of South America, Cape Leeuwin in Australia, Cape Agulhas (the southernmost point of Africa), and Land's End in Cornwall, England. Capes were critical landmarks in the age of sail, and many carry lighthouses to warn shipping of hazardous waters.

Influence on settlement

Capes often became sites of ports, naval stations and lookouts because of their commanding views over sea routes, and some, such as Cape Town, grew into major cities.
