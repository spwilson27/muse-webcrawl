Significance level

The significance level α is the area of the rejection region under the null hypothesis.

The significance level of a statistical hypothesis test, denoted by the Greek letter α (alpha), is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true — that is, the probability of a Type I error. It is chosen by the researcher before the test is performed. A result is declared statistically significant when its p-value is less than or equal to α.

Conventions

The most common choice is α = 0.05, a convention popularised by R. A. Fisher, who suggested that a result deviating by about two standard errors (roughly the 5% level) was worth noting. More stringent levels, such as 0.01 or 0.001, are used where false positives are costly; particle physics demands a "5-sigma" standard corresponding to α ≈ 3 × 10−7 before announcing a discovery. The complement 1 − α is the confidence level of a corresponding confidence interval.

Relationship to the p-value

The p-value is the probability, under the null hypothesis, of observing data at least as extreme as those obtained. If p ≤ α, the null hypothesis is rejected. Because the p-value is random, setting α = 0.05 guarantees that about 5% of true null hypotheses will be wrongly rejected in the long run. Testing many hypotheses without correction inflates the family-wise error rate, a problem addressed by procedures such as the Bonferroni correction.

Criticism

Misuse of significance thresholds has been widely criticised: results just below and just above 0.05 differ negligibly yet are treated as opposites, and "p-hacking" exploits flexible analyses to cross the line. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement on p-values, and in 2019 a commentary in Nature signed by over 800 scientists called for retiring the concept of statistical significance as a bright-line rule in favour of reporting effect sizes and uncertainty.
