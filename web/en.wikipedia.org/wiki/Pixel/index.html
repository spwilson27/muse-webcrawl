Pixel

In digital imaging, a pixel (abbreviation of picture element) is the smallest addressable element of a raster image, or the smallest point in an all-points-addressable display device. Each pixel is a sample of an original image; collectively, the pixels of a display or image form a grid whose resolution — for example 1920 × 1080 — determines how much detail can be represented.

Basics

A pixel stores a colour value, commonly encoded as separate red, green and blue (RGB) components of 8 bits each, giving about 16.7 million possible colours. On most LCD and OLED screens each pixel is composed of coloured subpixels whose relative brightness produces the perceived colour. In image sensors such as those in digital cameras, each photosite typically senses only one colour through a Bayer filter mosaic, and the full-colour image is reconstructed by demosaicing.

Resolution and density

The number of pixels in an image or display is its resolution; camera sensors are rated in megapixels (millions of pixels). Pixel density, measured in pixels per inch (ppi), governs sharpness at a given viewing distance: high-density "retina"-class displays exceed the eye's ability to distinguish individual pixels at normal distances. Pixel aspect ratio, square on most modern displays, was non-square in some older video standards.

Related concepts

The term is extended in several fields: in computer graphics a texel is a pixel of a texture, a voxel is a volumetric pixel in 3-D space, and displays may render vector content by rasterising it into pixels. Pixel art — imagery drawn at the level of individual pixels — is both an early-graphics constraint and a deliberate aesthetic.

Etymology

"Pixel" first appeared in print in 1965, used by Frederic C. Billingsley of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory for elements of scanned space-probe images; it combines pix, an abbreviation of "pictures", with el for "element".
