ʻAʻā

ʻAʻā
TypeLava flow morphology
CompositionBasaltic
Origin of nameHawaiian language

ʻAʻā is one of three basic types of lava flow surface morphology, alongside pāhoehoe and block lava. The term comes from the Hawaiian language, reputedly from the exclamation of pain made when walking barefoot across its jagged surface.

Characteristics

ʻAʻā flows have a rough, clinkery surface of broken lava blocks called clinker, produced when viscous lava loses gas and its crust is torn apart by continued movement beneath. A typical flow has a dense central core sandwiched between fragmental top and bottom layers. ʻAʻā flows advance as the front collapses and tumbles forward, burying rubble beneath the flow.

Formation

ʻAʻā forms when lava of relatively high viscosity or high effusion rate cools and degasses rapidly, commonly as flows steepen downslope; pāhoehoe flows frequently transform into ʻAʻā as they travel away from the vent, though the reverse never occurs.
