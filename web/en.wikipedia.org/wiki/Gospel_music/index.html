Gospel music is a traditional genre of Christian worship music created by and for African-American religious communities, though it has also been performed by artists of many backgrounds. Characterized by dominant vocals — often by a choir — with strong use of harmony, call and response, and lyrics rooted in Christian faith, gospel grew out of spirituals, hymns and blues in the Black churches of the United States.

History

The genre coalesced in the 1920s and 1930s, when Chicago composer Thomas A. Dorsey — the "father of gospel music" — blended blues and jazz idioms with sacred lyrics in songs such as "Take My Hand, Precious Lord". Mahalia Jackson carried gospel to concert halls and international audiences, while Sister Rosetta Tharpe's guitar-driven performances fed directly into rock and roll. Quartets, choirs such as the Edwin Hawkins Singers ("Oh Happy Day", 1969), and later artists like Andraé Crouch and Kirk Franklin fused gospel with soul, R&B and hip hop.

Influence

Gospel's vocal techniques, harmonies and emotional delivery profoundly shaped soul, Motown, rock and contemporary R&B; singers from Aretha Franklin and Sam Cooke to Whitney Houston began in church choirs. Southern gospel, a related mostly white tradition built around four-part male quartets, developed in parallel.
