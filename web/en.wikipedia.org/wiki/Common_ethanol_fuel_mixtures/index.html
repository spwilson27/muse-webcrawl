Common ethanol fuel mixtures

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ethanol fuel blends are commonly sold worldwide with designations indicating the percentage of ethanol by volume. The "E" number describes the ethanol share of the mixture: E10 is 10% ethanol and 90% gasoline, while E85 — sold for flex-fuel vehicles — contains between 51% and 83% ethanol depending on season and region.

Properties and issues

Ethanol has a higher octane rating than gasoline but a lower energy density — roughly two-thirds that of gasoline per unit volume — so high-ethanol blends reduce fuel economy. Ethanol is hygroscopic and can cause phase separation in stored fuel, and high blends can damage elastomers and metals in engines not designed for them, which is why E15 and E85 are restricted to approved or flex-fuel vehicles. Low-level blends such as E10 are generally compatible with the modern vehicle fleet and are used to meet renewable-fuel mandates and reduce carbon monoxide emissions.

Regional adoption

Brazil, which began its Proálcool programme in 1975, mandates anhydrous ethanol blending (around E27 in recent years) and sells hydrous E100 at pumps nationwide. The United States Renewable Fuel Standard drove near-universal adoption of E10. The European Union's EN 228 standard permits E5 and E10 grades.
