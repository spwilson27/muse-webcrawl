Aurelian Walls

The Aurelian Walls (Italian: Mura Aureliane) are a line of city walls built around Rome between AD 271 and 275, during the reign of the emperor Aurelian. Enclosing some 13.7 square kilometres in a circuit of about 19 kilometres (12 miles), they replaced the much earlier Servian Wall and remained Rome's defensive perimeter into the modern era; large stretches survive today, making them among the best-preserved ancient city walls in Europe.

Construction

By the third century Rome had long outgrown the Servian Wall and stood undefended while Germanic tribes threatened Italy. Aurelian ordered a rapid circuit in brick-faced concrete, roughly 8 metres high at first, incorporating existing structures — aqueducts, the Praetorian camp, private houses and even the Pyramid of Cestius — to save time and money. Under Maxentius and then Honorius in the early fifth century the walls were heightened to about 16 metres and strengthened, with a covered gallery added along much of the circuit.

History

The walls defended Rome through the sieges of the Gothic Wars in the sixth century, when Belisarius held the city against the Goths, and remained the city's military boundary for over a millennium. They were finally breached by modern artillery at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, the event that completed the unification of Italy and made Rome its capital.

The walls today

Most of the circuit survives, maintained by the city of Rome; several gates — among them Porta San Sebastiano, Porta San Paolo and Porta Maggiore — house museums or mark the starts of ancient roads such as the Appian Way. Long sections can be walked beside, particularly in the southern part of the city.
