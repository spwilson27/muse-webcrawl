Orthogonal polynomials

The first few Legendre polynomials, an orthogonal family on [−1, 1].

In mathematics, an orthogonal polynomial sequence is a family of polynomials such that any two different polynomials in the sequence are orthogonal to each other under some inner product. The most widely used orthogonal polynomials are the classical orthogonal polynomials, consisting of the Hermite polynomials, the Laguerre polynomials, and the Jacobi polynomials (together with their special cases the Legendre, Chebyshev, and Gegenbauer polynomials). The field of orthogonal polynomials developed from the theory of continued fractions in the late 19th century.

Definition

Given a positive measure μ on the real line and a weight function w(x), the orthogonal polynomials pn of degree n satisfy an orthogonality relation of the form ∫ pn(x) pm(x) w(x) dx = 0 for n ≠ m. Every such sequence obeys a three-term recurrence relation, and conversely Favard's theorem states that any sequence satisfying such a recurrence is orthogonal with respect to some measure.

Classical families

  • Legendre polynomials Pn(x) — weight 1 on [−1, 1]; they arise in solving Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates.
  • Chebyshev polynomials Tn(x) — weight (1 − x2)−1/2 on [−1, 1]; central to approximation theory and minimax approximation.
  • Hermite polynomials — weight e−x2 on the whole real line; eigenfunctions of the quantum harmonic oscillator.
  • Laguerre polynomials — weight e−x on [0, ∞); they appear in the hydrogen atom's radial wave functions.
  • Jacobi polynomials — a two-parameter family generalising Legendre, Chebyshev, and Gegenbauer polynomials.

Properties and applications

The classical families satisfy second-order differential equations (the Sturm–Liouville form exemplified by Rodrigues' formula), possess generating functions, and have real, simple zeros that interlace — a property exploited in Gaussian quadrature, where the zeros of the n-th orthogonal polynomial serve as optimal nodes for numerical integration. Orthogonal polynomials are used throughout mathematical physics, approximation theory, probability (for example in the theory of random matrices), and numerical analysis; their asymptotic theory involves the Riemann–Hilbert method developed in the 1990s.
