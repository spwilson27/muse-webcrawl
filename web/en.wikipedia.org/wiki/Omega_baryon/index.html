Omega baryon

The omega baryons are a family of subatomic hadron particles represented by the symbol Ω and characterized by containing no up or down quarks. They are baryons — composite particles made of three quarks — with strangeness, charm or bottom content. The best-known member is the Ω−, composed of three strange quarks (sss).

The Omega-minus particle

The Ω− was first postulated by Murray Gell-Mann in 1962 as part of the Eightfold Way classification of hadrons. Its existence, mass and decay properties were predicted in advance, and its discovery at Brookhaven National Laboratory in 1964 — in a bubble chamber photograph by a team led by Nicholas Samios — was a landmark confirmation of the quark model. The Ω− has a mass of about 1672 MeV/c², electric charge −1, spin 3/2, and a mean lifetime of about 0.82 × 10−10 seconds, decaying mainly into a lambda baryon and a kaon, or into a Xi baryon and a pion.

Other omega baryons

Heavy-flavour members of the family include the charmed omega Ω0c (ssc), first observed in the 1980s; the bottom omega Ω−b (ssb), discovered in 2008 by the DØ and CDF experiments at Fermilab; and excited states such as the Ω(2012) observed by the Belle experiment. Doubly charmed and charmed-bottom omega states are also predicted by the quark model.
