Single-board computer

A single-board computer (SBC) is a complete computer built on a single circuit board, with microprocessor(s), memory, input/output and other features required of a functional computer. Unlike a desktop personal computer, single-board computers often do not rely on expansion slots for peripheral functions; instead, everything — processor, RAM, storage interfaces, networking and GPIO pins — is soldered or socketed on the one board. They are widely used in embedded systems, education, prototyping and hobbyist computing.

History

Early single-board computers of the 1970s, such as the MOS Technology KIM-1 (1976) and the boards used in the Dyna-micro and early industrial systems, were aimed at developers and engineers. SBCs became standard in industrial control, where rugged boards with long product lifecycles run machinery, kiosks and vehicles. The category was transformed for consumers by the Raspberry Pi (2012), a low-cost, credit-card-sized board running Linux, designed to promote computer science education; tens of millions were sold, and it spawned a large ecosystem of similar boards.

Characteristics and uses

Modern SBCs are usually built around an ARM or RISC-V system-on-chip, boot from microSD or eMMC storage, draw only a few watts, and expose general-purpose input/output (GPIO) pins for sensors, motors and displays. Typical uses include media centres, retro-game consoles, home automation hubs, network-attached storage, robotics, and thin clients, as well as commercial products in signage and vending. Higher-end boards add features such as PCIe, SATA, multiple Ethernet ports and neural-network accelerators.

Comparison with microcontrollers

An SBC runs a full operating system and can multitask, whereas a microcontroller board (such as an Arduino) runs a single firmware program with tight real-time control but far less computing power. Many projects combine both, using an SBC for networking and interface tasks and a microcontroller for precise timing.
