Religious violence

Religious violence is a term that covers phenomena in which religion is either the subject or the object of violent behaviour. All the major world religions contain narratives, symbols, and metaphors of violence and war, and religious violence is often bound up with political, ethnic and economic conflict. Scholars debate how far religion itself, as distinct from other social identities, is a cause of violence.

History

Examples commonly cited include the Crusades, the European wars of religion of the 16th and 17th centuries, sectarian conflict in Northern Ireland, communal violence in South Asia (such as the Partition of India in 1947 and anti-Sikh violence in 1984), the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, Buddhist–Muslim violence in Myanmar and Sri Lanka, and terrorism by groups such as al-Qaeda and Islamic State, as well as anti-abortion violence and violence against religious minorities by majorities.

Definitions and scholarly debate

Some scholars, such as William T. Cavanaugh in The Myth of Religious Violence (2009), argue that "religion" cannot be cleanly separated from "secular" politics and that the category of religious violence is itself a modern construction used to delegitimise certain actors. Others point to distinctive features of religiously motivated violence — sacred values that resist compromise, apocalyptic expectations, and rituals of martyrdom — that can intensify conflict. Sociologists distinguish between violence committed in the name of religion by state and non-state actors, and violence directed at religious groups (religious persecution).

By tradition

Every major tradition has internal critics and peace movements as well as violent episodes. Academic study of religious violence overlaps with terrorism studies, genocide studies and the anthropology of religion, and examines texts, institutions, and the political contexts in which sacred language is used to mobilise violence.
