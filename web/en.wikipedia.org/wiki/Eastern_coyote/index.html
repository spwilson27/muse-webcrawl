Eastern coyote

The eastern coyote is a wild North American hybrid canid descended from a hybrid population of coyotes, eastern wolves, western coyotes, and domestic dogs that emerged in the early 20th century in the Great Lakes region and spread through the northeastern United States and eastern Canada. It is sometimes called the coywolf, though researchers generally prefer "eastern coyote" because the animal's genome is predominantly coyote.

Characteristics

Eastern coyotes are noticeably larger than western coyotes, typically weighing 15–23 kg (35–50 lb), with broader skulls and stronger jaws attributable to their wolf ancestry. Their mixed ancestry also gives them greater genetic diversity and adaptability to both forested and open habitats. They prey on small mammals like western coyotes but, hunting in family groups, can also take adult white-tailed deer, especially in winter.

Genetic studies indicate that eastern coyotes in the Northeast derive on average roughly 60–65% western coyote, about 25% wolf, and around 10% domestic dog ancestry, with proportions varying by region. Their taxonomic status is debated, and they are generally managed as coyotes.
