Apulia (Italian: Puglia [ˈpuʎʎa]) is a region of Italy in Southern Italy bordering the Adriatic Sea to the east and the Ionian Sea to the southeast. It forms the "heel" of the Italian peninsula. Its capital city is Bari; other important cities include Taranto, Foggia, Brindisi, and Lecce.

Geography

Apulia is the least mountainous region of Italy; its landscape consists largely of the broad Tavoliere plain in the north, the limestone Murge plateau in the centre, and the Salento peninsula in the south. The Gargano promontory, a mountainous spur on the Adriatic, hosts an ancient beech forest protected as a national park and UNESCO site.

History

The region was settled by the Iapygians and colonised by Greeks of Magna Graecia; Taranto was one of the great cities of the Greek world. Roman Apulia was famous for grain, wine, and the Via Appia, which ended at Brindisi. After the fall of Rome it passed through Byzantine, Lombard, Saracen, and Norman hands; the Norman and Swabian rulers, notably Emperor Frederick II — who built the mysterious octagonal Castel del Monte — left a rich legacy of castles and cathedrals. Apulia later belonged to the Kingdom of Naples and then to unified Italy.

Culture and sights

Apulia is known for the trulli of Alberobello, conical dry-stone houses that are a UNESCO World Heritage Site; the Baroque architecture of Lecce, nicknamed the "Florence of the South"; the pilgrimage basilica of San Giovanni Rotondo, associated with Padre Pio; and the cliff town of Polignano a Mare. Regional cuisine features olive oil (Apulia produces a large share of Italy's output), durum wheat pasta such as orecchiette, burrata cheese, and Primitivo wines.

Economy

Agriculture remains central, alongside steel at Taranto (long among Europe's largest steelworks), aerospace, and a rapidly growing tourism sector. The ports of Bari, Brindisi, and Taranto link Italy with Greece and the Balkans.
