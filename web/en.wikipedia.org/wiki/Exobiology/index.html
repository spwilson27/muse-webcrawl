Exobiology

Exobiology is the study of the origin, evolution, distribution, and future of life in the universe. It is closely related to — and in modern usage often treated as a synonym of — astrobiology, though historically exobiology emphasised the search for life beyond Earth, while astrobiology also encompasses the study of life on Earth in its cosmic context.

History

The term "exobiology" was coined by the American molecular biologist Joshua Lederberg in 1960, in connection with the beginnings of the space age and the first serious scientific discussions of searching for life on other planets. NASA established an exobiology programme in the early 1960s, funding research into life-detection instruments. The Viking landers of 1976 carried the first dedicated biology experiments to the surface of Mars; their results were ambiguous and are now generally attributed to reactive soil chemistry rather than biology.

Scope

Exobiology draws on astronomy, planetary science, organic chemistry, microbiology and geology. Its central questions include how life originates from non-living chemistry (abiogenesis), what environments can sustain life, how life and its environment co-evolve, and how life might be detected remotely through biosignatures — gases or surface features in an exoplanet's atmosphere or spectrum that are difficult to explain without biology.

Life in extreme environments

The discovery of extremophiles — organisms thriving in boiling acid springs, deep-sea hydrothermal vents, subglacial lakes and highly radioactive environments — greatly expanded the range of conditions considered compatible with life, and with it the list of potentially habitable worlds, including the icy moons Europa and Enceladus.
