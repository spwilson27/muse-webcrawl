Gravity Probe B (GP-B) was a NASA physics mission launched on 20 April 2004 to test two predictions of Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity: the geodetic effect (the warping of space and time by Earth's mass) and frame-dragging (the twisting of spacetime by Earth's rotation). Developed primarily at Stanford University, it was one of the longest-running projects in NASA history, conceived in the early 1960s.

Experiment

The spacecraft carried four ultra-precise gyroscopes — fused quartz spheres coated with niobium, among the most perfect spheres ever made — cooled to near absolute zero with liquid helium and kept in a nearly perfect vacuum. As the satellite orbited Earth in a polar orbit locked onto the guide star IM Pegasi, changes in the gyroscopes' spin axes were read out by SQUID magnetometers. General relativity predicted the spin axes would drift by 6,606 milliarcseconds per year from the geodetic effect and 39 milliarcseconds per year from frame-dragging.

Results

Data analysis proved more difficult than expected because of larger-than-foreseen torque disturbances on the gyroscopes, traced to electrostatic patch effects on the rotor surfaces. After extensive modelling, the final results announced in May 2011 measured a geodetic drift of −6,601.8 ± 18.3 milliarcseconds per year and a frame-dragging drift of −37.2 ± 7.2 milliarcseconds per year, in agreement with Einstein's predictions and confirming both effects. NASA described it as an "epic" verification of general relativity; critics noted the measurement precision (~19% for frame-dragging) fell short of the original goals relative to the mission's roughly $750 million lifetime cost.

Legacy

Regardless of the debate over its cost-effectiveness, GP-B developed technologies — drag-free satellite control, cryogenics, SQUIDs, and precision gyroscopes — that influenced other missions, and it stands as a direct experimental confirmation of frame-dragging, as complementing the indirect evidence from LAGEOS laser ranging.
