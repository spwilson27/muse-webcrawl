The vertebrae (singular: vertebra) are the individual, irregular bones that make up the vertebral column (spine) in vertebrate animals. Stacked together and separated by intervertebral discs, they protect the spinal cord, support the body, and permit movement of the trunk and head. Taken together, the animal group Vertebrata is named after these bones.

Structure

A typical vertebra consists of two main parts:

  • The vertebral body (centrum), the thick, disc-shaped anterior portion that bears weight. Bodies increase in size down the column as loads increase.
  • The vertebral arch, which encloses the vertebral foramen; the stacked foramina form the vertebral canal housing the spinal cord. The arch is formed by paired pedicles and laminae.

Seven processes project from the arch: one median spinous process (palpable along the back), two transverse processes laterally — both serving as lever arms and attachment sites for muscles and ligaments — and four articular processes forming the facet joints that guide motion between adjacent vertebrae. Notches between the pedicles of adjacent vertebrae form the intervertebral foramina, through which spinal nerves exit.

Regional groups in humans

Adults typically have 33 vertebrae: 24 presacral (mobile) vertebrae plus the fused sacrum and coccyx.

Function

The vertebrae protect the spinal cord and nerve roots, transmit the weight of the head and trunk to the pelvis, provide attachment for muscles, ribs, and ligaments, and — together with the intervertebral discs and facet joints — allow flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation of the trunk.

Clinical significance

Vertebrae are affected by fractures (including osteoporotic compression fractures), herniated discs, spondylolisthesis (forward slip of one vertebra on another), scoliosis and other curvatures, infections (vertebral osteomyelitis), and metastatic cancer. The spinal canal's narrowing (spinal stenosis) can compress the cord or cauda equina.
