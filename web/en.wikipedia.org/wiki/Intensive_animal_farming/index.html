Intensive animal farming

Intensive animal farming, also known as factory farming or industrial livestock production, is a system of raising animals — including cattle, pigs, poultry, and fish — at high stocking densities in confined facilities, using industrial methods to maximise output of meat, milk and eggs at the lowest cost. It became dominant in industrialised countries during the 20th century through mechanisation, formulated feeds, selective breeding, veterinary pharmaceuticals and vertical integration of production. Supporters credit it with making animal protein cheap and abundant; critics raise concerns about animal welfare, environmental damage, antibiotic resistance and public health.

Practices

Typical features include housing large numbers of genetically similar animals indoors or in feedlots; battery cages for laying hens; gestation crates and farrowing crates for sows; veal crates; high-energy concentrate feeds based on corn and soy; routine use of antibiotics for growth promotion and disease prevention (restricted or banned in the European Union and increasingly elsewhere); and breeding for rapid growth, which in broiler chickens has produced birds reaching slaughter weight in about five to six weeks. Manure is commonly stored in lagoons and spread on land.

Environmental and health issues

Livestock production contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions, water use, deforestation for feed crops, and nutrient pollution causing eutrophication. Dense confinement facilitates the emergence and spread of zoonotic diseases such as avian influenza and swine influenza, and the routine use of medically important antibiotics in animals is a recognised driver of antimicrobial resistance. Large facilities (in the United States, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs) are regulated for water pollution but remain controversial in rural communities because of odour, air quality and waste.

Welfare and regulation

Animal welfare legislation varies widely. The European Union has banned barren battery cages (2012) and partially restricts sow stalls; several US states, including California under Proposition 12, have adopted confinement standards. Consumer demand has driven growth in cage-free, free-range, organic and certified humane production, as well as interest in plant-based and cultivated meat alternatives, though intensive systems still produce the great majority of the world's meat and eggs.
