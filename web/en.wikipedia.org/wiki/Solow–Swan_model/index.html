Solow–Swan model

The Solow–Swan model or exogenous growth model is an economic model of long-run economic growth. It attempts to explain long-run economic growth by looking at capital accumulation, labour or population growth, and increases in productivity largely driven by technological progress. At its heart is a neoclassical (aggregate) production function, often specified to be of Cobb–Douglas type.

The model

Output depends on physical capital, labour and the stock of knowledge. Because capital exhibits diminishing returns, an economy that saves more grows faster only temporarily: capital per effective worker converges to a steady state at which investment just offsets depreciation and the dilution from population and technology growth. In the long run, growth of output per worker depends solely on the (exogenous) rate of technological progress.

Implications

The model predicts conditional convergence: poorer economies should grow faster than richer ones once differences in savings, population growth and human capital are controlled for — a prediction with partial empirical support (Mankiw, Romer and Weil, 1992, who augmented the model with human capital). Solow's growth-accounting work (1957) attributed most of U.S. growth to the residual of technical progress rather than capital deepening.

Criticism and extensions

Treating technology as exogenous motivated endogenous growth theory (Romer, Lucas), which models innovation and education directly. The model remains the standard starting point of growth economics teaching and empirical growth regressions.
