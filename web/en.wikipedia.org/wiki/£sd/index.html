£sd

£sd (sometimes written Lsd) is the popular name for the pre-decimal system of currency used in the United Kingdom and many parts of the British Empire and Commonwealth before decimalisation. The letters stand for the Latin librae, solidi and denarii — pounds, shillings and pence — the system inherited from Roman coinage through Carolingian reforms and used in Britain for well over a thousand years.

The system

The core relationships were:

  • 1 pound (£1) = 20 shillings
  • 1 shilling (1s) = 12 pence (old pence, written "d")
  • 1 pound = 240 old pence

Amounts were written like "£2 7s 6d" (two pounds, seven shillings and sixpence, read "two pounds seven and six"). A wide range of coins existed or had existed: the farthing (¼d), halfpenny, penny, threepence, sixpence, shilling, florin (2s), half crown (2s 6d) and crown (5s), plus the gold sovereign (£1) and the guinea (21 shillings), which long survived as a unit of account for professional fees, horses and art auctions. The complexity of the system demanded extensive mental arithmetic in schools.

Decimalisation

The United Kingdom decimalised its currency on Decimal Day, 15 February 1971: the pound remained unchanged, but was divided into 100 new pence. The shilling became exactly 5 new pence, and shilling and florin coins circulated as 5p and 10p pieces for years afterwards. Ireland decimalised the same day; most other Commonwealth countries had converted earlier (Australia in 1966, New Zealand in 1967, South Africa in 1961).

Legacy

The notation "£sd" also entered humorous usage in British publishing because of its resemblance to the initials of the hallucinogenic drug LSD, a coincidence exploited by satirists in the 1960s.
