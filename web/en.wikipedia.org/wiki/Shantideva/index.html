Shantideva, 8th-century Indian Buddhist monk and author.

Shantideva (Sanskrit: Śāntideva; c. 685 – c. 763 CE) was an 8th-century Indian Buddhist monk and scholar at the monastic university of Nalanda. He is best known as the author of the Bodhicaryāvatāra ("Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva"), one of the most widely read and commented works of Mahāyāna Buddhism, and of the Śikṣāsamuccaya ("Compendium of Training"), an anthology of Mahāyāna sutra passages arranged as a guide to practice.

Life

Traditional biographies, preserved in Tibetan histories such as those of Buton and Taranatha, relate that Shantideva was born a prince in Saurastra (in modern Gujarat) and renounced the throne on the eve of his coronation after a vision of the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī and the goddess Tārā. He travelled to Nalanda, where he was ordained. Outwardly he appeared idle — doing nothing but eating, sleeping, and walking about, earning the nickname Bhusuku ("one who eats, sleeps, and defecates") — while inwardly practising in secret.

According to the legend, the other monks, seeking to expose and expel him, demanded that he recite scripture publicly from a high throne without steps. Shantideva ascended miraculously, asked whether they wished to hear something known or something new, and recited the Bodhicaryāvatāra. When he reached the ninth chapter's discussion of emptiness, he is said to have risen into the air and vanished, his voice continuing to the end of the text. He then departed Nalanda and lived as a wandering yogin, performing various miraculous works.

Works

Bodhicaryāvatāra

The Bodhicaryāvatāra is a poem of roughly 900 verses in ten chapters describing the path of the bodhisattva: the generation of bodhicitta (the mind of awakening), confession, vigilance, patience, heroic perseverance, meditation, and wisdom (the ninth chapter, a celebrated exposition of Madhyamaka philosophy and the emptiness of phenomena), concluding with a dedication of merit. Its sixth chapter, on patience, contains the famous reflection:

There is no evil equal to hatred, and no fortitude equal to patience. Thus one should strive in various ways to meditate on patience.

The eighth chapter's meditation on the equality and exchange of self and others is among the most influential passages in Mahāyāna ethics. The text survives in Sanskrit and in Tibetan and Chinese translations, and attracted over a hundred Indian commentaries.

Śikṣāsamuccaya

The Śikṣāsamuccaya ("Compendium of Training") is a prose anthology of quotations from Mahāyāna sutras — many otherwise lost in Sanskrit — organised around a set of summary verses (kārikā) attributed to Shantideva himself. It presents the discipline of the bodhisattva in terms of giving, morality, and the protection and purification of body, possessions, and merit.

Philosophy and influence

Shantideva is classified in Tibetan tradition as a proponent of the Prāsaṅgika Madhyamaka school. His writings became central to the Tibetan Buddhist curriculum: the Bodhicaryāvatāra is memorised and taught in monasteries of all schools, and its presentation of bodhicitta practice underlies the lojong (mind training) tradition. In the modern era, the work has been translated into English many times — notably by Kate Crosby and Andrew Skilton as The Way of the Bodhisattva — and is frequently cited by the 14th Dalai Lama, who regards it as a principal source of his own teaching.
