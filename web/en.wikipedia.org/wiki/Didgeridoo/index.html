Didgeridoo

ClassificationWind instrument (aerophone)
OriginAboriginal peoples of northern Australia
MaterialEucalyptus hollowed by termites; beeswax mouthpiece
TechniqueCircular breathing; continuous drone

The didgeridoo (also spelled didjeridu) is a wind instrument developed by Aboriginal peoples of northern Australia, possibly as long as 1,000–1,500 years ago. Traditionally it is made from a length of eucalyptus whose interior has been hollowed out by termites; the player vibrates the lips against a beeswax mouthpiece while using circular breathing to maintain an unbroken drone.

Construction and sound

Instruments are typically 1 to 1.5 metres long, longer instruments producing lower pitches — usually between about C♯ and F♯ below middle C. Over the basic drone, players add rhythms and timbres with tongue movements, vocalisations, and "toots" (overblown notes), imitating animal and ancestral sounds. In Yolngu culture of Arnhem Land the instrument is called the yiḏaki, and strict cultural protocols surround its making and ceremonial use; in many groups it is traditionally played by men.

Modern use

From the late 20th century the didgeridoo spread worldwide into world-music, meditation and popular recordings, played by artists such as Charlie McMahon and Xavier Rudd, and has even been investigated as an aid for snoring and sleep apnoea because of the breathing training it provides. Its global popularity has also prompted debate about cultural appropriation and respect for Indigenous ownership.
