Ghost Festival

The Ghost Festival or Hungry Ghost Festival (traditional Chinese: 中元節; simplified Chinese: 中元节, Zhōngyuán Jié) is a traditional festival held in many East and Southeast Asian countries, based on the beliefs of Chinese folk religion, Buddhism and Taoism. It falls on the 15th night of the seventh month of the Chinese calendar — the climax of Ghost Month, when the gates of the afterlife are believed to open and the spirits of the dead, including ancestors and wandering "hungry ghosts", roam the world of the living.

Origins

The festival combines several strands. In Taoism the day is the birthday of the Earth Official (Diguan Dadi), one of the Three Officials, who pardons sins. In Mahayana Buddhism it coincides with Ullambana, from the story of the monk Mulian (Maudgalyayana), who sought to rescue his mother from suffering as a hungry ghost; the Buddha instructed him to make offerings to the sangha on the 15th day of the seventh month. Confucian ancestor veneration provided the framework of offerings to family dead, while folk belief extended charity to neglected, kinless spirits so that they would not trouble the living.

Practices

Families prepare elaborate meals — often served at an empty place setting — and offer food, tea and wine at home altars and temples. Joss paper shaped as money, houses, clothes and goods is burned so the dead may use it in the afterlife. On the festival night, floating water lanterns and lotus-shaped lights are released on rivers to guide lost souls back. In Singapore and Malaysia, communities stage getai concerts and Chinese opera with front rows of seats left empty for the unseen guests. Throughout Ghost Month, superstitious avoidance is common: weddings, moving house, swimming and major purchases are often postponed.

Regional variations

In Vietnam the festival (Vu Lan) strongly emphasises filial piety and honouring mothers. In Japan, related observances merged into the Buddhist Obon in mid-August. In Fujian and Taiwan the whole seventh month is observed with communal feasts (pudu) in which neighbourhoods and companies sponsor tables of offerings for the "good brothers", as ghosts are euphemistically called.
