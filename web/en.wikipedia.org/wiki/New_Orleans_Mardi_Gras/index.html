Mardi Gras in New Orleans

The holiday of Mardi Gras is celebrated in New Orleans, Louisiana, with one of the world's most famous Carnival festivals. The season begins on Twelfth Night (January 6) and culminates on Fat Tuesday — the day before Ash Wednesday — with several weeks of parades, balls, and street celebrations organized by social clubs known as krewes. Signature customs include costumed riders throwing beads and trinkets ("throws") to crowds, king cakes, flambeaux carriers, and the colours purple, green, and gold, symbolising justice, faith, and power.

History

French colonists brought pre-Lenten Carnival traditions to Louisiana in the early 18th century; masked balls and street revelry are documented from the colonial period. The Mistick Krewe of Comus staged the first organized parade with floats and a thematic pageant in 1857, giving Mardi Gras its modern form. Rex, founded in 1872, established the official colours and anthem and the figure of the King of Carnival. Twentieth-century additions included the African American Krewe of Zulu (1909), famous for its hand-painted coconut throws, and the satirical super-krewes — Bacchus (1968), Endymion, and Orpheus — which field enormous floats and celebrity monarchs.

The season

Parades roll principally along St. Charles Avenue and Canal Street in New Orleans and in suburbs such as Metairie, following a published schedule over the final two weeks, with the biggest parades concentrated in the last five days. Balls hosted by old-line krewes feature debutante presentations, while the public festivities fill the French Quarter and beyond. The celebration generates an economic impact estimated at over a billion dollars and draws more than a million visitors in typical years.

Interruptions and modern era

Parades were cancelled during the Civil War, both World Wars, the 1918 influenza pandemic, and — for the first time in over forty years — in 2021 due to COVID-19, when residents famously decorated their homes as "house floats." Following Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the 2006 celebration was widely seen as a symbol of the city's recovery. Mardi Gras Indians — African American masking groups in elaborate hand-sewn feathered suits — and traditions such as the North Side Skull and Bone Gang form a parallel, deeply rooted strand of the festival.
