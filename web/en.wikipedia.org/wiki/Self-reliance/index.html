Self-reliance

Self-reliance is the quality of relying on one's own judgement, abilities, and resources rather than depending on others. The term is most strongly associated with Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay "Self-Reliance", published in Essays: First Series (1841), a foundational text of American transcendentalism and individualism.

Emerson's essay

Emerson urges readers to trust their own intuitions and resist the pressure of social conformity: "Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist." The essay contains some of his most quoted lines, including "Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string", "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds", and "To be great is to be misunderstood." Self-reliance for Emerson is not economic independence but spiritual and intellectual authenticity — attending to the divine spark within rather than imitating others or deferring to institutions and tradition. The essay also warns against the deadening effects of property, travel undertaken for distraction, and charitable conformity.

Wider meanings and influence

Beyond Emerson, self-reliance names a practical ideal in many movements: homesteading and self-sufficiency, scouting, survivalism, personal finance, and development policy, where building local capacity is preferred to dependency on aid. Philosophically it intersects with autonomy, stoicism, and Thoreau's experiment at Walden. Critics have noted that an absolute ethic of self-reliance can ignore structural disadvantage and the inescapably social conditions of human life; communitarian and care-ethics perspectives emphasise interdependence instead. Nonetheless, Emerson's essay remains a touchstone of American letters and is widely taught in schools.
