Yellow jersey

The yellow jersey (French: maillot jaune, pronounced [majo ʒon]) is the distinctive jersey worn by the overall leader of the Tour de France — the rider with the lowest cumulative time — and, by imitation, by the leaders of many other cycling stage races. At the Tour it is the sport's most coveted garment; the rider who wears it on the final day in Paris is the race champion.

History

The jersey was introduced in 1919, sixteen years after the first Tour, so that spectators and journalists could pick out the race leader in the peloton. Race director Henri Desgrange chose yellow because the organizing newspaper, L'Auto, was printed on yellow paper. The first rider to wear it was the Frenchman Eugène Christophe, who received it on 18 July 1919 before the stage from Grenoble to Geneva; Christophe reportedly complained that spectators mocked him as a canary. The jersey lapsed briefly in 1924–1929 in some form and has been awarded after every stage since.

Records and rules

The final winner wears yellow into Paris, where the jersey is presented on the Champs-Élysées podium. Eddy Merckx holds the record for the most days spent in the yellow jersey (96), and also shares the record of five overall Tour victories with Jacques Anquetil, Bernard Hinault, and Miguel Indurain. Lance Armstrong's seven wins were stripped in 2012 for doping. Riders value leading the race even without winning it: a single day in yellow is a career highlight and carries a daily prize plus bonus payments for the wearer's team. Other Tour classifications have their own jerseys — green for points, polka-dot for mountains, and white for the best young rider — but none approaches the yellow jersey's fame, and the term has passed into general usage for the leader of any competition.
