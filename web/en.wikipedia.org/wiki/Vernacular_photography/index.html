Vernacular photography

Vernacular photography refers to photographs made by amateur or unknown photographers who take everyday life and commonplace subjects as their themes. It encompasses snapshots, family albums, photo-booth pictures, school portraits, commercial studio work, and even applied genres such as police, medical and real-estate photography — images made for use rather than exhibition. The term parallels "vernacular architecture": ordinary production by non-specialists, using local conventions.

History and study

The mass diffusion of easy cameras, beginning with Kodak's Brownie in 1900 and its slogan "You press the button, we do the rest", created an enormous archive of informal images of daily life. Since the late 20th century, historians and curators have treated these photographs as serious cultural documents: Geoffrey Batchen's essay "Vernacular Photographies" (2000) argued for expanding photographic history beyond fine-art masters, and exhibitions such as the Metropolitan Museum's Other Pictures (2000), drawn from Thomas Walther's collection of anonymous snapshots, brought vernacular work into major museums. Collectors prize accidental surrealism, double exposures, decapitated-by-the-frame compositions and other happy accidents typical of snapshot practice.

Characteristics

Vernacular photographs tend to record rituals and possessions considered worth remembering — birthdays, holidays, new cars and houses, military service, pets — and to favour frontal, centred compositions. Social historians mine them for evidence about clothing, interiors, work and leisure, while the rise of camera phones and social media has made the vernacular mode the dominant form of photography worldwide, even as the aesthetics of the snapshot have been deliberately adopted by professional and fashion photographers.
