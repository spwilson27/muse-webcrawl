LDPC

Low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are a class of error correction codes which provide a feasible method for constructing codes that approach the theoretical limit of channel capacity, the Shannon limit. Like turbo codes, LDPC codes permit reliable information transfer over noisy channels at rates close to capacity with practically implementable decoders.

A Tanner graph representing an LDPC code's parity-check constraints

History

LDPC codes were first introduced by Robert G. Gallager in his 1960 doctoral thesis at MIT and published in 1962–1963. Largely forgotten for decades because the required computations exceeded the practical hardware of the era, they were independently rediscovered in the mid-1990s by David MacKay and Radford Neal, and by Michael Tanner's earlier graph-based formulation, sparking intensive research after the success of turbo codes.

Construction and decoding

An LDPC code is a linear block code defined by a sparse parity-check matrix — one containing only a small number of non-zero entries per row and column. The sparsity enables iterative decoding with the sum-product (belief propagation) algorithm on the code's bipartite Tanner graph, in which variable nodes and check nodes exchange probabilistic messages. Ensembles of irregular LDPC codes can perform within a fraction of a decibel of the Shannon limit.

Applications

LDPC codes have been adopted in numerous standards, including 10 Gigabit Ethernet (10GBASE-T), Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11n/ac/ax), DVB-S2 satellite broadcasting, WiMAX, DOCSIS 3.1 cable, and the data channel of the 5G NR mobile standard. They are also used in deep-space communications and in flash memory controllers.
