In astrophysics, dark flow is a hypothesised large-scale streaming motion of galaxy clusters that cannot be explained by the distribution of matter within the observable universe. The term was coined after a 2008 study by Alexander Kashlinsky and colleagues reported, using the kinematic Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect in data from the WMAP satellite, that hundreds of galaxy clusters out to billions of light-years appeared to be moving at 600–1,000 km/s in a common direction, roughly toward the constellations Centaurus and Hydra.

The claim

Standard cosmology attributes clusters' "peculiar velocities" to the gravity of nearby structures such as the Great Attractor and the Shapley Supercluster, and such motions should fall off with distance. Kashlinsky's team instead found a coherent flow persisting across their whole sample, suggesting a gravitational pull from mass concentrations beyond the observable universe — possibly structures that were carried outside our horizon by cosmic inflation before the Big Bang's hot phase.

Reception and criticism

The result was controversial from the outset. Other groups questioned the statistical methods and the handling of systematic errors, and analyses of later data from the Planck satellite found no statistically significant dark flow, placing limits consistent with the standard ΛCDM model. The idea remains a live minority topic in cosmology, sometimes invoked in discussions of multiverse models, but the consensus is that the claimed flow has not been confirmed.
