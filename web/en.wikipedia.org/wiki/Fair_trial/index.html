Fair trial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fair trial is a trial conducted in accordance with the principles of justice — impartially, openly, without undue delay, and with equality of arms between the parties. The right to a fair trial is guaranteed by Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, Article 14 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments in the United States, and most national constitutions.

Its components typically include an independent and impartial tribunal established by law; the presumption of innocence; prompt information about the charge; adequate time and facilities to prepare a defence; legal assistance (free where necessary — Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963 in the US); the right to examine witnesses; an interpreter where needed; public hearing and judgment (with limited exceptions); and protection against self-incrimination and double jeopardy. Remedies for breach include exclusion of evidence, retrial and compensation. What fairness requires in mass-atrocity trials, terrorism cases and administrative proceedings short of criminal charges remains contested terrain.
