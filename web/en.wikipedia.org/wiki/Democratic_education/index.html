Democratic education

Democratic education is an educational philosophy and practice in which students participate meaningfully in decisions affecting their learning and school community — including what and how they learn, and often the rules and governance of the school itself. It is grounded in ideals of self-determination, equal respect and learning for democratic citizenship.

History

Ideas of democratic schooling trace to progressive educators such as John Dewey, who argued in Democracy and Education (1916) that schools should themselves be embryonic democracies. A. S. Neill founded Summerhill School in England in 1921, where lessons were optional and a school meeting of pupils and staff, each with one vote, made the rules. Later schools include Sudbury Valley School (founded 1968 in Massachusetts), where students direct their own activities and the school is run by a weekly School Meeting with a judicial system of peers.

Features

Common features include self-directed learning, age mixing, school meetings with equal votes for students and staff, community-made rules and conflict resolution by peers, and the absence of compulsory curricula, grades or tests in the most radical models. The International Democratic Education Conference (IDEC) has met annually since 1993, and networks such as EUDEC link democratic schools worldwide.

Criticism

Critics question whether children will acquire structured knowledge without a set curriculum and whether outcomes for disadvantaged students match those of conventional schooling; supporters reply that graduates show strong autonomy, motivation and civic skills.
