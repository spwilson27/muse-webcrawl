Relativistic jet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A relativistic jet is a narrow beam of ionised matter accelerated close to the speed of light, emitted along the rotation axis of an astronomical object such as an active galactic nucleus, quasar, gamma-ray burst or accreting stellar-mass black hole. The best-known example is the jet of the supergiant elliptical galaxy M87, first observed in 1918 by Heber Curtis.

Formation

Jets are thought to be launched from the inner accretion disk around a compact object. In the Blandford–Znajek mechanism, magnetic field lines threading a rotating black hole extract rotational energy and collimate outflowing plasma; in the Blandford–Payne model, fields anchored in the disk itself fling material away centrifugally. Radiation pressure and magnetic hoop stresses help collimate the flow into stable beams extending, in the largest radio galaxies, for millions of light-years.

Observational effects

Because the jet moves at relativistic speed, its radiation is strongly beamed. Relativistic beaming explains phenomena such as superluminal motion, one-sided jets, and the classification of blazars as active galaxies whose jets point almost directly at Earth. Jets emit across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio synchrotron radiation to inverse-Compton gamma rays, and are major channels by which black holes feed energy back into their host galaxies.
