DetailsTenth cranial nerve (CN X)

The vagus nerve, also called the pneumogastric nerve, is the tenth cranial nerve (CN X). Its name derives from the Latin vagus, "wandering", because it has the longest and most widely distributed course of any cranial nerve, extending from the brainstem through the neck and thorax to the abdomen.

Structure and function

The vagus emerges from the medulla oblongata and exits the skull via the jugular foramen. It carries roughly 80% afferent (sensory) fibres and 20% efferent fibres. Its parasympathetic output slows the heart, stimulates digestion and peristalsis, and constricts the bronchi; its motor branches supply most muscles of the palate, pharynx, and larynx via the recurrent laryngeal nerve — the left recurrent laryngeal famously looping under the aortic arch. It forms the main parasympathetic supply to the gut as far as the splenic flexure of the colon.

Clinical significance

Excess vagal activity can cause vasovagal syncope (fainting). Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), delivered by an implanted pulse generator, is an approved treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression and is under investigation for inflammatory diseases, exploiting the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Historical vagotomy (surgical section of the vagus) was once used to treat peptic ulcers by reducing gastric acid secretion.
