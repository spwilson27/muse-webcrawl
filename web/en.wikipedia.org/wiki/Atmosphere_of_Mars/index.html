The atmosphere of Mars is the layer of gases surrounding the planet Mars. It is primarily composed of carbon dioxide (95%), molecular nitrogen (2.8%) and argon (2%), with trace levels of oxygen, carbon monoxide and water vapour. It is much thinner than Earth's: average surface pressure is about 600 pascals, less than one percent of Earth's sea-level pressure.

Structure and weather

Because the atmosphere is thin and dry, temperatures swing widely — from about −140 °C at the winter poles to nearly 30 °C at the equator on summer afternoons. Seasonal sublimation and deposition of CO₂ at the polar caps changes total atmospheric pressure by up to 25% over the Martian year. Winds raise planet-encircling dust storms, and thin water-ice clouds, fogs and frosts occur regularly.

Methane

Trace amounts of methane have been reported by orbiters and the Curiosity rover, with occasional apparent spikes. Because methane is destroyed by sunlight within a few centuries, its presence would imply an active source — geological or possibly biological — though some measurements conflict and the topic remains debated.

Atmospheric loss

NASA's MAVEN mission showed that Mars is losing atmosphere to space through solar-wind sputtering, at roughly 1–2 kg per second, increasing during solar storms. Early Mars, with a thicker atmosphere and a protective magnetic field, likely sustained liquid water on its surface; loss of that atmosphere is a central explanation for the planet's present cold, dry state.
