Genetic linkage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic linkage is the tendency of DNA sequences that are close together on a chromosome to be inherited together during the meiosis phase of sexual reproduction. Two genes that are physically near one another are less likely to be separated onto different chromatids during chromosomal crossover, and are therefore said to be genetically linked. Conversely, genes on different chromosomes, or far apart on the same chromosome, assort independently.

Discovery

The phenomenon was first described in 1905–1906 by William Bateson, Edith Rebecca Saunders and Reginald Punnett, who found that certain trait combinations in sweet peas did not assort independently as Mendel's second law predicted. Thomas Hunt Morgan's work with the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster from 1910 onwards provided the explanation: genes are arranged linearly on chromosomes, and crossing over can separate linked genes with a probability that increases with the distance between them. Morgan's student Alfred Sturtevant, as an undergraduate in 1913, used recombination frequencies between linked genes to construct the first genetic map.

Recombination frequency and map distance

The recombination frequency between two loci is the proportion of offspring in which a recombination event has separated them. It ranges from 0 (completely linked) to 50% (unlinked — indistinguishable from independent assortment). A recombination frequency of 1% corresponds approximately to a map distance of 1 centimorgan (cM), a unit named after Morgan. Mapping functions such as Haldane's and Kosambi's correct for multiple crossovers that make raw frequencies underestimate true distances over long intervals.

Linkage analysis

Because linkage decays predictably with distance, it can be used to locate genes responsible for inherited diseases. In linkage analysis, researchers follow the inheritance of a disease through families together with genetic markers of known position; statistically significant co-segregation, measured by the LOD score (a LOD of 3 or more is conventionally taken as significant evidence), indicates the disease gene lies near the marker. Linkage analysis localised the genes for Huntington's disease (1983) and cystic fibrosis (1989), among many others. In population genetics, the related concept of linkage disequilibrium — the non-random association of alleles at different loci — underlies genome-wide association studies and haplotype mapping.
