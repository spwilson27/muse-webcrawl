The Liao dynasty (Chinese: 遼朝; pinyin: Liáo cháo), also known as the Khitan Empire, was a dynasty and empire in East Asia that ruled from 916 to 1125 over what is now Mongolia, Manchuria, northern China, the Korean Peninsula's northern borderlands, and parts of the Russian Far East. It was founded by Yelü Abaoji, khan of the Khitan people, around the time of the collapse of the Tang dynasty, and was the first state to control all of Manchuria.

History

Abaoji unified the Khitan tribes, adopted the trappings of a Chinese emperor, founded the Supreme Capital (Shangjing, near modern Lindong, Inner Mongolia) and introduced a written script for Khitan — the "large script" (920) and later a "small script". His successor, Emperor Taizong, received the Sixteen Prefectures of Yan and Yun from the Later Jin in 938, giving Liao a foothold south of the Great Wall, and took the dynastic name "Great Liao" in 947.

Liao's most formidable rival was the Song dynasty of China. After decades of war, the Chanyuan Treaty of 1005 established a century of peace in which Song paid Liao annual tribute of silver and silk. Liao developed a distinctive dual administration: a Northern Chancellery governing the nomadic peoples in steppe fashion, and a Southern Chancellery administering the settled Chinese population on Tang lines. The empire maintained five capitals, elaborate court culture, and diplomacy with Goryeo Korea, which it invaded three times (993–1019) without lasting success.

Fall and legacy

In the early 12th century the Jurchen under Aguda rebelled, founded the Jin dynasty, and allied with Song. Liao collapsed quickly: the last emperor, Tianzuo, was captured in 1125. A Khitan remnant under Yelü Dashi fled west and founded the Qara Khitai (Western Liao) in Central Asia, which endured until 1218. The Russian and Persian words for China ("Kitay", "Khata") derive from "Khitan", reflecting Liao's reach. Liao is remembered for pioneering the dual-system model later imitated by the Jin, Yuan and Qing conquest dynasties.
