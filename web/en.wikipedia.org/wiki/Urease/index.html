Urease

EC number3.5.1.5
ReactionUrea + H2O → CO2 + 2 NH3
CofactorNickel (2 Ni2+ per active site)
Found inBacteria, fungi, plants, some invertebrates
Urease was the first enzyme ever crystallized (1926)

Urease (EC 3.5.1.5) is an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea into carbon dioxide and ammonia. It is found in numerous bacteria, fungi, algae, plants, and some invertebrates, and is notable historically and biochemically: in 1926 James B. Sumner crystallized urease from jack beans, the first crystallization of any enzyme, demonstrating that enzymes are proteins — work that earned him a share of the 1946 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In 1975 urease was also identified as the first known nickel-containing enzyme.

Structure and mechanism

Bacterial ureases are typically (αβγ)3 heterotrimers, while plant and fungal ureases are homo-oligomers of a single subunit homologous to the fused bacterial subunits. The active site contains two Ni2+ ions bridged by a carbamylated lysine residue and a hydroxide ion. Urea binds across the two nickel ions, and the bridging hydroxide attacks its carbonyl carbon, releasing ammonia and carbamate, which spontaneously decomposes to a second ammonia and CO2. Accessory proteins (UreD, UreE, UreF, UreG) are required to assemble the active metallocentre in vivo.

Biological and clinical significance

In soil, microbial urease drives the breakdown of urea fertilizer, and its excessive activity can waste nitrogen as volatilized ammonia, motivating the use of urease inhibitors such as NBPT in agriculture. In medicine, the urease of Helicobacter pylori generates ammonia that neutralizes gastric acid, helping the bacterium colonize the stomach; this activity is the basis of the urea breath test used to diagnose infection. Urease-producing urinary pathogens such as Proteus mirabilis alkalinize urine and promote the formation of struvite kidney stones.
