Airway management

Airway management is the medical process of ensuring an open pathway for gas exchange between a patient's lungs and the atmosphere, by clearing or preventing obstructions of the airway. It is a fundamental skill in anaesthesiology, emergency medicine, intensive care and pre-hospital care, and forms the "A" of the ABC (airway, breathing, circulation) approach to resuscitation.

Basic techniques

Simple manoeuvres include the head-tilt/chin-lift and jaw thrust, which lift the tongue away from the posterior pharynx, and the recovery position for spontaneously breathing unconscious patients. Suction removes blood, vomit and secretions. Oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways hold the airway open, and bag-valve-mask ventilation can oxygenate a patient who is not breathing adequately.

Advanced techniques

Supraglottic airway devices, such as the laryngeal mask airway, sit above the glottis and are widely used in anaesthesia and resuscitation. Tracheal intubation — placing a tube through the mouth (or nose) into the trachea, usually with a laryngoscope or video laryngoscope — remains the definitive method of securing the airway, protecting it from aspiration and permitting controlled ventilation. Confirmation of tube placement relies on capnography (detection of exhaled carbon dioxide) as well as clinical signs.

Surgical airway

When intubation and ventilation fail ("can't intubate, can't oxygenate"), a surgical airway — cricothyrotomy (needle or surgical) or emergency tracheostomy — provides front-of-neck access to the trachea. Difficult-airway algorithms published by bodies such as the Difficult Airway Society guide clinicians through escalating plans A to D.

Assessment

Predictors of a difficult airway include limited mouth opening, reduced neck mobility, obesity, a short thyromental distance and a high Mallampati score. Pre-oxygenation, positioning and the presence of trained assistance and rescue equipment are emphasised in modern practice.
