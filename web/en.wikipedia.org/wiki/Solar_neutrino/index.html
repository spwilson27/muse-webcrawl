Solar neutrino

A solar neutrino is an electron neutrino produced in the Sun by nuclear fusion reactions. Solar neutrinos are the most numerous neutrinos reaching the Earth, with a flux at the surface of roughly 6.5×1010 per square centimetre per second. Because neutrinos interact only extremely weakly with matter, they pass out of the Sun's core almost unimpeded, carrying direct information about the nuclear reactions occurring there.

Production mechanisms

Most solar neutrinos are produced in the proton–proton chain, in which hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium. The lowest-energy neutrinos come from the basic pp reaction, while higher-energy neutrinos are produced by the 7Be electron-capture reaction and, most energetically (up to about 15 MeV), by the decay of 8B. A smaller contribution comes from the CNO cycle, which dominates energy production in more massive stars.

The solar neutrino problem

Beginning with the Homestake experiment of Raymond Davis Jr. in the late 1960s, detectors consistently measured only about one-third to one-half of the flux of solar neutrinos predicted by the standard solar model. This discrepancy, the solar neutrino problem, persisted for three decades through experiments such as Kamiokande, GALLEX, and SAGE.

Resolution

The problem was resolved by the discovery of neutrino oscillation: electron neutrinos produced in the Sun change flavour on their way to Earth, arriving in part as muon and tau neutrinos to which early detectors were insensitive. The Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (SNO) confirmed in 2001–2002 that the total flux of all flavours matched solar model predictions, while the electron-neutrino component was reduced. The MSW effect describes how matter inside the Sun enhances this conversion. Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics for the detection of cosmic neutrinos, and the oscillation explanation was further recognised by the 2015 Nobel Prize awarded to Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald.

Detection

Modern experiments such as Super-Kamiokande and Borexino detect solar neutrinos through elastic scattering on electrons and, in SNO's case, through interactions with heavy water. Borexino has measured neutrinos from the pp reaction, 7Be, pep, and 8B, and in 2020 reported the first detection of CNO-cycle neutrinos from the Sun.
