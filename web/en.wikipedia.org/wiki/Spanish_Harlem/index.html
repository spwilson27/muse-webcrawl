Spanish Harlem

East 116th Street in El Barrio

Spanish Harlem, also known as East Harlem or El Barrio, is a neighborhood of Upper Manhattan in New York City, bounded roughly by East 96th Street to the south, the Harlem River to the north and east, and Fifth Avenue to the west. It is one of the largest predominantly Hispanic communities in New York City, principally of Puerto Rican heritage — the historic heart of the Nuyorican community — with significant Dominican, Mexican, and other Latino populations.

History

East Harlem began as a rural Dutch settlement and later hosted successive immigrant communities: Germans, then Irish, then, from the late 19th century, one of America's largest Italian communities — Italian Harlem, home of the feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel and of future mayor Fiorello La Guardia. After the First World War, and especially after the Second World War, Puerto Rican migration transformed the area, which became known as Spanish Harlem or El Barrio ("the neighborhood"). Writers such as Piri Thomas (Down These Mean Streets, 1967) and artists including the Young Lords chronicled and organised within the community; the Nuyorican Poets Café movement grew from its cultural life.

Culture

Spanish Harlem is a cradle of Latin music in the United States: Tito Puente grew up there, and salsa, Latin jazz, and boogaloo all flourished in its clubs and streets. Cultural landmarks include El Museo del Barrio, founded in 1969, and the annual Puerto Rican Day Parade, which passes along Fifth Avenue. Ben E. King's "Spanish Harlem" (1960) and Bob Dylan's writing made the name famous worldwide.

The neighborhood shares its history with the rest of East Harlem: waves of urban renewal and public-housing construction, disinvestment in the 1970s, and, in recent decades, gentrification pressures spreading north from the Upper East Side. It remains a centre of Puerto Rican political, religious, and artistic life in New York.
