Occupation of the Baltic states

The occupation of the Baltic states is the term used for the occupation and annexation of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania by the Soviet Union in 1940 under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, their occupation by Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1944/45, and their renewed incorporation into the USSR from 1944/45 until the restoration of their independence in 1990–91.

Background and 1940 annexation

The three states had won independence from the Russian Empire after the First World War and joined the League of Nations. Secret protocols to the August 1939 Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact assigned them to the Soviet sphere of influence. In autumn 1939 Moscow forced "mutual assistance" treaties and military bases on all three; in June 1940, under ultimatums and full military occupation, Soviet-controlled governments were installed, stage-managed elections followed, and in August 1940 the three countries were admitted to the USSR as union republics.

The United States, the United Kingdom, and many other Western governments never recognized the annexation de jure, maintaining Baltic diplomatic legations throughout the Cold War. The Soviet and later Russian position treated the incorporation as lawful, a difference that remains politically significant.

German occupation and second Soviet occupation

Germany's invasion in 1941 substituted Nazi rule (Reichskommissariat Ostland) and the near-annihilation of the Baltic Jewish communities. From 1944 the Red Army retook the region; Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania were reconstituted as Soviet republics. The postwar decades brought armed partisan resistance, mass deportations (notably Operation Priboi in March 1949, in which more than 90,000 people were deported), collectivization, industrialization, and large-scale immigration that sharply reduced the titular nationalities' share of population, especially in Estonia and Latvia.

Restoration of independence

In the late 1980s, popular fronts (Sąjūdis in Lithuania, the Popular Front of Latvia, and the Estonian Popular Front) channeled demands for sovereignty. On 23 August 1989 some two million people joined hands in the Baltic Way, a 600-km human chain across the three capitals protesting the pact's fiftieth anniversary. Lithuania declared independence restored on 11 March 1990; Estonia and Latvia followed with transitional declarations and, after the failure of the August 1991 Moscow coup, full restoration was internationally recognized. Russian troops withdrew by 1994. The three states frame 1991 as the restoration of interrupted statehood rather than the creation of new states.
