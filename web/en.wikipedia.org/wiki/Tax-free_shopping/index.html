Tax-free shopping


A tax-free refund desk at an international airport

Tax-free shopping allows travellers to purchase goods free of value-added tax (VAT) or other sales taxes in the country of purchase, on condition that the goods are exported unused within a certain period. In practice, shoppers usually pay the tax at the till and reclaim it on departure, often through a refund operator which charges a fee. The scheme operates in many countries, notably in the European Union for residents of non-EU countries.

How it works

Typically the traveller must:

  1. Shop at a participating retailer and request a tax-free form, presenting a passport or other proof of non-residence.
  2. Meet a minimum purchase threshold, which varies by country — for example a total per receipt or per day at the same store.
  3. Present the goods, receipts, and forms to customs on leaving the country (or the EU) to obtain an export validation stamp or electronic validation.
  4. Claim the refund from the refund operator or retailer, in cash, by card credit, or by cheque, minus an administration fee.

Goods generally must be exported within three months of purchase and must be unused — customs may require items to be available for inspection, so travellers are advised to keep purchases in hand luggage until validation.

Differences from duty-free

Tax-free shopping is often confused with duty-free shopping. Duty-free shops, usually at airports and seaports, sell goods without local taxes at the point of sale. Tax-free shopping, by contrast, takes place at ordinary high-street retailers, with the tax refunded afterwards. Some jurisdictions operate both schemes side by side.

By region

European Union: Visitors residing outside the EU can reclaim VAT on goods exported in personal luggage. Member states set their own minimum spend and procedures; electronic validation systems such as France's PABLO and Spain's DIVA have replaced paper stamping in many locations. The United Kingdom abolished its VAT Retail Export Scheme at the end of the Brexit transition period in 2021, a decision criticised by retailers and tourism bodies.

Japan: Foreign tourists can buy consumables and general goods tax-free at licensed shops on presentation of a passport, with records transmitted electronically to customs.

Other countries: Similar schemes exist in South Korea, Thailand, Turkey, Argentina, and elsewhere; in the United States, some states such as Louisiana and Texas offer partial sales-tax refunds to international visitors through private operators.

Criticism

Critics note that refund operators retain a significant share of the tax, that queues and paperwork deter claims, and that a substantial fraction of eligible VAT is never reclaimed. Supporters argue the schemes encourage tourist spending and retail employment.
