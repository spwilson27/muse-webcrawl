Airline codes are the standardized identifiers used throughout the aviation industry for airlines, flights, tickets, baggage, and air traffic control. Each airline holds up to three main code systems: a two-character IATA airline designator, a three-letter ICAO airline designator, and an ICAO telephony designator (call sign) used in radio communication with air traffic control.

IATA codes

IATA (International Air Transport Association) two-character codes — such as BA for British Airways, AA for American Airlines, UA for United Airlines, LH for Lufthansa, EK for Emirates, and FR for Ryanair — appear in timetables, reservations, tickets, boarding passes, and baggage tags, combined with a flight number of up to four digits (e.g., BA249). Because there are limited combinations, some codes contain digits (e.g., U2 for easyJet, 5Y for Atlas Air), and codes of defunct airlines may be reassigned. A separate three-digit IATA "accounting code" prefixes ticket numbers to identify the issuing carrier.

ICAO codes and call signs

ICAO three-letter designators are used in flight plans and air traffic services: BAW for British Airways, whose call sign is the distinctive "Speedbird"; AAL ("American"); UAL ("United"); DLH ("Lufthansa"); UAE ("Emirates"); and VIR ("Virgin"). Call signs are chosen to be distinctive and pronounceable, sometimes with historical flavor — Pan Am used "Clipper," and Aer Lingus uses "Shamrock." To avoid confusion between similar flight numbers, airlines may add letters to call signs in busy airspace.

Other code systems

Closely related codes include IATA and ICAO airport codes (LHR/EGLL for London Heathrow), IATA aircraft equipment codes (e.g., 388 for the Airbus A380-800, 77W for the Boeing 777-300ER), and codes for booking classes, meal services, and special service requests used inside reservation systems. Codes are catalogued in IATA's Airline Coding Directory and ICAO Document 8585.
