Lead and copper rule

Lead and Copper Rule
AgencyU.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
Statutory basisSafe Drinking Water Act
First issued1991
Action levelsLead 15 ppb (lowered to 10 ppb by the 2024 improvements); copper 1.3 ppm

The Lead and Copper Rule (LCR) is a United States federal regulation that limits the concentration of lead and copper allowed in public drinking water at the consumer's tap, as well as limiting the levels of water corrosivity. Issued by the Environmental Protection Agency in 1991 under the Safe Drinking Water Act, it replaced an earlier standard measured at the treatment plant with monitoring at high-risk homes.

How it works

Because lead in drinking water comes chiefly from the corrosion of lead service lines, solder, and brass fixtures rather than from the source water, the rule is built around a treatment-technique requirement. Water systems sample first-draw water at taps in homes most likely to have lead plumbing. If the 90th-percentile result exceeds the action level — 15 parts per billion for lead under the 1991 rule — the system must take steps including corrosion-control treatment, public education, and lead service line replacement.

Revisions

Failures surrounding the Flint water crisis exposed weaknesses in sampling and enforcement. The 2021 Lead and Copper Rule Revisions adjusted sampling sites and required inventories of lead service lines. The 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements went further: they lowered the lead action level to 10 ppb and required most water systems to replace all lead service lines within ten years.
