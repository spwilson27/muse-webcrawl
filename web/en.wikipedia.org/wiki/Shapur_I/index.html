Shapur I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The rock relief of Shapur I triumphing over the emperors Philip and Valerian at Naqsh-e Rostam]

Shapur I (AD 215–270; Middle Persian: Šābuhr) was the second king of kings (shahanshah) of the Sasanian Empire, reigning c. 240/242–270, and the most successful of all Rome's Persian adversaries. Succeeding his father Ardashir I, founder of the dynasty, Shapur campaigned against Rome for three decades: he killed the emperor Gordian III in battle (by the Persian account, at Misiche, 244), took tribute from Philip the Arab, sacked Antioch twice, deported whole cities' populations into his new foundations in Khuzestan and Fars, and in 260 at Edessa captured the emperor Valerian alive — the supreme humiliation of Roman arms, carved in triumph on the cliff reliefs of Naqsh-e Rostam and Bishapur, where three emperors appear dead, kneeling and captive beneath his horse.

The Roman wars

Shapur's own inscription (the SKZ, on the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht at Naqsh-e Rostam, in three languages) is his autobiography in stone: 37 Roman cities taken in the first campaign, Syria ravaged, then the 260 campaign — Dura-Europos, Antioch again, Cappadocia — crowned by Valerian's capture with his army of (the inscription says) 70,000. Palmyra's Odaenathus checked the retreat and humiliated the Persian rear, the one reverse Shapur's inscription passes over. Captive Romans built his dams and cities (the Band-e Kaisar at Shushtar still bears Caesar's name); Valerian died in captivity, his fate (flayed skin displayed, in hostile Roman moralising) uncertain.

The empire builder

At home Shapur founded Bishapur and Gundeshapur, organised Zoroastrian orthodoxy under the priest Kartir (whose career began in this reign), patronised the prophet Mani — whose missionary journeys began under Shapur's protection — and received embassies from Rome to China. His titles ('King of Kings of Iran and non-Iran') announced the programme: Iran-shahr restored to mythic extent, Rome to be its tributary. No later Sasanian matched his record against the Romans; the reliefs he left remain Persia's answer to Rome's triumphal art.
