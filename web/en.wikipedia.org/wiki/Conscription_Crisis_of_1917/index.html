Conscription Crisis of 1917

[Image placeholder: Montreal anti-conscription protest, 1917]

The Conscription Crisis of 1917 (French: Crise de la conscription) was a political and military crisis in Canada during the First World War. It arose from widespread opposition, centred in French-speaking Quebec, to the Conservative government of Prime Minister Robert Borden's introduction of compulsory military service through the Military Service Act of 1917.

Background

Canada had entered the war in 1914 with a volunteer army, and Borden had promised no conscription. But by 1916 casualties had outstripped enlistment, and after visiting the front Borden concluded that compulsory service was necessary. In Quebec, opinion had long been alienated by Regulation 17 restricting French-language schooling in Ontario, and by the sense that Canada's war was Britain's war. French-Canadian leaders such as Henri Bourassa denounced conscription fiercely.

The crisis

To win the 1917 "Khaki election", Borden formed a Union Government with pro-conscription Liberals, while breaking with Wilfrid Laurier, and passed the Wartime Elections Act and Military Voters Act, which enfranchised soldiers and female relatives of servicemen while disenfranchising many recent immigrants. The Unionists won the election, but Quebec voted overwhelmingly for Laurier's Liberals, leaving the province almost unrepresented in government. Enforcement of the Act in 1918 sparked riots in Quebec City at Easter 1918, in which civilians were killed by soldiers. Only a limited number of conscripts reached the front before the armistice, but the crisis deepened the linguistic divide in Canadian politics for generations.
