Psi (parapsychology)

In parapsychology, psi is the unknown factor responsible for extrasensory perception (ESP) and psychokinesis (PK) — experiences that are not explained by known physical or biological mechanisms. The term was coined in 1942 by psychologists Robert Thouless and B. P. Wiesner, who chose the Greek letter psi (Ψ), the first letter of the Greek word psychē ("soul" or "mind"), partly because it was neutral and implied no particular mechanism.

Terminology

Thouless and Wiesner divided psi into psi-gamma, corresponding to ESP phenomena such as telepathy, clairvoyance and precognition, and psi-kappa, corresponding to psychokinesis — the supposed influence of mind on matter. Later writers have distinguished receptive psi from active psi, and "psi-missing", the tendency of some subjects to score significantly below chance, has also been interpreted by parapsychologists as an expression of psi.

Scientific status

Psi is a central concept of parapsychology, but after more than a century of research there is no generally accepted, replicable evidence for psi phenomena, and the field is considered a pseudoscience by the mainstream scientific community. Critics argue that positive experimental results — in card-guessing, Ganzfeld, and random-number-generator studies — are best explained by methodological flaws, selective reporting, and statistical issues, while parapsychologists maintain that meta-analyses show small but real effects and that the phenomena are inherently elusive. Because "psi" is defined largely by exclusion (anomalous information or energy transfer), it has no accepted theoretical mechanism within physics or neuroscience.
