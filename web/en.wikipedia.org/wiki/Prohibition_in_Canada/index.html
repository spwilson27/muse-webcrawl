Prohibition in Canada

Prohibition in Canada came about as a result of the temperance movement, and during the First World War as an effort to preserve grain for the war effort. Unlike the United States, which enacted a nationwide ban under the Eighteenth Amendment, Canada's prohibition was primarily provincial and relatively short-lived, with most provinces repealing their bans during the 1920s.

History

The Canada Temperance Act of 1878 allowed local governments to ban alcohol sales. During the First World War, most provinces introduced prohibition, and the federal government banned the production and importation of alcohol above 2.5% strength in 1918 as a wartime measure. After the war, provinces held plebiscites; Quebec rejected prohibition in 1919, and other provinces followed through the 1920s, replacing bans with government-controlled liquor sales. Prince Edward Island held out longest, until 1948.

Rum-running

Although domestic sale was restricted in much of Canada, production for export remained legal, and Canadian distillers and "rum-runners" supplied the much larger illicit market in the prohibition-era United States, smuggling liquor across the Great Lakes, the Detroit River, and the Atlantic coast.
