Bwindi Impenetrable National Park

Bwindi Impenetrable National Park is a national park in southwestern Uganda, on the edge of the Albertine Rift, the western arm of the East African Rift. Covering about 331 square kilometres (128 sq mi) of steep, densely forested mountains, it is one of the most biologically diverse areas in Africa and is best known for its population of mountain gorillas — around 450 or more, roughly half the world total. The park was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994.

Ecology

"Bwindi" derives from a local word meaning "impenetrable", reflecting the dense ground cover of herbs, vines, and shrubs beneath the forest canopy. The forest is a rare meeting of lowland and montane communities, with more than 160 tree species and over 100 fern species recorded. Its fauna includes roughly 120 mammal species — among them chimpanzees, L'Hoest's monkeys, elephants, and antelopes — more than 350 bird species including many Albertine Rift endemics, over 200 butterfly species, and numerous frogs and chameleons found nowhere else.

Mountain gorillas and tourism

Bwindi's mountain gorillas (Gorilla beringei beringei) live in numerous family groups, several of which are habituated to humans for tourism and research. Gorilla trekking from trailheads at Buhoma, Ruhija, Rushaga, and Nkuringo is Uganda's premier tourist attraction; permits are limited and revenue is shared with surrounding communities. The first group, Mubare, was opened to tourists in 1993.

People and conservation

The forest was historically home to Batwa pygmy hunter-gatherers, who were evicted when the reserve and park were created and remain marginalised; some now work as guides presenting Batwa culture. The park faces pressures from dense surrounding populations, poaching (mainly snares set for antelope), and disease transmission to gorillas, mitigated by veterinary programmes such as those of Conservation Through Public Health.
