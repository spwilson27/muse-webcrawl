Low-intensity conflict

Typical actorsStates, insurgents, militias, special forces
MethodsGuerrilla warfare, counterinsurgency, terrorism, subversion, peacekeeping

Low-intensity conflict (LIC) is a military conflict, usually localised, between two or more state or non-state groups which is below conventional war and above the routine, peaceful competition between states. It frequently involves protracted struggles of competing principles and ideologies and ranges from subversion and the use of armed force short of full-scale war to insurgency and counterinsurgency.

Definition and scope

The United States Department of Defense has defined low-intensity conflict as a political-military confrontation between contending states or groups below conventional war and above routine peaceful competition, often involving protracted struggles in which political, economic, informational and psychological instruments matter as much as military ones. Activities typically classed as LIC include insurgency and counterinsurgency, counterterrorism, peacekeeping, foreign internal defence, and support to civil authorities.

History

Although such warfare is ancient, the concept gained prominence during the Cold War, when superpower rivalry was channelled into proxy conflicts in Asia, Africa and Latin America. British experience in Malaya, French experience in Indochina and Algeria, and American experience in Vietnam all informed LIC doctrine. Later terms — asymmetric warfare, hybrid warfare, and "operations other than war" — overlap substantially with the concept.
