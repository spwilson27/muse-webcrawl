Umber is a natural brown earth pigment containing iron oxide and manganese oxide. It is darker than the related pigments ochre and sienna, and has been used as a colourant since prehistoric times. The name derives from terra d'ombra ("earth of shadow") in Italian, or possibly from Umbria, the Italian region from which it was historically obtained; the finest historical grades came from Cyprus.

Raw umber (left) and the warmer, darker burnt umber produced by heating it.

Variants

Raw umber is a yellowish-brown to greenish-brown pigment in its natural state. When heated (calcined), it becomes burnt umber, a richer, warmer reddish-brown widely used for shadows and underpainting ("imprimatura"). The manganese content makes umber a fast-drying pigment in oil paint and an effective drier.

History and use

Umber appears in cave painting and was used throughout antiquity and the Renaissance. It became a staple of the Baroque palette: Caravaggio and Rembrandt relied on umber-rich earth tones and shadow undermodelling (verdaccio-like techniques use umber mixtures). Umbers darken oil paint mixtures without the coolness of black, and are used in watercolour, gouache, and as a colorant for wood stain and ceramics. In the 20th century, synthetic iron oxides partly replaced natural umbers but the pigment remains in production.

Colour

As a colour name in English, "umber" was first recorded in the 16th century. The shade is a medium-dark, desaturated brown with a slightly greenish or greyish cast in the raw form.
