The bouzouki (Greek: μπουζούκι, plural bouzoukia) is a Greek musical instrument of the long-necked lute family, with a round body, a very long neck, and metal strings played with a plectrum. It is the central instrument of rebetiko, the urban Greek music of the early 20th century, and remains prominent in Greek laïko (popular) music. The instrument's name derives from the Turkish bozuk ("broken" or "modified"), referring to its altered tuning compared with the Turkish bağlama, from which it descends.

History

Long-necked lutes have existed in the Greek world since antiquity — the ancient pandura is a distant ancestor — but the modern bouzouki entered Greece with refugees and migrants from Anatolia, especially after the population exchange of 1923. In the hashish dens (tekedes) and working-class tavernas of Piraeus and Athens it became the voice of rebetiko, the music of the underclass manges. Early masters such as Markos Vamvakaris, who led the famed Piraeus Quartet in the 1930s, established the instrument's classic style.

After the Second World War the bouzouki moved from the margins to the mainstream of Greek music. Manolis Chiotis is credited with introducing the four-course (tetrachordo) instrument and popularising faster, more virtuosic playing, while composers such as Mikis Theodorakis and Manos Hadjidakis brought the bouzouki sound to film scores and concert halls — Theodorakis's music for Zorba the Greek (1964) made the instrument famous worldwide. Virtuosos such as Vassilis Tsitsanis (primarily a composer-player) and Giorgos Zampetas defined its postwar idiom.

Types and tunings

The traditional trichordo has three pairs (courses) of strings, tuned D–A–D, and is associated with the older rebetiko style and its modal improvisations (taximia). The tetrachordo, developed in the 1950s, adds a fourth course and is tuned C–F–A–D, facilitating guitar-like chord shapes and faster melodic runs. The body is built of wooden staves, the soundboard often decorated with elaborate inlay, and the scale length is long — roughly 66–69 cm — giving the instrument its bright, ringing sustain.

Smaller relatives include the baglamas (a miniature, high-pitched bouzouki, small enough to be concealed from the police in the rebetiko era) and the tzouras, intermediate in size.

Playing and repertoire

The bouzouki carries both melody and rhythmic chordal accompaniment in Greek ensembles, which typically combine it with guitar, baglamas, accordion, and violin. Its repertoire includes the improvised taximi introductions, zeibekiko dances in 9/4 time, hasapiko dances, and the vast song catalogue of rebetiko and laïko. Distinctive techniques include rapid tremolo picking, open-string drones, and turns and grace notes borrowed from Byzantine and Anatolian modal practice (the dromoi, or "roads," corresponding to Ottoman makam modes).

Irish bouzouki

In the late 1960s and 1970s Irish folk musicians — notably Johnny Moynihan, Dónal Lunny, and Andy Irvine — adopted the Greek instrument, adapting it with a flatter back, guitar-style body, and tunings such as G–D–A–D for accompanying Irish traditional music. The Irish bouzouki, usually built by luthiers with a teardrop or guitar-shaped body, became a standard rhythm instrument in bands such as Planxty and The Bothy Band and spread into folk traditions internationally.
