Collective intelligence (CI) is shared or group intelligence that emerges from the collaboration, collective efforts and competition of many individuals and appears in consensus decision making. The term appears in studies of animal behaviour — such as the coordinated movement of ant colonies, bird flocks and fish schools — and, increasingly, in research on human groups and human–computer networks.

Human collective intelligence

Research at institutions such as MIT's Center for Collective Intelligence examines how groups of people and computers can be connected so that they act more intelligently than any individual alone. A well-known finding is the "c factor": groups display a general collective intelligence, measured by performance across many tasks, that correlates more strongly with members' social sensitivity and equal conversational turn-taking than with the average or maximum individual IQ. James Surowiecki's The Wisdom of Crowds (2004) argued that diverse, independent groups can outperform experts under the right conditions.

Applications

Applications include collaborative encyclopaedias such as Wikipedia, open-source software, citizen science projects, prediction markets, deliberative democracy platforms and crowdsourcing of problem solving. Digital networks dramatically lower the cost of aggregating contributions, though they also introduce risks such as groupthink, cascading errors and manipulation.
