The Great Attractor is a region of gravitational attraction in intergalactic space and the apparent central gravitational point of the Laniakea Supercluster of galaxies that includes the Milky Way, as well as about 100,000 other galaxies. The observed attraction suggests a localized concentration of mass on the order of 1016 solar masses. However, it is obscured by the Milky Way's galactic plane, lying in the Zone of Avoidance, so direct observation in visible light is difficult; X-ray and radio surveys have revealed the rich Norma Cluster (ACO 3627) near its centre.

The anomaly was inferred in the 1970s and 1980s from peculiar velocities: galaxies in the local volume, including the Milky Way and the rest of the Local Group, are streaming at roughly 600 km/s toward the Centaurus–Norma region beyond what the uniform expansion of the universe (the Hubble flow) would predict. The "Seven Samurai" astronomers' surveys of elliptical galaxy peculiar motions publicized the Great Attractor in 1986–87.

Explanation and larger structure

The pull is now understood to arise from the combined mass of the Norma and Centaurus superclusters and the surrounding large-scale structure, with a further contribution from the even more massive Shapley Supercluster lying beyond; modern flow models treat the Great Attractor as the basin centre of the Laniakea Supercluster, defined in 2014 by R. Brent Tully and collaborators. Its position is roughly 200–250 million light-years away, near the Norma Cluster, in the direction of the constellations Norma and Centaurus.
