Palimpsest

Palimpsest
EtymologyGreek palímpsēstos, "scraped again"
MaterialsParchment (vellum), occasionally papyrus or wax tablets
Famous examplesArchimedes Palimpsest; Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus; Codex Nitriensis

A palimpsest is a manuscript page, scroll, or writing surface from which earlier text has been scraped or washed off so that the material could be reused for new writing. Because parchment was expensive and labour-intensive to produce, the practice was widespread in late antiquity and the Middle Ages. The word comes from the Greek palímpsēstos (παλίμψηστος), "scraped again". Palimpsests are of great scholarly importance because the erased undertext often preserves otherwise lost works of ancient literature, science, and scripture.

Production and survival

To prepare a palimpsest, scribes scraped the surface of the parchment leaf with a pumice stone or knife, or washed it with milk or other agents, then wrote the new text — sometimes rotating the page so the new lines ran across the old. Traces of the original iron-gall ink often survive in the parchment. The undertexts of surviving palimpsests date largely from the 4th to 8th centuries, overwritten in the 7th to 13th centuries. Many palimpsests are preserved in monastic libraries such as those of St Catherine's Monastery in Sinai, the Vatican Library, and the monastery of St Gall.

Famous palimpsests

The most celebrated example is the Archimedes Palimpsest: a 13th-century Byzantine prayer book written over erased 10th-century copies of works by Archimedes, including the unique surviving texts of The Method of Mechanical Theorems and the Stomachion. The Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus, a 5th-century Greek Bible overwritten with sermons of Ephrem the Syrian, is a major witness to the biblical text. Other significant finds include Cicero's De re publica beneath a commentary on the Psalms, works of Fronto, and early legal and liturgical texts. Angelo Mai pioneered the chemical recovery of undertexts in the early 19th century, though his reagents sometimes damaged manuscripts.

Modern recovery techniques

Since the 20th century, scholars have used ultraviolet photography to make faded underwriting legible. Modern multispectral imaging — photographing leaves under many wavelengths of light and processing the images statistically — has dramatically expanded what can be read, and X-ray fluorescence imaging has been used to reveal the iron in inks hidden beneath later text or bindings.

Figurative use

The word is used metaphorically for anything that bears layered traces of earlier forms: landscapes, cities, memories, and artworks are all described as palimpsests. The figurative sense entered wide currency in the 19th century, notably through Thomas De Quincey's essay "The Palimpsest" (1845).
