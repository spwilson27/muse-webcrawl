The Gadsden Purchase (Spanish: Venta de La Mesilla, "the Sale of La Mesilla") was an 1853–1854 agreement between the United States and Mexico in which the United States paid Mexico $10 million for 29,640 square miles (76,800 km²) of land in what is now southern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico. It was the last major territorial acquisition by the United States in the contiguous United States.

Background

After the Mexican–American War and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), disputes remained over the exact location of the border in the Mesilla Valley. American officials, including President Franklin Pierce's administration, also wanted the land to build a southern transcontinental railroad route that would avoid snow and difficult mountain passes. James Gadsden, a railroad promoter and the U.S. minister to Mexico, was sent to negotiate with Mexican president Antonio López de Santa Anna.

Treaty terms

The treaty was signed on December 30, 1853. The U.S. Senate reduced the amount of land and the payment from Gadsden's original draft before ratifying it in 1854. Mexico, financially strained, accepted $10 million. The purchase fixed the present-day border between the two countries west of El Paso, Texas.

Aftermath

The acquired territory was initially attached to New Mexico Territory; much of it became part of Arizona when Arizona Territory was organized in 1863. The Southern Pacific Railroad eventually built its southern route through the region in the early 1880s. The purchase was controversial in both countries: many Mexicans viewed it as a further national humiliation, contributing to Santa Anna's fall, while in the United States it was entangled in sectional debates over the expansion of slavery.
