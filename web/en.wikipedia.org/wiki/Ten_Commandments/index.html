The Ten Commandments (Biblical Hebrew: עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים, "the ten words"; also called the Decalogue) are a set of biblical principles relating to ethics and worship that play a fundamental role in Judaism and Christianity. According to the Book of Exodus, God inscribed them on two stone tablets and gave them to Moses on Mount Sinai after the Israelites' escape from Egypt. They appear twice in the Hebrew Bible, in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, with slight variations in wording.

The commandments

The text enjoins worship of God alone; prohibits idolatry and misuse of God's name; commands observance of the Sabbath and honouring one's parents; and prohibits murder, adultery, theft, false testimony and coveting. Traditions differ in how the text is divided into ten: Jewish counting treats "I am the LORD your God" as the first word and combines the coveting prohibitions; most Orthodox and Reformed churches separate the prohibition of other gods from that of idols; Catholic and Lutheran counting combines those and divides the coveting clause into two (against a neighbour's wife and goods).

Interpretation and influence

The commandments are embedded in the covenant narrative and elaborated in later law codes; scholars note parallels with ancient Near Eastern treaty conventions. In Judaism they are recited liturgically and traditionally said to comprise, with the rest of the Torah, 613 commandments. In Christianity their standing is debated between covenant theologies, though they remain a standard summary of moral teaching in catechisms. In modern times, displays of the commandments on public buildings — especially in the United States — have generated church–state litigation, including the Supreme Court cases Stone v. Graham (1980) and Van Orden v. Perry (2005).
