Domino theory

Domino theory
EraCold War, 1950s–1980s
Popularised byPresident Dwight D. Eisenhower, 7 April 1954
Areas appliedSoutheast Asia; later Latin America and the Middle East

The domino theory was a geopolitical theory that a political event in one country would cause similar events in neighbouring countries, like a falling domino causing an entire row of dominoes to topple. Prominent in United States foreign policy during the Cold War, it held that if one country in a region came under communist influence or control, the surrounding countries would follow.

History

President Dwight D. Eisenhower articulated the "falling domino" principle at a press conference on 7 April 1954, in reference to Indochina: if Vietnam fell to communism, he argued, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Burma, Malaya, and Indonesia might follow, with strategic consequences reaching Japan, the Philippines, Australia, and New Zealand. The theory underpinned U.S. support for South Vietnam and was invoked by the Kennedy, Johnson, and Nixon administrations to justify escalation in the Vietnam War.

Assessment

After Saigon fell in 1975, Laos and Cambodia did become communist, but the wider cascade did not occur: Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia remained non-communist, and Vietnam soon fought wars with Cambodia and China. Historians debate whether the domino theory was ever valid — some argue U.S. intervention bought time for other Southeast Asian states to consolidate; critics contend the theory misunderstood the nationalist, rather than monolithically communist, character of the region's movements.
