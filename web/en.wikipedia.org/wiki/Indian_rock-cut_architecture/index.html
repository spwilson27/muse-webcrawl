Indian rock-cut architecture

Indian rock-cut architecture is a diverse set of architectural traditions in which temples, monasteries, halls and dwellings were carved directly out of solid natural rock. India has more than 1,500 rock-cut structures, most created between the 3rd century BCE and the 10th century CE by Buddhist, Hindu and Jain patrons.

Development

The earliest surviving examples are the Barabar Caves in Bihar, dedicated by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka to the Ajivika sect. Buddhist cave complexes (chaitya prayer halls and vihara monasteries) followed along trade routes in the Western Ghats at sites such as Bhaja, Karla, Kanheri and Ajanta, the latter famous for its paintings. Later Hindu and Jain excavation culminated in the great monolithic temples of Ellora — including the Kailasa temple, excavated top-down from a single rock under the Rashtrakutas — and the cave temples of Elephanta, Badami and Mahabalipuram.

Technique

Unlike built masonry, rock-cut structures were subtractive: craftsmen excavated from the top down, imitating wooden and stone structural forms in the living rock.
