Slieve Gullion

Slieve Gullion (Irish: Sliabh gCuillinn, "mountain of the steep slope" or "mountain of the holly") is a mountain in the south of County Armagh, Northern Ireland, rising to 573 metres (1,880 ft). It is the eroded remnant of a volcanic complex and the centrepiece of the Ring of Gullion, a distinctive ring of low hills (a ring dyke) surrounding the mountain, designated an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty. The mountain is steeped in Irish mythology.

Geology

Slieve Gullion is part of a Palaeogene volcanic centre, active about 60 million years ago during the opening of the North Atlantic. The Ring of Gullion is one of the earliest ring dykes described in geological literature — a roughly circular intrusion formed as magma filled a ring-shaped fracture above a subsiding volcanic caldera. The mountain's core is a layered intrusion of granophyre and gabbro.

Archaeology and mythology

On the summit stands the highest surviving passage tomb in Ireland, the South Cairn (often called the Calliagh Berra's House), dating to the Neolithic; its passage is aligned so that the setting sun at the winter solstice illuminates its chamber. A smaller cairn, the North Cairn, is of Bronze Age date. Beside them lies a small lake, the Calliagh Berra's Lough.

In Irish mythology, Slieve Gullion is associated with the witch-hag Cailleach Bhéarra, who according to legend tricked the hero Fionn mac Cumhaill into diving into the summit lake, from which he emerged an old man with white hair. The mountain also features in the Táin Bó Cúailnge cycle: the hero Cú Chulainn received his name and his weapons in episodes connected with the area, and defended the nearby Gap of the North.

Slieve Gullion Forest Park

The western slopes host Slieve Gullion Forest Park, with a scenic drive, walking trails and the popular "Giant's Lair" children's trail and adventure play park, drawing on the area's legends. Paths from the forest park lead to the summit cairns, from which there are views across the ring dyke, the Mourne Mountains, Carlingford Lough and, on clear days, as far as the Wicklow Mountains.
