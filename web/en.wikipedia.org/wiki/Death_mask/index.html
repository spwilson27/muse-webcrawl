A death mask is a likeness of a person's face, usually made by taking a cast or impression from the corpse shortly after death. Death masks are mementos of the dead and were traditionally used to create portraits and sculptures of deceased rulers, artists, and other notable figures. In many cultures, related funerary masks were placed on the dead as part of burial rites, from the gold mask of Tutankhamun in ancient Egypt to the Roman imagines of ancestors.

History

Funerary masks date to antiquity; the Mycenaean "Mask of Agamemnon" (c. 1550 BC) and Egyptian mummy masks are famous examples. In medieval and early modern Europe, death masks formed part of the funeral effigies of royalty. From the 18th century, plaster death masks of famous people—composers, writers, statesmen—were widely collected and displayed. Noted examples include the masks of Ludwig van Beethoven, Napoleon Bonaparte, Abraham Lincoln (from life and death casts), and John Keats.

Notable examples

L'Inconnue de la Seine, the mask of an unidentified young woman pulled from the River Seine in Paris in the late 19th century, became a celebrated artistic icon, reproduced in countless homes and later used as the face of the Resusci Annie CPR training manikin. The mask of the outlaw Ned Kelly and the death mask of Oliver Cromwell—which was displayed for centuries and whose authenticity illustrates the difficulty of verifying such relics—are also well known.

Making a death mask

Traditionally, the face was coated with oil or grease, plaster bandages or plaster of Paris applied to form a mould, and from that mould a positive cast in plaster, wax, or bronze produced. Hair and eyelashes could become embedded in the plaster, and the weight of the material could distort soft features, so surviving masks are not perfectly reliable records of appearance. Photography largely replaced the practice in the 20th century.
