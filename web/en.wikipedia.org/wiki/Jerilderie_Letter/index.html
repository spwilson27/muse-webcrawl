The Jerilderie Letter, Ned Kelly's manifesto, dictated during the Jerilderie raid of 1879.

The Jerilderie Letter is a document of some 8,300 words dictated by the Australian bushranger Ned Kelly to his gang member Joe Byrne in February 1879, during the Kelly gang's occupation of the town of Jerilderie, New South Wales. Part autobiography, part political manifesto, the letter sets out Kelly's account of his life and grievances against the police and the colonial establishment, and is regarded as one of the most remarkable documents in Australian history — a visionary, violent statement that has been compared to a founding text of Australian identity.

Background and contents

Kelly had earlier dictated a shorter statement, the so-called Cameron Letter (December 1878), addressed to a member of the Victorian parliament. At Jerilderie he attempted to have a much longer letter printed and distributed: after robbing the town's bank, he entrusted the manuscript to Edwin Living, a bank accountant, for delivery to a newspaper editor, but it was instead handed to the authorities and suppressed; it was not published in full until decades later.

In the letter Kelly defends his family's record, denounces the police as corrupt and cowardly, recounts episodes such as the Stringybark Creek killings from his point of view, warns his enemies of retribution, and speaks for impoverished Irish-Australian selectors against wealthy squatters and officials. It ends with a storm of threats and curses against the Queen's representatives in the colony.

Style and significance

The letter is written in an energetic vernacular style, full of metaphor and shifts of tense and voice, and has been studied by literary critics — Peter Carey drew directly on its voice for his Booker Prize-winning novel True History of the Kelly Gang (2000). Historians value it as a rare extended statement from a working-class colonial Australian and as evidence in the long-running debate over whether Kelly was a criminal, a social bandit, or a would-be revolutionary.

Provenance

The original manuscript, written out by Byrne, passed through official hands and eventually to the State Library of Victoria, which acquired it in 1960s; pages are held principally by the State Library, with a related draft (the "Kelly Letter" of 1879 drafts) also in public collections. Facsimiles and transcripts are widely reproduced in editions of Kelly documents.
