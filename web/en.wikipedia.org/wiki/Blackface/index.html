Blackface

19th-century minstrel shows popularized blackface entertainment in the United States

Blackface is the practice of performers darkening their skin with makeup to portray a caricature of a Black person. It is most associated with the minstrel shows of the 19th- and early 20th-century United States, in which white performers in blackface enacted demeaning stereotypes of African Americans for white audiences. Blackface is now widely considered deeply offensive and racist, and public controversies continue to arise when it appears in entertainment, festivals, and politics in the United States and other countries.

Minstrel shows

Blackface performance emerged in the 1830s. Performer Thomas D. Rice popularized the song-and-dance character "Jim Crow," and by the 1840s minstrel troupes such as the Virginia Minstrels and Christy's Minstrels were a mass entertainment industry. Stock characters included the happy-go-lucky slave, the dandified "Zip Coon," and the "Mammy." Minstrelsy spread American popular music — Stephen Foster wrote many of his songs for minstrel shows — while embedding racial caricatures in the culture. After the Civil War, African American performers also worked in minstrelsy, often themselves wearing blackface, partly subverting and partly constrained by its conventions.

20th century

Blackface persisted in vaudeville, early film, and radio. Al Jolson performed in blackface in The Jazz Singer (1927), the first feature-length "talkie." The radio (and later television) comedy Amos 'n' Andy, created by white actors imitating Black speech, became a national phenomenon before the NAACP campaigned against it. In Britain, The Black and White Minstrel Show ran on BBC television until 1978 despite protests. By the later 20th century blackface had become broadly unacceptable in mainstream American entertainment.

Modern controversies

Revelations of blackface in yearbook photos and at parties have damaged the careers of politicians and public figures in the United States and Canada, and Halloween costumes, sporting-event traditions, and festival figures (such as the Dutch Zwarte Piet) remain contested internationally. Defenders sometimes cite tradition or intent, while critics argue the practice's history of caricature and mockery makes it inherently degrading regardless of intent.

Related practices

Related forms include "yellowface" and "redface" casting of white actors as Asian or Native American characters, and the use of brownface. All have drawn similar criticism as casting practices in film and theatre have diversified.
