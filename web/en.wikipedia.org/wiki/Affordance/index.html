Affordance
Introduced byJames J. Gibson (1966; developed in The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception, 1979)
FieldEcological psychology; later design and HCI
Popularised in design byDon Norman, The Psychology of Everyday Things (1988)

An affordance is a relationship between the properties of an object or environment and the capabilities of an agent, which determines how the object could possibly be used. The term was coined by the perceptual psychologist James J. Gibson: a chair, for a human, affords sitting; a horizontal surface at knee height affords support; water affords drinking to some animals and drowning to others. For Gibson, affordances are properties of the environment taken relative to the animal, and they are perceived directly, without needing inference from sensory data.

Gibson's concept

Gibson introduced the term in The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems (1966) and developed it in The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979). Affordances are neither purely objective nor purely subjective: a step affords climbing only relative to a creature with suitable legs, yet it does so whether or not the creature notices. The theory is central to ecological psychology and has influenced embodied cognition and robotics.

In design

Don Norman adopted the word in The Psychology of Everyday Things (1988) for the perceived action possibilities of designed objects — a plate on a door affords pushing, a handle affords pulling — and it became a foundational concept in human–computer interaction and user-experience design. Norman later clarified that design usually deals in perceived affordances and "signifiers" (cues communicating where an action is possible), which may differ from the real affordances Gibson meant. HCI researchers, notably William Gaver, further distinguished perceptible, hidden and false affordances.
