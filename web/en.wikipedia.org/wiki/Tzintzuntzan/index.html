Tzintzuntzan

Tzintzuntzan (Purépecha: "place of the hummingbirds") is a municipality and archaeological site in the modern Mexican state of Michoacán, on the northeastern shore of Lake Pátzcuaro. In the Late Postclassic period it was the capital of the Tarascan (Purépecha) state, the second-largest empire in Mesoamerica and the principal rival that repeatedly resisted Aztec expansion.

History

The Purépecha state consolidated during the 14th and 15th centuries under rulers known as the cazonci. After an earlier capital at Pátzcuaro and a period of tripartite rule with Ihuatzio, Tzintzuntzan became the empire's dominant capital, probably around the mid-15th century. At its height the city may have held 25,000–35,000 inhabitants, governing a territory of some 75,000 square kilometres around Lake Pátzcuaro.

Archaeology

The site's most distinctive features are the Yácatas — five semicircular stepped pyramids set upon a huge rectangular platform roughly 400 metres long, overlooking the lake. This combination of rectangular base and round towers is unique in Mesoamerican architecture. Excavations have also uncovered elite residences, workshops, and evidence of the Tarascans' famed metallurgy in copper and bronze.

Spanish conquest and modern town

The last cazonci, Tangáxuan II, submitted to Spanish forces in 1530 but was subsequently executed by Nuño de Guzmán. The modern town grew around a Franciscan mission founded in the 16th century, whose convent complex — including an open chapel and some of Mexico's oldest olive trees — still stands. Today Tzintzuntzan is a pueblo mágico known for pottery, straw crafts, and Day of the Dead observances around Lake Pátzcuaro.
