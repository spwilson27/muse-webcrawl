Sarnoff's law


Named after: David Sarnoff
Field: Network theory, media economics

Sarnoff's law states that the value of a broadcast network is proportional to the number of viewers. It is attributed to David Sarnoff, the pioneering broadcasting executive who built RCA and NBC. Unlike Metcalfe's law, in which the value of a network grows with the square of the number of users, Sarnoff's law describes a one-to-many network: a single broadcaster transmits to an audience, so value grows only linearly with audience size (V ∝ n).

Context

Sarnoff's law is often cited in network theory as the earliest and simplest of the network-value laws, contrasting with:

  • Metcalfe's law — value proportional to n², for many-to-many connection networks such as telephones and the Internet;
  • Reed's law — value proportional to 2ⁿ, for group-forming networks.

The law captures the economics of twentieth-century radio and television broadcasting, in which revenue scaled with the number of listeners or viewers reached by a single transmitter or channel.
