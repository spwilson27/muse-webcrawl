Mollusk

Mollusks (phylum Mollusca) are a large and diverse group of invertebrate animals, including snails, slugs, clams, oysters, squids, and octopuses. With roughly 85,000–100,000 recognized living species, they are among the largest animal phyla in number of described marine species. Most mollusks live in the sea, but many gastropods and bivalves inhabit fresh water, and gastropods are the only mollusks that have colonized land.

Anatomy

Despite their diversity, mollusks share a basic body plan: a soft body consisting of a muscular foot used for locomotion, a visceral mass containing the organs, and a mantle — a fold of tissue that may secrete a calcium carbonate shell. Many mollusks feed with a radula, a ribbon-like structure bearing rows of tiny teeth, though bivalves lack one and instead filter-feed. The mantle cavity houses the gills (ctenidia) in aquatic species.

Classification

Living mollusks are divided into classes including Gastropoda (snails and slugs, the largest class), Bivalvia (clams, oysters, mussels, scallops), Cephalopoda (squids, octopuses, cuttlefish, nautiluses), Polyplacophora (chitons), and Scaphopoda (tusk shells). Cephalopods are noted for their intelligence and complex nervous systems.

Human use

Mollusks are an important food source worldwide, and shells have historically served as money, jewelry, and decoration; pearls come from certain bivalves. Some mollusks are agricultural or ecological pests, and some serve as intermediate hosts for parasites. See Gastropoda and Cephalopod.
