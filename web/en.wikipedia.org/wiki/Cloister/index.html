Cloister

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cloister (from Latin claustrum, "enclosure") is a covered walk, open gallery, or open arcade running along the walls of buildings and forming a quadrangle or garth. Cloisters are a defining feature of monasteries, convents, and cathedral closes, providing a sheltered, contemplative space connecting the church, chapter house, refectory, and dormitory.

The cloister of Monreale Cathedral in Sicily, with its richly carved paired columns.

History

The form derives from the Roman peristyle courtyard and the early Christian atrium. The Plan of Saint Gall (9th century) already shows the cloister as the organising heart of the ideal Benedictine monastery, placed on the south side of the church where it caught the sun. Romanesque and Gothic cloisters, often with elaborately carved capitals illustrating biblical scenes, served for processions, reading, teaching (the cloister school), copying of manuscripts, and the daily ritual of the mandatum (foot-washing).

Architecture

A typical cloister consists of four walks around an open court (the garth), which might contain a garden, a well, and the monks' burial ground. Arcades face inward, traditionally with paired columns in Romanesque examples and traceried openings in Gothic ones. The east walk usually gave access to the chapter house and the warming room; the south walk ran along the refectory and often contained the lavatorium where monks washed before meals.

Figurative use

Because cloisters enclosed religious communities from the world, "cloistered" became a general adjective for secluded, sheltered, or withdrawn lives and places, and "cloister" a verb meaning to seclude.
