Ebenezer Howard, founder of the garden city movement

Sir Ebenezer Howard (29 January 1850 – 1 May 1928) was an English urban planner and social reformer, best known as the founder of the garden city movement. His book To-Morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform (1898), reissued as Garden Cities of To-Morrow (1902), proposed self-contained satellite communities combining the advantages of town and country, and led directly to the founding of Letchworth and Welwyn Garden City in Hertfordshire.

Life

Howard was born in the City of London, the son of a baker and confectioner. After clerking in the City he emigrated to America in 1871, trying farming in Nebraska and then working as a shorthand reporter in Chicago, where he encountered the city's parks and the rebuilding after the Great Fire. Returning to London in 1876, he worked for the rest of his career as a parliamentary stenographer (with the firm that became Hansard reporters). He read widely in social reform literature — the utopian novel Looking Backward by Edward Bellamy was a major influence — and in 1898 published his proposals.

The garden city idea

Howard's famous "three magnets" diagram contrasted the attractions and disadvantages of Town and Country, proposing a third magnet, "Town-Country", combining high wages and social opportunity with fresh air, low rents and natural beauty. A garden city would be built on a cooperatively purchased estate of about 6,000 acres, housing some 32,000 people on 1,000 acres, ringed by a permanent agricultural green belt. Land would be held in trust for the community: rents would service the purchase debt and thereafter fund municipal services and pensions, capturing rising land values for residents rather than landlords. When full, a garden city would not sprawl but spawn a new satellite, linked by rail — a polycentric "Social City".

Letchworth and Welwyn

The Garden City Association (founded 1899) bought an estate at Letchworth in 1903, planned by Barry Parker and Raymond Unwin in the Arts and Crafts manner. After the First World War Howard himself, aged 69, founded Welwyn Garden City (1919), buying the land at auction and living there for the rest of his life. He was knighted in 1927 and died at Welwyn in 1928.

Legacy

Howard's ideas profoundly shaped twentieth-century planning: the British New Towns programme after the Town and Country Planning Act 1947, garden suburbs worldwide, and the green belt policies of London and other cities all descend in part from his model, as do the profession of town planning itself (the Town and Country Planning Association traces to his Garden City Association).
