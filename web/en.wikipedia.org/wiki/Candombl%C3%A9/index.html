Candomblé is an African diasporic religion that developed in Brazil during the 19th century. It arose through the blending of the traditional religions brought by enslaved West and Central Africans — principally Yoruba, Fon and Bantu traditions — in the context of the Portuguese colonial slave trade.

Beliefs and practice

Practitioners worship deities known as orixás (in the Ketu nation), voduns (Jeje) or nkisis (Angola), each associated with natural forces and identified, under colonial pressure, with Catholic saints. Ritual life centres on the terreiro (temple), led by a priest (babalorixá) or priestess (ialorixá). Ceremonies involve drumming, song in African languages, offerings, and possession trance in which the deity is believed to mount the initiate.

History

Candomblé was persecuted under slavery and into the twentieth century, when temples required police permits; it is now recognised as part of Brazil's cultural heritage. The historic Terreiro da Casa Branca in Salvador, Bahia, among the oldest continuously operating temples, was founded in the nineteenth century and is a listed heritage site.
