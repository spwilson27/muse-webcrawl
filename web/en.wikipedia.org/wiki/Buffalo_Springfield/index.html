Buffalo Springfield was a Canadian-American rock band formed in Los Angeles in 1966 by Canadians Neil Young and Bruce Palmer and Americans Stephen Stills, Richie Furay, and Dewey Martin. The band is best known for the song "For What It's Worth", written by Stills, which became an anthem of 1960s counterculture and protest. Though active for barely two years and commercially modest in their day, Buffalo Springfield were pioneers of folk rock and country rock, and launched the careers of Young, Stills, and Furay. They were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1997.

History

The band formed after Young and Palmer, driving to Los Angeles in a hearse, chanced upon Stills and Furay stuck in traffic on Sunset Boulevard. Taking their name from a steamroller manufactured by the Buffalo-Springfield Roller Company, the group quickly landed a residency at the Whisky a Go Go and signed with Atlantic's Atco label.

Their debut album, Buffalo Springfield (1966), included "For What It's Worth", written after the Sunset Strip curfew riots of November 1966; it reached the top ten in 1967. Internal conflict, drug busts, and Palmer's deportation troubles destabilised the group. Two further albums followed — Buffalo Springfield Again (1967), containing Young's orchestral "Expecting to Fly" and Stills's "Bluebird", and the posthumous Last Time Around (1968) — before the band dissolved in May 1968.

Legacy

After the split, Stills formed Crosby, Stills & Nash, Furay co-founded Poco, and Young launched a solo career before joining CSN&Y. The surviving core members reunited in 2010, performing at the Bridge School Benefit and touring in 2011, though a planned fuller reunion did not continue.
