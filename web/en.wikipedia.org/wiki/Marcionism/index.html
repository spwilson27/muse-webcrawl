Marcionism

Marcionism was an early Christian dualistic belief system that originated in the teachings of Marcion of Sinope at Rome around 144 AD. Marcion taught that the God of the Old Testament was a different, inferior being — the Demiurge, a jealous and wrathful creator — from the unknown, loving Father God who sent Jesus Christ. Accordingly he rejected the Hebrew scriptures entirely and held that his own canon, consisting of an edited Gospel (the Evangelikon, a version of Luke) and ten Pauline epistles (the Apostolikon), was the true Christian scripture.

Teachings

For Marcion, the creator god of the Jews was just but harsh, bound by his own law, whereas the true God was pure love and mercy, previously unknown to the world. Jesus, in consequence, was not born of Mary in the ordinary way but appeared suddenly, fully grown, in the synagogue at Capernaum in the reign of Tiberius; his body was a phantasm rather than real flesh (a docetic Christology). Salvation came by faith in this God alone, not by works of the law. Marcionites practised rigorous asceticism, including celibacy and abstinence from meat and wine.

Influence and decline

Marcion was excommunicated by the Roman church in 144 AD and responded by building a well-organised rival church that spread across the Roman Empire and beyond, surviving especially in Syria and Mesopotamia for centuries. The proto-orthodox reaction to Marcion — notably Tertullian's five books Against Marcion — spurred the formation of the New Testament canon, the creeds' insistence that the God of creation and the Father of Jesus Christ are one, and the rule of faith. Adolf von Harnack famously argued that Marcion was the first to conceive of a closed Christian Bible. Marcionite communities faded by the fifth century in the West, though remnants lingered longer in the East.
