Parallel editing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parallel editing is a film editing technique in which two or more scenes are intercut with one another, implying that the action in the scenes is occurring simultaneously, or creating thematic links between storylines. When the intercutting occurs between simultaneous events, it is commonly known as cross-cutting. Parallel editing allows filmmakers to build suspense, compress time, contrast characters or settings, and converge separate plot threads at a climax.

History

The technique was pioneered in early narrative cinema. Edwin S. Porter's The Great Train Robbery (1903) contained early parallel action, and D. W. Griffith systematized cross-cutting in films such as The Lonely Villa (1909) and the multi-strand epic Intolerance (1916), which interweaves four stories set in different historical periods. Soviet montage theorists of the 1920s, including Lev Kuleshov and Sergei Eisenstein, examined how juxtaposing shots generates meaning beyond the shots themselves.

Uses

  • Suspense: alternating between a victim and an approaching threat, or a rescuer racing to arrive in time ("last-minute rescue")
  • Chase sequences: cutting between pursuer and pursued
  • Convergence: independent storylines gradually intercut more rapidly as they meet
  • Contrast: juxtaposing wealth and poverty, or parallel actions by different characters (as in the baptism/murder sequence of The Godfather, 1972)
  • Telephone conversations and split-screen alternatives

Related techniques

Parallel editing is related to, but distinct from, the use of flashbacks (which shift time rather than alternate between simultaneous threads) and montage sequences. In literary fiction, alternating chapters between viewpoints achieves a comparable effect.
