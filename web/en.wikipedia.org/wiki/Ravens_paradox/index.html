Raven paradox


A black raven — and, paradoxically, a green apple — as evidence about ravens.

The raven paradox, also known as Hempel's paradox, is a paradox arising from the logic of scientific confirmation, proposed by German logician Carl Gustav Hempel in the 1940s. It concerns the hypothesis "All ravens are black" and what counts as evidence for it.

The paradox

The hypothesis "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." Observing a black raven confirms the first statement; by equivalence, observing a non-black non-raven — for example, a green apple — should confirm the second, and thus the original hypothesis. Yet it seems absurd that seeing a green apple provides evidence that all ravens are black.

Proposed resolutions

Bayesian accounts hold that the green apple does confirm the hypothesis, but only by a vanishingly small amount, because non-black non-ravens vastly outnumber ravens. Other responses reject the equivalence condition, appeal to background knowledge, or argue that confirmation is contrastive. The paradox remains a standard test case in the philosophy of science.
