Magneto

A magneto is an electrical generator that uses permanent magnets to produce periodic pulses of alternating current, in contrast to a dynamo or alternator that uses field coils and produces continuous output. Magnetos are historically important as ignition systems for internal combustion engines, where they generate the high-voltage pulse needed to fire spark plugs without requiring a battery, and in early telephony, where hand-cranked magnetos produced the ringing signal on party lines.


An aircraft ignition magneto showing its gear drive and ignition leads.

Operation

The basic magneto consists of a permanent magnet and an armature wound with a coil. Rotating the magnet relative to the coil (or the coil relative to the magnet) produces a changing magnetic flux that induces an alternating current. In the classic ignition magneto, the armature carries a primary winding of few turns of heavy wire and a secondary winding of many thousands of turns of fine wire. A contact breaker interrupts the primary current at the optimum instant, and the resulting collapse of the magnetic field induces a high-voltage pulse — often 10,000 to 20,000 volts — in the secondary winding, which is delivered to the spark plug.

Ignition magnetos

Ignition magnetos were standard on early automobiles, tractors, motorcycles, and aircraft engines. In aviation, dual magneto ignition systems remain in widespread use on piston engines because they are self-contained: the engine continues to run even if the aircraft's electrical system fails, and having two spark plugs per cylinder fired by separate magnetos improves reliability and combustion.

In small engines — chainsaws, lawn mowers, and outboard motors — a simple flywheel magneto, in which magnets embedded in the flywheel pass an ignition coil mounted on the crankcase, provides the spark. Modern variants use electronic triggering (capacitor discharge ignition) instead of contact breaker points.

Telephone magnetos

In manual telephone systems, a hand-cranked magneto in each subscriber's telephone set generated an alternating current of about 75–100 volts at 16–20 Hz, which rang the bells of other telephones on the line and signalled the operator at the exchange. Such crank telephones remained in rural service well into the twentieth century.

History

The first magnetos were magneto-electric machines developed in the 1830s, shortly after Faraday's discovery of electromagnetic induction in 1831. Hippolyte Pixii built an early hand-cranked machine in 1832. By the late nineteenth century the high-tension magneto, developed by engineers including Robert Bosch, had become the standard ignition source for the emerging automobile industry, before being largely displaced by battery-and-coil ignition in cars during the 1920s and 1930s.
