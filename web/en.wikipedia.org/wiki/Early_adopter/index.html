Early adopter

OriginEverett Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations (1962)
Share of marketAbout 13.5% (after innovators)
RelatedTechnology adoption life cycle

An early adopter is a person or organisation that begins using a new product, technology or innovation well before the majority of the market. The term comes from Everett Rogers's diffusion of innovations theory, set out in his 1962 book of the same name, which divides adopters of an innovation into five categories: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards.

Characteristics

In Rogers's model, early adopters make up roughly 13.5% of a population, following the innovators (2.5%). They tend to have high social status, greater financial resources and more contact with innovators and with information sources outside their local network. Crucially, they serve as opinion leaders: other potential adopters look to them for advice and evaluations, so winning over early adopters is widely regarded as the key to an innovation spreading to the mainstream.

In marketing

Businesses often target early adopters through beta programmes, launch events and influencer or review seeding, accepting the higher support costs and the risk that an unfinished product may draw criticism. Early adopters typically pay the highest prices and tolerate imperfections in exchange for novelty, status, or competitive advantage. Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm (1991) argues that many technology products fail in the "chasm" between the early adopter market and the early majority, whose expectations are more pragmatic.
