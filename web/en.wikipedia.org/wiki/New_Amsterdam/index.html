New Amsterdam (Dutch: Nieuw Amsterdam) was a 17th-century Dutch settlement established at the southern tip of Manhattan Island that served as the capital of the colony of New Netherland. Founded in 1626, it grew into a fortified trading town centered on Fort Amsterdam. In 1664 the English seized the colony and renamed the settlement New York, after the Duke of York.

History

Founding

Dutch exploration of the region began with Henry Hudson's voyage of 1609 for the Dutch East India Company. In 1624 the Dutch West India Company sent the first settlers to the colony, and in 1626 Director-General Peter Minuit arrived. According to tradition, Minuit purchased Manhattan Island from the Lenape for trade goods valued at 60 guilders — later famously (and inaccurately) rendered as "$24 worth of beads and trinkets."

Growth

The settlement clustered around Fort Amsterdam at the island's southern tip, near present-day Battery Park. A defensive wall built in 1653 along the northern edge of the town gave Wall Street its name. Under Director-General Peter Stuyvesant, who governed from 1647 to 1664, the town received a municipal charter in 1653 and grew to roughly 1,500 inhabitants. New Amsterdam was notably diverse for its time, with settlers speaking many languages and including Dutch, Walloons, Germans, Scandinavians, Africans (both enslaved and free), and a small Jewish community that arrived in 1654.

English conquest

In August 1664, an English fleet under Colonel Richard Nicolls demanded the colony's surrender. Stuyvesant wanted to resist, but the colonists refused to fight, and New Amsterdam surrendered without bloodshed. The Dutch briefly retook the city in 1673 during the Third Anglo-Dutch War, renaming it New Orange, but returned it to England by the Treaty of Westminster in 1674.

Legacy

Dutch influence survives in New York place names such as Harlem (Haarlem), Brooklyn (Breukelen), the Bronx, Staten Island, and Bowery (from bouwerij, farm). The street plan of lower Manhattan and sites such as the Battery preserve traces of the Dutch town.
