Neo-orthodoxy

Karl Barth (1886–1968), whose commentary on Romans launched the movement.

Neo-orthodoxy, also known as dialectical theology or the theology of crisis, is a movement in Protestant theology that developed in the aftermath of the First World War as a reaction against nineteenth-century liberal theology and its perceived optimism about human progress and religion. Its leading figures were the Swiss theologian Karl Barth, Emil Brunner, and Rudolf Bultmann, along with Friedrich Gogarten and Eduard Thurneysen; in the United States it influenced Reinhold Niebuhr and H. Richard Niebuhr.

Origins

The disillusionment produced by the First World War — and by the support of liberal German theologians for the Kaiser's war policy — precipitated the movement. Barth's commentary on The Epistle to the Romans (first edition 1919; radically rewritten 1922) struck contemporaries, in Karl Adam's famous phrase, like "a bombshell on the theologians' playground". Beginning in 1922 the journal Zwischen den Zeiten ("Between the Times") gave the group a platform.

Key ideas

Neo-orthodoxy stressed the transcendence of God — his "wholly other" character — and the primacy of revelation: God is known not through human reason, culture or religious experience, but through his self-disclosure in Jesus Christ and the Word of God. Against liberalism's focus on human religious feeling, dialectical theology emphasized human sinfulness, the "infinite qualitative distinction" between God and humanity (a phrase taken from Kierkegaard), and faith as response to the preached and scriptural Word.

The movement was never unified: Barth and Brunner famously split in 1934 over natural theology, and Bultmann's program of demythologizing took his work in a different direction. Barth's mature Church Dogmatics (1932–1967) became the movement's monumental statement.

Influence and reception

Neo-orthodoxy dominated Protestant theology in Europe and North America through the middle of the twentieth century, reshaped preaching and doctrinal study, and informed the Barmen Declaration (1934) of the Confessing Church against Nazi influence. From the 1960s it was criticized from several directions — by confessional orthodox theologians as insufficiently biblical, by liberals as authoritarian, and by newer movements such as liberation and postliberal theology — but Barth's work in particular continues to be widely studied.
