Tragic hero

A tragic hero is a literary character who makes a judgement error that inevitably leads to their own destruction. The concept originates in ancient Greek tragedy and was codified by Aristotle in his Poetics, where the hero's downfall — arising not from vice but from a mistake or frailty, the hamartia — arouses pity and fear in the audience and produces catharsis.

Aristotle's characteristics

According to Aristotle, a tragic hero should:

  • be of noble stature and occupy a high status, yet also be human and relatable;
  • be essentially virtuous and just, so that the fall is not deserved punishment;
  • fall through hamartia — an error of judgement or a character flaw such as Oedipus's pride or ignorance of his parentage;
  • undergo peripeteia, a reversal of fortune, and anagnorisis, a recognition of the truth of their situation;
  • evoke in the audience both pity and terror, culminating in catharsis.

Examples

Classical examples include Oedipus in Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, who fulfils the prophecy he flees; Antigone, destroyed by conflicting duties; and Euripides' Medea. Shakespeare adapted the pattern to Renaissance tragedy: Hamlet, Macbeth, Othello, and King Lear each fall through a characteristic error — indecision, ambition, jealousy, and pride. In modern literature and drama, the tragic hero may be an ordinary person, as in Arthur Miller's Willy Loman in Death of a Salesman, whose essay "Tragedy and the Common Man" argued that the common man is as fit a subject for tragedy as any king.

Related concepts

The Byronic hero and the antihero diverge from the classical pattern, lacking either the hero's nobility or his moral stature. Critics also discuss the "tragic flaw" as an oversimplification of hamartia, which may be nothing more culpable than a mistake made in ignorance.
