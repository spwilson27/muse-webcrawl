The genotype–phenotype distinction is drawn in genetics following Wilhelm Johannsen's proposal in 1911 to distinguish between the heredity of an organism — its genotype — and what that heredity produces — its phenotype. The distinction is fundamental to modern evolutionary theory and genetics.

Meaning

Several features of the distinction are important in biology:

The genotype determines potential, not outcome. The phenotype is the actual outcome of the development of an organism, shaped by the genotype in interaction with the environment. Only genotypic differences are inherited: acquired phenotypic changes (such as a suntan or learned behaviour) do not normally alter the genotype passed to offspring, which is why natural selection on phenotypes translates into evolution only through the genotypes they are associated with. The mapping is many-to-many: different genotypes can yield indistinguishable phenotypes (for example, AA and Aa with a dominant allele), while one genotype can produce different phenotypes across environments, a phenomenon called phenotypic plasticity, described by the norm of reaction.

Conceptual relatives

The distinction parallels Francis Crick's central dogma of molecular biology (information flows from DNA to protein, not from protein back to DNA), August Weismann's separation of germ line from soma, and Richard Dawkins's distinction between replicators and vehicles. It is deliberately challenged by fields such as epigenetics, developmental systems theory and niche construction, which argue for reciprocal causation between genes, organisms and environments.

The genotype–phenotype map

Explaining how genotypes map to phenotypes — through gene regulation, development and physiology — is the subject of the genotype–phenotype map concept, a central focus of evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo).
