The Treaty of Córdoba was signed on 24 August 1821 in the city of Córdoba, Veracruz, between Agustín de Iturbide, commander of the Army of the Three Guarantees, and Juan O'Donojú, the last Spanish chief political officer (jefe político superior) of New Spain. It effectively conceded the independence of Mexico, concluding the Mexican War of Independence (1810–1821).

Terms

The treaty endorsed the Plan of Iguala (February 1821): Mexico would become a sovereign, constitutional — and Catholic — empire or monarchy, inviting a European prince (ideally Ferdinand VII or another Bourbon) to its throne; if none accepted, Mexicans could choose their own monarch. Peninsular Spaniards and American-born creoles were to enjoy equal rights, and the property and persons of Spaniards were protected. Spanish troops were to withdraw from Mexico City.

Aftermath

Iturbide entered Mexico City on 27 September 1821 and Mexican independence was proclaimed the next day. The Spanish Cortes repudiated the treaty in February 1822, and Spain did not formally recognise Mexico until 1836, but it lacked the power to reverse the outcome. No European prince accepted the throne, so Iturbide was crowned Emperor Agustín I in 1822, before abdicating the following year.
