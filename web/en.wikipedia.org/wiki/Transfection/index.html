Transfection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transfection is the process of deliberately introducing naked or purified nucleic acids (DNA or RNA) into eukaryotic cells. The term is a blend of trans- and infection. Transfection of animal cells typically involves opening transient pores or "holes" in the cell membrane to allow the uptake of material, or complexing the nucleic acid with carriers that the cell takes up by endocytosis.

Methods

Chemical methods include calcium phosphate co-precipitation and cationic lipid (liposome) reagents such as lipofectamine. Physical methods include electroporation, in which electric pulses permeabilise the membrane; microinjection; and gene guns. Viral-mediated delivery is usually termed transduction rather than transfection.

Stable and transient transfection

In transient transfection, the introduced DNA is expressed but not integrated into the genome, so expression is lost as cells divide. In stable transfection, the foreign DNA becomes integrated into the host genome (or maintained as an episome) and is passed to daughter cells, typically selected for using a resistance marker. Stable cell lines generated this way are workhorses for producing recombinant proteins.

Applications

Transfection is used to study gene function, produce recombinant proteins, generate induced pluripotent stem cells, and deliver gene-editing reagents such as CRISPR components. Delivery of mRNA by lipid nanoparticles, a relative of lipofection, underlies mRNA vaccine technology.
