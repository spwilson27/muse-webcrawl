A condom is a sheath-shaped barrier device used during sexual intercourse to reduce the probability of pregnancy or a sexually transmitted infection (STI). There are both external condoms, worn over the penis, and internal condoms, worn inside the vagina or anus. When used correctly every time, external condoms are highly effective both as contraception and in preventing infections including HIV, gonorrhea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis, though they provide less protection against infections spread by skin-to-skin contact such as herpes and HPV.

History

Barrier devices are ancient: Gabriele Falloppio described a linen sheath against syphilis in 1564. Condoms of animal intestine and bladder were used in the eighteenth century, washed and reused. Vulcanised rubber (from the 1840s–50s) allowed the first mass-produced condoms, and latex condoms introduced in the 1920s were thinner, cheaper, and had a longer shelf life, eventually dominating production.

Use and effectiveness

External condoms are unrolled onto the erect penis before any contact; oil-based lubricants must not be used with latex, as they degrade it. Effectiveness studies estimate about 2 pregnancies per 100 women per year with perfect use and roughly 13 with typical use. Public-health agencies promote condom use as a central component of safer-sex programmes.

Varieties and materials

Most condoms are latex; alternatives for people with latex allergies include polyurethane and polyisoprene. Natural-membrane ("lambskin") condoms prevent pregnancy but their pores can pass viruses. Condoms are sold in many sizes, textures, and with features such as spermicidal lubricant, and are regulated as medical devices in most countries.
