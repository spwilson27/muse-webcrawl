Discounting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Discounting is a financial mechanism in which a debtor obtains the right to delay payments to a creditor, for a defined period of time, in exchange for a charge or fee. Essentially, the party that owes money in the present purchases the right to delay payment until some future date at which point the future payment is worth less than a payment today. This transaction is similar to borrowing, with the difference being that the debtor is the party who sets the interest rate, whereas with borrowing the creditor is the party who sets the rate.

The discount, or charge, is the difference between the original amount owed in the present and the amount that has to be paid in the future to settle the debt. Discounting also commonly refers to the practice of investors assigning a present value to future cash flows: because money can earn interest, a sum received in the future is worth less than the same sum received today. The concept is central to time value of money, bond valuation, and cost–benefit analysis.

Calculation

If r is the annual discount rate and t is the number of years until payment, the present value PV of a future amount FV is:

PV = FV / (1 + r)t

With continuous compounding, the discount factor becomes e−rt. The reciprocal operation — computing the future value of a present sum — is called compounding.

Discount rate

The discount rate used in a valuation reflects the opportunity cost of capital and the risk of the cash flows. In corporate finance it is often the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Central banks also set a policy rate historically called the discount rate — in the United States, the rate at which eligible depository institutions borrow from the Federal Reserve's discount window.

Types of discounting

  • Bank discount — interest deducted in advance on short-term instruments such as Treasury bills.
  • Trade discount — a reduction from list price given between businesses.
  • Cash discount — a reduction offered for early payment of an invoice (for example "2/10 net 30").
  • Hyperbolic discounting — a behavioural-economics model in which people discount future rewards at a rate that declines over time, producing time-inconsistent preferences.
