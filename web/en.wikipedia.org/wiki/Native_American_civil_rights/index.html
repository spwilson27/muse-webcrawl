Native American civil rights

Key laws: Indian Citizenship Act (1924), Indian Civil Rights Act (1968), Indian Self-Determination Act (1975)
Key events: Alcatraz occupation (1969), Wounded Knee occupation (1973), Dakota Access Pipeline protests (2016)

The Native American civil rights movement concerns the struggle of Indigenous peoples in the United States for sovereignty, treaty rights, citizenship, voting rights, religious freedom and an end to discriminatory federal policies. Unlike other civil rights movements, its central demand has been not only equality but recognition of tribes as sovereign political entities with rights guaranteed by treaties.

Historical outline

Nineteenth-century federal policy dispossessed tribes through removal, allotment of communal land under the Dawes Act (1887) and forced assimilation in boarding schools. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 granted U.S. citizenship to all Native Americans, though some states barred voting for decades after. The Indian Reorganization Act (1934) ended allotment, but the "termination" policy of the 1950s dissolved the federal status of over a hundred tribes.

The Red Power era and after

The late 1960s and 1970s saw the rise of the American Indian Movement (AIM, founded 1968), the occupation of Alcatraz Island (1969–71), the Trail of Broken Treaties (1972) and the Wounded Knee occupation (1973). Legislative gains followed: the Indian Civil Rights Act (1968), the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975), the American Indian Religious Freedom Act (1978), the Indian Child Welfare Act (1978) and the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988). Contemporary activism includes the 2016 Standing Rock protests against the Dakota Access Pipeline and campaigns over voting access, missing and murdered Indigenous women, and land-back claims.
