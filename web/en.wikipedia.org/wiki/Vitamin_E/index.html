Vitamin E

Vitamin E
ClassFat-soluble vitamin
Forms4 tocopherols, 4 tocotrienols
Main dietary formα-Tocopherol
SourcesVegetable oils, nuts, seeds, leafy greens
Recommended intake (adults)15 mg/day (US RDA)

Vitamin E is a group of eight fat-soluble compounds — four tocopherols and four tocotrienols — that function as antioxidants in the body. α-Tocopherol is the form most active in humans and the one preferentially retained by the liver. Vitamin E protects cell membranes from damage by free radicals, particularly by preventing the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids.

Discovery and chemistry

The vitamin was discovered in 1922 by Herbert Evans and Katharine Bishop, who found a dietary factor in leafy vegetables and wheat germ necessary for reproduction in rats; it was named "tocopherol" from Greek words for childbirth and bearing. The compounds share a chromanol ring with a hydrophobic side chain, saturated in tocopherols and unsaturated in tocotrienols.

Health effects

Deficiency is rare and usually results from fat-malabsorption disorders or genetic defects in the α-tocopherol transfer protein; it causes nerve and muscle damage, loss of coordination (ataxia) and weakened immunity. Despite its antioxidant role, large clinical trials have generally not shown that high-dose vitamin E supplements prevent cardiovascular disease or cancer, and very high doses may increase the risk of hemorrhagic stroke and interfere with blood clotting.

Sources

Good dietary sources include sunflower, safflower and wheat germ oils, sunflower seeds, almonds, hazelnuts, peanuts, spinach and other green leafy vegetables. Vitamin E is also widely used as a food additive antioxidant (E306–E309) and in cosmetics.
