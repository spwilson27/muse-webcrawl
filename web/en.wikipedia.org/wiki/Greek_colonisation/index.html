Greek colonisation

Greek (and Phoenician) colonies around 550 BCE.

Greek colonisation refers to the expansion of Archaic Greeks across the Mediterranean and Black Sea between roughly 800 and 480 BCE, during which the city-states (poleis) of mainland Greece and the Aegean founded hundreds of new, politically independent settlements — called apoikiai ("homes away from home") — from Spain in the west to the Caucasus in the east. Plato is said to have remarked that the Greeks sat around the Mediterranean "like frogs around a pond".

Causes

Scholars debate the mixture of motives: pressure on limited agricultural land at home (stenochoria), population growth, drought and famine, political exile after civil strife (stasis), and the search for trade — especially metals such as iron, copper and tin, and for grain. Early ventures, such as the Euboean trading post at Pithekoussai on Ischia (c. 770 BCE), were as much commercial stations (emporia) as colonies.

Process of foundation

A colony was normally organised by a mother city (metropolis), which appointed a founder (oikist), who consulted the oracle of Apollo at Delphi, recruited settlers, divided the land of the new site into allotments, and established the cults and constitution. The oikist was often honoured with a hero-cult after death. Sacred fire was carried from the hearth of the metropolis. Relations between colony and mother city were usually ties of sentiment, cult and diplomacy rather than control, though exceptions — such as Corinth's relations with Corcyra — could turn hostile.

Regions

  • Southern Italy and Sicily — "Magna Graecia": Taras (Tarentum), Sybaris, Croton, Rhegion and Neapolis (Naples) in Italy; Syracuse, Gela, Akragas and Himera in Sicily.
  • The western Mediterranean — Massalia (Marseille, c. 600 BCE), which founded Nice and Antibes; Emporion in Spain.
  • North Africa — Cyrene in Libya (c. 630 BCE), which spawned the Pentapolis; Naucratis in Egypt, a special licensed trading port.
  • The Hellespont and Black Sea — Byzantium and Chalcedon on the Bosporus; Miletus alone was credited with dozens of colonies including Sinope, Trapezus and Olbia, securing grain, fish and timber.
  • The Adriatic and Illyrian coasts — Epidamnos (Dyrrhachium), Apollonia and the island colonies of Issa and Pharos.

Consequences

Colonisation spread Greek language, coinage, alphabetic writing, urban planning and art around the Mediterranean. It also brought conflict with Phoenicians and Carthaginians in the west, with Etruscans in Italy, and with the indigenous peoples whose land was taken — sometimes by agreement, sometimes by force or enslavement. Prosperous colonies founded colonies of their own (Syracuse founded Camarina; Massalia founded a string of coastal towns), and some, like Syracuse, grew to rival the greatest cities of old Greece. The movement slowed after about 500 BCE as suitable sites filled and the Persian and Carthaginian powers expanded.
