Photoresist

A photoresist is a light-sensitive material used in several processes, such as photolithography and photoengraving, to form a patterned coating on a surface. Exposure to light changes the solubility of the resist in a developer solution, so that after development either the exposed or the unexposed regions remain on the substrate as a protective mask for etching, deposition or implantation.

A silicon wafer coated with coloured photoresist before exposure.

Positive and negative resists

In a positive resist, the regions exposed to light become soluble and are washed away during development, leaving a pattern corresponding to the transparent parts of the mask. In a negative resist, exposure causes the resist to polymerise or cross-link and become insoluble, so the unexposed regions are removed instead. Positive resists generally offer higher resolution; negative resists adhere well and are chemically robust.

Chemistries

Classic resists for near-ultraviolet exposure are based on novolak resin with diazonaphthoquinone (DNQ) as a photoactive compound. Deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography uses chemically amplified resists, in which a photoacid generator produces acid on exposure that catalytically deprotects the polymer during a post-exposure bake, greatly increasing sensitivity. Resists for extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography at 13.5 nm are an active area of development, including metal-oxide formulations.

Processing

A typical sequence is: surface preparation, spin coating, soft bake, exposure through a mask or by direct writing, post-exposure bake (for chemically amplified resists), development, and hard bake. The remaining resist then masks the substrate during processing and is finally stripped.

Applications

Photoresists are fundamental to manufacturing integrated circuits, printed circuit boards, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), flat-panel displays, and printing plates.
