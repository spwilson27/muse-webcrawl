Élie Metchnikoff (15 May 1845 – 15 July 1916), born Ilya Ilyich Mechnikov, was a Russian zoologist best known for his pioneering work in immunology. He is credited with the discovery of phagocytosis, the process by which certain cells engulf and digest foreign particles and microorganisms, and is regarded as a founder of cellular immunology.

Early life and education

Metchnikoff was born in the village of Ivanovka, near Kharkov, in the Russian Empire (now Ukraine). His father was an officer of the Imperial Guard, and his mother was the daughter of a Jewish writer. He studied natural sciences at the University of Kharkov, where he completed his degree in two years, and then travelled to Germany to study marine biology on the island of Heligoland and at the universities of Giessen, Göttingen and Munich.

Career

After earning his doctorate from the University of Saint Petersburg in 1868, Metchnikoff taught zoology and comparative anatomy at the University of Odessa and later at Saint Petersburg. His research on the intracellular digestion of invertebrates led him, in 1882, to the famous experiment at Messina, Sicily, in which he inserted rose thorns into the larvae of starfish and observed that mobile cells surrounded and attacked the foreign objects. He named these cells phagocytes, from the Greek words for "devouring cells".

In 1888 Metchnikoff joined the newly founded Pasteur Institute in Paris, where he remained for the rest of his life, serving as deputy director from 1904.

Phagocytosis and the Nobel Prize

Metchnikoff's theory that phagocytes were the body's first line of defence against infection was initially resisted by proponents of the humoral theory of immunity. In 1908 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Paul Ehrlich "in recognition of their work on immunity" — an award that acknowledged both the cellular and humoral schools.

Later work

In his later years Metchnikoff studied the ageing process, founding the field he called gerontology, and advocateded the consumption of fermented milk products such as yoghurt, arguing in The Prolongation of Life (1907) that lactic-acid bacteria could suppress harmful putrefactive bacteria in the gut. His work stimulated early research into probiotics.
