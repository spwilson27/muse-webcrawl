Direct imaging

In astronomy, direct imaging is a method of detecting exoplanets and other faint companions by photographing them directly, rather than inferring their presence from their effects on the host star. Because a planet is typically a billion times fainter than its star in visible light, direct imaging requires coronagraphs and adaptive optics to block the star's glare.

Technique

Young giant planets still glow with the heat of their formation, making them brightest in the infrared. Instruments combine extreme adaptive optics, a coronagraph to occult the star, and angular or spectral differential imaging to remove residual starlight (speckles). The method probes orbital separations of tens of astronomical units, complementary to the transit and radial-velocity methods.

Discoveries

The first directly imaged planetary-mass companion was 2M1207b (2004), orbiting a brown dwarf. In 2008 the four-planet system HR 8799 and the planet Fomalhaut b were announced; Beta Pictoris b followed, and its orbital motion has been filmed over years. Direct imaging also yields spectra of exoplanet atmospheres, revealing water, carbon monoxide, and methane.

Future

The James Webb Space Telescope and planned extremely large telescopes aim to image smaller, older planets, with long-term goals of photographing Earth analogues around nearby stars.
