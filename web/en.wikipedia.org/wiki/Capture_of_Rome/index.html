The capture of Rome (Italian: Presa di Roma) on 20 September 1870 was the final event of the long process of Italian unification known as the Risorgimento. Troops of the Kingdom of Italy breached the Aurelian Walls at Porta Pia and occupied Rome, ending the thousand-year temporal rule of the popes over the Papal States and making Rome the capital of a united Italy.

Background

After the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy in 1861, Rome and its surrounding territory remained under Pope Pius IX, protected by a French garrison. Giuseppe Garibaldi's attempts to take the city were defeated in 1862 and 1867. The outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War in 1870 forced Napoleon III to withdraw the French troops, removing the pope's shield.

The assault

General Raffaele Cadorna's corps of about 50,000 men advanced on Rome in September 1870. After Pius IX rejected demands for a peaceful surrender, Italian artillery opened a breach in the walls near Porta Pia on the morning of 20 September. Bersaglieri stormed through the breach; the papal forces, ordered by the pope to offer only token resistance sufficient to make clear the occupation was by force, ceased fire. Italian losses were 49 dead; papal losses were 19.

Aftermath

A plebiscite on 2 October 1870 overwhelmingly approved annexation to Italy, and Rome became the Italian capital in 1871. Pius IX declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican" and refused to recognise the Italian state, originating the "Roman Question", which was settled only by the Lateran Treaty of 1929 creating Vatican City.
