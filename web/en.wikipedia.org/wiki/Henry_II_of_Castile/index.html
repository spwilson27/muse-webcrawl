Henry II (13 January 1334 – 29 May 1379), called Henry of Trastámara or the Fratricidal (el Fratricida), was the first King of Castile and León from the House of Trastámara. He became king in 1369 by defeating his half-brother Peter the Cruel at the Battle of Montiel, after which he killed Peter with his own hands, according to the chroniclers, an act that earned him his epithet.

Henry was the third of the ten illegitimate children of Alfonso XI of Castile and his mistress Eleanor de Guzmán. Born in Seville as a twin of Fadrique Alfonso, he was made Count of Trastámara and lord of other Galician and Leonese territories, becoming the standard-bearer of the bastard line against the legitimate king.

The civil war

Henry's rebellion against Peter fused with the Hundred Years' War between England and France. In 1366, supported by French and Aragonese troops under the mercenary captain Bertrand du Guesclin, Henry invaded Castile and had himself crowned at Burgos, driving Peter into exile. Peter counter-attacked with the help of Edward, the Black Prince of England, and crushed Henry at the Battle of Nájera (1367), restoring Peter for two years. Henry returned in 1369 with French support; Peter was besieged in the castle of Montiel and, lured into a trap by du Guesclin, was killed in a struggle with Henry, who thereby secured the throne.

Reign

As king, Henry had to reward the nobility who had backed him and the foreign powers that had financed his campaigns; his grants of lands and titles — the famous mercedes enriqueñas — founded the great fortunes of the late-medieval Castilian high nobility. In foreign policy he confirmed the alliance with France, and Castilian fleets decisively aided France against England, notably at the Battle of La Rochelle (1372), where the English fleet was destroyed. He fought wars against Portugal and Navarre and intervened in the War of the Two Peters' aftermath with Aragon. To pay his debts and rewards he debased the coinage and convened the Cortes repeatedly.

Henry's reign also saw rising violence against Jewish communities in Castile, fanned by the propaganda of his supporters, who had accused Peter of favouring Jews; pogroms during the civil war foreshadowed the great massacres of 1391. He died at Santo Domingo de la Calzada in May 1379 and was succeeded by his son John I. Through his descendants, the Trastámara dynasty came to rule Castile, Aragon, Navarre, and Naples, and his great-great-granddaughter was Isabella I of Castile.
