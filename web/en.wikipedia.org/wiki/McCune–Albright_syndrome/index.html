McCune–Albright syndrome is a complex genetic disorder affecting the bone, skin and endocrine systems. It is characterised by a classic triad: polyostotic fibrous dysplasia of bone, café-au-lait skin pigmentation, and precocious puberty or other hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies. It is a rare disease with estimated prevalence between 1 in 100,000 and 1 in 1,000,000.

Cause

The syndrome is caused by a postzygotic (mosaic) activating mutation in the GNAS gene (usually R201H or R201C), which encodes the alpha subunit of the stimulatory G protein (Gsα). The mutation arises early in embryonic development, so only some tissues carry it; constitutive Gsα signalling raises cyclic AMP in affected cells, driving excess hormone secretion, skin pigmentation and abnormal bone-forming stem cells. The condition is not inherited.

Signs and symptoms

Fibrous dysplasia can affect any bone but commonly involves the femur ("shepherd's crook" deformity), skull base and ribs, causing fractures, pain, deformity and cranial nerve compression. Endocrine features include gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty (especially in girls), hyperthyroidism, growth hormone excess, neonatal Cushing syndrome and phosphate-wasting. Café-au-lait macules often have jagged "coast of Maine" borders and respect the midline, reflecting the mosaic distribution.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by imaging (bone scintigraphy, X-ray, CT) and genetic testing of affected tissue where needed. There is no cure; management targets each manifestation — bisphosphonates for bone pain, aromatase inhibitors or other agents for precocious puberty, treatment of hyperthyroidism and GH excess, and orthopaedic surgery for fractures and deformity.
