Beeldenstorm

The Beeldenstorm (Dutch for "image storm") was a wave of iconoclastic destruction of Catholic religious art and church fittings that swept the Low Countries in the summer and autumn of 1566. It began at Steenvoorde in West Flanders on 10 August 1566 and spread rapidly through Flanders, Brabant and Holland, becoming a defining prelude to the Dutch Revolt against Spanish rule.

Background

Calvinism had spread rapidly in the prosperous cloth towns of Flanders despite persecution under Philip II of Spain. In 1566, amid unemployment in the textile industry and high bread prices, Reformed preachers held mass open-air "hedge sermons" outside town walls, drawing thousands. The regent, Margaret of Parma, temporarily relaxed enforcement of the heresy laws, emboldening the movement.

The storm

After a sermon near Steenvoorde, a band of about twenty people broke into the monastery of St Lawrence and smashed its images. The example spread: on 20 August the great Cathedral of Our Lady in Antwerp was systematically emptied of statues, altarpieces and ornaments over two nights, and similar scenes followed in Ghent, Tournai, Valenciennes, Amsterdam, Utrecht and dozens of other towns. Much of the destruction was disciplined — valuables were inventoried for the poor or town councils — rather than simple looting.

Consequences

Philip II answered with force: in 1567 the Duke of Alba arrived with a Spanish army and established the Council of Troubles, whose executions — including those of the counts of Egmont and Horn — pushed the Netherlands into open revolt and the Eighty Years' War. The Beeldenstorm remains the largest episode of iconoclasm in early modern Europe and explains the famously bare interiors of many Dutch churches today.
