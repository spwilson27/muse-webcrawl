Australian Aboriginal English

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distribution of Aboriginal English varieties across Australia

Australian Aboriginal English (AAE) is a group of dialects of the English language used by many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. It developed during the colonisation of Australia from the interaction of Indigenous languages with the English of settlers, and exists on a continuum from varieties close to standard Australian English ("light" forms) to heavily creole-influenced varieties ("heavy" forms), related to but distinct from Kriol, the English-based creole of northern Australia.

Features

AAE shares much of its phonology and vocabulary with general and rural Australian English but differs in accent, grammar, and discourse conventions shaped by Indigenous languages. Characteristic grammatical features include variable use of standard inflections, distinctive pronoun usage in some varieties (including forms drawn from creole), and the use of discourse markers such as eh and tag-like unna ("isn't it") in northern varieties. The lexicon includes words from Aboriginal languages, such as deadly meaning "excellent," and standard words with shifted meanings—shame denoting embarrassment arising from cultural obligations, country denoting one's traditional lands, and kinship terms applied more broadly than in standard English.

Social and cultural role

AAE is an important marker of Aboriginal identity and solidarity, and its pragmatics reflect Indigenous cultural values: indirect questioning, extended silences in conversation, and gratuitous concurrence (agreeing to questions regardless of one's actual view), which can cause serious miscommunication in legal, medical, and educational settings. Linguists and educators increasingly argue that AAE should be recognised as a legitimate dialect in its own right rather than treated as deficient English, and that schooling should build bidialectal competence in both AAE and standard Australian English.

History

The variety arose in the early contact period around Sydney Cove and other settlements, through pidgins used between Indigenous people and colonists, and spread as Aboriginal people were displaced and concentrated on missions and reserves, where children acquired English-based varieties as a first language while retaining features of ancestral languages.
