Federal Credit Union Act

The Federal Credit Union Act is an Act of Congress enacted in 1934 that established the federal law under which credit unions in the United States are chartered, regulated and insured. Signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 26 June 1934, during the Great Depression, it made credit unions — member-owned, cooperative, non-profit financial institutions — available in every state as a federal alternative to state charters.

Provisions

The Act provides for the chartering of federal credit unions organised around a "common bond" of membership, such as a shared employer, occupation, association or community. It set out their cooperative governance, with members holding one vote each regardless of the size of their deposits, and their exemption from federal taxation. The law is codified at 12 U.S.C. §§ 1751 et seq.

Administration

Administration of the Act passed through several agencies before becoming the responsibility of the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA), created in 1970, which charters and supervises federal credit unions and insures member share accounts through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund. Major amendments in 1998 (the Credit Union Membership Access Act) followed a Supreme Court ruling and broadened fields of membership.
