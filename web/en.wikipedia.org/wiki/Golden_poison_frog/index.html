Golden poison frog

Scientific namePhyllobates terribilis
FamilyDendrobatidae
RangePacific coast rainforest of Colombia
StatusEndangered

The golden poison frog (Phyllobates terribilis), also known as the golden dart frog or golden poison arrow frog, is a poison dart frog endemic to the Pacific coast rainforests of Colombia. It is considered one of the most toxic animals on Earth.

Description and toxicity

Adults are brightly coloured — yellow, orange, or pale green — an aposematic warning to predators, and measure about 5 cm (2 in). The skin secretes batrachotoxin, a potent alkaloid neurotoxin that blocks voltage-gated sodium channels; an average frog carries enough toxin to kill many large animals, and merely touching one can be dangerous. Like other dart frogs, it obtains its alkaloids from dietary arthropods such as mites and ants; captive-bred frogs are non-toxic. The frog was described to science only in 1978 by Charles Myers and colleagues.

Habitat and conservation

The species inhabits a small area of lowland rainforest near the Saija River basin in Cauca Department. The Emberá people traditionally used its secretion to poison blowgun darts. The frog is listed as Endangered owing to its tiny range and ongoing habitat destruction from logging, agriculture, and mining, as well as collection for the pet trade.
