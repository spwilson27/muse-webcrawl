Master–slave morality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Master–slave morality is a central concept in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche, developed above all in Beyond Good and Evil (1886) and On the Genealogy of Morality (1887). Nietzsche argued that human moral systems historically fall into two basic types, whose values are mirror images of one another.

Master morality

In master morality — the morality of the strong, noble and self-affirming — the primary distinction is between "good" and "bad", where "good" means noble, powerful, healthy and excellent, qualities the masters first applied to themselves, and "bad" means the common, weak and lowly, applied afterwards to others almost as an afterthought. Its virtues are strength, courage, pride, creativity and open enmity; consequences and intentions matter less than excellence of character. Nietzsche associated it with aristocratic cultures such as archaic Greece and Rome.

Slave morality

Slave morality arises, in Nietzsche's genealogy, when the oppressed — unable to act against their masters — take imaginative revenge through a "slave revolt in morality". Driven by ressentiment, they invert the master's values: powerlessness becomes "goodness", humility and meekness become virtues, and the masters themselves are condemned as "evil". Here the primary pair is "good" and "evil", and slave morality is reactive: it begins by saying No to an external enemy. Nietzsche saw its triumphs in the spread of Judaism's priestly values and then of Christianity, and he regarded much modern morality — with its emphasis on pity, equality and utility — as its heir.

Interpretation

Nietzsche's aim was diagnostic rather than simply partisan: he thought both moralities persist, mixed, in modern souls, and he hoped for creators of new values who would move "beyond good and evil". Commentators debate how far Nietzsche endorses master morality, noting his admiration for nobility combined with his criticism of cruelty and his praise for self-overcoming in figures such as Goethe. The distinction has been influential in moral psychology, cultural criticism and studies of moral language.
