Gibbs–Duhem equation

In thermodynamics, the Gibbs–Duhem equation describes the relationship between changes in chemical potential for components in a thermodynamic system:

S dT − V dP + Σi Ni dμi = 0

where Ni is the number of moles of component i, dμi the infinitesimal change in chemical potential for this component, S the entropy, T the absolute temperature, V the volume and P the pressure. The equation is named after Josiah Willard Gibbs and Pierre Duhem.

Derivation

The equation follows from the fact that the Gibbs free energy is a first-order homogeneous function of the amounts of the components. Differentiating the Euler integrated form of the fundamental relation and subtracting the general differential expression for dG yields the Gibbs–Duhem relation directly.

Applications

At constant temperature and pressure, the equation reduces to Σi xi dμi = 0, showing that the chemical potentials of components in a mixture cannot change independently. This underlies the Duhem–Margules equation for vapour pressures and the Gibbs phase rule, and it is used to test the thermodynamic consistency of experimental activity-coefficient data.
