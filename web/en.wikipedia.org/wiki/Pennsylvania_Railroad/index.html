Pennsylvania Railroad


Reporting mark: PRR
Locale: Northeastern and Midwestern United States
Dates of operation: 1846–1968
Successor: Penn Central
Headquarters: Philadelphia, Pennsylvania

The Pennsylvania Railroad (reporting mark PRR), legally the Pennsylvania Railroad Company, was an American Class I railroad founded in 1846 and headquartered in Philadelphia. For much of its existence it was the largest transportation enterprise in the world, and it paid dividends continuously for more than a century.

History

Chartered by the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania in 1846, the PRR built from Harrisburg to Pittsburgh, completing an all-rail route between Philadelphia and Pittsburgh in the 1850s that used the famous Horseshoe Curve near Altoona. Under presidents such as J. Edgar Thomson and Thomas A. Scott, the railroad expanded west to Chicago and St. Louis and east to New York, acquiring or leasing hundreds of smaller lines. By 1882 it was the largest railroad in the world by traffic and revenue.

The PRR electrified its busy Northeast Corridor and suburban lines in the early 20th century, introducing the iconic GG1 electric locomotive in the 1930s. Its New York Pennsylvania Station, opened in 1910, was a Beaux-Arts masterpiece whose demolition in 1963 galvanized the historic preservation movement.

Decline and merger

After World War II, competition from automobiles, trucks, and airlines eroded the PRR's traffic. In 1968 it merged with its longtime rival, the New York Central Railroad, to form Penn Central, which declared bankruptcy in 1970—the largest corporate failure in American history at the time. Its viable lines were later divided among Conrail, Amtrak, Norfolk Southern, and CSX.

Legacy

The PRR's tuscan red passenger trains, its standardised steam locomotives built at the Altoona Works, and its keystone herald remain enduring symbols of American railroading.
