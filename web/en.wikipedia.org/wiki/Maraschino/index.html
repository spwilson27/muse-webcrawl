Maraschino (/ˌmærəˈskiːnoʊ/) is a clear, relatively dry liqueur made from the distillation of marasca cherries, a sour variety of cherry grown principally around Zadar, in Dalmatia, Croatia. It is known for its distinctive flavour, derived not only from the fruit but from the crushed cherry pits, which contribute an almond-like note. The liqueur is traditionally bottled in straw-covered flasks and is an essential ingredient in many classic cocktails.

History

Maraschino was first produced by Dominican apothecaries in Zadar in the 16th century. Commercial production began in 1759, when Francesco Drioli founded a factory in Zadar; the Luxardo family established a rival distillery there in 1821. The liqueur became fashionable in royal courts and fashionable hotels across 19th-century Europe and America and was exported worldwide from the Dalmatian coast. After the Second World War the Luxardo family, exiled from Zadar, rebuilt their distillery at Torreglia near Padua, Italy, where production continues.

Production

Sour marasca cherries are fermented along with their leaves, stems and crushed stones; the fermented mash is distilled, and the distillate is aged in ash-wood vats, then diluted and sweetened with sugar syrup. Unlike the sweet red "maraschino cherries" used as garnishes — which are made by brining and dyeing other cherry varieties — the liqueur itself is colourless and comparatively dry.

Use in cocktails

Maraschino liqueur is a cornerstone of pre-Prohibition bartending and appears in classics such as the Aviation, the Hemingway Special, the Last Word, the Martinez and the Improved Whiskey Cocktail. Its bittersweet, nutty cherry flavour is potent, so recipes typically call for small amounts — a quarter ounce or a few teaspoons.
