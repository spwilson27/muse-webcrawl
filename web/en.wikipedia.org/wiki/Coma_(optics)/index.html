Coma (optics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In optics (especially in telescopes), coma (from the Greek κόμη, "hair") is an aberration in which each off-axis point of light is imaged as a roughly pear-shaped or comet-like spot, with the blurred edges pointing away from the optical axis. Coma is one of the five Seidel (third-order monochromatic) aberrations. It arises because rays through different zones of a lens or mirror are magnified by different amounts, so an off-axis point image is built from a stack of displaced circles.

Characteristics

For a single annular zone of the aperture, an off-axis point forms a circle in the focal plane; successive zones produce circles of increasing diameter whose centres are increasingly displaced, tracing out a 60-degree wedge-shaped flare resembling a comet's tail. The aberration grows with the cube of the aperture and linearly with field angle, so it is worst in fast (low f-number) systems and toward the edge of the field. Reversing the orientation of a lens can convert positive (outward-flaring) coma to negative coma, a fact exploited in lens design.

Correction

Coma can be minimised by choosing appropriate lens shapes (a properly bent singlet can be coma-free for one conjugate pair), by symmetric lens construction, and in reflecting telescopes by adding a coma corrector — a small multi-element lens group near the focal plane. The classic parabolic Newtonian telescope is free of spherical aberration on-axis but shows strong coma off-axis, which is why fast Dobsonian telescopes are often fitted with correctors such as the Paracorr. Aplanatic designs (corrected simultaneously for spherical aberration and coma), such as the Ritchey–Chrétien telescope used in most modern professional observatories including the Hubble Space Telescope, satisfy the Abbe sine condition.
