Evaluation Assurance Level

The Evaluation Assurance Level (EAL1 through EAL7) of an IT product or system is a numerical grade assigned following the completion of a Common Criteria security evaluation, an international standard in effect since 1999. The increasing assurance levels reflect added assurance requirements that must be met to achieve Common Criteria certification, involving more detailed documentation, analysis, and testing of the evaluated product.

Overview

An EAL is a measure of the depth and rigor of an evaluation, not a measure of the security of the product itself. A higher EAL means the product was evaluated and tested more thoroughly; it does not necessarily mean the product is more secure in absolute terms. Certification at a given EAL is intended to give consumers confidence that the product's security features behave as documented and were subjected to a specified level of independent scrutiny.

Implications of higher levels

Higher EALs require progressively more rigorous evidence: from basic functional testing at EAL1, through structured design documentation and vulnerability analysis, up to semiformal and formal mathematical specification and verification of the design at EAL5–EAL7. Because of the cost and effort involved, most commercial products are certified at EAL4 or below. Higher levels are typically pursued for high-assurance applications such as smart cards, multilevel secure systems, and certain military or governmental products.

Mutual recognition

Under the Common Criteria Recognition Arrangement (CCRA), participating nations mutually recognize evaluations up to certain assurance levels (historically EAL4, later EAL2 under the revised arrangement with collaborative Protection Profiles). Products are commonly evaluated against a Protection Profile (PP) that states the functional and assurance requirements for a category of products; certification then reports both the EAL and the profile conformance.

Criticism

Critics note that an EAL rating applies only to the specific evaluated configuration and version of a product, that evaluation can lag behind product releases, and that the rating says nothing about vulnerabilities outside the evaluated scope. Debates also exist about whether the cost of high-EAL evaluation yields commensurate real-world security benefits.
