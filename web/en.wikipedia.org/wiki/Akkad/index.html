Akkad (Akkadian: Akkadû) was a city and the surrounding region in ancient Mesopotamia, in the northern part of later Babylonia, roughly corresponding to the area around modern Baghdad. It gave its name to the Akkadian Empire and to the Akkadian language, the lingua franca of the ancient Near East for much of the second millennium BC.

The city

The city of Akkad was the capital of Sargon (reigned c. 2334–2279 BC), who made it the seat of the first territorial empire in Mesopotamian history. Despite its importance, the site of Akkad has never been conclusively identified; it is generally sought on or near the Tigris, possibly in the vicinity of modern Baghdad, and may lie buried beneath later settlement or river deposits.

The Akkadian Empire

Sargon, a former courtier at Kish, conquered the Sumerian city-states of the south and extended his rule from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean. His successors, especially his grandson Naram-Sin, who took the title "King of the Four Quarters" and was represented as a god, brought the empire to its greatest extent. The empire administered conquered territories through appointed governors, standardized weights and measures, and used Akkadian alongside Sumerian in administration.

The empire collapsed around 2154 BC amid internal disorder, drought, and invasions by the Gutians from the Zagros Mountains, according to later Mesopotamian tradition, which remembered the fall as a divine curse in the composition known as the Curse of Akkad.

Legacy

"Akkad" remained a geographic and cultural term for northern Babylonia for two thousand years, paired with "Sumer" in the royal title "King of Sumer and Akkad". Akkadian, written in cuneiform adapted from Sumerian, became the diplomatic language of the Late Bronze Age, used in the Amarna letters and across Assyria and Babylonia.
