Beta-lactam antibiotic

Beta-lactam antibiotics (β-lactam antibiotics) are a class of antibiotics that contain a beta-lactam ring in their chemical structure. They include the penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and monobactams, and together form the most widely used group of antibacterial agents in medicine.

Mechanism of action

Beta-lactams bind to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan strands in the bacterial cell wall. By acylating the active site of these transpeptidases, the drugs block the final stage of cell-wall synthesis; the weakened wall cannot withstand osmotic pressure and the bacterium lyses. Beta-lactams are therefore bactericidal, and are most active against growing bacteria.

Classes

Penicillins, beginning with Alexander Fleming's penicillin (1928), fuse the beta-lactam ring to a five-membered thiazolidine ring. Cephalosporins, derived from a fungus isolated in Sardinia in the 1940s, use a six-membered dihydrothiazine ring and are grouped into generations of increasing Gram-negative coverage. Carbapenems such as meropenem have an exceptionally broad spectrum and stability against many beta-lactamases. Monobactams, represented by aztreonam, contain the beta-lactam ring alone and are active only against aerobic Gram-negative bacteria.

Resistance

The most common resistance mechanism is production of beta-lactamase enzymes that hydrolyse the ring. Others include altered PBPs — the basis of methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) — and reduced outer-membrane permeability in Gram-negative organisms. Beta-lactamase inhibitors such as clavulanic acid are co-formulated with some penicillins to protect them.

Adverse effects

Beta-lactams are generally well tolerated. Hypersensitivity reactions range from rash to anaphylaxis; a small proportion of people report penicillin allergy, though true IgE-mediated allergy is less common than the label suggests.
