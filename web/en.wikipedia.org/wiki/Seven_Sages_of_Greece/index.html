Seven Sages of Greece

The Seven Sages of Greece (Greek: οἱ ἑπτὰ σοφοί, hoi hepta sophoi) were celebrated figures of early Greek wisdom — statesmen, lawgivers, and philosophers of the 7th and 6th centuries BCE — traditionally credited with founding Greek philosophy and political thought. The canon of seven names, though varying between sources, is among the most famous groupings of the ancient Greek world.

The canonical seven

The list given by Diogenes Laërtius, following the most common tradition, comprises:

SageCityFamous saying
ThalesMiletus"Know thyself"
SolonAthens"Nothing in excess" (meden agan)
ChilonSparta"Do not desire the impossible"
PittacusMytilene"Know thine opportunity"
BiasPriene"Most men are bad"
CleobulusLindos"Moderation is best"
PerianderCorinth"Forethought in all things"

Other figures sometimes included in variant lists are Myson of Chenae, Anacharsis the Scythian, Epimenides of Crete, and Pherecydes of Syros. Several of the maxims attributed to the sages were inscribed at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi.

Legends

A famous anecdote tells of a golden tripod (in other versions a cup or bowl) fished from the sea or dedicated by an oracle, which was passed from sage to sage, each deferring to another as wiser, until it returned to Thales or was dedicated to Apollo. The story illustrates the ideal of wisdom combined with humility.

Legacy

The sages stand at the head of the Greek wisdom tradition. Thales is conventionally regarded as the first philosopher; Solon as the foundational Athenian lawgiver. Their terse maxims (gnomai) set the pattern for Greek ethical reflection and were quoted throughout antiquity.
