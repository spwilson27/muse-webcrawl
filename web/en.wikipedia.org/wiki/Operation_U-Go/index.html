Operation U-Go

Date: March – July 1944
Location: Manipur, British India
Result: Allied victory

Operation U-Go (Japanese: ウ号作戦, U-gō sakusen), also known as the Imphal offensive, was the Japanese offensive launched in March 1944 against British and Indian forces in the Manipur region of north-eastern India during the Second World War. The Fifteenth Army under Lieutenant-General Renya Mutaguchi crossed the Chindwin River aiming to seize Imphal and Kohima, cut Allied supply lines to China, and — in the hopes of its planners — spark an uprising against British rule in India. The offensive ended in one of the worst defeats suffered by the Imperial Japanese Army, with the Battles of Imphal and Kohima turning the tide of the Burma campaign.

Background

By 1944 the Allies were preparing their own advance into Burma. Mutaguchi, commanding the Fifteenth Army, persuaded his superiors to pre-empt them with an invasion of India, accompanied by the Indian National Army under Subhas Chandra Bose. The plan assumed that the attacking divisions could live substantially off captured Allied supplies; logistics were minimal and the monsoon deadline gave the operation only about a month to succeed.

The offensive

Japanese forces crossed the Chindwin in early March 1944. The 33rd Division advanced on Imphal from the south, the 15th Division from the east, and the 31st Division under Kotoku Sato moved on Kohima, whose small garrison held a vital road junction. The British Fourteenth Army under William Slim had deliberately withdrawn to the Imphal plain, where it could be supplied by air and fight on ground of its choosing. At Kohima, a scratch garrison of about 2,500 men held off Sato's division in desperate fighting — including the famous battle around the tennis court of the Deputy Commissioner's bungalow — until relieved.

Defeat and retreat

Starved of supplies, decimated by disease, and unable to break the Allied defence, the Japanese divisions disintegrated during the monsoon retreat in June and July 1944. Of roughly 85,000 Japanese troops committed, more than 50,000 were lost, the majority to starvation and sickness rather than battle. The defeat, combined with the Allied victory in the concurrent Battle of Imphal, opened the way to the reconquest of Burma in 1945.
