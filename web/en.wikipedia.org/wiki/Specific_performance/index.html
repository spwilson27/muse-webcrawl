Specific performance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A judge's gavel beside a signed contract on a desk
A judge's gavel beside a signed contract on a desk

Specific performance is an equitable remedy in contract law by which a court orders a party to perform its contractual obligations, rather than merely paying monetary damages for breach. Because it descends from the English Court of Chancery, it is granted only where damages would be inadequate — classically, in contracts for the sale of land, where every parcel is treated as unique.

Requirements

A claimant must show a valid, enforceable contract, its own readiness to perform, and the inadequacy of damages — easiest for land and unique goods, hardest for ordinary commodities and personal services, which courts will not specifically enforce (in part because of prohibitions on involuntary servitude). The remedy remains discretionary: unclean hands, hardship, impossibility or the need for constant court supervision may lead a judge to refuse it.

In common and civil law

English and American courts grant specific performance exceptionally; civil-law systems, by contrast, generally treat performance in kind as the primary remedy — as under French and German law and the international Vienna Sales Convention (Article 46), though even there practical limits apply. The Sale of Goods Act 1979 (UK, s. 52) allows the remedy for specific or ascertained goods.

Famous cases

In Cohen v Roche (1927) Hepworth chairs were held not unique, while in the American case Sedmak v Charlie's Chevrolet a limited-edition Corvette was specifically enforced. Specific performance is also the standard expectation in land sales: a buyer can compel conveyance, and a seller can compel the buyer to complete.
