Operator (physics)

In physics, an operator is a function over a space of physical states to another space of physical states. In quantum mechanics, operators are fundamental: every measurable physical quantity (observable) is represented by a linear operator acting on the Hilbert space of states, and the values that measurement can return are the eigenvalues of that operator.

Operators in quantum mechanics

Observable quantities correspond to Hermitian (self-adjoint) operators, whose eigenvalues are real. Standard examples include the position operator (multiplication by the coordinate), the momentum operator (proportional to the derivative with respect to position, −iħ∂/∂x), the Hamiltonian (total energy) operator, and the angular momentum operators. Wavefunctions that are simultaneously eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are the stationary states of the system, with eigenvalues equal to the allowed energies, as formulated in the Schrödinger equation Ĥψ = Eψ.

Properties

Operators in general do not commute: the commutator of position and momentum is [x̂, p̂] = iħ, which underlies the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Two observables can be measured simultaneously, with common eigenstates, only if their operators commute. Non-observable operators are also important: the unitary time-evolution operator advances states in time, while creation and annihilation operators raise and lower occupation numbers in quantum field theory. In classical mechanics the word "operator" is used much less, but analogous structures include the Hamiltonian function and symmetry transformations acting on phase space.
