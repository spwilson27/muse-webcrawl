Horner syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Left-sided Horner syndrome: drooping lid and smaller pupil

Horner syndrome, also known as oculosympathetic paresis, is a combination of symptoms that arises when the sympathetic trunk — the pathway of sympathetic nerves to the eye and face — is damaged. It is named after the Swiss ophthalmologist Johann Friedrich Horner, who described it in 1869, though earlier descriptions exist.

Signs and symptoms

The classic findings on the affected side are ptosis (a slight drooping of the upper eyelid from weakness of Müller's muscle), miosis (a constricted pupil, making the anisocoria more apparent in dim light), and an apparent enophthalmos (the eye appears sunken, an illusion caused by the narrowed palpebral fissure). Decreased sweating (anhidrosis) on the affected side of the face may occur, and in congenital cases the affected iris may be lighter in colour (heterochromia). There may also be a dilation lag — the affected pupil redilates more slowly in darkness.

Causes

The sympathetic pathway is a three-neuron chain from the hypothalamus, through the brainstem and cervical spinal cord (ciliospinal centre of Budge), out to the superior cervical ganglion, and along the carotid arteries to the eye. Lesions at any level can cause Horner syndrome: first-order causes include stroke and demyelination; second-order causes include Pancoast tumours of the lung apex, trauma, and neck surgery; third-order causes include internal carotid artery dissection, cavernous sinus lesions, and cluster headache. In children, congenital Horner syndrome may be associated with birth trauma or neuroblastoma.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is confirmed pharmacologically: cocaine drops fail to dilate the affected pupil, while apraclonidine drops reverse the anisocoria because of denervation supersensitivity of the iris dilator. Imaging is directed by the suspected level of the lesion to exclude dangerous causes such as carotid dissection and apical lung tumours.
