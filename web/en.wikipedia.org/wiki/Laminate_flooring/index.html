Laminate flooring

Laminate flooring (also called floating wood tile in the United States) is a multi-layer synthetic flooring product fused together with a lamination process. Laminate flooring simulates wood (or sometimes stone) with a photographic appliqué layer under a clear protective layer. The inner core layer is usually composed of melamine resin and fibre board materials. It is a popular alternative to solid hardwood flooring because it is cheaper, durable and relatively easy to install and maintain.

History

Laminate flooring was invented in 1977 by the Swedish company Perstorp and sold under the brand name Pergo, which has become something of a generic trademark for the product. Perstorp had been producing high-pressure decorative laminates since the 1920s, and a design team at the company developed the idea of using the material as a floor covering. The product reached the European market in the early 1980s and North America in the mid-1990s, growing rapidly in popularity thereafter.

Construction

A typical laminate plank consists of four layers, fused under high pressure:

  1. Wear layer — a transparent aluminium-oxide or melamine coating that resists scratches, stains and fading.
  2. Design layer — a high-resolution printed image of wood, stone or tile.
  3. Core layer — high-density fibreboard that gives the plank strength and moisture resistance.
  4. Backing layer — a melamine layer that stabilises the plank and protects against moisture from below.

Durability is graded by the AC (abrasion criteria) rating, from AC1 (light domestic use) to AC5 (heavy commercial use).

Installation

Most modern laminate flooring uses a click-lock tongue-and-groove system and is installed as a "floating floor" — the planks are locked to each other but not fixed to the subfloor, with an expansion gap left around the perimeter. An underlayment of foam or cork is usually laid first to dampen sound and cushion the floor. Because the planks are not nailed or glued down, damaged sections can in principle be replaced, and the material can be installed over many existing floors.

Care and limitations

Laminate is resistant to scratches and stains but vulnerable to moisture ingress through the joints: standing water can cause the fibreboard core to swell. It generally cannot be refinished like solid wood, though its photographic surface can be very realistic, including embossed textures registered to the printed grain. Concerns have been raised about formaldehyde emissions from the resins used in some products, and standards such as CARB Phase 2 and the European E1 class regulate such emissions.
