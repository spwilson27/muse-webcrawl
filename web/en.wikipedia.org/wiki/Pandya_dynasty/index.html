The Pandya dynasty, also referred to as the Pandyas of Madurai, was an ancient Tamil dynasty of South India and among the three great lineages — with the Cheras and Cholas — celebrated in Sangam literature. Ruling from the far south of the Indian peninsula, with their capital at Madurai and an early pearl-fishery port at Korkai, the Pandyas are attested from at least the 4th century BCE, when the Greek ambassador Megasthenes described a southern kingdom ruled by a queen, through to their final decline in the 16th century CE.

Early history

Sangam poems praise Pandya kings such as Nedunjeliyan, victor at the battle of Talaiyalanganam. The dynasty's fortunes were eclipsed for roughly three centuries by the Kalabhra interregnum, until Kadungon (r. c. 590–620) restored Pandya power. The Early Pandyas fought repeated wars against the Pallavas and Cholas, and their kingdom was a great centre of Tamil learning and of both Hindu devotion and Jain scholarship.

Imperial Pandyas

After a period of Chola domination in the 10th–11th centuries, the dynasty revived spectacularly under Jatavarman Sundara Pandya I (r. 1251–1268), who defeated the Cholas, Cheras and the Hoysalas, invaded Sri Lanka, and made the Pandya state the paramount power of South India. His successors controlled a lucrative trade in pearls, horses and textiles, described by the Venetian traveller Marco Polo, who visited the port of Kayal and praised the kingdom's wealth.

Decline

A succession dispute between the brothers Jatavarman Sundara Pandya III and Jatavarman Vira Pandya II fatally weakened the state. Malik Kafur, general of the Delhi Sultanate, sacked Madurai in 1311, and the short-lived Madurai Sultanate followed in 1335. The Pandyas survived as regional chiefs under Vijayanagara overlordship, fading from history by the 1600s.

Culture and architecture

The Pandyas patronised Tamil literature through the legendary Sangam academies of Madurai. In architecture they developed the towering, sculpture-covered gateway towers (gopuram) that define the Tamil temple skyline; rock-cut and structural temples at sites such as Kalugumalai, Sittanavasal (with its celebrated Jain paintings) and Tirupparankunram mark their early work, and later rulers greatly enlarged the Meenakshi–Sundareswarar temple complex at Madurai.
