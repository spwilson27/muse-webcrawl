Units of information

Base unitBit (binary digit)
Common unitByte (8 bits)
Information-theory unitNat, hartley

Units of information are measures used to quantify data in computing and information theory. The fundamental unit is the bit, a binary digit that can hold one of two values. In information theory, the amount of information in an event is measured in bits when logarithms are taken to base 2, in nats when natural logarithms are used, and in hartleys (or bans) for base 10.

Primary units

The byte, conventionally eight bits, is the standard addressable unit of memory in most computer architectures. Larger quantities are expressed with decimal prefixes (kilobyte, megabyte, gigabyte) or binary prefixes defined by the IEC (kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte), a distinction that matters because the decimal kilobyte is 1,000 bytes while the kibibyte is 1,024 bytes.

Historical units

Early computing used a variety of units, including the nibble (four bits), the word (whose size varied by machine), and decimal units such as the dit. Claude Shannon's 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" established the bit as the standard measure of information content.

Multiples

Modern storage and transmission are commonly measured in megabytes through exabytes, while specialized fields use units such as the qubit in quantum computing.
