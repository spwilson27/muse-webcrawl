Indigenous languages of the Americas

The Indigenous languages of the Americas are the languages native to North, Central and South America spoken by Indigenous peoples from before European colonisation to the present. They comprise well over a thousand historically attested languages grouped into dozens of families — such as Algonquian, Iroquoian, Siouan, Uto-Aztecan, Mayan, Quechuan, Aymaran, Tupian and Arawakan — plus many isolates, reflecting at least 15,000 years of settlement and diversification.

History and endangerment

Before 1492, tens of millions of people spoke these languages, and some — Nahuatl, Maya languages, Quechua and Guarani — served as imperial or lingua franca languages. European colonisation, disease and assimilation policies caused catastrophic decline. Today many languages are endangered or extinct, though several have large speaker communities: Quechua has millions of speakers in the Andes, Guarani is co-official and widely spoken in Paraguay, and Nahuatl, Maya, Navajo and Inuktitut each retain substantial communities. Revitalisation movements, immersion schools and official recognition are under way across the hemisphere.

Characteristics

American Indigenous languages are typologically diverse; polysynthetic languages such as Inuktitut and Mohawk can build entire sentences into single words. Writing systems ranged from Maya hieroglyphs and Aztec pictorial codices to Andean quipu knot records.
