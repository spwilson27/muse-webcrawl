Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) framework is a general framework for modelling the evolution of interest rate curves, introduced by David Heath, Robert Jarrow, and Andrew Morton in 1992.

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework is a general model for the evolution of the term structure of interest rates, developed by David Heath, Robert A. Jarrow, and Andrew Morton in papers from 1987 to 1992, notably "Bond Pricing and the Term Structure of Interest Rates: A New Methodology" (Econometrica, 1992).

The model

Unlike short-rate models that specify the dynamics of a single instantaneous rate, HJM models the entire forward rate curve f(t, T) — the rate agreed at time t for borrowing at future time T — as a stochastic process. Under the risk-neutral measure, the drift of the forward rates is not free but is determined by their volatilities: the famous HJM drift condition states that the drift equals the volatility multiplied by its integral over maturities. Consequently the model is completely specified by the initial forward curve (taken from market prices, so it fits the observed term structure exactly) and the volatility structure.

Applications and successors

The framework underlies the pricing of interest rate derivatives such as caps, swaptions and bond options. In general it is non-Markovian and infinite-dimensional, so practical implementations use finite-factor restrictions. The LIBOR market model (Brace–Gatarek–Musiela) can be viewed as a discrete-tenor descendant of HJM tailored to the rates actually quoted in the market.
