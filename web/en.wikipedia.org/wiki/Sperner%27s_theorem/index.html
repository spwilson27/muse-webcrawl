Sperner's theorem

Sperner's theorem, in combinatorics, describes the largest possible families of finite sets none of which contains any other set in the family. Such a family is called an antichain in the Boolean lattice, or a Sperner family. The theorem is named after Emanuel Sperner, who published it in 1928.

Statement

A family of subsets of an n-element set, none of which contains another, has at most C(n, floor(n/2)) members — the largest binomial coefficient for that n. This bound is achieved by taking all subsets of a fixed size k where k is as close as possible to n/2, since all subsets of the same size form an antichain and the middle level(s) of the subset lattice are the largest.

Proofs

Several proofs are known. The Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin inequality approach counts maximal chains through the family and shows the sum over members of 1/C(n, |A|) is at most 1, from which Sperner's bound follows because binomial coefficients are maximised at the middle. Other proofs use shifting (compression) techniques or the Kruskal–Katona theorem, which generalises the result.

Applications

Sperner's theorem is a foundational result of extremal set theory and underlies results in coding theory, information theory and the theory of partially ordered sets, including characterisations of the width of posets.
