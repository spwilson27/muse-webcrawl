Typical sizeAt least 10 hectares
PurposePublic access to countryside near towns and cities

A country park is an area of countryside set aside for public recreation, principally for people living in towns and cities to enjoy open space, natural surroundings and informal leisure close to home. The concept was formalised in the United Kingdom by the Countryside Act 1968, which empowered local authorities in England and Wales to designate and manage country parks; Scotland followed with similar legislation. There are now several hundred country parks in England alone.

Features

Country parks are generally at least 10 hectares in size and provide facilities such as car parks, waymarked walks, picnic areas, visitor centres, toilets and play areas, while remaining predominantly natural in character — woodland, grassland, heath, lakes and riverbanks. Many occupy former country-house estates, gravel pits, reservoirs or reclaimed industrial land. They are usually free to enter, though parking charges are common.

Hong Kong

Outside the UK, Hong Kong operates an extensive and famous system of country parks, established from 1976 under its Country Parks Ordinance. Covering about 44,000 hectares — roughly 40 per cent of the territory's land area — they protect the hills and catchments surrounding the dense urban areas, and include Sai Kung, Tai Mo Shan and the parks of Lantau Island. The MacLehose Trail and other long-distance paths run through them.

Purpose and management

Country parks serve a dual role: recreation for urban populations and conservation of landscape and wildlife. Unlike national parks, they do not usually cover large wild areas or carry strong statutory landscape protection; they are managed locally, often by county or district councils, charities or bodies such as the National Trust.
