DNA paternity testing

DNA paternity testing is the use of DNA profiles to determine whether an individual is the biological parent of another individual. Paternity testing can be especially important when the rights and duties of the father are in issue and a child’s paternity is in doubt. Tests can also determine the likelihood of someone being a biological grandparent. Though genetic testing is the most reliable standard, older methods also exist, including ABO blood group typing, analysis of other proteins and enzymes, and HLA typing.

Method

The DNA test is performed by collecting buccal (cheek) cells found on the inside of a person’s cheek using a buccal swab or mouthwash. The samples are then analysed for short tandem repeat (STR) markers — highly variable stretches of DNA that are inherited from each parent. A child’s DNA profile must receive one allele at each marker from the mother and one from the father; an alleged father who lacks the obligate paternal alleles at multiple markers is excluded, while a man who matches is reported with a probability of paternity typically exceeding 99.9 per cent. Mitochondrial DNA sequencing is occasionally used, particularly where samples are degraded, and legal tests follow a documented chain of custody so that results are admissible in court.

History

Early paternity testing relied on blood group inheritance and could only exclude a wrongly accused man, never positively confirm paternity. The introduction of DNA fingerprinting by Alec Jeffreys in 1984–85 transformed the field, and the first commercial DNA paternity tests became available in the late 1980s. Direct-to-consumer home testing kits became widespread in the 2000s, sold in pharmacies and online, although results from kits without verified identity and chain of custody are generally used for personal knowledge only.

Applications and law

Paternity tests are used in child support and custody proceedings, immigration cases, inheritance disputes, and adoption. Laws governing testing vary by jurisdiction: some countries, such as France, restrict private paternity testing outside court proceedings, while others regulate the consent required from each party. Testing can also be performed before birth, either invasively (amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling) or, increasingly, non-invasively using fetal DNA circulating in the mother’s blood.
