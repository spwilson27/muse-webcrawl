The Northern and Southern dynasties (Chinese: 南北朝; pinyin: Nán-Běi Cháo; 420–589) was a period of disunity in Chinese history that followed the Eastern Jin and the Sixteen Kingdoms and preceded the reunification of China under the Sui dynasty. During the period, the north was ruled by a succession of non-Han and Sinicised nomadic dynasties, while the south was ruled by a sequence of ethnically Han courts centred on Jiankang (modern Nanjing).

Political history

In 420, Liu Yu, a general of the Eastern Jin, deposed the last Jin emperor and founded the Liu Song (420–479), the first of four Southern dynasties: Liu Song, Southern Qi (479–502), Liang (502–557), and Chen (557–589). These courts maintained the Han cultural tradition in the south and presided over the economic development of the Yangtze region.

In the north, after the fragmentation of the Sixteen Kingdoms, the Northern Wei (386–534) — founded by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei people — unified northern China by 439 under Emperor Taiwu. The Northern Wei vigorously promoted Buddhism, sponsored the carving of the Yungang and Longmen grottoes, and under Emperor Xiaowen (r. 471–499) imposed Sinicisation reforms, moving the capital to Luoyang and adopting Han surnames, language, and dress. The dynasty split in 534 into Eastern and Western Wei, succeeded in 550 and 557 by the Northern Qi and Northern Zhou. In 581 Yang Jian, a Northern Zhou general, founded the Sui; his son Emperor Wen reunified China in 589 by conquering the Chen.

Culture and society

Despite political fragmentation, the period was culturally fertile. Buddhism took deep root, reshaping Chinese art, philosophy, and social life; the great cave-temple complexes at Yungang, Longmen, and Dunhuang date largely from this era. The north–south division fostered distinctive regional cultures, while continuous warfare and the collapse of the old aristocratic order opened careers to men of humbler birth. Agricultural colonisation of the south and the introduction of new crops supported rapid population growth there, and the north developed heavy military organisation and pastoral-nomadic traditions blended with Han institutions.
