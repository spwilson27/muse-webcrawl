Peace of Münster

Signed30 January 1648; ratified 15 May 1648
LocationMünster, Westphalia
PartiesSpain; the Dutch Republic
EffectSpain recognises Dutch independence; ends the Eighty Years' War
ContextPart of the Peace of Westphalia

The Peace of Münster is the treaty between the Kingdom of Spain and the Lords States General of the United Netherlands, signed on 30 January 1648 and ratified on 15 May 1648 in Münster, Westphalia. By it, Spain formally recognised the independence of the Dutch Republic, bringing the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) to an end. Together with the Treaties of Münster and Osnabrück of October 1648, which ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire, it forms the Peace of Westphalia, a landmark in the history of European diplomacy and the modern state system.

Background

The northern provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands had revolted against Philip II of Spain in 1568. They abjured Spanish sovereignty in 1581 and organised themselves as the Republic of the Seven United Netherlands, but Spain never conceded their independence, and war — interrupted by the Twelve Years' Truce of 1609–1621, during which the Republic was treated as a de facto sovereign — consumed enormous resources on both sides. By the 1640s, with Spain also at war with France and its finances exhausted, and Dutch opinion divided between a war party around the stadtholder and peace-minded regents of Holland, negotiations opened at Münster in 1646 as part of the general Westphalian peace congress.

Terms

Spain recognised the United Provinces as "free and sovereign states" over which it claimed nothing. The frontier essentially followed the military situation: the Republic kept its conquests in Brabant and Flanders (the "Generality Lands") and its overseas gains against the Portuguese (then under the Spanish crown), including in Brazil and the East and West Indies trade — trade concessions confirmed Dutch merchants' access. The Scheldt remained closed to Antwerp's commerce, to Amsterdam's benefit. Catholics in the Generality Lands were not granted public worship, despite Spanish hopes. The treaty comprised 79 articles; the oath of ratification was sworn in Münster's town hall on 15 May 1648, an event immortalised in Gerard ter Borch's small masterpiece showing the Dutch envoys raising their hands.

Significance

The Peace ratified a transformation already achieved by arms: a small republic of merchants had defeated the greatest empire of the age and stood at the apex of its Golden Age. For Spain it registered relative decline, though it retained the Southern Netherlands. In Dutch memory the treaty is a founding document of national independence — 1648, not 1581, became the conventional date of the Republic's recognised birth — and the Münster ratification scene one of the most reproduced images of Dutch history.
