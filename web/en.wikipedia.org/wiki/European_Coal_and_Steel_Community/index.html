The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) was a European organisation created after the Second World War to integrate and regulate the coal and steel industries of its member states. Established by the Treaty of Paris in 1951 and in force from 1952, it united France, West Germany, Italy and the Benelux countries — Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg — under a common market for coal and steel administered by supranational institutions. The ECSC was the first organisation based on the principles of supranationalism and is regarded as the foundation of the modern European Union.

Origins

The community grew out of the Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950, in which French foreign minister Robert Schuman proposed placing Franco-German coal and steel production under a single High Authority. The plan, devised largely by Jean Monnet, aimed to make war between France and Germany "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible" by pooling the raw materials of war-making. It also sought to revive European industry and bind West Germany peacefully into Western Europe.

Institutions

The ECSC's institutions prefigured those of the later European Communities: a supranational High Authority (the forerunner of the European Commission), a Council of Ministers, a Common Assembly (forerunner of the European Parliament), and a Court of Justice. The High Authority, first presided over by Monnet, could take decisions binding on member states, levy a tax on coal and steel production — the first European tax — and enforce competition rules.

History and legacy

The common market for coal opened in February 1953 and for steel in May 1953; internal tariffs and quotas were abolished, and trade among the Six grew rapidly. The ECSC's success encouraged the same states to sign the Treaties of Rome in 1957, creating the European Economic Community and Euratom. The three communities' institutions were merged in 1967. The Treaty of Paris was concluded for 50 years, and the ECSC duly expired on 23 July 2002; its assets and functions were absorbed by the European Community, and its remaining funds were devoted to research in the coal and steel sectors.
