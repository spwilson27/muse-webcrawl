Protease inhibitor (biology)

In biology and biochemistry, protease inhibitors, or antiproteases, are molecules that inhibit the function of proteases (enzymes that cut peptide bonds in proteins). Many naturally occurring protease inhibitors are proteins.

Occurrence and function

Protease inhibitors are found in all forms of life. In animals, they regulate processes such as blood clotting, complement activation and inflammation: alpha-1 antitrypsin protects lung tissue from neutrophil elastase, and serpins (serine protease inhibitors) control cascades of proteolysis in the blood. Plants accumulate protease inhibitors in seeds and tubers, where they deter insect pests by blocking digestive enzymes. Microorganisms produce inhibitors such as leupeptin and pepstatin.

Classification

Inhibitors are grouped by the class of protease they target — serine, cysteine, aspartic, metallo- and threonine proteases — or by their structural families, for example serpins, Kazal-type inhibitors, Kunitz-type inhibitors and cystatins. Many act as tight-binding, substrate-like molecules that occupy the enzyme's active site; others bind exosites or act through conformational trapping, as alpha-2-macroglobulin does.

Medical significance

Imbalance between proteases and their inhibitors underlies diseases including emphysema (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency), pancreatitis and some cancers. Engineered protease inhibitors are important drugs: HIV protease inhibitors transformed AIDS therapy, and inhibitors of the proteasome and of viral polymerases-associated proteases are used against cancer and hepatitis C.
