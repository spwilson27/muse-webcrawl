The Albian is, in the international geological timescale, the sixth and youngest age of the Early Cretaceous Epoch, spanning the interval from about 113.0 to 100.5 million years ago. It follows the Aptian and is followed by the Cenomanian, the first age of the Late Cretaceous.

Definition

The stage was proposed in 1842 by the French palaeontologist Alcide d'Orbigny, who named it after Alba, the Latin name of the River Aube in France. Its base (GSSP) is defined at the Col de Pré-Guittard section in Drôme, south-eastern France, by the first appearance of the planktonic foraminiferan Microhedbergella renilaevis; the top is marked by the first appearance of the foraminiferan Rotalipora globotruncanoides.

Fauna and flora

During the Albian the great marine transgression of the Cretaceous continued, and widespread chalk and greensand deposits accumulated — the Gault Clay and Upper Greensand of southern England date from this age. Dinosaurs such as the iguanodontians and early hadrosauroids diversified, the first snakes appear in the fossil record toward its end, and flowering plants (angiosperms) rose toward ecological dominance on land.
