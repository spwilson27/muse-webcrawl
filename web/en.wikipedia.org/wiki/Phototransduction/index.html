Phototransduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The phototransduction cascade in a rod cell.

Phototransduction (more specifically visual phototransduction) is the process by which light energy is converted into electrical signals in the photoreceptor cells of the retina — the rods and cones. It is a biochemical cascade that amplifies the signal: a single photon can produce a measurable response in a rod.

The cascade

In darkness, cyclic GMP (cGMP) keeps sodium channels in the rod outer segment open, and the cell is depolarised, steadily releasing the neurotransmitter glutamate. When a photon is absorbed by the photopigment rhodopsin, its chromophore 11-cis-retinal isomerises to all-trans-retinal, converting rhodopsin to its active form (metarhodopsin II). This activates the G protein transducin, which activates a phosphodiesterase (PDE6) that hydrolyses cGMP. Falling cGMP closes the channels, hyperpolarising the cell and reducing glutamate release — the electrical signal that begins vision. Recovery is mediated by rhodopsin kinase, arrestin, and calcium feedback through recoverin and guanylyl cyclase.

Significance

Vertebrate phototransduction is one of the best-understood G-protein signalling pathways and a model for signal transduction generally. Mutations in its components cause retinal diseases including retinitis pigmentosa, congenital stationary night blindness, and achromatopsia. Invertebrates such as fruit flies use a distinct cascade in which light opens TRP channels and depolarises the photoreceptor.
