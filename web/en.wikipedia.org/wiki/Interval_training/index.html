Interval training

Interval training is a type of training exercise that involves a series of high-intensity workouts interspersed with rest or relief periods. The high-intensity periods are typically at or close to anaerobic exercise, while the recovery periods involve activity of lower intensity, allowing the athlete to accumulate more total work at high intensity than continuous exercise would permit.

History

Interval methods were systematised in the 1930s by German coach Woldemar Gerschler and physiologist Hans Reindell, and popularised by runners such as Emil Zátopek. The Swedish fartlek ("speed play") method, developed by Gösta Holmér, is a less structured relative. Modern variants include high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and protocols such as the Tabata method of 20-second efforts with 10-second rests.

Physiology

Interval training improves maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max), anaerobic capacity, and metabolic efficiency. Research indicates that short interval sessions can produce cardiovascular and muscular adaptations comparable to much longer moderate continuous exercise, though effects vary with protocol and population.

Practice

Sessions are described by work interval duration and intensity, recovery duration, and the number of repetitions. Interval training is used in running, cycling, swimming, rowing, and general fitness programmes, but the high intensity demands adequate recovery and carries elevated injury risk if progressed too quickly.
