Tangent

Tangent
Geometry
Related concepts: Circle, Secant line, Derivative

In geometry, the tangent line (or simply tangent) to a plane curve at a given point is the straight line that "just touches" the curve at that point. Leibniz defined it as the line through a pair of infinitely close points on the curve. More precisely, a straight line is said to be a tangent of a curve y = f(x) at a point x = c if the line passes through the point (c, f(c)) on the curve and has slope f'(c), where f' is the derivative of f.

As a point moves along a curve, the tangent line is the best linear approximation to the curve near that input value. For this reason, the tangent line is often called the linear approximation or best linear approximation of the function at that point (also known as the Taylor series of degree one).

Tangent to a circle

A tangent line to a circle is a line that touches the circle at exactly one point, called the point of tangency, and never enters the circle's interior. The radius of the circle drawn to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line. It follows from this that tangent segments drawn from a common external point to a circle have equal lengths.

History

Euclid makes several references to the tangent (Greek: ἐφαπτομένη ephaptoménē) to a circle in Book III of the Elements (c. 300 BC). Apollonius of Perga defined a tangent in relation to conic sections. In the 17th century, Pierre de Fermat, René Descartes, and others developed algebraic methods for finding tangents, laying groundwork for the infinitesimal calculus of Newton and Leibniz.

Generalizations

The tangent concept extends to surfaces in space: the tangent plane to a surface at a point is the plane that "just touches" the surface there. For a differentiable manifold, the generalization is the tangent space, an abstract vector space that captures the directions in which one can pass through the point.
