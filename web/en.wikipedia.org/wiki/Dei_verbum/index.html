Dei verbum (Latin for "the Word of God") is the Second Vatican Council's Dogmatic Constitution on Divine Revelation. Promulgated by Pope Paul VI on 18 November 1965, it is one of the principal documents of the council and the Catholic Church's most authoritative modern teaching on Scripture, Tradition, and revelation.

Content

The constitution teaches that God reveals himself fully in Jesus Christ, and that this revelation is transmitted in the Church through both Sacred Scripture and Sacred Tradition, which together form a single deposit of faith interpreted by the Church's magisterium. It affirms the divine inspiration and inerrancy of the Bible in matters of salvation, while insisting that the biblical books must be read according to their literary forms and historical contexts — an endorsement of the historical-critical method that followed Pius XII's Divino afflante Spiritu (1943).

Key points

  • Revelation is God's self-communication in deeds and words, culminating in Christ, not merely a set of propositions.
  • Scripture and Tradition are not two independent sources but two modes of transmitting the one Gospel.
  • Easy access to Scripture should be provided to all the faithful, encouraging translations from the original languages and lay Bible reading and study.
  • The Gospels' historical character is affirmed, while acknowledging the stages of tradition between Jesus and the written text.

Drafting history

An early schema, De fontibus revelationis, was famously rejected by the council fathers in November 1962 — a turning point of Vatican II — and a mixed commission including Cardinal Augustin Bea rewrote it in a more biblical and ecumenical spirit before its final approval by 2,344 votes to 6.
