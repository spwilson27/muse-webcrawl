Low birth weight

Low birth weight (LBW) is defined by the World Health Organization as a birth weight of an infant of 2,499 g (5 lb 8.1 oz) or less, regardless of gestational age. Infants born with a weight below 1,500 g are classified as very low birth weight (VLBW), and those below 1,000 g as extremely low birth weight (ELBW). Low birth weight is either the result of preterm birth (before 37 weeks of gestation) or of an infant being small for gestational age because of intrauterine growth restriction, or both.

Causes and risk factors

Risk factors for low birth weight include maternal undernutrition, anaemia and infections (including malaria and HIV in endemic regions), high blood pressure and pre-eclampsia, smoking and alcohol use during pregnancy, adolescent pregnancy, multiple pregnancy (twins and higher-order multiples), a short interval between pregnancies, and low maternal weight gain. In high-income countries the dominant cause is preterm birth; in low-income countries intrauterine growth restriction contributes a larger share.

Health consequences

Low birth weight infants face increased risks of neonatal death, hypothermia, feeding difficulties, infections and respiratory distress syndrome in the short term. In the longer term, low birth weight is associated with impaired growth and cognitive development, and — according to the developmental origins of health and disease hypothesis — with higher rates of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes in adult life. More than 80% of neonatal deaths worldwide occur in low birth weight infants.

Prevention and care

Interventions include adequate maternal nutrition and micronutrient supplementation, malaria prevention, smoking cessation, treatment of infections and hypertension, and good antenatal care. For the newborn, recommended care includes keeping the baby warm — notably through kangaroo mother care (continuous skin-to-skin contact), which reduces mortality in low birth weight infants — support for early and exclusive breastfeeding, and prompt treatment of infections. The World Health Assembly set a global target of a 30% reduction in low birth weight by 2025 relative to 2012 levels.
