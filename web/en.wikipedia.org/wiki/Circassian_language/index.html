Circassian language

Circassian written in the Cyrillic-based alphabet.

Circassian, also known as Cherkess, is a language continuum spoken by the Circassians, an indigenous people of the northwestern Caucasus. It comprises two literary standards: Adyghe (West Circassian) and Kabardian (East Circassian), which are highly mutually intelligible and are sometimes treated as dialects of a single Circassian language. Together they have roughly half a million to eight hundred thousand speakers, concentrated in the Russian republics of Adygea, Kabardino-Balkaria, and Karachay-Cherkessia, and in a large diaspora in Turkey, Jordan, Syria, and elsewhere that descends from Circassians expelled from the Caucasus in 1864.

Classification and features

Circassian belongs to the Northwest Caucasian (Abkhaz-Adyghe) family, alongside Abkhaz, Abaza, and the extinct Ubykh. The languages are famous for their enormous consonant inventories — Kabardian has some 50 consonants and Adyghe more still in some dialects — combined with very small vowel systems (often analysed as two or three vowels). Verbs are highly complex and polysynthetic, packing prefixes for subject, object, direction, location, and negation around a root, and the languages are ergative in alignment.

Writing and status

Circassian languages were historically written in Arabic script; Latin-based alphabets were devised in the 1920s, replaced by Cyrillic-based alphabets in the late 1930s, which remain in official use in Russia. Adyghe is an official language of Adygea and Kabardian of Kabardino-Balkaria and Karachay-Cherkessia, used in schools, media, and literature, though UNESCO classes the Circassian varieties as vulnerable or endangered, particularly in the diaspora where transmission to children is weakening.
