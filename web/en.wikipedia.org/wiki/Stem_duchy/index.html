Stem duchy

A stem duchy (German: Stammesherzogtum) was a constituent duchy of the German kingdom in the early and high Middle Ages. The stem duchies emerged from the older tribal territories of the Germanic peoples — the Saxons, Franconians, Bavarians, Swabians and Thuringians — as the power of the Carolingian dynasty waned in the late ninth and early tenth centuries.

The five classic stem duchies of the Ottonian realm were the duchies of Saxony, Franconia, Bavaria, Swabia and Lorraine. Led by dukes who were often the king's rivals as much as his subordinates, they formed the principal building blocks of the East Frankish, later German, kingdom under the Ottonian and Salian dynasties.

Origins

After the division of the Carolingian Empire at the Treaty of Verdun (843), East Francia developed along lines that roughly followed older tribal settlement areas. As royal authority weakened during the invasions of the Vikings, Magyars and Saracens, local counts and margraves accumulated power. By about 911, when the last Carolingian king of East Francia died, the magnates of the five stem duchies effectively elected the new king, Conrad of Franconia, and thereafter Henry the Fowler, duke of Saxony.

The dukes and the crown

The stem dukes retained quasi-royal rights within their territories, including command of the tribal levy and leadership of their people's contingent in war. Henry I and Otto I sought to bind the duchies to the crown by appointing close relatives and loyal followers, and Otto's brother Bruno combined the roles of archbishop of Cologne and duke of Lorraine. Nevertheless, rebellions by dukes such as Eberhard of Franconia (939) and Liudolf of Swabia (953–954) showed the limits of royal control.

Decline

From the later eleventh century the stem duchies fragmented as counts, bishops and lesser dynasts built up their own territorial lordships. The fall of Henry the Lion in 1180, which dismembered Saxony and Bavaria into smaller principalities, is often taken as the end of the stem-duchy era, though the ideal of tribal duchies continued to shape the political geography of the Holy Roman Empire through the territories of the later electoral states.
