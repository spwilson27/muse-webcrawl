Sharpening

Sharpening is the process of creating or refining a sharp edge on a tool or implement — such as a knife, chisel, axe, saw or drill bit — by grinding material away with an abrasive surface. A freshly sharpened edge consists of two bevels meeting at a fine apex; dullness results when the apex is worn, rolled or chipped.

Methods

The commonest sharpening medium is the sharpening stone, or whetstone, used wet or dry and graded from coarse (for repairing damage and setting a bevel) to fine (for refining and polishing the edge). The blade is drawn across the stone at a controlled angle, typically 15–20 degrees per side for kitchen knives, raising a burr that is then worked down on progressively finer stones. Honing with a steel does not remove significant material but realigns a rolled edge, while stropping on leather charged with abrasive paste gives a final polish.

Other methods include bench grinders and belt sanders (fast, but risk overheating the steel and destroying its temper), guided jig systems that fix the angle, ceramic rods, diamond plates, and pull-through sharpeners. Industrial blades may be sharpened by dedicated grinding machines or by specialist services.

Edge geometry

Sharpening is a trade-off between sharpness and durability: thin, acute edges cut with little force but chip and roll easily, while obtuse edges withstand abuse. Different tools therefore take different edge forms — hollow, flat, convex or chisel grinds, single or double bevels — and serrated edges require specialised stones or files that fit the serrations.
