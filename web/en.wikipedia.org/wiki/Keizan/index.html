Keizan Jōkin (1268–1325), often known simply as Keizan, was a Japanese Zen Buddhist monk regarded as the second patriarch of the Sōtō school of Zen in Japan, after its founder Dōgen. Keizan is credited with expanding Sōtō Zen from the remote monastery of Eihei-ji into a popular movement that spread throughout Japan, blending Zen practice with elements of esoteric Buddhism and local religious practice.

Life

Keizan was born in 1268. Tradition holds that his mother was a devoted worshipper of Kannon (Avalokiteśvara) and that he was ordained as a young boy. He studied under Jakuen and later under Tettsū Gikai, a third-generation disciple in Dōgen's lineage, from whom he received dharma transmission. He became abbot of Daijō-ji in Kaga province, and in 1321 he founded Sōji-ji on the Noto Peninsula (the temple was later moved to Tsurumi, Yokohama, in 1911), which became one of the two head temples of the Sōtō school alongside Eihei-ji.

Teachings and works

Keizan's best-known work is the Denkoroku ("Record of Transmitting the Light"), a collection of fifty-two biographies tracing the transmission of the Zen lamp from Śākyamuni Buddha through the Indian and Chinese patriarchs to Dōgen and his successor Ejō. He also composed the Keizan Shingi, regulations for monastic life, and the Zazen Yōjinki ("Notes on What to Be Aware of in Zazen"). Under Keizan and his disciples, especially Gasan Jōseki, Sōtō Zen developed the five-households (gosō) system that structured the school's subsequent expansion.
