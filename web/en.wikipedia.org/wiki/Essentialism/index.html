Essentialism

Essentialism is the view that objects have a set of attributes that are necessary to their identity. In early Western thought, Plato's idealism held that all things have such an "essence"—an "idea" or "form". In Categories, Aristotle similarly proposed that all objects have a substance that, as George Lakoff put it, "make the thing what it is, and without which it would be not that kind of thing".

In philosophy

Essentialism has been influential in metaphysics, philosophy of language and philosophy of science. Saul Kripke and Hilary Putnam defended a form of essentialism about natural kinds: substances such as water have underlying essences (for example, H2O) discovered by science, and terms refer to those kinds in every possible world. Critics in the analytic tradition, following Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance, argue that many categories have no defining essence.

In biology and psychology

Ernst Mayr argued that pre-Darwinian taxonomy was hampered by "typological thinking"—an essentialist view of species—and that Darwinian population thinking replaced it. In developmental psychology, "psychological essentialism" describes the tendency, present from early childhood, to treat categories as if they were defined by hidden, inherent natures.

In social thought

In discussions of gender, race and identity, essentialism denotes the assumption that such groups share fixed, inherent characteristics. Feminist and post-structuralist writers have often criticised essentialist accounts, though "strategic essentialism"—the provisional adoption of a group identity for political purposes—was proposed by Gayatri Spivak.
