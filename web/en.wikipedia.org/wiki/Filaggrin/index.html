Filaggrin

Filaggrin (filament aggregating protein) is a filament-associated protein that binds to keratin fibers in epithelial cells. It is essential for the formation and hydration of the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the skin, and hence for the skin barrier function. Filaggrin is encoded by the FLG gene, located within the epidermal differentiation complex on chromosome 1q21.

Structure and processing

Filaggrin is first synthesised as profilaggrin, a giant, highly phosphorylated precursor of over 400 kDa stored in keratohyalin granules of the granular layer of the epidermis. Profilaggrin contains 10–12 tandem filaggrin repeats joined by linker peptides. During the transition of keratinocytes into corneocytes, profilaggrin is dephosphorylated and proteolytically cleaved to release individual filaggrin monomers. These aggregate keratin intermediate filaments into tight bundles, collapsing the cell into the flattened shape characteristic of corneocytes. Later, filaggrin is degraded by enzymes including caspase-14, calpain-1, and bleomycin hydrolase into free amino acids and their derivatives — such as pyrrolidone carboxylic acid and urocanic acid — which together form much of the natural moisturizing factor (NMF) that keeps the stratum corneum hydrated and acidic.

Clinical significance

Loss-of-function mutations in FLG are the strongest known genetic risk factor for atopic dermatitis (eczema). Two mutations, R501X and 2282del4, are especially common in people of European ancestry, carried by roughly 9% of the population. Filaggrin deficiency impairs the skin barrier, increasing transepidermal water loss and allowing allergens and microbes to penetrate, which is thought to promote allergic sensitisation and the "atopic march" toward food allergy, allergic rhinitis, and asthma.

Homozygous or compound heterozygous FLG loss-of-function mutations cause ichthyosis vulgaris, the most common inherited disorder of keratinisation, characterised by dry, scaly skin, palmar hyperlinearity, and keratosis pilaris. Filaggrin mutations have also been associated with increased risk of hand eczema, contact sensitisation, and more severe asthma in the presence of eczema.

History

Filaggrin was first characterised in the late 1970s and early 1980s from its ability to aggregate keratin filaments in vitro. The landmark association between FLG mutations, ichthyosis vulgaris, and atopic dermatitis was reported in 2006 by the group of W. H. Irwin McLean in Dundee, reshaping understanding of eczema as a barrier-driven disease.
