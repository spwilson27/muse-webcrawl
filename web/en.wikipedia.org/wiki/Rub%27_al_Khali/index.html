Rub' al Khali

The Rub' al Khali (Arabic: ٱلرُّبْع ٱلْخَالِي, "the Empty Quarter") is the largest contiguous sand desert in the world, covering most of the southern third of the Arabian Peninsula. It spans some 650,000 square kilometres across Saudi Arabia, Oman, the United Arab Emirates and Yemen.

Geography

The desert is roughly 1,000 km long and 500 km wide. Its dunes, some rising 250 m, are interspersed with gravel plains and salt flats (sabkhas). Summer temperatures can exceed 50 °C, and rainfall is among the lowest on Earth, often under 35 mm a year. Beneath the sands lie major oil fields, including Shaybah and the fringes of Ghawar, the world's largest conventional oil field.

Exploration

Bertram Thomas made the first recorded crossing by a European in 1930–31, followed in 1932 by Harry St John Philby. Wilfred Thesiger crossed it twice in the 1940s with Bedu companions, an experience recounted in his classic Arabian Sands (1959). The desert was historically a barrier to travel, skirted by the frankincense routes of antiquity; the legendary lost city of Ubar has been associated with its southern fringes.
