English possessive

In English, the possessive is a form of a noun or pronoun that indicates possession or a close relationship. It is most often formed with the ending -'s (as in "the cat's whiskers"), traditionally called the possessive case or genitive case. Personal pronouns have distinct possessive forms: determiners such as my, your, his, her, its, our, their and independent forms such as mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs.

Formation

For singular nouns and plurals not ending in -s, the possessive adds -'s (child's, children's). For regular plurals ending in -s, only an apostrophe is added (the cats' whiskers). Style guides differ on singular nouns already ending in -s, giving forms such as Charles's or Charles'. The possessive marker attaches to the end of a whole noun phrase — "the King of Spain's daughter" — which has led many linguists to analyse -'s as a clitic rather than a case inflection.

History

The ending descends from the Old English genitive singular -es. In the Early Modern period a folk etymology reinterpreted it as a contraction of his (yielding spellings like "the king his crown"), which helped entrench the apostrophe in its modern use. The possessive apostrophe's placement was standardised only in the 19th century, and confusion with plurals remains common — the erroneous "greengrocer's apostrophe".

Uses beyond possession

The possessive expresses many relations besides ownership: origin ("Shakespeare's plays"), measure ("a day's work"), part–whole relations and subjective or objective roles in nominalisations ("the city's destruction"). A related construction, the double possessive ("a friend of mine"), combines a possessive with an of-phrase.
