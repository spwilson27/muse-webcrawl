Dancehall is a genre of Jamaican popular music that originated in the late 1970s. Named after the dance halls where local sound systems played to working-class crowds, it developed from roots reggae into a faster, sparser, and eventually fully digital style, distinguished by deejays "toasting" or rapping over prerecorded rhythm tracks called riddims.

History

Origins (1979–1985)

As roots reggae's international moment passed, producers such as Henry "Junjo" Lawes and bands like the Roots Radics stripped rhythms down for the dancehall audience, with deejays including Yellowman, Barrington Levy, and Eek-A-Mouse filling the space with witty, topical, and sometimes deliberately outrageous ("slack") lyrics. Engineers such as Scientist shaped the era's heavy sound at Channel One and King Tubby's.

Digital revolution (1985–)

In 1985 Wayne Smith's "Under Mi Sleng Teng", built by King Jammy on a Casio keyboard preset, launched the digital era: cheap synthesized riddims replaced studio bands, and one riddim could voice dozens of songs. Shabba Ranks carried dancehall to global stardom in the early 1990s, followed by Buju Banton, Beenie Man, and Bounty Killer. Sean Paul's 2000s hits brought the genre to the top of international charts, and dancehall's rhythms and vocal styles were absorbed into reggaeton, hip-hop, Afrobeats, and pop worldwide. Artists such as Vybz Kartel, Popcaan, Spice, and Shenseea have defined its modern era.

Characteristics and culture

Dancehall culture centers on the sound system session: selectors, deejays, elaborate fashion, and an ever-evolving vocabulary of dances created by figures like Bogle. Its lyrics range from social commentary and Rastafari spirituality to boastful "gun talk" and explicit slackness, periodically attracting controversy and censorship debates in Jamaica and abroad.
