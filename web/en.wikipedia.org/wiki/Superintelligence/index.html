Superintelligence

A superintelligence is a hypothetical agent that possesses intelligence far surpassing that of the brightest and most gifted human minds. Philosopher Nick Bostrom, whose 2014 book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies popularised the concept, defines it as "any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest."

Forms

Bostrom distinguishes three forms of superintelligence:

  • Speed superintelligence — a system that can do everything a human mind can do, but much faster.
  • Collective superintelligence — a network of smaller intelligences whose combined performance greatly exceeds any single cognitive system.
  • Quality superintelligence — a system at least as fast as a human mind and far smarter in a qualitative sense.

Paths to superintelligence

Proposed routes include advanced artificial intelligence, whole brain emulation (uploading), brain–computer interfaces, genetic enhancement, and networks of augmented humans and computers. Some theorists argue that a sufficiently advanced AI could undergo an "intelligence explosion" — a recursive cycle of self-improvement first described by I. J. Good in 1965 — rapidly outstripping human capability.

Risks and the control problem

Superintelligence is central to debates about existential risk from artificial intelligence. The control problem concerns how to ensure that a superintelligent system's goals remain aligned with human values. Bostrom's "orthogonality thesis" holds that intelligence and final goals are independent — a system can be arbitrarily intelligent while pursuing arbitrary objectives — and the "instrumental convergence" argument suggests that many goal systems would converge on similar instrumental aims, such as self-preservation and resource acquisition. Researchers in AI alignment study these problems, while critics dispute the likelihood of the scenarios or the feasibility of proposed safeguards.
