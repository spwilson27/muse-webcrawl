Denarius

A silver denarius of Augustus, the standard coin of Roman currency for centuries

The denarius (plural denarii) was the standard Roman silver coin from its introduction in the Second Punic War, around 211 BC, until the reign of Gordian III in the mid-3rd century AD, when it was gradually replaced by the antoninianus. Its name survives in the word for money in several languages and in the symbol "d." for the pre-decimal British penny.

History

First struck at about 4.5 grams of nearly pure silver, the denarius was tariffed at ten asses — hence its name, from deni, "containing ten" — and later at sixteen asses after retariffing around 141 BC. The coin became the backbone of Roman taxation, military pay and everyday commerce across the empire; a denarius is traditionally identified as the "tribute penny" of the New Testament.

Debasement

Successive emperors reduced the denarius's weight and silver content to finance expenditure — Nero began the process, and by the 240s the coin contained barely half silver. Progressive debasement fed inflation and undermined confidence, leading Caracalla to introduce the double-denarius antoninianus in 215 AD, which soon displaced its predecessor.
