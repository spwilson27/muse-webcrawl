V. Gordon Childe

Vere Gordon Childe (14 April 1892 – 19 October 1957) was an Australian archaeologist who specialised in the study of European prehistory. He was one of the most influential archaeologists of the twentieth century, known for his excavation of the Neolithic settlement of Skara Brae in Orkney and for coining the concepts of the Neolithic Revolution and the Urban Revolution.

Life and career

Born in Sydney, Childe studied at the University of Sydney and then at the University of Oxford. Politically active as a socialist, he worked for a time as private secretary to the New South Wales premier John Storey and published How Labour Governs (1923), an analysis of the labour movement. Turning to archaeology, he travelled extensively in Europe and in 1927 became the first Abercromby Professor of Archaeology at the University of Edinburgh. From 1946 until his retirement in 1956 he directed the Institute of Archaeology in London.

Work

In works such as The Dawn of European Civilization (1925) and Man Makes Himself (1936), Childe synthesised the prehistory of Europe and the Near East, interpreting cultural change through a Marxist-influenced materialist framework. His "revolutions" model — agriculture transforming human society in the Neolithic, followed by the rise of cities, writing, and metallurgy in the Urban Revolution — shaped archaeological thought for decades. His popular books reached a wide readership, and his later What Happened in History (1942) became a Penguin bestseller.

Death and legacy

After retiring, Childe returned to Australia, where he died in 1957 after falling from Govett's Leap in the Blue Mountains; a letter he left indicated he had taken his own life, fearing senility. His emphasis on synthesis, economic forces, and broad comparison remains influential, and his books are still read as classics of archaeological literature.
