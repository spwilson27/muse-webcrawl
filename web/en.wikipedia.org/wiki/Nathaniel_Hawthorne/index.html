Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864) was an American novelist and short-story writer, a central figure of nineteenth-century American literature. Born in Salem, Massachusetts — a descendant of a judge at the Salem witch trials, a legacy that haunted his work — he wrote dark, allegorical fiction exploring sin, guilt and inherited moral burdens in Puritan New England.

Life and career

Hawthorne graduated from Bowdoin College in 1825, where his classmates included Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and future president Franklin Pierce. After years of near-seclusion writing tales, he published Twice-Told Tales (1837) and worked at the Boston Custom House and at the Brook Farm utopian community to support himself. He married Sophia Peabody in 1842. A surveyor's post at the Salem Custom House, lost to a political change, freed him to write his masterpiece, The Scarlet Letter (1850), followed by The House of the Seven Gables (1851), The Blithedale Romance (1852) and The Marble Faun (1860). In gratitude for a campaign biography of Pierce, he was appointed United States consul at Liverpool, serving from 1853 to 1857.

Themes and legacy

Hawthorne's tales, such as "Young Goodman Brown" and "The Minister's Black Veil", and his novels revolve around secret sin, ancestral guilt, and the ambiguity of moral judgement, rendered in a rich, symbolic prose. He added the "w" to his birth surname Hathorne, apparently to distance himself from his witch-trial ancestor. A close friend of Herman Melville, who dedicated Moby-Dick to him, Hawthorne died in 1864 while travelling in New Hampshire and is buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, Concord. His work helped define the American romance and remains a staple of the national canon.
