Khmer Rouge Tribunal

Official name: Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC)
Location: Phnom Penh, Cambodia
Established: 2003 (agreement); operational from 2006
Mandate: Trial of senior Khmer Rouge leaders for crimes committed 1975–1979

The Khmer Rouge Tribunal, formally the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC), is a hybrid court established by an agreement between the Royal Government of Cambodia and the United Nations to try the senior leaders and those most responsible for the crimes committed during the Khmer Rouge regime of Democratic Kampuchea (17 April 1975 – 6 January 1979), under which an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people died.

Structure

The ECCC is a Cambodian court with international participation: Cambodian and foreign judges, co-prosecutors and co-investigating judges sit together, and supermajority voting rules are designed to prevent either side from dominating decisions. Its jurisdiction covers genocide, crimes against humanity, grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions, and offences under the 1956 Cambodian Penal Code.

Cases

  • Case 001 — Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), commandant of the S-21 prison at Tuol Sleng, was convicted in 2010 of crimes against humanity and war crimes; on appeal in 2012 his sentence was increased to life imprisonment. He died in 2020.
  • Case 002 — Nuon Chea, the regime's chief ideologue, and Khieu Samphan, its head of state, were tried in two phases. Both received life sentences for crimes against humanity in 2014 and for genocide against the Vietnamese (and, for Nuon Chea, the Cham) in 2018. Nuon Chea died in 2019; Khieu Samphan's appeal was rejected in 2022, making his conviction the tribunal's final verdict.
  • Cases 003 and 004 — investigations of mid-level commanders Meas Muth, Yim Tith and Ao An were controversially dismissed amid disagreement between the court's national and international sides and Cambodian government opposition.

Legacy

The tribunal convicted three people over its life and cost more than US$300 million, drawing criticism for its expense, slowness and political interference. It has also been credited with documenting the Khmer Rouge period, involving hundreds of thousands of Cambodian visitors and civil parties, and contributing to education and memorialisation. Having completed its judicial work, the court moved to residual functions, including archives and outreach.
