Attribution (psychology)

In social psychology, attribution is the process by which individuals explain the causes of behaviour and events. The study of attribution began with Fritz Heider's work in the 1940s–50s, which introduced the distinction between internal (dispositional) attributions — to personality, ability, or effort — and external (situational) attributions — to circumstances or luck. Attribution theory examines how these explanations are formed and how they go systematically wrong.

Theoretical models

Harold Kelley's covariation model (1967) proposes that people attribute behaviour by weighing consensus (do others act the same way?), distinctiveness (does the person act this way only here?), and consistency (does the person always act this way in this situation?). Bernard Weiner's model classifies attributions for success and failure along locus (internal/external), stability, and controllability, linking them to emotions and motivation.

Attributional biases

  • Fundamental attribution error (correspondence bias): over-attributing others' behaviour to disposition and underestimating situational causes.
  • Actor–observer asymmetry: explaining one's own behaviour situationally but others' behaviour dispositionally.
  • Self-serving bias: attributing successes to oneself and failures to external factors.
  • Defensive attribution and just-world bias: blaming victims to preserve a belief that the world is fair.

Applications

Attribution research informs clinical psychology (attributional styles in depression), education (how students explain success and failure), organisational behaviour, and health psychology. Interventions that retrain attributions — for example, encouraging struggling students to attribute setbacks to controllable effort rather than fixed ability — can improve persistence and achievement.
