The Barmen Declaration (German: Barmer Theologische Erklärung) was a statement adopted by German Protestants in May 1934 in opposition to the Nazi-aligned "German Christian" movement and the regime's attempt to subordinate the church to National Socialist ideology. Drafted principally by the Swiss theologian Karl Barth, with contributions from Hans Asmussen and others, it became the founding theological document of the Confessing Church.

Content

The declaration consists of six theses, each pairing a scriptural affirmation with a rejection of a corresponding error. It asserts that Jesus Christ, as attested in Holy Scripture, is the one Word of God whom the church must hear and obey, and it rejects the doctrine that the church could acknowledge "other events and powers" — such as the Führer or the supposed laws of history — as sources of God's revelation. It denies that the church may conform its message and order to prevailing ideological and political convictions, and it affirms that the state's divinely appointed task is limited to justice and peace, rejecting totalitarian claims on human life.

Context and legacy

The declaration was adopted at the first Confessional Synod of the German Evangelical Church, held in Barmen (now a district of Wuppertal) from 29 to 31 May 1934, in response to the German Christians' capture of church governance and the introduction of the "Aryan paragraph" into church law. Although it did not directly condemn Nazi antisemitism or the regime's political crimes — an omission later criticised — it asserted the church's independence and became a landmark of Protestant resistance and of 20th-century confessional theology. It is included in the confessional standards of several Reformed churches worldwide, and its theses influenced post-war statements such as the Belhar Confession in South Africa.
