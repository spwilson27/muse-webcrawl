Part ofAutonomic nervous system
LocationWalls of the gastrointestinal tract

The enteric nervous system (ENS) is one of the main divisions of the autonomic nervous system and consists of a mesh-like network of neurons embedded in the lining of the gastrointestinal system. It can operate independently of the brain and spinal cord, though it normally communicates with the central nervous system via the vagus nerve and sympathetic pathways. Because of its size — several hundred million neurons in humans — and its autonomy, it is often called the "second brain".

Structure

The ENS is organised into two principal plexuses. The myenteric plexus (Auerbach's plexus) lies between the muscle layers of the gut wall and primarily controls gastrointestinal motility, including peristalsis. The submucosal plexus (Meissner's plexus) lies in the submucosa and regulates local blood flow, glandular secretion, and absorption. Enteric neurons use many of the same neurotransmitters as the brain, including serotonin — most of the body's serotonin is found in the gut — dopamine, and acetylcholine.

Function and clinical significance

The ENS coordinates the reflexes of digestion: mixing and propelling contents, secreting enzymes and mucus, and sensing the chemical and mechanical state of the gut. It interacts bidirectionally with the brain along the gut–brain axis, and dysfunction is implicated in disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome and Hirschsprung's disease, in which enteric ganglia fail to develop in a segment of colon.
