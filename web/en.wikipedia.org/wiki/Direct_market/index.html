Direct market

The direct market is the dominant distribution and retail network for American comic books, consisting of speciality comics shops buying non-returnable stock at a discount from distributors, rather than the returnable newsstand system through which comics were traditionally sold. It emerged in the 1970s and reshaped the economics and content of mainstream comics.

The system grew from fan-organised distribution pioneered by Phil Seuling, whose Sea-Gate Distributors began buying comics directly from publishers around 1972–74 at deeper discounts in exchange for taking them non-returnable and in pre-ordered quantities. As newsstand sales withered in the early 1980s, publishers came to rely on thousands of speciality shops; by the mid-1990s Diamond Comic Distributors had become the near-monopoly distributor to the market, a position later challenged by Penguin Random House and Lunar Distribution.

Because shops order by pre-solicitation months in advance based on customer pull-lists, the direct market rewards established franchises and loyal readerships, supports a culture of collecting and back-issue sales, and made possible the independent and creator-owned comics boom of the 1980s. Its non-returnable model also exposed retailers to risk during speculative bubbles, notably the crash of the mid-1990s.
