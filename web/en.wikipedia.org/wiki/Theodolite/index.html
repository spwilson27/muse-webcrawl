Theodolite

TypeSurveying instrument
MeasuresHorizontal and vertical angles
Used inSurveying, geodesy, construction, meteorology, navigation
RelatedTotal station, transit, level, tachymeter

A theodolite is a precision optical instrument for measuring angles between chosen visible points in the horizontal and vertical planes. Surveyors use it to establish lines, prolong alignments, set out structures, determine differences of elevation indirectly, and build the angular framework from which positions are calculated. In its classic form the instrument is mounted on a tripod, levelled with footscrews and a bubble or electronic sensor, and aimed with a telescope that can rotate about both a vertical axis and a horizontal trunnion axis.

The word is often applied loosely to the American surveying transit, but instrument makers traditionally reserve “theodolite” for instruments whose graduated circles are read with micrometers, verniers or optical encoders to seconds of arc. Modern electronic theodolites display angles digitally; when distance measurement is integrated, the instrument becomes a total station.

Principles

A measurement begins by centring the instrument over a survey mark, usually with an optical or laser plummet, and levelling it so the vertical axis is plumb. The telescope is pointed at a target and the horizontal circle reading is recorded. Turning the telescope to a second target gives a second reading; the difference is the horizontal angle. Vertical angles are measured from the zenith or from the horizontal, depending on the circle convention, and can be combined with a measured distance to compute height differences by trigonometric levelling.

Accuracy depends on eliminating instrumental errors. Reading the circle in “face left” and “face right” positions and averaging the results reduces collimation error, trunnion-axis error and index error. Careful observers also distribute initial circle readings around the circle, close angles around the horizon, and check backsights to detect movement of the tripod.

Construction

The main parts are the tribrach and levelling screws; the lower plate carrying the horizontal circle; the alidade, which rotates above it; standards supporting the telescope; the vertical circle; clamps and tangent screws for fine motion; and reading devices. Optical theodolites use glass circles etched with fine graduations and an internal optical path that brings circle images to a micrometer eyepiece. Electronic instruments replace direct reading with encoders and a liquid-crystal display, while retaining the same geometry.

History

Angle-measuring instruments descend from the dioptra of antiquity and from medieval astrolabes and quadrants. The term theodolite appeared in sixteenth-century England in Leonard Digges’s surveying writings. Jesse Ramsden’s great theodolite of 1787, built for the Principal Triangulation of Great Britain, showed the precision possible with divided circles and Ramsden’s engine. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, makers such as Troughton & Simms, Wild Heerbrugg, Zeiss and Kern refined compact repeating and direction theodolites for national surveys, tunnels, dams and astronomical field work.

Applications

Theodolites remain useful where angular control is needed without the cost or complexity of a total station: setting out building grids, aligning machinery, measuring deformation, observing meteorological balloons, checking towers and bridges, and teaching the geometry of surveying. In geodesy they historically supplied the angle observations for triangulation networks before satellite positioning and electronic distance measurement changed practice.

Errors and care

Heat shimmer, wind, unstable ground, parallax, imperfect levelling and mis-centring all degrade results. Good field procedure includes firm tripod placement, shading the instrument, focussing carefully, using well-defined targets, repeating observations, and applying calibration checks for circle graduation and axis relationships.
