Biologic medical product

Biologic medical product

A biologic medical product, also known as a biologic or biological medicinal product, is any pharmaceutical drug product manufactured in, extracted from, or semi-synthesized from biological sources. Biologics are distinct from chemically synthesized small-molecule drugs in that they are produced by living organisms or cells, often using recombinant DNA technology, and are typically large, complex molecules or mixtures of molecules.

Biologics include a wide variety of products such as vaccines, blood and blood components, allergenics, somatic cells, gene therapies, tissues, and recombinant therapeutic proteins such as insulin and monoclonal antibodies. They are used to treat conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, cancer, diabetes, psoriasis, and rare genetic disorders.

Characteristics

Unlike conventional drugs, which are usually small molecules with well-defined chemical structures that can be manufactured by predictable chemical processes, biologics are large and structurally complex. Their manufacture involves living systems—bacteria, yeast, or mammalian cells—so the final product is sensitive to small changes in the manufacturing process. This is often summarized by the industry aphorism "the process is the product." Because of this sensitivity, biologics require rigorous characterization, cold-chain storage, and careful regulatory oversight.

Regulation

In the United States, biologics are licensed by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), principally through its Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) and Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). The Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act of 2009 created an abbreviated approval pathway for biosimilars—products shown to be highly similar to an approved reference biologic with no clinically meaningful differences. In the European Union, biologics are authorized through the European Medicines Agency, frequently by the centralized procedure.

Biosimilars

Because living production systems cannot be replicated exactly, follow-on versions of biologics cannot be identical copies in the way generic small-molecule drugs are. Instead, regulators approve biosimilars based on analytical, animal, and clinical studies demonstrating biosimilarity. Some biosimilars receive an "interchangeable" designation in the United States, permitting pharmacy-level substitution subject to state law.
