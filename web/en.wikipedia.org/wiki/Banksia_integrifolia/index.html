Banksia integrifolia

Banksia integrifolia, commonly known as the coast banksia, is a species of tree in the family Proteaceae native to the east coast of Australia, from Victoria to central Queensland, and one of the most widely distributed of all Banksia species. It was among the first banksias collected at Botany Bay in 1770 during Captain James Cook's voyage, and was formally described by Carl Linnaeus the Younger in 1782; its specific epithet refers to its entire (untoothed) adult leaves.

Description

A highly variable species, it ranges from a prostrate shrub in exposed coastal sites to a tree of 25 metres in sheltered forest. Young plants bear toothed leaves in whorls; mature foliage is dark green above and silvery-white beneath. The lemon-yellow flower spikes, up to 12 cm long, appear mainly from autumn to winter and are rich in nectar, attracting honeyeaters, insects and the grey-headed flying fox. Old spikes develop the characteristic banksia "cones" studded with woody follicles.

Cultivation and ecology

Hardy and adaptable, coast banksia is widely planted in gardens, parks and streetscapes in Australia, tolerating salt spray, sandy soils and frost. Three subspecies are generally recognised: subsp. integrifolia, subsp. compar of Queensland, and subsp. monticola of montane New South Wales. The species plays an important role in coastal ecosystems as a winter nectar source.
