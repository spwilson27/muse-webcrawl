Córdoba is a city in Andalusia, southern Spain, and the capital of the province of Córdoba. It lies on the banks of the Guadalquivir river. Once a Roman and Visigothic settlement, it became the capital of the Islamic Emirate and then Caliphate of Córdoba, when it was one of the largest and most advanced cities in the world.

History

Córdoba was a Roman colony, Corduba, and birthplace of the philosopher Seneca the Younger. After the Muslim conquest of 711 it rose rapidly; under Abd al-Rahman I it became capital of an independent emirate, and under the Caliphate proclaimed in 929 it reached perhaps half a million inhabitants, with famed libraries, mosques and scholars such as Averroes and Maimonides. After the caliphate collapsed in the 11th century the city declined, and it was conquered by Ferdinand III of Castile in 1236.

Main sights

The city's great monument is the Great Mosque–Cathedral (La Mezquita), begun in 784 and famous for its forest of double arches; a Renaissance cathedral was inserted into its centre after the Christian conquest. Other sights include the Roman bridge, the Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos, the medieval Jewish quarter with its synagogue, and the flower-filled patios celebrated each May in the Festival of the Patios.

Climate

Córdoba has a hot Mediterranean climate with some of the highest summer temperatures in Europe, frequently exceeding 40 °C in July and August, while winters are mild.
