Roman Curia

The Roman Curia (Latin: Curia Romana) comprises the administrative institutions of the Holy See and the central body through which the affairs of the Catholic Church are conducted. It evolved, like comparable royal courts of medieval Europe, from the feudal court of the pope and performs for the Bishop of Rome functions analogous to those a government performs for a state. The Curia is headquartered in and around Vatican City.

Structure

Since Pope Francis's apostolic constitution Praedicate evangelium (effective 5 June 2022), the Curia consists of the Secretariat of State; sixteen Dicasteries (including the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, for Bishops, for Promoting Integral Human Development and for Evangelization, which the pope himself heads); judicial institutions such as the Apostolic Penitentiary and the Roman Rota; economic bodies including the Secretariat for the Economy; and various offices and commissions. Heads of dicasteries need no longer be cardinals, and lay people — including women — may lead them.

History

The Curia grew from the papal household and chancery of the early Middle Ages. Pope Sixtus V's Immensa aeterni Dei (1588) organised it into congregations, the system largely retained until reforms by Pius X (1908), Paul VI (1967) and John Paul II (Pastor bonus, 1988). Reform of the Curia was a major theme of the 2013 conclave that elected Pope Francis, whose nine-year drafting process produced Praedicate evangelium, emphasising the Curia's service to evangelisation and to local bishops rather than power for its own sake.
