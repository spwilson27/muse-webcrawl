Airspeed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An airspeed indicator in a light aircraft

Airspeed is the speed of an aircraft relative to the air mass through which it is flying. It differs from ground speed, which is measured relative to the ground and is affected by wind. Airspeed governs aerodynamic forces such as lift and is therefore the speed pilots primarily fly by.

Types of airspeed

  • Indicated airspeed (IAS) — read directly from the airspeed indicator, driven by the pitot-static system
  • Calibrated airspeed (CAS) — IAS corrected for instrument and position error
  • Equivalent airspeed (EAS) — CAS corrected for compressibility at the given altitude
  • True airspeed (TAS) — EAS corrected for air density; the actual speed through the air mass
  • Mach number — ratio of TAS to the local speed of sound, crucial at high altitude

Measurement

Airspeed is derived from the difference between total (pitot) pressure and static pressure, sensed by probes on the fuselage and processed by air data computers in modern aircraft. Icing or blockage of these probes can produce dangerous misreadings.

Units

Airspeed is usually expressed in knots (nautical miles per hour), or km/h in some countries; gliders often use km/h.
