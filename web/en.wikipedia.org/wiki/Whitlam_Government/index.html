The Whitlam Government was the federal executive government of Australia from 1972 to 1975, led by Prime Minister Gough Whitlam of the Australian Labor Party. It came to power at the 1972 election — under the slogan "It's Time" — ending 23 years of Liberal–Country Party coalition rule, and it pursued an ambitious program of social and institutional reform before its extraordinary dismissal by Governor-General Sir John Kerr on 11 November 1975, an event known as the Dismissal.

Reforms

In its three years the government introduced universal health insurance (Medibank), abolished university tuition fees, ended conscription and withdrew Australian troops from Vietnam, recognised the People's Republic of China, granted land rights advances for Aboriginal Australians (including the symbolic hand-back of land to Vincent Lingiari at Wattie Creek), lowered the voting age to 18, introduced the Trade Practices Act and the Racial Discrimination Act 1975, established the Family Court and no-fault divorce, funded urban sewerage and regional development programs, and created institutions such as the Australia Council for the Arts and the National Gallery.

Controversies and the Dismissal

The government was beset by scandals, including the Loans Affair, in which ministers sought unauthorised overseas loans through intermediaries. After the opposition-controlled Senate blocked supply bills in 1975, Kerr dismissed Whitlam and commissioned Liberal leader Malcolm Fraser as caretaker prime minister on condition that he secure supply and call an election. Whitlam's words on the steps of Parliament House — "Well may we say 'God save the Queen', because nothing will save the Governor-General" — became famous. The Fraser coalition won the ensuing election in a landslide.

Legacy

Despite its short life, many Whitlam-era reforms endured, and the government remains a landmark in Australian political history for the scale of its program and the constitutional crisis that ended it.
