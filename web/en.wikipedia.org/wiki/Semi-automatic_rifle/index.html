Semi-automatic rifle

A semi-automatic rifle is a self-loading rifle that fires one round each time the trigger is pressed, automatically ejecting the spent cartridge case and chambering a fresh round ready for the next shot. The shooter must release and press the trigger again to fire again; this distinguishes it from a fully automatic rifle, which continues firing while the trigger is held.

AR-15-pattern rifles are the best-selling semi-automatic rifles in the United States.

History

Semi-automatic rifles entered military service beginning with designs such as the Mondragón (1908) and became standard with the M1 Garand in the Second World War. Post-war military semi-automatics such as the SKS were largely displaced in service by select-fire assault rifles, while semi-automatic versions and purpose-built designs flourished in civilian markets. Early civilian successes included John Browning's designs and the Remington Model 8 (1906); the Ruger 10/22 rimfire (1964) and the AR-15 family became enduring bestsellers.

Uses

Civilian semi-automatic rifles are used for hunting, target and practical shooting competition, and home defense. They range from .22 LR plinkers and varmint rifles to precision and big-game rifles in full-power cartridges. Military forces still use semi-automatic rifles in specialized roles, notably designated marksman and sniper rifles such as the M14-derived and SVD Dragunov families.

Regulation

Legal treatment varies widely: many countries restrict semi-automatic centerfire rifles by license category, feature, or capacity, and some have banned categories of them following mass shootings. In the United States they are generally regulated like other rifles under federal law, with additional restrictions in some states; the political debate over "assault weapons" centers largely on semi-automatic rifles with military-style features.
