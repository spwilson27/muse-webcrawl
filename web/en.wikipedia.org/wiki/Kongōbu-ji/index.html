Kongōbu-ji (金剛峯寺) is the head temple of the Kōyasan Shingon school of Japanese Buddhism, located on Mount Kōya (Kōyasan) in Wakayama Prefecture, Japan. Originally, the name Kongōbu-ji referred to the whole mountain-top complex of temples founded by the monk Kūkai; it now designates the principal temple, which administers the roughly 120 temples of the mountain and the school's millions of adherents. The name means "Temple of the Diamond Mountain Peak".

History

Kūkai received permission from Emperor Saga in 816 to found a monastic centre on Mount Kōya, a remote plateau some 800 metres above sea level in the Kii Mountains, chosen for its lotus-petal arrangement of eight peaks. Construction of the central garan (sacred precinct) with its great pagoda (Konpon Daitō) began soon after. Kūkai entered eternal meditation (nyūjō) at Okunoin in 835 and is believed by devotees to remain alive in meditation awaiting the future Buddha Maitreya. The mountain grew into one of Japan's great centres of pilgrimage and learning; the temple was rebuilt many times after fires, and the present main hall (Shinden) dates from the 19th century. In 2004 the site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the "Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range".

Buildings and treasures

The temple's principal buildings include the main hall, the Banryūtei — Japan's largest rock garden, created in 1984, whose raked gravel and granite boulders depict a pair of dragons emerging from a sea of cloud — and reception halls decorated with sliding-screen paintings of the Kanō school. The temple treasury holds mandalas, ritual implements and portraits associated with Kūkai. Nearby stand the Konpon Daitō pagoda, the Fudōdō (a National Treasure and the oldest surviving building on the mountain, from the Kamakura period), and the Okunoin cemetery, Japan's largest, where over 200,000 tombs line the path to Kūkai's mausoleum through an ancient cedar forest.

Mount Kōya complex

About half of Kōyasan's temples offer lodging (shukubō) to pilgrims and visitors, serving vegetarian Buddhist cuisine (shōjin ryōri) and allowing guests to join morning prayers and meditation. The town is also the starting point of the Choishi Michi pilgrimage trail and a variant of the Kii Mountain pilgrimage routes.
