Curie's law

Curie's law
Susceptibility χ falls as 1/T for a paramagnet obeying Curie's law
Named afterPierre Curie
Established1895 (Curie's doctoral research)
Applies toParamagnetic materials at low field / high temperature
Related lawCurie–Weiss law

Curie's law states that the magnetic susceptibility of a paramagnetic material is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature. For a material in a weak applied field it may be written:

χ = C / T,   equivalently   M = C · (B / T)

where M is the magnetisation, B the applied magnetic field, T the temperature in kelvins, and C a material-specific constant called the Curie constant. The law was established experimentally by Pierre Curie in his 1895 doctoral thesis, in which he mapped how the susceptibility of paramagnetic substances such as oxygen and various salts varies with temperature.

Derivation

A simple statistical-mechanical model treats the material as a set of independent magnetic moments that can align with or against the field. Thermal agitation opposes alignment: the higher the temperature, the weaker the net magnetisation for a given field. In the classical limit (worked out by Paul Langevin in 1905) and in the quantum treatment using the Brillouin function, the low-field, high-temperature approximation of the magnetisation reduces exactly to the 1/T dependence observed by Curie, with the Curie constant determined by the density and size of the atomic moments: C is proportional to n·μeff²/(3kB).

Validity and modifications

The law holds when the magnetic moments do not interact appreciably and when kBT is much larger than the Zeeman energy of a moment in the field. At low temperatures or high fields the magnetisation saturates and Curie's law fails. When interactions between moments matter, the law is generalised by the Curie–Weiss law, χ = C/(T − θ), which introduces the Weiss constant θ and describes the approach to ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic ordering. Plots of inverse susceptibility against temperature ("Curie plots") remain a standard tool for measuring effective magnetic moments in chemistry and condensed-matter physics.
