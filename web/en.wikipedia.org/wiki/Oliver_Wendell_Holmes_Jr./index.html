Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.

Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (March 8, 1841 – March 6, 1935) was an American jurist who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1902 to 1932. One of the most influential common-law judges in American history, he is remembered for his concise, epigrammatic opinions, his legal philosophy set out in The Common Law (1881), and celebrated dissents — in Lochner v. New York and Abrams v. United States — that helped shape modern First Amendment doctrine. He retired at 90, then the oldest serving justice, and was nicknamed "The Great Dissenter."

Early life and Civil War

Son of the physician-poet Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr., Holmes graduated from Harvard College in 1861 and enlisted in the Union Army at the outbreak of the Civil War. He was wounded three times — at Ball's Bluff, Antietam and Chancellorsville — an experience that profoundly shaped his worldview. He later graduated from Harvard Law School (1866) and practised in Boston.

Jurisprudence

His 1881 book The Common Law opens with the famous line: "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience." His 1897 essay "The Path of the Law" introduced the "bad man" theory of law and anticipated legal realism. As chief justice of Massachusetts (1899–1902) and then on the U.S. Supreme Court, Holmes generally deferred to legislatures; his Lochner dissent protested that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics." In Schenck (1919) he coined "shouting fire in a crowded theatre" and the "clear and present danger" test; months later his Abrams dissent articulated the "marketplace of ideas" rationale for free speech. His legacy is complicated by his majority opinion in Buck v. Bell (1927), upholding forced sterilisation with the notorious phrase "three generations of imbeciles are enough."
