Spring offensive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

German infantry during the 1918 Spring Offensive on the Western Front

The term spring offensive refers to military operations launched in the spring season of a campaigning year. The most famous example is the German spring offensive of 1918 (also known as the Kaiserschlacht, "Kaiser's battle", or the Ludendorff offensive) on the Western Front during the First World War.

The 1918 offensive

Beginning on 21 March 1918 with Operation Michael, the German Army launched a series of attacks against the Allied armies. Germany had transferred large numbers of divisions from the Eastern Front following Russia's withdrawal from the war under the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, and General Erich Ludendorff sought a decisive victory before American forces arrived in strength.

Using infiltration tactics by specially trained stormtroopers, preceded by short, intense artillery bombardments devised by Colonel Georg Bruchmüller, the Germans achieved the deepest advances on the Western Front since 1914. Successive operations — Michael, Georgette, Blücher–Yorck, and Gneisenau — created large salients but failed to achieve a strategic breakthrough. German casualties were irreplaceable, supply lines were overextended, and by July 1918 the initiative had passed to the Allies, whose Hundred Days Offensive ended the war in November.

Other spring offensives

  • The Kerensky Offensive (July 1917) — Russia's last major effort in the First World War, planned for spring but delayed.
  • The Tet Offensive (January 1968), launched during the Vietnamese spring festival of Tết.
  • The Easter Offensive (1972), North Vietnam's spring invasion of South Vietnam.
  • Various spring campaigns in the Korean War and in modern conflicts, timed to exploit improving weather and ground conditions.
