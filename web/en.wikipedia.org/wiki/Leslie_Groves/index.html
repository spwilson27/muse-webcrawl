Leslie Groves

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Leslie Groves
Born: 17 August 1896, Albany, New York
Died: 13 July 1970
Known for: Directing the Manhattan Project (1942–1947)

Leslie Richard Groves Jr. (1896–1970) was a United States Army Corps of Engineers officer who directed the Manhattan Project, the programme that developed the atomic bomb during the Second World War.

Groves had just supervised construction of the Pentagon when, in September 1942, he took command of the atomic programme. He selected the key sites — Oak Ridge for uranium enrichment, Hanford for plutonium production and Los Alamos for weapon design — and chose J. Robert Oppenheimer to lead the Los Alamos laboratory, a decision doubted at the time because of Oppenheimer's lack of administrative experience and his political associations, but vindicated by the result. Groves imposed strict compartmentalisation and security while driving the industrial-scale effort to completion in under three years.

After the war he headed the Armed Forces Special Weapons Project and retired as a lieutenant general in 1948, afterwards writing a memoir, Now It Can Be Told (1962). His reputation is that of a brusque, demanding but exceptionally effective administrator; the security culture he built also fed postwar controversies, including the 1954 hearing that stripped Oppenheimer of his clearance.
