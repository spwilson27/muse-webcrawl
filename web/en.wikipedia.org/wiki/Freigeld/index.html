Freigeld

Stamp scrip, a practical form of demurrage currency.

Freigeld (German for "free money") is a monetary concept proposed by the German-Argentine economist Silvio Gesell in his work The Natural Economic Order (1916). Freigeld is a currency that loses value over time — a demurrage currency — designed to circulate continuously rather than be hoarded.

Concept

Gesell argued that conventional money enjoys an unfair advantage over goods, which rust, rot, or incur storage costs, allowing money holders to demand interest. Freigeld would carry a periodic carrying cost, implemented for example through stamps that must be purchased and affixed to banknotes to keep them valid (a small percentage of face value per period). This negative interest would encourage spending and lending without interest, which Gesell believed would eliminate unemployment and economic crises caused by hoarding.

Experiments and influence

The most famous practical experiment was the Wörgl experiment in Austria (1932–33), where a local stamp scrip reduced unemployment during the Great Depression before being shut down by the central bank. Similar scrip circulated in the town of Schwanenkirchen in Germany. John Maynard Keynes discussed Gesell's ideas with some sympathy in The General Theory, and the economist Irving Fisher promoted stamp scrip in the United States.
