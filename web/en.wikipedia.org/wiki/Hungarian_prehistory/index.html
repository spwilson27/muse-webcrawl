Hungarian prehistory

Hungarian prehistory (Hungarian: magyar őstörténet) spans the history of the Hungarians (Magyars) before the Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin in 895–907. It covers the origins of the Hungarian language among the Uralic peoples, the life of the proto-Hungarians on the Eurasian steppe, and their successive migrations westward under pressure from Turkic peoples, until the seven tribes and their Kabar allies crossed the Carpathians under Árpád.

The traditional stages of the Magyar migration: Magna Hungaria, Levedia, and Etelköz.

Origins

Hungarian belongs to the Ugric branch of the Uralic language family, its closest relatives being Khanty and Mansi of western Siberia. The proto-Ugric homeland is usually placed east of the Ural Mountains. Over the first millennium CE the proto-Hungarians adopted a semi-nomadic pastoralist life on the steppe, acquiring equestrian culture and many loanwords from Iranian and Turkic neighbours.

Magna Hungaria, Levedia, and Etelköz

Medieval sources and later scholarship identify three way-stations. Magna Hungaria ("Great Hungary"), visited in the 1230s by the Dominican friar Julian, lay in the Volga–Kama region (roughly modern Bashkortostan), where Hungarian-speaking groups remained after the main body moved west. Levedia, named for the chieftain Levedi, lay in the Don region, where the Magyars lived alongside the Khazar Khaganate in the 9th century. Driven west by the Pechenegs around the 880s, the tribes settled in Etelköz ("between the rivers"), between the Dnieper and the Carpathians, where, according to tradition, the seven tribes elected Árpád as their prince by a blood oath.

The Conquest

After campaigning in the Balkans as Byzantine allies in 895 and suffering Pecheneg attacks on their rear, the Hungarians crossed the Carpathians and occupied the Carpathian Basin, defeating local Moravian, Frankish, and Bulgarian forces; the campaign culminated at the Battle of Pressburg in 907. Archaeology of the conquest period — horse burials, sabretache plates, and characteristic jewellery — complements the written record of sources such as Constantine VII's De Administrando Imperio and the Hungarian chronicles.
