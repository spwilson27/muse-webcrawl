Asterism (astronomy)

The Big Dipper (Plough) is the best-known asterism in the northern sky.
Asterism
DefinitionA recognisable pattern of stars as seen from Earth
StatusInformal; not officially recognised by the IAU
ExamplesBig Dipper, Summer Triangle, Great Square of Pegasus, Teapot

In observational astronomy, an asterism is a popularly recognised pattern of stars in the night sky. Asterisms may form part of one official constellation, span several, or (historically) constitute a constellation that is no longer recognised. Unlike the 88 official constellations defined by the International Astronomical Union, asterisms have no official boundaries or status.

Well-known asterisms

The Big Dipper (or Plough) comprises the seven brightest stars of Ursa Major. The Summer Triangle joins Vega, Deneb and Altair across three constellations. Others include the Great Square of Pegasus, the Teapot in Sagittarius, the Northern Cross in Cygnus, the Sickle in Leo, Orion's Belt, and the False Cross in the southern sky. Some asterisms are telescopic curiosities, such as the Coathanger cluster.

Characteristics

The stars of an asterism are usually not physically related; they lie at very different distances and merely appear grouped from Earth's vantage point. Asterisms serve as signposts for amateur astronomers learning the sky and "star-hopping" to faint objects. Different cultures have recognised different asterisms in the same stars, reflecting their own folklore and practical needs.
