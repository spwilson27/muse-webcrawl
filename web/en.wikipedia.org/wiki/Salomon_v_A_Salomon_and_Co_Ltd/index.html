Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd
CourtHouse of Lords
Decided16 November 1896
Citation[1897] AC 22
Key holdingA duly incorporated company is a separate legal person from its shareholders

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 is a landmark English company law case decided by the House of Lords. It established the fundamental principle that a company incorporated under the Companies Acts is a legal person separate from its members, even where one individual dominates it, and that the company's debts are not the debts of its shareholders. The decision underpins the doctrine of separate legal personality and limited liability throughout the common law world.

Facts

Aron Salomon, a leather merchant and boot manufacturer, transferred his business to a company, A Salomon & Co Ltd, in which he, his wife and five children held the shares (to satisfy the statutory minimum of seven members). The company paid him partly in cash and shares and partly in secured debentures. When the business failed during a trade depression and went into liquidation, the unsecured creditors received nothing, while Salomon, as secured debenture holder, stood to be paid in priority. The liquidator argued the company was merely Salomon's agent or trustee.

Judgment

The High Court and Court of Appeal had held against Salomon, reasoning that Parliament had intended companies to have genuinely independent participants. The House of Lords unanimously reversed: because the company had been duly registered and the statutory requirements met, it was "a different person altogether" from its incorporators, and the motives of the incorporators were irrelevant. Lord Macnaghten's speech stressed that the company is a legal person distinct from the subscribers to its memorandum.

Legacy

The case has been followed ever since, with courts lifting the corporate veil only in narrow, exceptional circumstances. It enabled sole traders to incorporate with limited liability and remains the starting point of modern company law in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth jurisdictions.
