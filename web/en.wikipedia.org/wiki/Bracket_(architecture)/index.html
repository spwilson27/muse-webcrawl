A bracket is an architectural element that projects from a wall or other vertical surface to support or appear to support a weight above it — a shelf, balcony, cornice, beam, statue, or the deep eaves of a roof. Brackets are usually made of wood, stone, plaster, or metal and are often shaped in a right triangle, an S-curve (ogee), or a scroll, with the vertical member fastened to the wall and the horizontal member carrying the load. Although closely related to the corbel — which is typically a solid block or series of stepped blocks built into the wall — the bracket is usually a distinct piece applied to, or projecting from, the surface. Over time many brackets became primarily decorative, signalling support where the structure is actually carried by other means.

Types and terminology

In classical architecture, small repeated brackets called modillions support the corona of a Corinthian or Composite cornice, while the simpler block-like dentils appear in Ionic cornices. A console is an S-shaped bracket, often carved with acanthus scrolls, used for keystones, balconies, and mantelpieces. In timber construction, curved knee braces stiffen post-and-beam joints, and in Victorian and Italianate buildings, paired wooden or cast-iron eave brackets became a signature exterior ornament. In Gothic architecture, brackets (also called corbels in this context) support statues in niches and the springing of vault ribs.

History

Bracket-like supports are as old as masonry construction: corbelled projections appear in ancient Egyptian, Mycenaean, and Roman building. In medieval Europe, corbels carried wall walks, turrets, and hoardings on castles, and supported the projecting upper storeys of timber-framed houses. Renaissance architects codified the console and modillion in treatises. The nineteenth century industrialised the bracket: pattern books offered endless scroll-sawn wooden designs for porches and cornices, and cast iron made elaborate brackets cheap. Modernist architecture largely abandoned applied brackets in favour of the clean cantilever, though they persist in traditional and revival styles.

Bracket systems in world architecture

The most elaborate structural bracket tradition is that of East Asia. In Chinese architecture, dougong (斗拱) is a system of interlocking wooden brackets — the block (dou) and bow-shaped arm (gong) — set atop columns to support the great overhanging tiled roof. Dougong transfers the roof's weight to the columns, distributes loads, and gives timber buildings resilience in earthquakes; its complexity and number became a visible measure of a building's rank, codified already in the Song-dynasty building manual Yingzao Fashi (1103). The system spread to Japan as tokyō and to Korea, where it developed into distinct regional styles. In Islamic architecture, the related muqarnas — tiers of small corbelled niches — perform a comparable decorative-structural transition beneath domes and cornices.

Modern use

Today brackets survive in cabinetry, shelving, countertop and sink supports, sign and awning mounts, and in structural steel and engineered-timber connections, where the bracket's basic geometry — triangulating a load back into a vertical support — remains fundamental. Engineers use the same principle in gusset plates and cantilevered brackets for cranes, balconies, and bridge decks.
