Feral animal

A feral animal is one that has escaped from a domestic or captive status and is living more or less as a wild animal, or one that is descended from such animals. Zoologists generally exclude from the feral category animals that were genuinely wild before they escaped from captivity; feral animals are those that were once domesticated and have returned to a wild state, or their offspring. Equivalently, a feral population is a population of animals descended from domesticated stock that lives independently of direct human care.

Definition

Feral animals differ from both domesticated animals, which depend on humans, and truly wild animals, which were never domesticated. A stray animal is an individual that has been lost or abandoned, whereas a feral population is self-sustaining and reproduces in the wild. Some animals become feral readily — cats, goats, pigs and pigeons among them — while others rarely survive outside human care.

Examples

Well-known feral populations include feral cats, which are found worldwide and are a significant threat to native wildlife; feral pigs in the Americas and Australia; feral horses such as the mustangs of the American West and the brumbies of Australia; feral goats on islands; feral donkeys (burros); and feral pigeons in cities. In Australia, feral camels, rabbits, and water buffalo have also established large populations.

Ecological effects

Feral animals can have major ecological and economic impacts. They may prey on native species, compete with them for food and habitat, spread disease to wildlife and livestock, and damage crops, fences, and water sources. Feral cats are considered among the world's most harmful invasive species, and have contributed to the extinction of numerous bird, mammal, and reptile species, especially on islands. Management strategies include trapping, fencing, sterilisation, and, controversially, culling.
