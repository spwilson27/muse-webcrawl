Load shedding

Load shedding (also loadshedding or rolling blackouts) is a last-resort measure used by an electric utility company to avoid a total blackout of the power distribution system when demand exceeds supply. Electricity delivery is deliberately stopped for periods of time over different parts of the distribution system, usually on a scheduled, rotating basis, in order to reduce the load on generating plant or the transmission network.

How it works

When generation capacity falls short — because of plant breakdowns, fuel shortages, drought at hydroelectric stations, or transmission failures — system operators disconnect feeders in blocks according to published schedules, restoring each area after a set interval. If demand suddenly exceeds supply without action, frequency falls and automatic under-frequency relays shed load to prevent cascading failure and damage to generators.

Notable cases

South Africa

South Africa's state utility Eskom has implemented recurring load shedding since 2007–2008, with the crisis intensifying from 2022: scheduled outages reached "stage 6" and beyond, with households and businesses losing power for up to 10–12 hours a day at the worst points in 2023. Load shedding severely damaged economic output and became a defining political issue; it eased notably during 2024 as plant performance improved and rooftop solar expanded.

Other countries

Rolling blackouts have been used in Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka (notably during its 2022 economic crisis), Zimbabwe, Nigeria (in effect, through grid rationing), Cuba, and in the 2000–2001 California electricity crisis, as well as briefly during the February 2021 Texas power crisis, when ERCOT ordered controlled outages during extreme cold.

Effects

Chronic load shedding disrupts industry, water pumping, hospitals, traffic systems, and telecommunications, increases reliance on diesel generators and solar-plus-battery systems, and raises costs across the economy. It differs from demand response and load management in being involuntary and supply-driven rather than a planned market or efficiency measure.
