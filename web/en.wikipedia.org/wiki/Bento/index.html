A bento (弁当, bentō) is the Japanese iteration of a single-portion take-out or home-packed meal, often for lunch, typically including rice or noodles with fish or meat and pickled or cooked vegetables, in a box. Japanese homemakers often spend time devising bento meals for their children, spouses, or themselves.

History

Packed meals in Japan trace to the Kamakura period (1185–1333), when dried rice (hoshi-ii) was carried for work and travel. In the Azuchi–Momoyama period, lacquered wooden boxes were made for enjoying meals at hanami (flower viewing) and tea ceremonies. In the Edo period, the makunouchi bento — originally eaten between acts of the theatre — spread among townspeople, while travellers bought ekiben at stations after railways arrived in the Meiji era. Elaborate bento for schoolchildren became a post-war norm; convenience-store bento industrialised the format from the 1980s.

Types

  • Makunouchi — formal, compartmented, with rice balls and many small items.
  • Ekiben — sold at railway stations, showcasing regional specialities.
  • Shōkadō bento — in a lidded, cross-divided lacquer box inspired by the artist Shōkadō Shōjō.
  • Kyaraben ("character bento") — decorated to depict characters from anime, manga and games, for children.
  • Soratoben, hokaben, kamameshi and others — airline, takeaway-shop and regional variants.

Composition and presentation

A traditional guide divides the box roughly into rice (staple), a grilled or fried main, simmered items seasoned with dashi, and pickles, with care for colour (red, green, yellow, white, dark) and for keeping flavours separate. Boxes range from disposable plastic trays to handcrafted lacquerware and magewappa (bentwood) of cedar.

Outside Japan

Bento-style packed lunches and compartmented boxes have become popular worldwide, and Korean dosirak and Taiwanese railway bentos reflect related traditions. In Hawaii, the "bento" is a recognised local meal format.
