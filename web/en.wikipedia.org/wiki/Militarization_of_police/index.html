Militarization of police

Militarization of police refers to the use of military equipment and tactics by law enforcement officers, including the use of armored personnel carriers, assault rifles, submachine guns, flashbang grenades, sniper rifles, and SWAT teams. The term also describes the adoption by police of military-style organization, intelligence gathering, and aggressive tactics historically associated with armed forces rather than civilian peacekeeping.

History

In the United States, police militarization is often traced to the creation of SWAT teams in the 1960s, notably by the Los Angeles Police Department under Daryl Gates, in response to riots and heavily armed offenders. The trend accelerated with the war on drugs in the 1980s and 1990s, and again after the September 11 attacks, when counterterrorism funding expanded transfers of military hardware to local departments.

A key mechanism in the United States is the Department of Defense's 1033 program, established by the National Defense Authorization Act of 1997, which permits the transfer of excess military property — including vehicles, aircraft, and weapons — to state and local law enforcement agencies. Successive administrations have alternately restricted and restored the range of equipment eligible for transfer.

Criticism and debate

Critics, including civil liberties organizations, argue that militarized equipment and tactics erode trust between police and communities, escalate encounters, and blur the line between soldier and officer. High-profile deployments, such as the police response to the 2014 Ferguson protests, intensified national debate. Defenders respond that officers face armed criminals and that protective equipment and specialized units save lives, including in hostage and active-shooter situations.

International perspectives

Many countries maintain gendarmerie-style forces — such as France's Gendarmerie nationale or Italy's Carabinieri — that are formally military bodies performing civilian policing, a model distinct from the militarization of an originally civilian force. Scholars distinguish between such institutional arrangements and the cultural and material militarization of ordinary police.
