Born–Haber cycle

The Born–Haber cycle is an approach to analyse reaction energies. It was developed by the German scientists Max Born and Fritz Haber in 1919. The cycle describes the formation of an ionic compound from its elements through a series of hypothetical steps, and is used primarily to calculate lattice energies (or lattice enthalpies), which cannot be measured directly.

Description

The cycle is an application of Hess's law: the total enthalpy change of a process is independent of the path taken. The standard enthalpy of formation of the ionic compound is equated to the sum of the steps needed to assemble it from gaseous ions — atomisation (sublimation) of the metal, ionisation of the metal atoms, dissociation of the non-metal molecules, electron attachment to the non-metal atoms, and finally the lattice enthalpy released when the gaseous ions condense into the solid crystal.

Example: sodium chloride

For NaCl, the cycle combines the enthalpy of sublimation of sodium, the first ionisation energy of sodium, half the bond dissociation enthalpy of Cl2, the electron affinity of chlorine, and the lattice enthalpy of formation. Because all other quantities are measurable, the lattice enthalpy can be extracted by difference and compared with theoretical values from electrostatic models such as the Born–Landé equation.

Applications

Born–Haber cycles are used to rationalise the stability of ionic compounds, to explain why some hypothetical compounds (such as NaCl2 or MgCl) do not form, and to estimate electron affinities and other quantities that are otherwise difficult to measure.
