The interplanetary dust cloud, or zodiacal cloud, is a cloud of cosmic dust (small particles floating in space) that pervades the planetary system of the Sun. It consists of dust grains produced by asteroid collisions, dust shed by comets as they pass through the inner Solar System, and debris from the Kuiper belt. Scattering of sunlight by these particles produces the zodiacal light, a faint triangular glow visible along the ecliptic after sunset or before sunrise, and the gegenschein opposite the Sun.

Origin and dynamics

Particles spiral slowly toward the Sun under Poynting–Robertson drag, so the cloud must be continually replenished: its total mass would otherwise drain away on timescales of tens of thousands of years. Infrared observations from IRAS, COBE and later missions revealed structure within the cloud, including dust bands associated with asteroid families (from recent collisions in the main belt) and a resonant ring of dust trapped near Earth's orbit. Analysis of the bands and of grains returned by the Stardust mission indicates that Jupiter-family comets supply a large fraction — plausibly the majority — of the dust reaching the inner Solar System, with asteroidal dust contributing a substantial minority.

Collection and study

Interplanetary dust particles (IDPs) reaching Earth are collected by high-altitude aircraft (NASA's ER-2 flights with oiled collector plates in the stratosphere), in Antarctic ice and deep-sea sediments, and as micrometeorites; an estimated tens of thousands of tonnes of cosmic dust fall to Earth each year. Their porous, anhydrous, carbon-rich compositions preserve primitive Solar System material. Spacecraft such as Helios, Ulysses, Galileo, Cassini and Parker Solar Probe have measured dust impacts in situ across the inner and outer Solar System.

Extrasolar analogues

Dust clouds around other stars produce "exozodiacal" emission that is a major noise source for proposed direct imaging of Earth-like exoplanets; surveys with the Large Binocular Telescope Interferometer (HOSTS) have measured typical exozodi levels in nearby systems.
