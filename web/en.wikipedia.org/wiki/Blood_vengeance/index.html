Blood vengeance, also known as blood revenge, is the customary obligation to avenge the killing or dishonour of a relative by killing the offender or a member of the offender's kin. It is the driving mechanism of the blood feud, in which retaliatory killings alternate between families or clans, sometimes for generations.

Customary codes

In many pre-state societies, vengeance was not uncontrolled violence but a rule-governed duty. In northern Albania, the Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini codified gjakmarrja (blood-taking): only the killer himself could legitimately be targeted, women and children were exempt, a family's house was inviolable sanctuary, and truces (besa) could suspend the obligation. Similar codes governed feuding in Montenegro, Corsica — where the vendetta became famous — Sicily, Pashtun society under Pashtunwali (badal, revenge), and among Bedouin groups.

Biblical and legal references

The Hebrew Bible prescribes the role of the "avenger of blood" (goel ha-dam), a kinsman charged with pursuing a murderer, but balances the custom with cities of refuge protecting those who killed unintentionally. The principle of lex talionis — "an eye for an eye" — limited retaliation to proportionate harm. Islamic law permits the victim's heirs to demand qisas (retaliation) or accept compensation (diyya) or forgiveness.

Decline and persistence

Centralised states progressively suppressed blood vengeance by monopolising legitimate violence and substituting fines and imprisonment. Nevertheless, feuds persisted into the twentieth century in parts of the Mediterranean and the Caucasus, and post-communist Albania saw a revival of gjakmarrja in the 1990s, confining thousands of children to their homes for fear of attack. Reconciliation movements, often led by religious figures, have worked to formally end feuds through public pardons.
