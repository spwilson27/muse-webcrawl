Incorporation (linguistics)

Incorporation
TypeMorphological / syntactic process
Typical hostVerb
Typical incorporated elementNoun, adverb, adjective

In linguistics, incorporation is a phenomenon by which a word, usually a verb, forms a kind of compound with, for instance, its direct object (object incorporation) or adverbial modifier, while retaining its original syntactic function. The compound verb thus formed is treated as a single word, both morphologically and phonologically.

Incorporation is characteristic of many polysynthetic languages, such as those of North America, Siberia, and northern Australia. In these languages a single verb form may incorporate a noun stem so that the resulting complex functions as a complete clause — for example, a verb meaning roughly "I have a horse" may be built from a stem meaning "horse" incorporated into a verb frame.

Noun incorporation

Noun incorporation is the most widely studied type. A transitive verb incorporates its object noun, which typically loses its independent modifiers and its status as a full argument. Marianne Mithun's influential typology distinguishes four functional types, ranging from lexical compounding, through the manipulation of case and argument structure, to discourse uses in which an incorporated noun introduces a referent that later pronouns pick up.

Other types

Languages also show incorporation of adverbs, adjectives, and other categories, and some analyses treat phenomena such as clitic climbing or certain forms of compounding as related. The theoretical analysis of incorporation — whether it is a morphological process or the result of syntactic head movement, as proposed by Mark Baker — has been a recurring topic in generative grammar.
