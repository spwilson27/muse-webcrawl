Rudolf Clausius

Rudolf Clausius
Born: 2 January 1822, Köslin, Prussia
Died: 24 August 1888, Bonn, Germany
Fields: Physics, thermodynamics
Known for: Second law of thermodynamics; entropy; kinetic theory of gases

Rudolf Julius Emanuel Clausius (1822–1888) was a German physicist and mathematician considered one of the founders of the science of thermodynamics. He reformulated Sadi Carnot's principle of the heat engine, giving the theory a far sounder basis, and in 1865 introduced the concept of entropy.

Work

In his landmark 1850 paper "On the Moving Force of Heat", Clausius reconciled Carnot's work with the emerging concept of energy conservation, producing what became the first and second laws of thermodynamics. He stated the second law in the form: heat can never pass from a colder to a warmer body without some other change occurring at the same time.

Clausius also contributed substantially to the kinetic theory of gases, introducing the concept of mean free path and refining James Clerk Maxwell's model. The Clausius–Clapeyron relation, describing phase transitions, bears his name, as does the Clausius–Mossotti relation in dielectrics.

Entropy

In 1865 Clausius coined the term entropy (from Greek for "transformation") for the quantity whose change measures the heat exchanged divided by temperature in a reversible process. He summarised the laws of thermodynamics in the famous lines: "The energy of the universe is constant. The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum." He taught at Zurich, Würzburg and finally Bonn, where he died in 1888.
