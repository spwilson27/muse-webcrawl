Brown bear

KingdomAnimalia
FamilyUrsidae
SpeciesUrsus arctos Linnaeus, 1758
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)
DistributionEurope, Asia, northwestern North America

The brown bear (Ursus arctos) is a large bear species found across Eurasia and northwestern North America. It is one of the largest living terrestrial members of the order Carnivora, rivaled in size only by the polar bear, to which it is closely related.

Description

Brown bears have long, thick fur with a moderately long mane at the back of the neck, and a distinctive hump of muscle over the shoulders that powers their forelimbs for digging. Adults typically weigh 130–300 kg, though large coastal males in Alaska — including the Kodiak bear, the largest recognised subspecies — can exceed 450 kg. Their diet is omnivorous: grasses, berries, roots, insects, fish, and mammals.

Behaviour

Brown bears are generally solitary except for mothers with cubs and gatherings at rich food sources such as salmon runs. In cold regions they hibernate in dens for up to seven months. Despite their size they can run at speeds of about 55 km/h (35 mph).

Subspecies and conservation

Recognised subspecies include the Eurasian brown bear, grizzly bear, Kodiak bear, and the extinct Atlas and California bears. The species as a whole is listed as Least Concern, with a global population around 200,000, though some isolated populations in Europe and Asia are endangered.
