Gargoyle

In architecture, a gargoyle is a carved or formed grotesque with a spout designed to convey water from a roof and away from the side of a building, thereby preventing rainwater from running down masonry walls and eroding the mortar. Architects often used multiple gargoyles on a building to divide the flow of rainwater off the roof to minimize potential damage from rainstorms.

Etymology

The term originates from the French word gargouille, meaning "throat" or "gullet", and is related to the gurgling sound of water passing through the spout. A carved figure that is purely decorative and does not serve as a waterspout is properly called a grotesque or chimera.

History

Waterspouts carved as animals were used in ancient Egyptian, Greek and Roman architecture. The form reached its height in the Gothic architecture of medieval Europe, particularly on cathedrals such as Notre-Dame de Paris, where elaborate gargoyles in the form of fantastical beasts, monsters and human figures projected from the upper walls. In the 19th century, the Gothic Revival renewed interest in gargoyles, and they were added to many new buildings and restored cathedrals.

Purpose and symbolism

Beyond their practical drainage function, gargoyles have been interpreted as protectors of the building, warding off evil spirits, or as moralising images illustrating sins and torments to a largely illiterate medieval audience. Some scholars suggest they may descend from apotropaic imagery intended to frighten away malevolent forces.
