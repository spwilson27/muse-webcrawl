In-group and out-group

In-group and out-group
FieldSociology, social psychology
Term coinedWilliam Graham Sumner, Folkways (1906)
Related theorySocial identity theory (Tajfel and Turner)
PhenomenaIn-group favouritism, out-group homogeneity

In sociology and social psychology, an in-group is a social group to which a person psychologically identifies as being a member. By contrast, an out-group is a social group with which an individual does not identify. People tend to favour members of their own in-group, evaluate them more positively, and perceive out-group members as more similar to one another than they really are (the out-group homogeneity effect).

Origins

The terms derive from William Graham Sumner's Folkways (1906), which described ethnocentrism as arising from the division of society into a "we-group" and "others-groups". Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments in the 1970s showed that even arbitrary, trivial group assignments — such as a stated preference for one painter over another — suffice to trigger in-group favouritism in allocating rewards.

Consequences

In-group/out-group categorisation underlies stereotyping, prejudice and intergroup conflict, but also cohesion, cooperation and identity. Social identity theory holds that people derive self-esteem from group membership and therefore seek positive distinctiveness for their in-group. Research on intergroup contact, common in-group identities and crossed categorisation explores ways to reduce bias between groups. Automatic associations between group membership and evaluation are also studied under the heading of implicit bias.
