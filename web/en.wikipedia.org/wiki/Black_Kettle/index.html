Black Kettle (Cheyenne: Motavato; c. 1803 – November 27, 1868) was a prominent leader of the Southern Cheyenne during the American Indian Wars of the mid-19th century. A committed advocate of peace with the United States, he survived the Sand Creek massacre of 1864 but was killed four years later when troops under George Armstrong Custer attacked his village on the Washita River.

Early life

Black Kettle was born around 1803, probably in the Black Hills region of present-day South Dakota. Little is known of his early life. By the 1850s he had risen to become a chief of the Wotapio band of the Southern Cheyenne, and he was recognized as one of the principal chiefs of the tribe.

Peace efforts

Black Kettle consistently sought accommodation with the United States government and white settlers. He was a signatory of the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1851 and later of the Treaty of Fort Wise in 1861, in which the Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho ceded much of their land in exchange for a reservation in present-day Colorado. Many Cheyenne rejected the treaty, and attacks by Dog Soldiers and other bands continued, undermining Black Kettle's position.

Sand Creek massacre

In November 1864, seeking protection, Black Kettle camped with about 600 Cheyenne and Arapaho near Fort Lyon, Colorado, under assurances of safety from the U.S. Army, flying both an American flag and a white flag over his lodge. On November 29, 1864, Colonel John Chivington led Colorado volunteers in a surprise attack on the camp at Sand Creek, killing an estimated 70 to 600 Native Americans, most of them women, children, and the elderly. Black Kettle escaped, though many of his family members were killed. Despite the massacre, he continued to counsel peace, remarking that he wanted to be friendly with the whites and to do what they said.

Treaty of Medicine Lodge

In October 1867 Black Kettle was among the signatories of the Medicine Lodge Treaty, which assigned the Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho a reservation in Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma). Government promises of provisions and protection were poorly kept, and hunger and raids by younger warriors raised tensions through 1868.

Death at the Washita

In November 1868 Black Kettle's village was camped on the Washita River. Black Kettle had recently traveled to Fort Cobb, where he was told by Colonel William B. Hazen that the army could not guarantee his safety unless he came in to the fort, and that Major General Philip Sheridan was at war with hostile bands. On the morning of November 27, 1868, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led the 7th Cavalry in a dawn attack on the village. Black Kettle and his wife, Medicine Woman Later, were shot and killed as they tried to cross the river. The Battle of Washita River remains controversial; Custer reported it as a battle, while Cheyenne accounts describe the killing of many noncombatants.

Legacy

Black Kettle is remembered as a peacemaker who persisted in seeking coexistence despite betrayal and violence. The Black Kettle National Grassland in Oklahoma and Texas is named in his honor, and the Washita Battlefield National Historic Site preserves the site of his village.
