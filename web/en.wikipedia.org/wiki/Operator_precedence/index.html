Operator precedence

Operator precedence is a collection of rules that reflect conventions about which operations to perform first in order to evaluate a given mathematical expression. For example, in mathematics and most computer languages, multiplication is granted a higher precedence than addition, so the expression 2 + 3 × 4 is understood to mean 2 + (3 × 4) = 14, rather than (2 + 3) × 4 = 20.

Definition

The precedence of an operator specifies how "tightly" it binds its operands. Operators with higher precedence become the operands of operators with lower precedence. Operations of equal precedence are resolved by associativity: most arithmetic operators are left-associative, while exponentiation and assignment are typically right-associative.

Order of operations

The conventional order taught in schools — often remembered by mnemonics such as PEMDAS or BODMAS — is:

OrderOperation
1Parentheses (brackets)
2Exponents and roots (orders)
3Multiplication and division (left to right)
4Addition and subtraction (left to right)

Special cases

Unary minus can create ambiguity: in mathematics −3² is conventionally −9, treating exponentiation as binding more tightly, although some calculators and spreadsheet programs return 9. The serial (Oxford) comma of operations and juxtaposed multiplication (as in 6 ÷ 2(1 + 2)) have likewise produced recurring public disputes, illustrating that precedence is a convention of notation rather than a law of mathematics.

Programming languages

Programming languages define their own precedence tables, often with many more levels than ordinary arithmetic — the C language, for instance, has fifteen levels. Style guides commonly recommend parentheses to make intent explicit wherever precedence is non-obvious.
