Shang Yang (Chinese: 商鞅; c. 390 – 338 BC), born Gongsun Yang and also known as Wei Yang, was a Chinese statesman, chancellor of the state of Qin, and a founding figure of Chinese Legalism. His reforms, carried out under Duke Xiao of Qin from 359 BC, transformed Qin from a backward frontier state into the centralised, militarised power that would conquer the other Warring States and unify China under the Qin dynasty in 221 BC.

Reforms

Shang Yang's two great reform programmes (359 and 350 BC) restructured Qin society around agriculture and war. Land was privatised and measured in standard units; taxes and corvée were regularised; weights, measures, and coinage were standardised. The hereditary aristocracy lost its privileges: rank was earned only by military merit, counted in enemy heads taken. The population was organised into mutually responsible groups of five and ten households, collectively punished for one another's crimes; people were required to report offences and were rewarded for doing so. Administration by appointed magistrates replaced feudal lordship, the capital was moved to Xianyang, and a uniform code of law was applied to all — famously including the crown prince's household, whose tutors were punished when he offended.

To establish the credibility of his laws, Shang Yang is said to have offered fifty pieces of gold to anyone who would move a log from one city gate to another; when a man did so and was paid on the spot, the populace concluded that the new laws would be enforced exactly as written.

Death and legacy

When Duke Xiao died in 338 BC, the new king, Huiwen — the same crown prince whose tutors Shang Yang had mutilated — had him arrested. Shang Yang fled and, in a celebrated irony, was refused lodging at an inn because his own law required innkeepers to check guests' credentials; he was captured and executed by being torn apart by chariots, and his family was exterminated. His reforms, however, were retained, and the text associated with his school, The Book of Lord Shang, remains a key source for Legalist thought. A century later, Qin's Legalist machinery enabled the campaigns, including the catastrophe at Changping, that destroyed Qin's rivals.
