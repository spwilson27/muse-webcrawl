Charles Edward Stuart

Charles Edward Louis John Sylvester Maria Casimir Stuart (31 December 1720 – 30 January 1788), often called Bonnie Prince Charlie or the Young Pretender, was the elder son of James Francis Edward Stuart and the grandson of James II of England. He led the Jacobite rising of 1745, the last attempt to restore the exiled House of Stuart to the British throne, advancing as far south as Derby before retreat, defeat at the Battle of Culloden in April 1746, and his famous flight through the Highlands.

The '45

Born and raised in Rome at the exiled Stuart court, Charles persuaded the French to support an invasion, but storms and British naval action wrecked the plan; he landed in Scotland in July 1745 with a handful of companions. Raising his father's standard at Glenfinnan on 19 August, he gathered a largely Highland army, took Edinburgh, and routed government forces at Prestonpans. The army crossed into England and reached Derby on 4 December, but, believing promised English and French support illusory, his council voted to retreat. At Culloden on 16 April 1746 the Jacobite army was destroyed by the Duke of Cumberland's forces.

Flight and exile

For five months Charles was hunted through the Hebrides with a price of £30,000 on his head; he was famously helped by Flora MacDonald, who disguised him as her maid "Betty Burke" for the crossing to Skye — an episode remembered in the "Skye Boat Song". He escaped to France in September 1746 but was expelled in 1748 under the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. His later years in exile were marked by plots, a secret visit to London, heavy drinking and decline. In 1772 he married Princess Louise of Stolberg-Gedern, a marriage that ended unhappily without children. On his father's death in 1766, Jacobites regarded him as "Charles III", though he was never recognised by any government. He died in Rome in 1788 and is buried in St Peter's Basilica.

Legacy

Romanticised in the nineteenth century, Bonnie Prince Charlie remains an enduring figure of Scottish popular culture, commemorated in song, story and monuments such as the Glenfinnan Monument raised in 1815.
