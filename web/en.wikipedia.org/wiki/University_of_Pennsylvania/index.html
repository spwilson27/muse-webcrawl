The University of Pennsylvania (Penn or UPenn) is a private Ivy League research university in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Founded by Benjamin Franklin, who served as its first president of the board of trustees, it is one of the nine colonial colleges chartered before the American Revolution and home to the first medical school in the United States (1765) and the first collegiate business school, the Wharton School (1881).

History

Penn traces its origins to a 1740 charity school building; Franklin's 1749 pamphlet Proposals Relating to the Education of Youth in Pensilvania led to the Academy and College of Philadelphia, chartered in 1755. The university became the first institution in the United States to bear the title "university" in 1779. In the 1870s it moved to its current campus in West Philadelphia.

Academics

Penn has twelve schools — four undergraduate and twelve graduate/professional, with undergraduate education through the College of Arts and Sciences, the Wharton School, the School of Engineering and Applied Science, and the School of Nursing. Its "One University" policy encourages interdisciplinary study. Wharton, Penn Law, the Perelman School of Medicine, and the School of Veterinary Medicine are among the most highly ranked in their fields. ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic digital computer, was built at Penn in 1945. The university is one of the largest recipients of federal research funding, with annual research expenditures exceeding $1.5 billion.

Campus and student life

The 299-acre urban campus centers on Locust Walk and College Green. Its athletic teams, the Quakers, compete in the Ivy League; Franklin Field (1895) is the oldest college football stadium still in use. Alumni and faculty include 36 Nobel laureates, numerous heads of state, and signers of the Declaration of Independence and the U.S. Constitution.
