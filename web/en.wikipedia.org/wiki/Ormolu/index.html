Ormolu

Ormolu (from the French or moulu, "ground gold") is the technique of applying finely ground, high-carat gold to bronze objects by means of a mercury amalgam, a process known as fire gilding or mercury gilding. The resulting gilt-bronze mounts were a hallmark of French decorative art from the reign of Louis XIV through the Empire period.

Process

A bronze casting was cleaned and coated with an amalgam of gold and mercury, then heated so that the mercury evaporated, leaving a matte gold layer that was burnished with agate tools. The work was extremely hazardous: mercury vapour caused severe poisoning among gilders, and the process was banned in France in the 1830s, after which electroplating replaced it.

Use

Ormolu mounts decorated furniture (as handles, escutcheons and protective corner mounts on commodes and cabinets by makers such as André-Charles Boulle), clocks, candelabra, chandeliers, porcelain and wall lights. Fine French ormolu of the eighteenth century, much of it produced by the guild of fondeurs-ciseleurs and master bronziers like Pierre Gouthière, is among the most prized work in the decorative arts.
