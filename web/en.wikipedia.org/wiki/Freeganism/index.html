Freeganism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freeganism is an ideology and practice of participating as little as possible in the conventional economy and consuming as few resources as possible, typically by recovering wasted goods — especially food — from the waste streams of retailers, restaurants, and institutions. The word combines "free" and "vegan"; the movement grew out of the anarchist and anti-globalisation countercultures of the 1990s, drawing on earlier traditions such as the Diggers of 1960s San Francisco, who distributed free food and ran free stores. Freegans are motivated variously by opposition to consumerism and corporate power, concern over food waste and the environment, and a desire for communal, non-monetary ways of meeting needs.

Practices

The best-known freegan practice is dumpster diving (called "skipping" in Britain) — retrieving edible food and usable goods discarded by supermarkets and bakeries, much of it still packaged and safe. Organisations such as Food Not Bombs, founded in 1980, recover surplus food to serve free public meals. Freegans also practise squatting or minimising housing costs, wild foraging and guerrilla gardening, repairing and swapping goods, hitchhiking and bike riding, and "really really free markets" at which goods and services are given without exchange. Some freegans work as little as possible ("voluntary unemployment"); others hold jobs but freeganise their consumption.

Criticism and reception

Critics argue that freeganism is parasitic on the very economy it rejects, that it can be a lifestyle available mainly to those with the security to return to conventional life, and that practices such as dumpster diving raise health and legal questions (laws on taking discarded goods vary by jurisdiction). Freegans respond that they expose the scale of waste — roughly a third of food produced globally is lost or wasted, according to FAO estimates — and prefigure alternatives. The movement has drawn media attention since the early 2000s, and some of its ideas overlap with the sharing economy, buy-nothing groups, and food-rescue charities, though freegans often distinguish their anti-capitalist stance from those projects.
