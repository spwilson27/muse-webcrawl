Gaius (fl. AD 130–180) was a celebrated Roman jurist. Scholars know little of his life; "Gaius" was merely his praenomen, and he appears to have lived and taught law in a provincial setting, possibly in the eastern Empire. Unlike most classical jurists, he held no known public office and was little cited by contemporaries, yet his writings became foundational.

The Institutes

Gaius's most famous work is the Institutes, a systematic textbook of Roman law in four books written around AD 161. It organises the whole of private law around a threefold division — persons, things, and actions (personae, res, actiones) — a structure that profoundly influenced later legal systems, including the Institutes of Justinian and, through them, the civil law tradition.

The work was long known only through later summaries until 1816, when Barthold Georg Niebuhr identified a palimpsest manuscript at Verona containing most of the original text beneath a later work of St. Jerome.

Influence

The Law of Citations (AD 426) named Gaius one of the five jurists whose opinions carried authority in court, alongside Papinian, Paul, Ulpian and Modestinus. Justinian's compilers drew heavily on him, and Justinian's own Institutes are closely modelled on Gaius's, referring to him as "Gaius noster" ("our Gaius").
