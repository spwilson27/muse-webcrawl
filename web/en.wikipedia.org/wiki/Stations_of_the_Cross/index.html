Stations of the Cross

The Stations of the Cross or the Way of the Cross, also known as the Way of Sorrows or the Via Crucis, is a Christian devotion commemorating Jesus Christ's last day on Earth as a man. The devotion consists of a series of fourteen images or stations depicting events in the Passion of Christ, from his condemnation by Pontius Pilate to his burial. The stations are commonly arranged along a path inside a church, or outdoors along a processional route, and devotees move from station to station, stopping at each to pray and meditate.

History

The devotion arose from the practice of pilgrims to the Holy Land who retraced the route Jesus was believed to have taken through Jerusalem to Calvary, the Via Dolorosa. From the early centuries of Christianity, pilgrims visited the sites associated with the Passion. In the medieval period, the Franciscans, who were granted custody of the Holy Land shrines in 1342, promoted the devotion throughout Europe. Reproductions of the holy places and, eventually, series of images representing the episodes of the Passion were erected in churches so that those unable to travel to Jerusalem could make the journey spiritually.

The number of stations varied considerably in the early centuries, from as few as seven to as many as thirty-seven. The conventional number of fourteen was fixed by Pope Clement XII in 1731, who also granted the devotion a plenary indulgence under the usual conditions. Pope Pius XI confirmed the number in 1931. In 1991, Pope John Paul II introduced a "Scriptural Way of the Cross" based strictly on episodes recorded in the Gospels, which he celebrated at the Colosseum in Rome on Good Friday.

The fourteen stations

  1. Jesus is condemned to death
  2. Jesus takes up his cross
  3. Jesus falls the first time
  4. Jesus meets his mother
  5. Simon of Cyrene helps Jesus carry the cross
  6. Veronica wipes the face of Jesus
  7. Jesus falls the second time
  8. Jesus meets the women of Jerusalem
  9. Jesus falls the third time
  10. Jesus is stripped of his garments
  11. Jesus is nailed to the cross
  12. Jesus dies on the cross
  13. Jesus is taken down from the cross
  14. Jesus is laid in the tomb

Of these, only eight (the 1st, 2nd, 5th, 8th, 10th, 11th, 12th, and 14th, in whole or part) have a clear scriptural basis; the falls of Jesus and the meeting with Veronica derive from tradition. Some modern versions add a fifteenth station, the Resurrection of Jesus.

Practice

The devotion is commonly prayed on Fridays, especially during Lent, and most solemnly on Good Friday. Worshippers move physically between the stations, often singing verses of the Stabat Mater hymn, traditionally attributed to Jacopone da Todi. Individual stations may be elaborately carved or painted sculptures in older churches, while modern sets are frequently simple crosses numbered with Roman numerals. Large outdoor sets exist at pilgrimage sites such as Lourdes and at the Colosseum in Rome, where the Pope traditionally leads the Good Friday observance.

In other traditions

Although most strongly associated with Roman Catholicism, the devotion is also practised by some Anglicans, Lutherans, and Methodists. The Eastern Orthodox Church has related services of the Passion during Holy Week, such as the Matins of the Twelve Gospels, but does not use the Stations in the Western form.
