Val di Noto

The Val di Noto is a historical and geographical area in the south-east of Sicily, Italy, corresponding roughly to one of the three ancient administrative divisions (valli) of the island. It is best known for its group of eight towns inscribed by UNESCO in 2002 as the World Heritage Site "Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto (South-Eastern Sicily)".

The 1693 earthquake and rebuilding

On 11 January 1693 a catastrophic earthquake devastated south-eastern Sicily, killing tens of thousands and destroying dozens of towns. The affected communities were rebuilt — some on new sites, as with Noto and Avola — in the flamboyant late-Baroque style now called Sicilian Baroque, using the local golden limestone, with sweeping staircases, convex and concave church façades, and theatrical town planning.

The World Heritage towns

UNESCO praised the towns as representing "the culmination and final flowering of Baroque art in Europe". The group includes the great cathedrals of Noto and Modica, the rebuilt city of Catania (with Giovanni Battista Vaccarini's elephant fountain), the ceramics town of Caltagirone, and Ragusa Ibla, rebuilt by the local aristocracy. The wider Val di Noto area today also draws visitors for its wine (Nero d'Avola, Cerasuolo di Vittoria), chocolate of Modica, and almond and olive production.
