Shield law

A shield law is legislation designed to protect reporters' privilege—the right of journalists to refuse to reveal confidential sources or unpublished information. Such laws are intended to preserve the free flow of information to the press and the public by assuring sources that their identities will not be disclosed.

United States

Most U.S. states have enacted shield laws, beginning with Maryland in 1896, and others recognize a qualified reporter's privilege through court decisions. The scope of protection varies widely: some statutes are absolute for confidential sources, while others provide only a qualified privilege that can be overcome by a showing of need. There is no general federal shield law, although the Department of Justice has internal guidelines limiting subpoenas to the press. In Branzburg v. Hayes (1972), the Supreme Court held that the First Amendment does not exempt reporters from testifying before grand juries, prompting many states to strengthen statutory protections.

Issues

Debates over shield laws center on who qualifies as a journalist in the era of blogs and independent online media, and whether protection should extend to non-confidential information. High-profile cases in which reporters were jailed for contempt after refusing to name sources have periodically renewed proposals for a federal shield law in the United States.

Other countries

Many other democracies protect journalistic sources through statute or constitutional interpretation; source protection is recognized in European human rights jurisprudence under freedom of expression guarantees.
