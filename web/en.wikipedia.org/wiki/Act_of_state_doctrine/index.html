Act of state doctrine

The act of state doctrine is a principle of law in the United States and other common-law jurisdictions under which courts refrain from questioning the validity of official acts of a foreign sovereign performed within its own territory. It is a rule of judicial restraint grounded in respect for foreign sovereignty and the separation of powers in foreign affairs.

United States doctrine

The classic formulation came in Underhill v. Hernandez (1897), where the U.S. Supreme Court held that "the courts of one country will not sit in judgment on the acts of the government of another, done within its own territory." The doctrine received its definitive statement in Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino (1964), involving Cuba's expropriation of sugar owned by an American company. The Court held that U.S. courts would not examine the validity of a foreign state's public acts, even if alleged to violate international law, absent a treaty or other unambiguous agreement.

Exceptions and limitations

  • The Hickenlooper Amendment — Congress responded to Sabbatino with legislation directing courts to adjudicate certain expropriation claims contrary to international law unless the President intervenes.
  • The Bernstein exception — where the executive branch advises that adjudication will not harm foreign relations, courts may proceed.
  • Commercial activity — in Alfred Dunhill of London v. Republic of Cuba (1976), a plurality suggested the doctrine does not shield purely commercial acts.
  • Territorial limitation — the act must have been performed within the foreign state's own territory (W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp., 1990), and the doctrine bars only challenges to the validity of the act, not suits that merely require examining a foreign official's motivations.

Other jurisdictions

Analogous doctrines exist in the United Kingdom under the foreign act of state doctrine, refined in cases such as Belhaj v. Straw (2017), and similar principles of non-justiciability appear in other legal systems.
