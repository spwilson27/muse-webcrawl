Barcarolle

OriginVenetian gondolier song
Meter6/8 or 12/8, moderate tempo
Notable examplesOffenbach, The Tales of Hoffmann; Chopin, Barcarolle in F-sharp major, Op. 60

A barcarolle (from the Italian barcarola, "boat song", from barca, "boat") is a traditional folk song sung by Venetian gondoliers, or a piece of music composed in that style. In classical music, two of the most famous barcarolles are Jacques Offenbach's "Belle nuit, ô nuit d'amour" from The Tales of Hoffmann and Frédéric Chopin's Barcarolle in F-sharp major for solo piano.

Characteristics

A barcarolle is characterised by a lilting rhythm reminiscent of the gondolier's stroke, almost invariably in 6/8 or 12/8 time at a moderate tempo. While many barcarolles are in major keys, a number are in minor keys or alternate between major and minor, giving the form a bittersweet quality. The melody typically flows smoothly above a gently rocking accompaniment figure representing the movement of water.

History

The songs of the Venetian gondoliers were famous throughout Europe in the 18th century; visitors to Venice described the gondoliers singing verses of Tasso and other poets to characteristic melodies. Composers began imitating the style in opera and instrumental music, and by the Romantic period the barcarolle had become a popular character piece evoking travel, water and nostalgia.

Notable barcarolles

Felix Mendelssohn included three pieces titled "Venetian Gondola Song" among his Songs Without Words. Chopin's Barcarolle, Op. 60 (1846), is considered one of his late masterpieces. Charles-Valentin Alkan, Gabriel Fauré (thirteen barcarolles for piano), and Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky ("June: Barcarolle" from The Seasons) also wrote notable examples. Offenbach's barcarolle from The Tales of Hoffmann — adapted from his earlier opera Die Rheinnixen — remains the best-known example in opera.
