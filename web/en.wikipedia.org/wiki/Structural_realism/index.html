Structural realism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural realism is a position in the philosophy of science that attempts to reconcile the competing claims of scientific realism and antirealism. It holds that what science reliably reveals about the world is its structure — the mathematical relations between entities — rather than the intrinsic nature of the entities themselves. The view is most prominently associated with the British philosopher John Worrall, who introduced the term in a landmark 1989 paper.

Background

Structural realism emerged as a response to two powerful arguments in the realism debate. The pessimistic meta-induction, associated with Larry Laudan, observes that many past scientific theories turned out to be fundamentally wrong about the nature of reality — phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether were all discarded — so we should expect current theories to suffer the same fate. Against this, the no-miracles argument, associated with Hilary Putnam, holds that the predictive success of mature science would be a miracle if its theories were not approximately true.

Worrall's proposed middle path was to note that theoretical change typically preserves the mathematical structure of earlier theories even when their ontological interpretations are abandoned. The classic example is the transition from Fresnel's ether-based theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory: Fresnel's equations were retained almost intact within Maxwell's framework, even though the ether was discarded.

Variants

Epistemic structural realism (Worrall's version) is an epistemological claim: structure is all we can know. It accepts that there may be unobservable entities with intrinsic natures, but insists that scientific knowledge extends only to relations among them. Critics press the so-called Newman problem: a purely structural claim, cashed out in set-theoretic terms, can be trivially satisfied by any collection of the right cardinality, threatening to make structural knowledge empty.

Ontic structural realism, developed by James Ladyman, Steven French, and others, makes the stronger metaphysical claim that structure is all there is: objects are ontologically subordinate to — or even eliminated in favour of — the relational structure described by fundamental physics, particularly quantum mechanics and general relativity. Supporters cite the indistinguishability of quantum particles and the underdetermination of individuality in quantum theory as evidence that objects lack the primitive individuality classical metaphysics assumed.

Criticism

Critics of ontic structural realism argue that the notion of a relation without relata is incoherent, or at least requires further metaphysical work. Others question whether the retention of mathematical form across theory change is as general as structural realists claim, and whether the position can adequately handle the success of sciences, such as biology or chemistry, whose theories are not straightforwardly mathematical.
