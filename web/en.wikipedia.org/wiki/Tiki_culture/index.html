Tiki culture

Tiki culture is an American-origin art, music, and entertainment movement based on a romanticised conception of Polynesian and other South Pacific island cultures. Flourishing from the 1930s through the 1960s, and revived in later decades, it produced tiki bars and restaurants decorated with carved tiki figures, bamboo, rattan, torches, and tropical murals, which served elaborate rum-based cocktails in distinctive ceramic mugs.

Origins

The movement began in 1933 or 1934 when Ernest Raymond Beaumont Gantt opened Don the Beachcomber in Hollywood, California, shortly after the repeal of Prohibition. Gantt, who had travelled the Caribbean and South Pacific, decorated his bar with artefacts and invented numerous rum cocktails, including the Zombie. Victor Bergeron, founder of Trader Vic's, became his great rival; Bergeron is credited with creating the Mai Tai in 1944. The returning servicemen of the Second World War, many of whom had been stationed in the Pacific, and the admission of Hawaii as a US state in 1959 helped fuel the fad.

Heyday and decline

At its peak, tiki style permeated American popular culture: exotica music by Martin Denny, Les Baxter, and Arthur Lyman; backyard luaus; Hawaiian shirts; tiki apartment buildings and bowling alleys. Walt Disney's Enchanted Tiki Room (1963) brought the aesthetic to Disneyland. From the late 1960s the style fell from fashion as tastes changed and as Polynesian kitsch came to be seen as dated or culturally appropriative. Most original tiki bars closed during the 1970s and 1980s.

Revival

Beginning in the 1990s, a revival driven by enthusiasts, urban archaeologists, and cocktail historians — notably Sven Kirsten's The Book of Tiki (2000) and the work of mixologist Jeff Berry — restored classic tiki drinks and led to a new generation of tiki bars worldwide. The revival sometimes distinguishes "tiki" as a specifically American mid-century fantasy rather than an authentic representation of Polynesian cultures.
