Overview

Colour blindness, more precisely colour vision deficiency, is a decreased ability to see colour or differences in colour. Most often inherited, it arises from absent or altered photopigments in the retinal cone cells. The commonest forms disturb red–green discrimination: protan defects (L-cone/red) and deutan defects (M-cone/green), both X-linked recessive — hence the strong male predominance. John Dalton, who had a deutan-type deficiency, gave the first scientific account (1798) of his own condition, whence the older name "Daltonism".

Testing and life impact

The Ishihara test (1917), arrays of coloured dots concealing numerals, is the standard screening tool. Colour vision deficiency rarely blinds, but it bars people from certain occupations (some aviation, maritime, electrical and military roles) and complicates colour-coded information; because red–green signalling is ubiquitous, accessible design increasingly avoids relying on colour alone. There is no treatment for the inherited forms, though tinted lenses and apps claim partial compensation. Acquired colour defects can result from eye disease, neurological disease or drugs.
