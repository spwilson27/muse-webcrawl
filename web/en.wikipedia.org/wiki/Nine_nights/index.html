Nine nights

Nine nights, also known as dead yard, is an African-Jamaican tradition in which mourners gather for a wake lasting — in its extended form — nine nights following the death of a community member, culminating in the "nine night" or "set-up" held on the night before burial. The observance blends West African spiritual practices with Christian elements and is widely celebrated in Jamaica and among the Jamaican diaspora in the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States.

History and origins

The tradition descends from practices brought to the Caribbean by enslaved Africans, who believed that the spirit of the deceased undertakes a journey back to Africa after death. Drawing on Ashanti and other West African beliefs, mourners held that the spirit (duppy in Jamaican patois) lingered near the home for nine days before departing for the ancestral world. The wake gave the spirit time and encouragement to leave peacefully, and helped the community ensure it would not return to trouble the living.

The wake

During the nights between the death and burial, family and friends gather at the home of the deceased. The gatherings feature singing of hymns and choruses, drumming, storytelling, games such as dominoes, and the serving of food and drink — traditionally including fried fish, bread, mannish water (goat soup), curry goat, and rum. The ninth night is the largest and most festive gathering, with music often provided by a Kumina or Mento band, and dancing continues into the early morning. A table may be set with food and white rum for the departing spirit.

Ritual elements

A central purpose of the nine nights is to persuade the spirit to leave the earth. Beds may be moved or turned, and the room where the person died rearranged, so the duppy cannot find its resting place. When the spirit is believed to have departed, mourners may shout or fire a salute. In many communities the final night includes the formal "turning out" of the spirit at midnight.

In the diaspora

Members of the Windrush generation carried nine nights to Britain, where it remains a fixture of Afro-Caribbean community life, sometimes adapted into church halls and community centres. Similar nine-night wakes are held in other parts of the Caribbean, including Grenada, Barbados, and Trinidad, and in parts of West Africa, where funeral celebrations span multiple days.
