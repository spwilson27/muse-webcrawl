Intertidal zone

The intertidal zone, also known as the foreshore, is the area above water level at low tide and underwater at high tide; in other words, it is the part of the littoral zone within the tidal range. This area can include several types of habitats with various species of life, such as seastars, sea urchins and many species of coral, and it is inhabited by organisms adapted to harsh, changing conditions.

Zonation

Biologists divide the intertidal zone into three or four subzones according to height above low water: the spray zone, and the high, middle and low intertidal zones. The longer an area is exposed, the greater the stresses of desiccation, temperature change and salinity variation its inhabitants face, so species are distributed in characteristic bands reflecting their tolerances and competitive interactions.

Ecology

The intertidal is a rich and well-studied ecosystem. At low tide, tide pools shelter small marine animals; exposed surfaces are colonized by algae, barnacles, limpets, mussels and periwinkles, preyed upon by crabs, sea stars and shorebirds. Influential ecological experiments, such as Robert Paine's work on the sea star Pisaster ochraceus, established the concept of the keystone species in this habitat.

Threats

Intertidal habitats are threatened by pollution, trampling and harvesting, coastal development and climate change, including sea-level rise and ocean warming.
