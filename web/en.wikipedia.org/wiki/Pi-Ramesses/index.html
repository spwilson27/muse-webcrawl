Pi-Ramesses (Egyptian: Per-Ra-mes-su, "Domain of Ramesses") was the new capital built by the Nineteenth Dynasty pharaoh Ramesses II (reigned 1279–1213 BC) at Qantir, in the eastern Nile Delta, near the earlier city of Avaris. At its height it was one of the largest cities of ancient Egypt, with a population estimated at around 300,000.

History

Ramesses II moved the royal residence north from Thebes and Memphis to exploit the Delta's strategic position, closer to Egypt's imperial holdings in the Levant and to the frontier with the Hittites. The site also had family associations: his father Seti I had built a summer palace there. Pi-Ramesses served as capital for roughly 150 years under the 19th and 20th Dynasties.

The city

The city sprawled over some 18 square kilometres in and around a branch of the Nile, with a great temple of Amun, royal palaces, military installations including chariotry workshops and stables for hundreds of horses, glass and faience factories, and harbours linking it to the Mediterranean. Contemporary hymns praise its abundance and splendour, and it has often been connected with the "Raamses" of the biblical Exodus narrative, though that identification is debated.

Decline

Around 1060 BC the Pelusiac branch of the Nile silted up, leaving the city without its harbour and water supply. The capital was moved to Tanis, whose builders extensively reused Pi-Ramesses' stonework — so much so that Tanis was long mistaken for Pi-Ramesses itself.

Archaeology

Excavations at Qantir, led for decades by Edgar Pusch and the Pelizaeus Museum (Hildesheim), have uncovered workshops producing bronze, glass and the blue pigment "Egyptian blue", monumental statue fragments, and stable complexes, confirming the identification of Qantir with the royal city.
