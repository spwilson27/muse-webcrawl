Domestication of the dog

AnimalDomestic dog (Canis lupus familiaris)
AncestorA Late Pleistocene population of wolves
WhenAt least 15,000 years ago; possibly 20,000–40,000
WhereEurasia (debated)

The domestication of the dog was the process by which the domestic dog diverged from a population of wolves during the Late Pleistocene, becoming the first species domesticated by humans — long before the advent of agriculture. Dogs accompanied hunter-gatherers, and the oldest widely accepted remains of a domestic dog are those of the Bonn-Oberkassel dog, buried beside humans in Germany about 14,200 years ago. Genetic studies indicate dogs descend from an extinct lineage of wolves distinct from modern wolf populations, with proposed places of origin in Europe, Siberia or East Asia, and debate continues over whether domestication happened once or more than once.

Commensal pathway

The leading theory holds that dogs were domesticated through a commensal pathway: bolder wolves that scavenged around human camps gained a food advantage, and natural selection in this new niche favoured shorter flight distances and reduced aggression. Humans and wolves may then have formed hunting partnerships, with dogs later used for guarding, hauling and herding. Over time, dogs diverged from wolves in skull shape, dentition, digestion of starches, and social cognition — dogs became adept at reading human gestures and forming attachments, a bond reinforced by the oxytocin feedback loop between dogs and their owners.

Spread and diversification

Dogs spread with humans across the world, reaching the Americas with the first migrants over 10,000 years ago and Australia (as the dingo) several thousand years later. For most of history dogs were landraces shaped by function and geography; the great majority of modern breeds were created only in the last 200 years through closed studbooks and intense artificial selection.
