Glass–Steagall Act of 1932

The Glass–Steagall Act of 1932 was a United States federal law enacted on February 27, 1932. It liberalized the Federal Reserve's ability to lend and allowed government securities to back Federal Reserve notes, easing deflationary pressure during the Great Depression.

Provisions

Named for Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall, the act permitted Federal Reserve Banks to make emergency advances to member banks on their promissory notes secured by approved assets, and broadened the collateral eligible to back the currency. It was one of the first legislative responses aimed at expanding credit and stopping the deflation of 1930–1932.

Distinction from the 1933 act

The 1932 act is often confused with the Banking Act of 1933, also co-sponsored by Glass and Steagall, which created federal deposit insurance and separated commercial from investment banking. The 1932 law instead addressed the Federal Reserve's lending and collateral rules.
