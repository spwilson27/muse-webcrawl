Eminent domain (also known as land acquisition, compulsory purchase, compulsory acquisition, or expropriation) is the power of a state, provincial, or national government to take private property for public use. It does not include the power to take and transfer ownership of private property from one property owner to another private party without a public purpose having been established. The taking is usually accompanied by payment of compensation to the owner.

United States law

In the United States, the power is limited by the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment: "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation." The clause was applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in 1897. Landmark cases include Berman v. Parker (1954) and Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984), which read "public use" broadly as public purpose, and the controversial Kelo v. City of New London (2005), which upheld taking homes for a private development plan said to serve economic development. The Kelo backlash led more than forty states to tighten their eminent domain laws.

Elsewhere

Most legal systems recognise an equivalent power: compulsory purchase in the United Kingdom, expropriation in Canada and much of the civil-law world, and resumption in Australia. The European Convention on Human Rights protects possessions under Protocol 1, Article 1, subject to takings in the public interest under conditions provided for by law.
