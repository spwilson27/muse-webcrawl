Ingroups and outgroups

In social psychology and sociology, an ingroup is a social group to which a person psychologically identifies as being a member. By contrast, an outgroup is a social group with which an individual does not identify. People may belong to many ingroups — based on nationality, ethnicity, religion, profession, fandom or family — and the same person can be an ingroup member in one context and an outgroup member in another. The concepts trace to the sociologist William Graham Sumner, who introduced them in his 1906 book Folkways, describing the attitudes of loyalty toward one's own group and hostility toward outsiders.

Ingroup favoritism

Ingroup favoritism (or ingroup bias) is the tendency to favour members of one's own group over outgroup members in evaluations, allocations of resources, and attributions. Experiments using the minimal group paradigm, pioneered by Henri Tajfel, showed that even arbitrary, trivial group assignments — such as a stated preference for one painter over another — are sufficient to produce discrimination in favour of the ingroup, a finding central to social identity theory, which explains the bias as a way of maintaining positive self-esteem through group membership.

Outgroup effects

Attitudes toward outgroups range from indifference to prejudice and hostility. Documented phenomena include the outgroup homogeneity effect — perceiving outgroup members as more similar to one another than ingroup members ("they are all alike; we are diverse") — and outgroup derogation, in which outgroup members are judged more harshly for the same behaviour. Under conditions of competition or perceived threat, ingroup/outgroup dynamics can escalate into intergroup conflict, as in Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment, where hostility between groups of boys was reduced by superordinate goals requiring cooperation. Research on intergroup contact suggests that contact under the right conditions can reduce prejudice between groups.
