Greek architecture

Ancient Greek architecture is the architecture produced by the Greek-speaking peoples of mainland Greece, the Aegean islands, and Greek colonies from about 900 BC until the 1st century AD, with the earliest remaining architectural works dating from around 600 BC. It is best known for its temples, built in stone and marble according to strict proportional systems organized into the classical orders: Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian.

Characteristics

Greek architecture emphasized public buildings above all temples, theaters, stoas, and civic structures of the polis. Temples were designed as the dwelling of a deity and the setting for outdoor sacrifice; their plans typically comprise a rectangular cella, porches, and a peristyle of columns. Greek architects pursued harmony through proportion and refined optical corrections—such as entasis, the slight swelling of columns, and the subtle curvature of the stylobate—to counteract visual distortion.

The orders

The Doric order, arising on the mainland, is sturdy and plain, with fluted columns lacking bases and a simple capital. The Ionic order, from the Aegean islands and Asia Minor, is slimmer, with volute capitals and a continuous frieze. The Corinthian order, the most ornate, features capitals carved with acanthus leaves and became common in the Hellenistic and Roman periods. Canonical examples include the Parthenon (Ionic and Doric mixed, 447–432 BC), the Temple of Hephaestus in Athens, the temples of Paestum and Agrigento in Magna Graecia, and the Erechtheion with its Porch of the Caryatids.

Later periods and influence

In the Hellenistic age, architecture expanded to grand urban planning, theaters such as Epidaurus, gymnasia, and monumental altars like the Pergamon Altar. Roman architecture absorbed Greek forms wholesale, and through Rome and the Renaissance the Greek orders became the foundation of Western classical architecture, revived again in the Greek Revival of the 18th and 19th centuries.
