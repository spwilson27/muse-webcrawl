Line of communication

A line of communication (or communications; abbreviated LOC) is the route connecting an operating military unit with its supply base. Supplies and reinforcements are transported along the line of communication, and casualties and prisoners are evacuated in the opposite direction. Therefore, a secure and open line of communication is vital for any military force to continue operating effectively.

Types

  • A sea line of communication (SLOC) is a maritime route between ports, used for trade and logistics by naval forces.
  • A ground line of communication (GLOC) is a land route, such as a road or railway, used to sustain deployed forces.
  • An air line of communication (ALOC) is an air route used for airlift and resupply.

Strategic importance

Throughout military history, armies that allowed their lines of communication to be cut have faced starvation, loss of ammunition and eventual defeat. Attacking an enemy's communications — while protecting one's own — is a central theme of strategy, from the campaigns of Napoleon, whose armies partly lived off the land to reduce their dependence on supply lines, to the Allied interdiction campaigns of the Second World War.

Long lines of communication are especially vulnerable. During Operation Barbarossa, German supply lines stretched deep into the Soviet Union and were hampered by partisan attacks, poor roads and the change of railway gauge. In the Pacific War, the United States Navy's submarine campaign against Japanese merchant shipping severed Japan's sea lines of communication to its resource areas in Southeast Asia, crippling its war economy.

The concept also underlies modern geopolitics: maritime chokepoints such as the Strait of Malacca, the Suez Canal and the Strait of Hormuz are critical nodes on the sea lines of communication over which much of world trade passes.
