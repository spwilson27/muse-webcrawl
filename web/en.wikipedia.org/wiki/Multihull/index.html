Multihull

A multihull is a boat or ship with more than one hull, in contrast to a monohull. Multihull vessels derive their stability from the wide separation of their hulls rather than from ballast, which allows them to be lighter, carry more sail for their displacement, and achieve high speeds. The most common types are catamarans (two hulls) and trimarans (three hulls).

Types

  • Catamaran — two hulls of equal size, joined by a bridging structure. Used for sailing yachts, passenger ferries, and naval vessels.
  • Trimaran — a main hull with two smaller outrigger hulls (amas) attached by crossbeams (akas).
  • Proa — a Pacific design with one large hull and a smaller outrigger, traditionally asymmetrical in sailing configuration.
  • Quadrimaran and pentamaran — rare designs with four or five hulls, mainly experimental or proposed for fast ferries.

History

Multihull designs are ancient in the Pacific, where Austronesian peoples developed outrigger canoes, double canoes, and proas that enabled the settlement of Oceania. European sailors encountered these fast, stable craft during voyages of exploration. Western multihull design developed in the 19th and 20th centuries, with notable contributions from Nathanael Herreshoff, who patented a catamaran design in 1876, and from postwar pioneers such as James Wharram and Dick Newick.

Characteristics

Multihulls have a high initial stability and a large deck area for their length, but they can be difficult to tack, have limited load-carrying capacity relative to monohulls of the same length, and — if capsized — are very difficult to right. Modern racing multihulls, including America's Cup catamarans and ocean-racing trimarans, are among the fastest sailing vessels in the world; large trimarans have set outright ocean speed records.
