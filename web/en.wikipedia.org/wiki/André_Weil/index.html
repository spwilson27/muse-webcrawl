André Abraham Weil (6 May 1906 – 6 August 1998) was a French mathematician, known for his foundational work in number theory and algebraic geometry. He was one of the most influential mathematical figures of the twentieth century, a leading member and early founder of the Bourbaki group, and the elder brother of the philosopher Simone Weil.

Life

Weil studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and completed his doctorate in 1928 with a thesis extending the Mordell theorem on rational points on elliptic curves. He travelled widely, held posts at Aligarh Muslim University in India, Strasbourg, and São Paulo, and moved to the United States during the Second World War, working at the University of Chicago from 1947 to 1958 before joining the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.

Work

Weil rebuilt the foundations of algebraic geometry on rigorous algebraic grounds, introduced the tools of abstract algebraic geometry used ever since, and in 1949 formulated the celebrated Weil conjectures on zeta functions of varieties over finite fields — profound statements linking geometry and number theory that guided decades of research and were finally proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974. He also created the theory of adeles and ideles in class field theory, founded the Weil representation, and contributed to the eventual proof strategy of Fermat's Last Theorem through the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture.
