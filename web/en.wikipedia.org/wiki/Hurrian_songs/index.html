Hurrian songs

The Hurrian songs are a collection of ancient music inscribed on clay tablets excavated at Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra, Syria) and dated to approximately 1400 BCE. They are among the oldest surviving examples of written musical notation anywhere in the world. The best-known piece, the Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal (designated h.6), is widely regarded as the oldest substantially complete notated song yet discovered.

Discovery and contents

The tablets were excavated in the 1950s from the royal palace archives of Ugarit. About three dozen fragments preserve portions of hymns in the Hurrian language written in cuneiform, with musical instructions in Akkadian technical terms referring to intervals and tunings of a lyre. The texts appear to describe the tuning of the instrument and the intervals to be played, keyed to dichords (two-string intervals) of a nine-stringed lyre.

The Hymn to Nikkal

Tablet h.6 contains a hymn to Nikkal, the Ugaritic goddess of orchards and wife of the moon god, accompanied by instructions for a singer and lyre accompaniment. Scholars including Laroche, Duchesne-Guillemin, Kilmer, and Crocker have produced competing transcriptions and interpretations of the notation, and numerous modern recordings and performances attempt to reconstruct how the hymn may have sounded. Because the notation encodes intervals rather than exact pitches and rhythm must be inferred from the text, all reconstructions remain partly conjectural.

Significance

The Hurrian songs demonstrate that the system of seven-note diatonic scales and named intervals, later familiar from Greek theory, was already systematized in the ancient Near East by the late second millennium BCE, drawing on even older Mesopotamian tuning texts. Together with the earlier Sumerian and Old Babylonian musical texts, they form the earliest substantial evidence for written music in world history.
