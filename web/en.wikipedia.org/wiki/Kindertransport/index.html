Kindertransport

The Kindertransport (German for "children's transport") was an organised rescue effort of children (but generally not their parents) from Nazi-controlled territory that took place in 1938–1939 during the nine months prior to the outbreak of the Second World War. The United Kingdom took in nearly 10,000 predominantly Jewish children from Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the Free City of Danzig.

Background

Following the Kristallnacht pogrom of 9–10 November 1938, British Jewish leaders appealed to Parliament. The government agreed to waive visa requirements for unaccompanied children under 17, provided private individuals or organisations guaranteed their care, foster homes and eventual emigration. Religious and humanitarian organisations, including the Quakers, cooperated in organising the transports.

The transports

The first Kindertransport left Berlin on 1 December 1938; the first from Vienna followed on 10 December. Children travelled by train to the Netherlands and then by ferry to Harwich, or by ship from other ports. The last transport from Germany left on 1 September 1939, the day the war began; the last from the Netherlands departed on 14 May 1940. Many children were placed with foster families; others stayed in hostels, camps and schools. Sponsors and organisers included Nicholas Winton, who arranged the rescue of 669 children from Czechoslovakia.

Legacy

Most of the children never saw their parents again, as the great majority of the parents were murdered in the Holocaust. After the war many remained in Britain or emigrated to Israel, the United States and elsewhere. The Kindertransport is commemorated by memorials at Liverpool Street station in London and elsewhere, and by associations of the surviving "Kinder".
