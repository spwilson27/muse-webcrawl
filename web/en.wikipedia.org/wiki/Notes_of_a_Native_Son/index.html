Notes of a Native Son is a collection of ten essays by the American writer James Baldwin, published in 1955. It was Baldwin's first work of nonfiction and one of the earliest works in the genre of Black nonfiction to receive widespread acclaim, establishing him as a major voice in American letters. The essays combine autobiography, literary criticism and analysis of race in America and Europe — themes Baldwin would develop throughout his career.

Contents

The collection is organised in three parts. Part One contains literary essays, including "Everybody's Protest Novel" (1949), his famous critique of Uncle Tom's Cabin and of Richard Wright's Native Son, and "Many Thousands Gone". Part Two turns to American life: "The Harlem Ghetto", "Journey to Atlanta" and the title essay, "Notes of a Native Son" — a moving account of his father's death and funeral in 1943, set against a Harlem race riot on the same day. Part Three reflects on his expatriate years in Paris, with "Encounter on the Seine", "A Question of Identity" and "Stranger in the Village", written about his stay in a small Swiss village.

Background and publication

Most of the essays first appeared in magazines such as Harper's, Partisan Review and Commentary between 1948 and 1955. Baldwin had left New York for Paris in 1948, and the book reflects his dual perspective as an American insider and exile. It followed his acclaimed first novel, Go Tell It on the Mountain (1953).

Reception and legacy

The book was warmly reviewed on publication and is now regarded as a classic of the essay form. The Modern Library ranked it among the 100 best English-language nonfiction books of the 20th century. Critics praise its fusion of the personal and the political and its prose style; "Notes of a Native Son" in particular is frequently anthologised as a model of the personal essay. The collection paved the way for Baldwin's later landmark works, including Nobody Knows My Name (1961) and The Fire Next Time (1963), and it lent its title, in homage, to later works about race and belonging.
