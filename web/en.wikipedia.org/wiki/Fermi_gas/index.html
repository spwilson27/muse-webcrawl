Fermi gas

A Fermi gas is an idealized model of a large ensemble of many non-interacting fermions—particles with half-integer spin that obey the Pauli exclusion principle. It is the quantum-mechanical version of an ideal gas for fermions and follows Fermi–Dirac statistics. Developed in 1926 by Enrico Fermi (and independently Paul Dirac), the model explains the behavior of electrons in metals, nucleons in nuclei, and matter in white dwarf stars.

Properties

Because no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state, at absolute zero the particles fill the lowest available energy states up to the Fermi energy EF. Even at zero temperature the gas exerts pressure—degeneracy pressure—arising purely from the exclusion principle and the particles' kinetic energy. At finite temperatures, occupation of states near the Fermi surface is described by the Fermi–Dirac distribution f(E) = 1 / (e(E−μ)/kBT + 1).

Applications

  • Electrons in metals: the free-electron model treats conduction electrons as a Fermi gas, explaining the small electronic heat capacity and temperature dependence of conductivity.
  • White dwarfs: electron degeneracy pressure supports a white dwarf against gravitational collapse up to the Chandrasekhar limit; in neutron stars, neutron degeneracy pressure plays the analogous role.
  • Nuclear physics: the Fermi gas model of the nucleus treats protons and neutrons as moving in a common potential well.
  • Ultracold atoms: dilute gases of fermionic atoms (e.g., 40K, 6Li) cooled to nanokelvin temperatures realize near-ideal Fermi gases and paired superfluid states (BCS–BEC crossover).

Contrast with Bose gas

The bosonic counterpart, the Bose gas, obeys Bose–Einstein statistics; at low temperature it undergoes Bose–Einstein condensation, in which a macroscopic fraction of particles occupies the ground state—behavior impossible for fermions.
