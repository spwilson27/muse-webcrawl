Financial modeling

Financial modeling is the task of building an abstract representation (a model) of a real-world financial situation, typically in order to perform financial analysis of a firm, a project, or an investment. It is a central activity in corporate finance, investment banking, equity research, and accounting.

Overview

A financial model usually summarizes the expected financial performance of a business or asset using numbers arranged in a spreadsheet. Models range from simple formulas to complex simulations. Common applications include:

  • Valuation of a company, security, or asset (e.g. using a discounted cash flow analysis)
  • Financial planning and forecasting
  • Decision making about mergers and acquisitions, including leveraged buyouts (LBO models)
  • Project finance and capital budgeting decisions
  • Portfolio management and risk analysis

Types of models

Three-statement models link the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement of a company into a single dynamic model. Other common types include discounted cash flow (DCF) models, comparable company analyses, precedent transaction analyses, merger models, and option pricing models.

Methodology

Most models are built in spreadsheet software such as Microsoft Excel. The builder inputs historical financial data, makes assumptions about growth rates, margins, interest rates, and other drivers, and then projects future performance. Models typically incorporate sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis to test how results change when assumptions vary.

Best practices

Professional models are expected to be transparent, flexible, and well-structured, with assumptions clearly separated from calculations. Errors in models can be costly, so checking formulas and reconciling outputs against benchmarks is standard practice.
