Seabird

Seabirds nesting in a dense coastal colony

Seabirds are birds that have adapted to life within the marine environment. While seabirds vary greatly in lifestyle, behaviour, and physiology, they often exhibit striking convergent evolution, as the same environmental problems have resulted in similar adaptations. Major groups include the penguins, albatrosses and petrels (tubenoses), pelicans and their relatives, gannets and cormorants, gulls, terns, skuas, and auks. There are roughly 350 species generally counted as seabirds.

Adaptations

Seabirds share adaptations for feeding at sea: many have salt glands above the eyes that excrete excess salt, allowing them to drink seawater; waterproof plumage; webbed feet; and, in tubenoses, an acute sense of smell for locating prey and their burrows. Feeding methods include surface seizing, plunge-diving (gannets, boobies), pursuit diving underwater using wings or feet (auks, penguins, diving petrels), kleptoparasitism (skuas and frigatebirds), and filter-feeding on krill by prions. Many species are superb long-distance travellers: the Arctic tern migrates between the Arctic and Antarctic, and sooty shearwaters traverse the entire Pacific basin.

Breeding

Compared with most birds, seabirds have a slow life history: they live long (albatrosses may breed into their 60s), delay breeding for years, lay small clutches — often a single egg — and invest heavily in each chick. The great majority nest in colonies, sometimes numbering millions of birds, on remote islands and cliffs where land predators are absent. Philopatry is strong: individuals typically return to breed where they were raised.

Relationship with humans

Seabirds have guided fishers to shoals, provided guano fertiliser of great economic value, and been harvested for food, feathers, and oil. Today they are among the most threatened groups of birds: major threats include invasive predators (rats, cats) introduced to breeding islands, bycatch in longline and gillnet fisheries, overfishing of forage fish, plastic ingestion, and climate change. Conversely, seabird island restoration — removing invasive species and attracting birds back — is a conservation success story.
