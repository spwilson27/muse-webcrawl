Flamboyant Gothic
PeriodLate 14th century – early 16th century
RegionFrance, then Europe
Preceded byRayonnant Gothic

Flamboyant (from French flamboyer, "to flame") is a form of late Gothic architecture that developed in France and Spain in the late 14th century and spread across Europe in the 15th century. It is characterised by double-curved, flame-like tracery in windows, elaborate stone carving, and dramatic, flowing forms.

Characteristics

The signature feature of Flamboyant is the soufflet and mouchette patterns in window tracery, which resemble flames and daggers. Mullions curve and counter-curve without the circular elements of earlier Rayonnant design. Façades and vaults display dense, lace-like stone ornament, such as the west front of Rouen Cathedral and the church of Saint-Maclou in Rouen.

Spread and decline

Notable examples include the Palais Jacques Cœur in Bourges, the transept façades of Beauvais Cathedral, and the chapel of the Château de Vincennes. Related late Gothic styles flourished elsewhere, such as the Perpendicular in England and the Manueline in Portugal. Flamboyant gradually gave way to Renaissance architecture in the early 16th century.
