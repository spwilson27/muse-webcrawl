The Jōmon period (縄文時代, Jōmon jidai) is the time in Japanese prehistory, traditionally dated between c. 14,000 and 1000 BCE, during which Japan was inhabited by a diverse hunter-gatherer society — the Jōmon people — that attained a high degree of sedentism and cultural complexity. The period is named for the cord-marked (jōmon) decoration on its pottery.

Chronology

Archaeologists divide the period into Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late and Final phases. Jōmon pottery, among the earliest ceramic traditions in the world, appeared as the climate warmed at the end of the last ice age.

Society and subsistence

Jōmon communities lived in pit dwellings gathered in villages such as Sannai-Maruyama in Aomori Prefecture, one of the largest known settlements. They subsisted on fishing, shellfish gathering (leaving great shell middens), hunting deer and boar, and intensive management of nut-bearing trees such as chestnut — a rich, stable subsistence base that supported sedentary life without agriculture for millennia. Trade moved obsidian, jade, salt and lacquer over long distances.

Art and ritual

The period produced elaborate flame-rimmed pottery vessels (kaen-gata doki) and clay figurines called dogū, many depicting stylised female forms, some with "goggle-eyed" (shakōki) designs. Stone circles, such as Ōyu in Akita, suggest ritual landscapes.

Transition

The Jōmon way of life was gradually transformed from around 1000 BCE by the arrival, via the Korean peninsula, of wet-rice agriculture and continental migrants — the beginning of the Yayoi period. Genetic research indicates the modern Japanese population descends from a mixture of Jōmon and Yayoi-era ancestry, while the Ainu of Hokkaido preserve a higher proportion of Jōmon heritage.
