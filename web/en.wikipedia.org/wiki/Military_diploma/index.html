Military diploma

A military diploma was a document issued by the Roman emperor to a soldier of the auxiliary forces (and certain other non-citizen troops) on his honourable discharge after the statutory term of service, usually 25 years. Cast in bronze, the diploma certified the grant of Roman citizenship to the recipient and the legal recognition of his marriage (conubium), and thereby the citizenship of his children.

Form and contents

Diplomas consisted of two bronze tablets hinged together, inscribed on both faces. The text reproduced an imperial constitution issued in Rome, where the master copy was displayed; it named the emperor, the provincial governor, the recipient's unit, commander, and the recipient himself with his origin, spouse and children. The names of seven witnesses were inscribed on the outer face, and the tablets were sealed shut so that the inner text could be verified in case of legal dispute.

Historical significance

More than a thousand diplomas or fragments survive, dating from AD 52, under Claudius, to the mid-3rd century. Because each diploma is precisely dated and lists the auxiliary units stationed in a province at that moment, they are among the most valuable primary sources for the organisation, deployment and recruitment of the Roman army and for the spread of Roman citizenship. The practice declined after the Constitutio Antoniniana of AD 212 granted citizenship to nearly all free men in the empire.
