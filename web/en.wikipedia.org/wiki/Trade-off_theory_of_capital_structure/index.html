Trade-off theory of capital structure

The trade-off theory of capital structure is the idea that a company chooses how much debt finance and how much equity finance to use by balancing the costs and benefits. The theory originated with Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller: their 1958 theorem showed that, in a frictionless market, capital structure is irrelevant to firm value; their 1963 correction incorporated corporate taxes, under which the interest tax shield makes debt appear valuable without limit. The trade-off theory modifies this by adding the costs of financial distress, so that an optimal debt level exists where the marginal tax benefit of debt equals its marginal distress cost.

Benefits of debt

The principal benefit is the tax shield: interest payments are tax-deductible in most jurisdictions, so debt reduces the firm's tax bill, effectively transferring value from the government to investors. Debt may also discipline managers by committing free cash flow to interest payments (the free cash flow hypothesis of Michael Jensen) and can signal management confidence.

Costs of debt

Against these benefits stand the expected costs of financial distress: direct costs of bankruptcy (legal and administrative fees), and usually larger indirect costs — lost customers and suppliers, fire sales of assets, foregone investment (debt overhang), and conflicts between shareholders and creditors (agency costs, such as asset substitution and risk-shifting). The expected cost rises with leverage and is higher for firms with intangible, firm-specific assets that lose value in distress.

Predictions and evidence

The static trade-off theory predicts that profitable firms with tangible assets and low earnings volatility should carry more debt, and that firms adjust toward a target leverage ratio. Empirical evidence is mixed: many profitable firms in practice carry little debt, a pattern more consistent with the rival pecking order theory, in which firms prefer internal funds, then debt, then equity because of information asymmetry. Dynamic versions of the trade-off theory, in which adjustment is costly and leverage drifts within a range, can reconcile several empirical puzzles.
