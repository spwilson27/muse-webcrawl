Ship burial

Ship burial
TypeFunerary practice
CulturesAnglo-Saxons, Vikings, Germanic peoples, Egyptians, others

PeriodAncient Egypt to early Middle Ages (in Europe, c. 6th–10th centuries CE)

A ship burial or boat grave is a burial in which a ship or boat is used either as the container for the dead and their grave goods, or as part of the grave goods themselves. The practice is most famously associated with early medieval Germanic peoples — Anglo-Saxons, Franks and Scandinavians of the Viking Age — but occurs in several cultures, including Pharaonic Egypt, where full-size vessels were interred beside pyramids.

Germanic and Viking burials

In Scandinavia, boat graves date from the late Iron Age; by the Vendel and Viking periods the richest burials used full-size ships set in mounds. The Oseberg ship (buried 834 CE) contained the bodies of two women, a richly carved wagon, sledges, tapestries and household goods; the Gokstad ship (c. 890) held a high-status man with smaller boats, animals and provisions. Elaborate ship funerals are described in literature: the funeral of Scyld Scefing in Beowulf, and the tenth-century Arab traveller Ahmad ibn Fadlan's eyewitness account of a Rus' chieftain's ship cremation on the Volga, in which the ship was burned with the dead man, his goods, and a sacrificed slave girl.

Sutton Hoo

The ship burial at Sutton Hoo in Suffolk, England (excavated 1939), is the richest known early medieval grave from Britain: a 27-metre ship buried in a mound around 625 CE containing a helmet, shield, sword, the famous gold and garnet regalia, silver bowls from Byzantium and coins from Francia. The deceased is widely identified as King Rædwald of East Anglia. No body survived the acidic soil, but the burial is interpreted as that of a king, combining pagan ship ritual with objects bearing Christian imagery.

Egypt

At Giza, the disassembled Khufu ship (c. 2500 BCE), a 43-metre cedar vessel buried in a pit beside the Great Pyramid, was reassembled in the twentieth century; such "solar barges" are thought to have served the king's journey in the afterlife. Earlier boat graves surround royal tombs at Abydos.

Other traditions and symbolism

Boat-shaped coffins and boat graves also occur in Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Interpretations of the rite emphasise the ship as a vehicle to the afterlife, a symbol of the deceased's status and wealth, and a marker of maritime identity. Many boat burials used smaller boats or stone settings in the shape of ships (stone ships), which replicated the form without a real vessel.
