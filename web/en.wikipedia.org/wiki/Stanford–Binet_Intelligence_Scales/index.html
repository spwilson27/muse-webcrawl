The Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales (or more commonly the Stanford–Binet) is an individually administered intelligence test that was revised from the original Binet–Simon Scale by Stanford University psychologist Lewis Terman in 1916. It is one of the oldest and most widely used measures of cognitive ability and a standard instrument for assessing intellectual giftedness and intellectual disability.

History

French psychologists Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon published their scale in 1905 to identify schoolchildren needing educational assistance. Terman's 1916 revision introduced the intelligence quotient as mental age divided by chronological age multiplied by 100, standardized the test on an American sample, and extended its range. Subsequent revisions appeared in 1937, 1960 (introducing deviation IQ scores), 1986 (fourth edition), and 2003 (fifth edition, SB5, edited by Gale Roid).

Fifth edition

The SB5 measures five factors — fluid reasoning, knowledge, quantitative reasoning, visual-spatial processing, and working memory — each in verbal and nonverbal domains, yielding Full Scale, Nonverbal, and Verbal IQ scores with a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15. It is used in educational placement, neuropsychological assessment, and research on cognitive development across the lifespan.
