Overview

A thermometer is a device that measures temperature or a temperature gradient. It has two basic elements: a temperature sensor in which some physical property changes with temperature (the expansion of a liquid, the resistance of a wire, the voltage at a junction, the colour of emitted radiation) and a means of converting that change into a numerical value.

History and types

Galileo's thermoscope (c. 1593) showed temperature changes without a scale; sealed liquid-in-glass thermometers came from Medici Florence, and Daniel Fahrenheit (1714, mercury and his scale), Anders Celsius (1742) and Lord Kelvin (1848, absolute scale) fixed the modern scales. Mercury thermometers, long standard in medicine and meteorology, are being phased out for toxicity (Minamata Convention). Today most measurement is electronic: thermocouples, platinum resistance thermometers, thermistors and infrared pyrometers, while Galileo thermometers survive as ornaments. Clinical, industrial and scientific standards (the ITS-90 scale) anchor calibration.
