Stop codon

In molecular biology, a stop codon (or termination codon) is a codon (a nucleotide triplet within messenger RNA) that signals the termination of the translation process of the current protein. Most codons in messenger RNA correspond to the addition of an amino acid to a growing polypeptide chain, but stop codons instead bind release factors, which cause the ribosomal subunits to disassemble and release the completed amino acid chain.

The three stop codons

In the standard genetic code there are three stop codons:

Codon (RNA)Name
UAAochre
UAGamber
UGAopal (or umber)

The names date from the discovery of amber mutants of bacteriophage T4; "ochre" and "opal" followed the colour theme. In DNA these appear as TAA, TAG, and TGA.

Mechanism and exceptions

Stop codons are recognised not by tRNAs but by release factor proteins — eRF1 in eukaryotes, RF1 and RF2 in bacteria — which trigger hydrolysis of the bond between the finished polypeptide and the final tRNA. Mutations that convert an amino-acid codon into a stop codon are called nonsense mutations and can cause genetic disease; cellular surveillance (nonsense-mediated decay) degrades many such transcripts. Some organisms deviate from the standard code: in many ciliates UAA and UAG encode glutamine, and engineered organisms can reassign stop codons — most famously UAG — to incorporate non-standard amino acids. UGA can also be contextually read as selenocysteine.
