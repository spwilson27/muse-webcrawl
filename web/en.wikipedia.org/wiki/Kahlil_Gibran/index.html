Gibran Khalil Gibran (6 January 1883 – 10 April 1931), usually referred to in English as Kahlil Gibran, was a Lebanese-American writer, poet, and visual artist, also considered a philosopher although he himself rejected the title. He is best known as the author of The Prophet, a book of 26 prose poetry fables published in 1923 that became one of the best-selling and most translated books of all time.

Life

Born in Bsharri in Ottoman-ruled Lebanon to a Maronite family, Gibran emigrated with his mother and siblings to the United States in 1895, settling in Boston. He studied art in Boston and Paris, and became a central figure of the Mahjar literary movement of Arab writers in America, writing in both Arabic and English.

Works and legacy

His English-language books, including The Madman (1918), The Forerunner (1920), Sand and Foam (1926), and posthumously The Wanderer (1932), are written in a biblical, aphoristic style. The Prophet has been translated into more than 100 languages and never gone out of print, and his lines are quoted at weddings and funerals worldwide.
