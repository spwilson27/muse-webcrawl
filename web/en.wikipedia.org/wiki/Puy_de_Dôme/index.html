Puy de Dôme

Elevation1,465 m (4,806 ft)
LocationPuy-de-Dôme, Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, France
RangeChaîne des Puys, Massif Central
TypeLava dome
Last eruptionc. 5760 BC

Puy de Dôme is a large lava dome in central France and one of the most famous volcanoes of the Chaîne des Puys, in the Massif Central. It lies about 10 km (6 miles) west of Clermont-Ferrand, in the department of Puy-de-Dôme, to which it gave its name. The summit, at 1,465 metres (4,806 ft), hosts a television and radio transmitter, a meteorological observatory, and the remains of a Roman temple dedicated to Mercury.

History

In 1648, Florin Périer, at the urging of Blaise Pascal, verified Evangelista Torricelli's theory that barometric readings were influenced by the weight of the air, by measuring the height of a column of mercury at several altitudes on the mountain. In 1875 a physics laboratory was built on the summit, and in 1956 a TDF (Télédiffusion de France) transmitter was erected.

In prehistoric times the mountain was a sacred site; the Romans built a temple of Mercury on the summit, whose ruins were discovered in 1872. According to legend, the hermit Saint Abraham settled there in the 5th century.

Sport

The Puy de Dôme is well known in cycling as a finish of the Tour de France. The road spirals around the cone, and the climb was the scene of the famous 1964 duel between Jacques Anquetil and Raymond Poulidor. After a long absence, the Tour returned in 2023, with the summit finish won by Michael Woods.

Access

Since 2012 the summit has been reached by the Panoramique des Dômes, a rack railway. The Chaîne des Puys, including Puy de Dôme, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2018 as part of the Chaîne des Puys – Limagne fault tectonic arena.
