David Allen Kolb (born 1939) is an American educational theorist and organisational behaviour scholar whose interests focus on experiential learning, the individual and social change, adult development and executive education. He is the developer of experiential learning theory (ELT), one of the most widely cited frameworks in education and management training.

Education and career

Kolb graduated from Knox College in 1961 and earned his MA and PhD in social psychology from Harvard University, where he was influenced by the work of David McClelland. He spent most of his career at the Weatherhead School of Management at Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, Ohio, where he became professor of organisational behaviour.

Experiential learning theory

In Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development (1984), Kolb drew on John Dewey, Kurt Lewin and Jean Piaget to define learning as "the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience". He modelled learning as a four-stage cycle:

  • Concrete experience — having a new experience;
  • Reflective observation — reviewing and reflecting on it;
  • Abstract conceptualisation — forming conclusions and concepts;
  • Active experimentation — testing the concepts in new situations, which generates further experience.

Effective learning, in Kolb's model, involves all four modes, and learners differ in the modes they emphasise. His Learning Style Inventory (LSI) classifies learners as diverging, assimilating, converging or accommodating according to their position on the two dimensions of grasping (experience vs. conceptualisation) and transforming (reflection vs. action) experience.

Reception and criticism

Kolb's cycle and learning-styles typology have been enormously influential in teacher education, medicine, business schools and corporate training, and the Learning Style Inventory has been used in thousands of studies. Critics have questioned the psychometric properties of the LSI and the empirical basis of "learning styles" matching in general, while defenders argue that ELT's core insight — the centrality of experience and reflection — stands independently of the inventory.
