Terbium metal

Terbium is a chemical element with the symbol Tb and atomic number 65. It is a silvery-white, rare earth metal that is malleable, ductile and soft enough to be cut with a knife. Terbium is the ninth member of the lanthanide series and is never found in nature as a free element, occurring instead in minerals such as monazite, xenotime and euxenite.

History

Swedish chemist Carl Gustaf Mosander discovered terbium in 1843 as a component of the mineral yttria from the mine at Ytterby, Sweden — the village that also gave names to yttrium, erbium and ytterbium. Mosander separated yttria into three oxides he called yttria, erbia and terbia; subsequent confusion in naming took decades to resolve, and pure terbium metal was not produced until the advent of ion-exchange separation techniques in the mid-twentieth century.

Properties

Terbium has a single stable isotope, terbium-159. The metal is relatively stable in air but oxidises slowly, and it reacts with water to form the hydroxide. Below its Curie temperature of about −43 °C it is ferromagnetic. Terbium compounds show intense green fluorescence, due to characteristic emission lines of the Tb³⁺ ion.

Applications

Terbium's main use is as the green phosphor in trichromatic fluorescent lamps, television and display screens, where terbium-activated phosphors provide efficient green light. It is added to the magnetostrictive alloy Terfenol-D (terbium–dysprosium–iron), used in sonar transducers and actuators, and small amounts improve the high-temperature performance of neodymium magnets in electric vehicle motors. Terbium-doped glasses and crystals are used in lasers and Faraday rotators for optical isolators.
