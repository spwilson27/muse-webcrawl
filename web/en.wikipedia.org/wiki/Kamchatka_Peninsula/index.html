Kamchatka Peninsula

The Kamchatka Peninsula (Russian: полуостров Камчатка) is a 1,250-kilometre-long peninsula in the Russian Far East, lying between the Pacific Ocean to the east and the Sea of Okhotsk to the west. Constituting most of Kamchatka Krai, it is famous for its volcanoes, geysers and brown bears, and is one of the least densely populated regions of Russia.

Geography

The peninsula's spine is the Sredinny Range, with the Eastern Range closer to the Pacific coast. It contains some 160 volcanoes, 29 of them active, including Klyuchevskaya Sopka, the highest active volcano in Eurasia. The Valley of Geysers, discovered in 1941, is one of the world's largest geyser fields. The Kamchatka River drains the central valley, and the west coast faces the shallow Sea of Okhotsk.

Flora and fauna

Kamchatka supports some of the world's densest brown bear populations — an estimated 10,000–14,000 animals — nourished by enormous salmon runs of all six Pacific species. Other wildlife includes Steller's sea eagles, sea otters, orcas and the snow sheep. Stone birch forests and tundra cover much of the peninsula, and Lake Kuril is a major bear-viewing and salmon-spawning site.

History

Indigenous Itelmen, Koryak and Even peoples inhabited the peninsula before Russian Cossacks under Vladimir Atlasov annexed it in 1697. Petropavlovsk was founded by Vitus Bering in 1740 and was besieged by an Anglo-French squadron during the Crimean War. In Soviet times Kamchatka was a closed military zone; it opened to visitors only in the 1990s. The "Volcanoes of Kamchatka" were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.

Economy

Fishing and fish processing dominate the economy, alongside tourism, mining and the Russian Pacific Fleet's submarine base at Vilyuchinsk. There are no rail or road links to the rest of Russia; access is by air or sea.
