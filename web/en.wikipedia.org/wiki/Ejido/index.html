Ejido

Ejido
CountryMexico
Legal basisArticle 27, Constitution of 1917
TypeCommunal land tenure

An ejido (Spanish: [eˈxiðo]) is an area of communal land in Mexico used for agriculture and other purposes, in which community members (ejidatarios) hold usufruct rights rather than outright ownership, under a system rooted in pre-Hispanic and Spanish colonial traditions and formalized after the Mexican Revolution.

History

The concept derives from lands surrounding villages in Spain and the Aztec calpulli. Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution enabled land reform, redistributing hacienda land to peasant communities, greatly expanded under President Lázaro Cárdenas in the 1930s. By the late 20th century, ejidos covered about half of Mexico's territory.

Structure

Ejidos are governed by an elected commissariat and assembly. Land includes parcels worked individually, communal lands, and settlement areas. Historically ejido land could not be sold, rented, or used as collateral.

1992 reform

Under President Carlos Salinas de Gortari, constitutional changes ended further redistribution and allowed ejidos to vote to certify parcels and, ultimately, adopt full private ownership (dominio pleno), partly in preparation for NAFTA.
