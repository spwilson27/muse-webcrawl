Keratomalacia

Keratomalacia is an eye disorder that results from vitamin A deficiency. It is characterised by progressive softening, clouding and necrosis of the cornea, and if untreated can lead to corneal perforation and permanent blindness. Keratomalacia is regarded as the most severe ocular manifestation of vitamin A deficiency and is a major cause of preventable childhood blindness in the developing world.

Signs and symptoms

The condition typically develops in children with prolonged dietary vitamin A deficiency, often aggravated by measles, diarrhoea or other infections that deplete vitamin A stores. Early signs include night blindness and xerophthalmia (dryness of the conjunctiva and cornea). In keratomalacia proper, the cornea becomes hazy, soft and necrotic; it may bulge or perforate, leading to iris prolapse, endophthalmitis and loss of the eye.

Causes

Vitamin A (retinol) is required for the maintenance of epithelial tissues and for the production of rhodopsin in the retina. Deficiency leads to keratinising metaplasia of the ocular surface and impaired mucus production by goblet cells, leaving the cornea dry and vulnerable to ulceration and secondary bacterial infection.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the characteristic appearance of the cornea in a malnourished child. Serum retinol levels may be measured but are often unavailable in the settings where the disease is common. The World Health Organization classifies keratomalacia within the xerophthalmia staging system as stage X3.

Treatment

Keratomalacia is a medical emergency. Treatment consists of immediate high-dose vitamin A supplementation according to WHO schedules, topical antibiotics to prevent secondary infection, protection of the eye, and treatment of underlying malnutrition and infections. Corneal scarring that has already occurred cannot be reversed by vitamin A, although timely treatment can save the fellow eye and prevent further damage.

Epidemiology

Vitamin A deficiency affects an estimated 190 million preschool-aged children worldwide, principally in sub-Saharan Africa and South and Southeast Asia. Keratomalacia historically accounted for a large share of blindness in children in these regions; supplementation and measles immunisation programmes have reduced its incidence.
