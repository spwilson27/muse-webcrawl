Experimental rock, also called avant-rock, is a subgenre of rock music that pushes the boundaries of common composition and performance technique, or experiments with the basic elements of the genre. Artists aim for a sense of innovativeness, often incorporating unconventional sounds, instruments, song structures, playing techniques and lyrical elements drawn from avant-garde and experimental music.

History

From the mid-1960s, acts such as the Velvet Underground, Frank Zappa and the Mothers of Invention, Captain Beefheart and the Beatles' studio experiments began stretching rock's conventions. In the 1970s the German krautrock scene (Can, Faust, Neu!), Brian Eno, and British groups such as Henry Cow further developed improvisation, tape manipulation and electronics. Later waves included the New York no wave scene, Sonic Youth and Swans, and 1990s post-rock bands such as Slint and Tortoise.

Characteristics

Common features include prepared or custom-built instruments, unusual time signatures, extended techniques, found sounds and field recordings, and structures that abandon verse–chorus form. Experimental rock overlaps with, but is distinct from, progressive rock and art rock: where those genres often emphasise virtuosity and complexity within recognisable frameworks, experimental rock questions the frameworks themselves.
