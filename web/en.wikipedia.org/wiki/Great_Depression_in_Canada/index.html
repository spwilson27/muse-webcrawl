The Great Depression in Canada was an economic crisis of the 1930s that was among the most severe in the country's history. Between 1929 and 1933, Canada's gross national product fell by roughly 40%, exports collapsed, and unemployment reached about 30% of the labour force at its peak in 1933. Because the Canadian economy depended heavily on exports of staples — wheat, lumber, minerals and newsprint — the collapse of world trade hit it exceptionally hard, and some studies rank Canada's downturn as the second-worst in the world after that of the United States.

Causes and course

The crash of 1929 on Wall Street — echoed by the Winnipeg and Toronto exchanges — combined with overproduction in wheat and other staples, a collapsing export market after protectionist measures such as the US Smoot–Hawley tariff, and a severe drought. On the Prairies the "Dirty Thirties" brought dust storms, grasshopper plagues and repeated wheat crop failures, reducing some farm incomes to near zero.

Social impact

With no national unemployment insurance, the unemployed relied on means-tested municipal relief ("the dole"), vouchers and charity. Thousands of single unemployed men roamed the country riding freight trains ("riding the rods"); the federal government placed many in relief camps run by the Department of National Defence, where they worked for 20 cents a day. In 1935 camp workers launched the On-to-Ottawa Trek, halted by the Regina Riot of 1 July 1935.

Political response

R. B. Bennett's Conservative government, elected in 1930, initially relied on tariffs and imperial trade preferences (the 1932 Ottawa Agreements). In 1935, influenced by Roosevelt's New Deal, Bennett proposed reform legislation, but his government fell to Mackenzie King's Liberals in the 1935 election. The Depression reshaped Canadian politics: new parties emerged, including the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF, 1932–33) and Social Credit, which won power in Alberta in 1935. The crisis led directly to lasting reforms — the creation of the Bank of Canada (1935), the Canadian Wheat Board (1935) and, in 1940, national unemployment insurance. Recovery came only with rearmament and the outbreak of the Second World War.
