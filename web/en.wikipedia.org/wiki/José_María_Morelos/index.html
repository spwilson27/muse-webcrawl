José María Teclo Morelos Pérez y Pavón (30 September 1765 – 22 December 1815) was a Mexican Roman Catholic priest, statesman, and military leader who led the second phase of the Mexican War of Independence (1811–1815) after the execution of Miguel Hidalgo. A brilliant strategist and organizer, he built a disciplined insurgent army in the south, convened the Congress of Chilpancingo, and articulated the founding ideals of the Mexican nation in his document Sentimientos de la Nación ("Sentiments of the Nation").

Early life

Morelos was born in Valladolid (renamed Morelia in his honor) to a poor family of mixed Spanish, indigenous, and African descent. He worked as a mule driver before entering the Colegio de San Nicolás, where Hidalgo was rector, and was ordained a priest in 1797. He served humble parishes in Michoacán, including Carácuaro, until Hidalgo's Grito de Dolores launched the independence revolt in September 1810.

Insurgent campaigns

Commissioned by Hidalgo to raise the south, Morelos campaigned along the Pacific coast, capturing the port of Acapulco and much of the southern mountains. After Hidalgo's death in 1811, Morelos became the insurgency's supreme leader, winning celebrated victories at Cuautla — where he withstood a 72-day siege in 1812 before breaking out — and at Oaxaca. He governed liberated territory, abolished slavery and tribute, and promoted equality across caste lines.

Congress of Chilpancingo and the Constitution

In September 1813 Morelos convened the Congress of Chilpancingo, which in November declared North America's independence from Spain. There Morelos presented the Sentimientos de la Nación, demanding popular sovereignty, republican government, abolition of slavery, equality before the law, and the division of powers. The congress proclaimed the Constitution of Apatzingán in October 1814, the first constitution of Mexico — though it never fully took effect as royalist forces under Agustín de Iturbide and Félix María Calleja regained the initiative.

Capture and death

Defeated at Puruarán (1814) and driven into retreat, Morelos was captured at Temalaca in November 1815 while escorting the congress to safety. He was tried by the Inquisition and a military court in Mexico City, degraded from the priesthood, and executed by firing squad at Ecatepec on 22 December 1815. His disciple Vicente Guerrero carried on the struggle until independence was consummated in 1821. Morelos is honored as one of Mexico's greatest national heroes; the state of Morelos and the city of Morelia bear his name, and his image has appeared on Mexican currency.
