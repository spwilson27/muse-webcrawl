Lulworth Cove is a cove near the village of West Lulworth, on the Jurassic Coast in Dorset, southern England. It is a classic textbook example of coastal erosion on a concordant coastline, where bands of rock run parallel to the shore. The cove receives roughly 500,000 visitors a year and is one of the most photographed features of the Dorset coast.

Formation

The cove formed where the sea breached a narrow band of hard Portland limestone that fronts the coast. Behind it lie much softer Wealden clays and sands, which the sea excavated rapidly once through the breach, hollowing out the nearly circular bay; resistant chalk at the back of the cove slowed the widening. Adjacent features illustrate the same geology at different stages: Stair Hole, a small incipient cove just to the west with visibly folded strata (the "Lulworth Crumple"), and nearby Durdle Door, a natural arch in the limestone band. Over the next several thousand years, continued erosion is expected to widen Lulworth Cove substantially.

Tourism and locality

West Lulworth village serves visitors with a heritage centre covering the geology of the Jurassic Coast World Heritage Site. The cove lies within the Lulworth Estate and beside the MoD's Lulworth Ranges, whose access restrictions govern walks eastward toward the fossil forest and the abandoned village of Tyneham.
