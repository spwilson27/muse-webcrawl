Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a long-term condition in which the kidneys progressively lose function over months to years. It is defined by abnormalities of kidney structure or function lasting more than three months — most practically, a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 ml/min/1.73 m² or evidence of kidney damage such as albuminuria (albumin in the urine). Early disease usually has no symptoms and is detected by blood and urine testing; advanced disease, called kidney failure, requires dialysis or transplantation to sustain life.

Staging

KDIGO guidelines classify CKD by cause, GFR category, and albuminuria. GFR categories run from G1 (≥90, with other evidence of damage) through G5 (<15, kidney failure). Albuminuria is graded A1 to A3 by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. Risk of progression and cardiovascular complications rises steeply in later stages.

Causes

The most common causes worldwide are diabetes mellitus (diabetic kidney disease) and hypertension. Other causes include glomerulonephritis, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, autoimmune diseases such as lupus, prolonged obstruction of the urinary tract, recurrent infections, and nephrotoxic drugs such as NSAIDs. In parts of Central America and Sri Lanka, an epidemic of CKD of unknown cause affects agricultural workers, possibly linked to heat stress and environmental exposures.

Signs and complications

As function declines, patients may develop fluid retention and leg swelling, fatigue, nausea, itching, breathlessness, and cognitive slowing. CKD strongly increases cardiovascular risk — most patients die of cardiovascular disease before reaching kidney failure — and causes anaemia, mineral and bone disorders, metabolic acidosis, and high potassium.

Management

Treatment aims to slow progression: tight control of blood pressure and blood sugar, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, and SGLT2 inhibitors, which have been shown to protect the kidneys. Dietary sodium restriction, avoiding nephrotoxins, and treating complications are standard. For kidney failure, options are haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplantation (the preferred treatment), or conservative care. CKD affects roughly one in ten adults globally and its prevalence is rising with ageing and diabetes.
