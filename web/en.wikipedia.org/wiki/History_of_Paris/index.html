History of Paris

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Île de la Cité, the original site of Paris.

The history of Paris begins in the 3rd century BCE with the Parisii, a Celtic tribe that settled on the Île de la Cité. Conquered by Julius Caesar in 52 BCE, the town became Roman Lutetia, then the capital of the Franks under Clovis around 508 CE. Paris grew into medieval Europe's largest city and a centre of theology and learning, endured the Hundred Years' War and the Wars of Religion, and was transformed under Louis XIV, the Revolution, Napoleon, and Baron Haussmann's renovation. In the 20th century it survived two world wars largely intact and remains France's political, economic and cultural capital.

Antiquity and the early Middle Ages

Lutetia spread over the left bank of the Seine with baths, an amphitheatre and a forum; Saint Denis was martyred on Montmartre around 250 CE. After the fall of Rome, Clovis made Paris his capital; Viking sieges in the 9th century, resisted under Count Odo, raised the prestige of the Capetian dynasty, whose kings from 987 made the city their seat.

Medieval and Renaissance Paris

Philippe Auguste (1180–1223) paved streets, built the Louvre fortress and new walls, and chartered the University of Paris, which drew scholars such as Thomas Aquinas and Abelard. Notre-Dame Cathedral was begun in 1163. Charles V built the Bastille; after the Hundred Years' War and Joan of Arc's failed siege of 1429, the city recovered under the Renaissance kings, who embellished the Louvre and the Hôtel de Ville. The St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of Protestants began in Paris in 1572.

Revolution, Haussmann and the Belle Époque

The storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 opened the French Revolution; Paris was the stage of the Republic, the Terror and Napoleon's coronation in 1804. Under Napoleon III, Prefect Haussmann (1853–1870) demolished the medieval core and cut the broad boulevards, parks and sewers of modern Paris. The Franco-Prussian War brought siege and the Paris Commune of 1871. The Belle Époque that followed produced the Eiffel Tower (1889), the Métro (1900) and the great exhibitions.

Twentieth century to today

Paris was declared an open city in 1940 and liberated in August 1944. Post-war growth filled the banlieues; May 1968 began with student protests in the Latin Quarter. Modern landmarks include the Pompidou Centre, the Louvre Pyramid and La Défense. The city hosted the Olympic Games again in 2024.
