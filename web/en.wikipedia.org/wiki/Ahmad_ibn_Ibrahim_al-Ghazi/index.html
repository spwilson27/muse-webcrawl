Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi (c. 1506 – 21 February 1543), nicknamed Ahmad Gurey ("Ahmad the Left-handed") in Somali and Grañ in Ethiopian sources, was the imam and effective ruler of the Adal Sultanate in the Horn of Africa. Between 1529 and 1543 he led a jihad that overran most of the Christian Ethiopian Empire, one of the most dramatic reversals in the region's history, before being defeated and killed with Portuguese assistance to Ethiopia.

Rise to power

Ahmad rose as a religious and military leader amid conflict between Adal's sultans and its imams. After defeating the sultan Abu Bakr ibn Muhammad's forces and consolidating control of Harar, he refused tribute to the Ethiopian emperor and in 1529 invaded Ethiopia, defeating an imperial army at the Battle of Shimbra Kure.

Conquests

Over the following decade Ahmad's armies, strengthened by Ottoman-supplied firearms and Turkish musketeers, conquered Dawaro, Shewa, Amhara, Lasta, and Tigray, and in 1540–41 defeated the emperor Gelawdewos's forces, devastating churches and monasteries. Emperor Lebna Dengel died in hiding in 1540. At the height of the occupation roughly three-quarters of Ethiopia was under Adal control; the chronicle Futuh al-Habasa ("Conquest of Abyssinia") by Arab Faqih records the campaigns.

Defeat and death

A Portuguese expedition under Cristóvão da Gama landed in 1541 with 400 musketeers. Da Gama was captured and executed in 1542, but the surviving Portuguese joined Gelawdewos, and at the Battle of Wayna Daga near Lake Tana on 21 February 1543 Ahmad was shot and killed, after which his army disintegrated. Adal power collapsed and the balance of the Horn was gradually restored, though the war's devastation left Ethiopia vulnerable to the subsequent Oromo migrations.
