Thing (assembly)


A medieval depiction of a Germanic thing

A thing (Old Norse: þing, pronounced [ˈθiŋɡ]; German: Ding; Dutch: ding) was the governing assembly in Germanic and especially Scandinavian societies during the Viking Age and the Middle Ages. The thing was a legal and political assembly where free men gathered to settle disputes, make laws, and decide matters of common concern.

The word survives in the names of several modern parliaments: the Icelandic Althing (Alþingi), the Norwegian Storting, the Danish Folketing, and the Faroese Løgting. It is also the origin of the English word "thing", which originally meant an assembly or lawsuit before it came to mean any object or matter.

History

Things were held at regular intervals at specific outdoor sites, often marked by standing stones or situated near burial mounds. All free men of the district could attend, though in practice speaking and voting were usually restricted by social standing and wealth. The assembly was presided over by a lawspeaker (lögsögumaðr), who recited the law from memory, and in Iceland by chieftains known as goðar.

Things were organized at several levels: local things for a district or herað, regional things, and national assemblies such as the Althing in Iceland, founded in 930 AD at Þingvellir, which is often considered one of the oldest parliamentary institutions in the world.

Functions

  • Legislation — new laws were proposed, debated, and adopted by acclamation.
  • Justice — lawsuits were heard, judgements passed, and outlaws declared.
  • Commerce — markets were frequently held in connection with assemblies.
  • Social ritual — marriages were arranged, alliances confirmed, and news exchanged.

Etymology

The Old Norse, Old English, and Old Frisian terms derive from Proto-Germanic *þingą, meaning "appointed time", hence a meeting at an appointed time. The German word Ding developed the same extended meaning of "object" as English "thing".
