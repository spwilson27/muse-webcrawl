Birdwatching

Birdwatching, or birding, is the observation of birds in their natural habitat as a recreational activity or citizen science. It can be done with the naked eye, through binoculars or a spotting scope, by listening to bird sounds, or by photography. Birdwatchers range from garden observers to "twitchers" who travel long distances to see rare vagrants, and many contribute records to monitoring schemes.

History

Interest in observing living birds, rather than collecting specimens, grew in late Victorian Britain alongside conservation movements and the publication of field guides. In the United States, the Audubon Christmas Bird Count began in 1900. Roger Tory Peterson's 1934 field guide system revolutionised identification, and post-war optics, cheap travel and, later, apps such as eBird and Merlin Bird ID hugely expanded participation.

Practice

Birders keep lists — life lists, year lists, local "patch" lists — and participate in events such as the Big Day and Global Big Day, on which tens of thousands of observers log millions of sightings. Citizen-science data from platforms such as eBird underpin research on migration, population change and climate effects. Birding is also a significant ecotourism industry, and debates continue about the carbon footprint of twitching and disturbance to rare birds.
