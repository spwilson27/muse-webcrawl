Partitions of Poland

The Partitions of Poland were three partitions of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth carried out by the Russian Empire, the Kingdom of Prussia, and the Austrian Habsburg Monarchy in 1772, 1793, and 1795. The partitions progressively stripped the Commonwealth of territory and population until the Polish state vanished from the map of Europe; Poland did not regain independence until 1918, after the First World War.

Background

By the mid-eighteenth century the Commonwealth, weakened by the liberum veto, magnate anarchy, and repeated foreign interference, had become a de facto Russian protectorate. Catherine II of Russia installed Stanisław August Poniatowski on the Polish throne in 1764, while Prussia under Frederick the Great sought to unite Brandenburg with East Prussia by absorbing Polish Royal Prussia, and Austria sought compensation in the south.

The three partitions

The partitions
First (1772)Russia, Prussia, Austria annex borderlands; ~30% of territory, ~35% of population lost
Second (1793)Russia and Prussia only; Commonwealth reduced to ~200,000 km²
Third (1795)Remaining territory divided; Commonwealth abolished

First Partition (1772). Agreed in St. Petersburg and imposed on a coerced Partition Sejm in 1773–75, it cost the Commonwealth its northern, eastern, and southern borderlands: Prussia took Royal Prussia (without Danzig and Thorn), Russia took Polish Livonia and eastern Belarus lands, and Austria took Galicia.

Second Partition (1793). Reformers' Constitution of 3 May 1791 provoked the Targowica Confederation and Russian invasion in the War in Defence of the Constitution. Russia and Prussia (Austria, preoccupied with France, took nothing) annexed vast eastern and western territories, leaving a rump state under Russian domination, ratified by the coerced Grodno Sejm.

Third Partition (1795). The Kościuszko Uprising of 1794 — sparked in part by the previous partition — ended in defeat; the three powers divided the remainder, Stanisław August abdicated, and the Commonwealth ceased to exist.

Aftermath

The partitioning powers made competing arrangements for Polish lands — Congress Poland under Russia, the Grand Duchy of Posen under Prussia, and the Free City of Kraków (later annexed by Austria). Polish national movements mounted repeated uprisings (notably in 1830–31 and 1863–64), and an independent Polish state was restored only in November 1918.
