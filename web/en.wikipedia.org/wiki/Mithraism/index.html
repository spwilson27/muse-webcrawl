Mithraism

Mithraism, also known as the Mithraic mysteries or the Cult of Mithras, was a Roman mystery religion centred on the god Mithras. Although inspired by Iranian worship of the Zoroastrian divinity (yazata) Mithra, the Roman Mithras is linked to a new and distinctive imagery, and the level of continuity between Persian and Greco-Roman practice is debated. The mysteries were popular among the Imperial Roman army from the 1st to the 4th century CE.

History

The cult first appeared in the Roman world in the late 1st century CE and spread rapidly across the empire, from Roman Britain to Mesopotamia, wherever soldiers, merchants and imperial administrators travelled. At its height in the 2nd and 3rd centuries it counted hundreds of temples, called mithraea, many of them underground or cave-like chambers designed to imitate the cave in which Mithras was believed to have slain the bull. The cult declined sharply in the 4th century as Christianity became dominant and imperial policy turned against pagan worship; by about 400 CE the last mithraea had been abandoned or destroyed.

Iconography

The central image of the cult is the tauroctony: Mithras, dressed in Anatolian costume with a Phrygian cap, kneeling on a bull and slaying it with a dagger while looking away toward the god Sol. Around the scene appear a dog, a snake, a scorpion, a raven, and the twin torchbearers Cautes and Cautopates. The imagery is rich in astronomical symbolism, and modern scholars have proposed that the tauroctony encodes a star map, with Mithras representing a constellation such as Perseus.

Ritual and organisation

Mithraism was a graded initiatory cult open only to men. Initiates passed through seven grades — Raven (corax), Bridegroom (nymphus), Soldier (miles), Lion (leo), Persian (Perses), Courier of the Sun (heliodromus), and Father (pater) — each associated with a planet. Congregations were small, typically a few dozen men, and shared ritual meals in the mithraeum, reclining on benches along the walls. No Mithraic scripture survives; knowledge of the cult comes from archaeology, sculpture, and the often hostile accounts of early Christian writers.

Relationship to other religions

Mithraism shared the religious marketplace of the Roman Empire with the cults of Isis, Cybele, and Sol Invictus, and with early Christianity, with which it shared some superficial features such as communal meals and a saviour figure — similarities noted (and attacked) by Church Fathers such as Justin Martyr and Tertullian. Unlike Christianity, however, Mithraism never sought mass conversion and remained a small, exclusive network of cells.
