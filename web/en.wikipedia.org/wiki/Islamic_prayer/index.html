Islamic prayer

Islamic prayer encompasses the various forms of prayer practised by Muslims. The central form is salah (Arabic: صلاة), the ritual prayer performed five times daily, which is one of the Five Pillars of Islam. Other forms include du'a (personal supplication), dhikr (remembrance of God), and special prayers for festivals, funerals and times of need.

Salah: the five daily prayers

Salah is performed at prescribed times determined by the position of the sun: Fajr (dawn), Dhuhr (midday), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (sunset) and Isha (night). Before praying, Muslims perform ritual ablution — wudu for minor impurity or ghusl for major impurity. The call to prayer (adhan) is announced by a muezzin, traditionally from a minaret.

Each prayer consists of a set number of units (rak'ah, plural rak'at): two at Fajr, four at Dhuhr and Asr, three at Maghrib and four at Isha. A rak'ah involves standing and reciting the opening chapter of the Qur'an, Al-Fatiha, followed by other verses; bowing (ruku); standing again; and two prostrations (sujud) with the forehead touching the ground. Prayers conclude seated with the tashahhud and the greeting of peace (taslim) to the right and left. Worshippers face the qibla, the direction of the Kaaba in Mecca, indicated in mosques by the mihrab niche.

Congregational and special prayers

On Fridays, the midday prayer is replaced by the congregational Friday prayer (jumu'ah), which includes a sermon (khutba) and is obligatory for adult Muslim men in Sunni tradition. Other communal prayers include the two Eid prayers at the end of Ramadan and at the Feast of Sacrifice; tarawih, performed at night during Ramadan; janazah, the funeral prayer; istisqa, for rain; and prayers during solar and lunar eclipses. Shia Muslims also observe particular mourning rituals and may combine the midday/afternoon and sunset/night prayers.

Supplication and remembrance

Beyond the ritual prayer, Muslims make personal supplications (du'a) in any language and posture, and practise dhikr — repetitive invocation of God's names or phrases such as subhan Allah ("glory be to God") — sometimes counted on prayer beads (misbaha). Sufi traditions developed elaborate communal dhikr ceremonies.
