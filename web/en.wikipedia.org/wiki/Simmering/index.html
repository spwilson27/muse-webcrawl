Simmering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A pot of soup simmering gently, with occasional small bubbles rising]

Simmering is a food preparation technique in which foods are cooked in hot liquids kept just below the boiling point of water — typically between about 85 °C and 96 °C (185–205 °F), where occasional small bubbles rise lazily to the surface. Simmering is gentler than boiling: agitation is minimal, so delicate foods keep their shape and tough meats become tender without tightening and toughening.

Simmering is the workhorse of the soup and sauce kitchen. Stocks, soups, stews and braises are all simmered; milk-based sauces are simmered to thicken without scorching, and rice and grains are often brought to the boil then finished at a simmer under a lid.

Contents
1. Technique
2. Uses

Technique

A liquid that is simmering shows small bubbles forming at the bottom and sides of the pot, rising now and then, with the surface barely shivering. Cooks adjust heat and use heavy pots or heat diffusers to hold this state; a rolling boil is avoided because it emulsifies fats into stock (making it cloudy), breaks up delicate ingredients and can toughen meat by squeezing out juices.

Uses

Classic simmered preparations include clear consommés (kept at the barest simmer so they stay transparent), poached and simmered sausages, compotes of fruit, curry sauces, and the long simmering of dried beans and lentils. Poaching is a still gentler cousin, carried out a few degrees cooler.
