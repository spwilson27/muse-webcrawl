Haemolysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Image placeholder: Haemolysis

Haemolysis (American English: hemolysis) is the rupture or destruction of red blood cells (erythrocytes), with the release of haemoglobin and other internal components into the surrounding fluid, typically blood plasma. Haemolysis may occur within the body (in vivo) or outside it (in vitro), for example in a blood sample. Small amounts of haemolysis occur normally as aged red blood cells are removed from circulation, principally by the spleen.

Causes in vivo

Excessive haemolysis causes haemolytic anaemia. Causes include inherited red-cell defects such as hereditary spherocytosis and enzyme deficiencies (e.g. G6PD deficiency), haemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia, immune-mediated destruction (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, haemolytic transfusion reactions, haemolytic disease of the newborn), infections such as malaria, certain drugs and toxins, mechanical damage from prosthetic heart valves, and microangiopathic processes. Haemolysis within blood vessels (intravascular) releases free haemoglobin into plasma, whereas extravascular haemolysis occurs mainly in the spleen and liver macrophages.

Clinical features and tests

Haemolysis leads to anaemia, jaundice from increased bilirubin, reticulocytosis, raised lactate dehydrogenase, reduced haptoglobin and, in chronic cases, gallstones and splenomegaly. Laboratory signs in a haemolysed specimen — pink or red plasma — can falsely elevate potassium and other measurements, and haemolysis is one of the most common reasons for rejection of laboratory samples.

In vitro haemolysis

Blood samples may haemolyse because of collection technique, vigorous shaking, extreme temperatures or bacterial contamination. Osmotic haemolysis occurs when red cells are placed in a hypotonic solution, swelling until they burst; this fragility is exploited in the osmotic fragility test.
