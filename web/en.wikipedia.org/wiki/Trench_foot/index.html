Trench foot

Trench foot, also known as immersion foot syndrome, is a medical condition caused by prolonged exposure of the feet to damp, unsanitary, and cold conditions. Unlike frostbite, it occurs without freezing temperatures, typically at temperatures between 0 °C and 16 °C (32–60 °F), when wet feet lose heat and circulation is constricted over many hours or days.

Symptoms

Affected feet may become numb, reddened or blotchy, swollen, and painful; blisters and tissue damage can develop, and in severe untreated cases the condition can progress to gangrene and require amputation. The condition was notorious among soldiers standing for days in waterlogged trenches during the First World War — hence the name — and it also appeared in the Second World War and the Falklands War, and remains a risk for homeless people and outdoor workers.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention centers on keeping feet dry: regular changes of socks, foot inspections, drying and elevating the feet, and avoiding tight boots. Militaries of the First World War eventually reduced cases by rotating troops out of flooded trenches and issuing whale oil and spare socks. Treatment involves gently rewarming and drying the feet and managing pain and any infection; severe cases require hospital care.
