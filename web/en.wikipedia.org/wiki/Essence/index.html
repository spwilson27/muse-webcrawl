Essence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Essence (Latin: essentia) is a property or set of properties that make an entity or substance what it fundamentally is, and which it has by necessity, and without which it loses its identity. Essence is contrasted with accident: a property that the entity or substance has contingently, without which the substance can still retain its identity.

History of the concept

Plato and Aristotle

The notion originates in Greek philosophy. Plato's theory of Forms held that sensible things participate in eternal, unchanging essences or Forms. Aristotle developed the concept systematically: in the Metaphysics he analysed substance (ousia) and identified essence with the "what-it-was-to-be" (to ti ên einai) of a thing — what is captured in its definition. For Aristotle, essence is bound up with form, as opposed to matter, and with the primary sense of substance.

Medieval philosophy

Scholastic philosophers translated Aristotle's term with the Latin coinage essentia, attributed to Roman usage and developed by Boethius and others. Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā) famously distinguished essence (māhiyya) from existence (wujūd) in everything except God, in whom they coincide. Thomas Aquinas adopted a version of this real distinction: in created beings essence and existence are distinct principles, and essence is the potency that existence actualises. Duns Scotus analysed essence in terms of a common nature contracted by haecceitas ("thisness").

Early modern philosophy

Descartes identified the essence of matter with extension and the essence of mind with thought. Locke distinguished nominal essence — the abstract idea by which we sort things — from real essence, the unknown inner constitution from which a thing's qualities flow. Kant relocated essence within the structure of concepts and judgements.

Hegel, existentialism, and critique

Hegel made essence (Wesen) the second major division of his Science of Logic, the sphere in which being reflects into itself and appearance is grounded. In the 20th century, existentialists such as Jean-Paul Sartre inverted the scholastic formula with the slogan "existence precedes essence", denying a pre-given human nature. Husserl's phenomenology, by contrast, made the intuition of essences (Wesensschau) central to its method. Modern analytic philosophy revived essentialism in modal metaphysics through the work of Saul Kripke and others, arguing for necessary properties grounded in the identity of things — for example, that water is essentially H2O.

Religious conceptions

In Christian theology, essence language became central through the doctrine of the Trinity and the Nicene term homoousios ("of the same essence") for the relation of the Son to the Father. In Eastern Orthodox theology, the essence–energies distinction associated with Gregory Palamas holds that God's essence is unknowable and imparticipable while his energies are genuinely God and can be experienced. In Hindu philosophy, related notions appear in the analysis of svabhāva (own-being); Buddhist Madhyamaka philosophy famously denies that anything possesses intrinsic essence (svabhāva) at all.
