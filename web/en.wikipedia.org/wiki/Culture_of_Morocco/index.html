Culture of Morocco

Placeholder image: Culture of Morocco

The culture of Morocco is a blend of Arab, Amazigh (Berber), African, and European influences. Located at the crossroads of Europe and Africa, Morocco has absorbed traditions from successive waves of inhabitants and rulers, from the indigenous Amazigh peoples through Arab dynasties, Andalusi refugees, and French and Spanish colonial periods.

Languages

Moroccan Arabic (Darija) is the most widely spoken vernacular, while Modern Standard Arabic and French are used in government, education, and business. Amazigh languages, including Tamazight, Tachelhit, and Tarifit, are spoken by a large share of the population; Tamazight became an official language under the 2011 constitution.

Religion

Islam is the state religion, and the vast majority of Moroccans are Sunni Muslims of the Maliki school. The king holds the title Commander of the Faithful. Morocco formerly had one of the largest Jewish communities in the Arab world, and Moroccan Jewish heritage remains visible in mellahs (Jewish quarters) of cities such as Fez, Marrakech, and Essaouira.

Cuisine

Moroccan cuisine is celebrated for its use of spices such as cumin, saffron, ginger, and cinnamon, and for dishes including couscous, tagines, pastilla, harira soup, and mechoui (roast lamb). Mint tea, poured from a height into small glasses, is a symbol of hospitality. Meals are often eaten communally, and bread accompanies nearly every dish.

Architecture and crafts

Moroccan architecture features riads (courtyard houses), kasbahs, ornate madrasas, and the intricate tilework known as zellige, along with carved plaster and cedarwood. Historic medinas such as those of Fez and Marrakech are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Traditional crafts include pottery, brass and leatherwork, weaving, and the production of argan oil in the southwest.

Music and festivals

Musical traditions include Andalusi classical music, chaabi, gnawa music (recognized by UNESCO in 2019), and Amazigh ahidus and ahwash performances. Major festivals include the Fes Festival of World Sacred Music and the Gnaoua World Music Festival in Essaouira. Religious and seasonal celebrations include Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, and local moussems (pilgrimage festivals).

Clothing

Traditional garments include the djellaba, a long hooded robe worn by both men and women; the kaftan, worn by women for formal occasions; and babouches, leather slippers. Head coverings vary by region and personal practice.
