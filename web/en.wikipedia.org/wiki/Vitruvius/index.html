Vitruvius

Vitruvius (Marcus Vitruvius Pollio; c. 80–70 BC – after c. 15 BC) was a Roman architect, engineer and author of the treatise De architectura (On Architecture), the only complete work on architecture to survive from classical antiquity. Written for Augustus in the late 1st century BC, its ten books cover building materials, temples, theatres, town planning, water supply, machines and war engines, and they profoundly influenced Renaissance and later architecture.

Life

Little is known of Vitruvius's life. He served as a military engineer under Julius Caesar and later under Augustus, designing artillery and supervising works, and he claims to have built a basilica at Fanum Fortunae (Fano). In old age he wrote De architectura, dedicating it to Augustus.

The Vitruvian triad

Vitruvius held that architecture must satisfy three principles: firmitas (strength/durability), utilitas (commodity/function) and venustas (beauty/delight) — the "Vitruvian triad" still quoted in architectural theory. He derived proportion from the human body, describing a man with outstretched limbs inscribed in both a circle and a square — the figure Leonardo da Vinci later drew as the Vitruvian Man.

Influence

The treatise survived in manuscript through the Middle Ages and was famously rediscovered by Poggio Bracciolini in 1414. Printed in the 1480s and translated with illustrations by Cesare Cesariano (1521) and others, it shaped Alberti, Palladio and the entire classical tradition of Western architecture.
