Intelligence

Intelligence has been defined in many ways: the capacity for abstraction, logic, understanding, self-awareness, learning, emotional knowledge, reasoning, planning, creativity, critical thinking, and problem-solving. More generally, it can be described as the ability to perceive or infer information, and to retain it as knowledge to be applied towards adaptive behaviors within an environment or context.

Intelligence is most often studied in humans but has also been observed in animals and plants.

Definitions

The definition of intelligence is controversial, varying in what its abilities are and whether or not it is quantifiable. Some groups of psychologists have suggested the following definitions:

  • "The capacity to learn or to profit by experience" — Dearborn (1921)
  • "The ability to adapt adequately to relatively new situations in life" — Pinter (1921)
  • "The aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment" — Wechsler (1944)

A more recent definition, given by a scientific board with 52 signatures ("Mainstream Science on Intelligence", 1994), describes intelligence as "a very general mental capability that, among other things, involves the ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, comprehend complex ideas, learn quickly and learn from experience."

History

The word intelligence derives from the Latin verb intellegere, to comprehend or perceive. In the Middle Ages, the word intellectus became the scholarly technical term for understanding. Francis Galton was influenced by Charles Darwin and was the first to attempt to create a standardized test for assessing intelligence. Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon created the first practical intelligence test in 1905, later revised as the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales.

Theories

General intelligence (g factor)

Charles Spearman proposed in 1904 that performances on different cognitive tests are positively correlated, suggesting a single general factor, g, alongside specific abilities. This finding, known as the positive manifold, is one of the most replicated findings in psychology.

Multiple intelligences

Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences (1983) proposes that intelligence is not a single general ability but comprises distinct modalities, including linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic intelligences.

Triarchic theory

Robert Sternberg's triarchic theory distinguishes analytical, creative, and practical intelligence. The Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory stratifies abilities into a hierarchy with fluid and crystallized intelligence below general intelligence.

Measurement

Intelligence quotient (IQ) tests standardize scores so that the population mean is 100 with a standard deviation of 15. Well-known tests include the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the Raven's Progressive Matrices. IQ scores predict educational attainment, job performance, and various health and social outcomes, though the extent and interpretation of these correlations remain debated.

Intelligence in other species

Animal cognition research examines problem-solving, tool use, self-recognition, and communication in species ranging from corvids and parrots to chimpanzees, dolphins, elephants, and cephalopods. Plant intelligence and collective intelligence in social insects are also studied, though with more controversy.

Artificial intelligence

The field of artificial intelligence seeks to create machines that can perform tasks requiring human-like intelligence. The Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, operationalized machine intelligence as indistinguishable conversational behavior.
