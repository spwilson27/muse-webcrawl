McArdle disease

McArdle disease
Synonyms: Glycogen storage disease type V, GSD V, myophosphorylase deficiency
Specialty: Medical genetics, neurology
Inheritance: Autosomal recessive
Gene: PYGM

McArdle disease is a metabolic disorder, one of the metabolic myopathies, more specifically a muscle glycogen storage disease, caused by a deficiency of the enzyme muscle glycogen phosphorylase (myophosphorylase). It is also known as glycogen storage disease type V (GSD V). The disease was reported in 1951 by Dr Brian McArdle of Guy's Hospital, London.

Signs and symptoms

The onset of McArdle disease is usually noticed in childhood, but the disease often goes undiagnosed until adulthood. Symptoms include exercise intolerance with muscle pain, early fatigue, muscle stiffness, and cramps that are relieved by rest. Patients frequently describe a "second wind" phenomenon: after roughly eight to ten minutes of gentle continued exercise, symptoms ease because the muscles begin to draw on blood-borne glucose and fatty acids instead of their own glycogen stores.

Severe exertion can provoke muscle contractures and rhabdomyolysis, the breakdown of muscle tissue releasing myoglobin into the blood; myoglobinuria may produce dark, cola-coloured urine and, in serious cases, acute kidney injury.

Cause

McArdle disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and is caused by mutations in the PYGM gene on chromosome 11, which encodes myophosphorylase, the muscle isoform of glycogen phosphorylase. Without this enzyme, skeletal muscle cannot break down its glycogen to glucose-1-phosphate, depriving contracting muscle of its principal rapid energy reserve. The liver and brain isoforms of the enzyme are normal, so blood glucose regulation is unaffected.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is suggested by the history of exercise intolerance and second wind, a raised resting creatine kinase level, and an abnormal exercise test showing a flat venous lactate response with an exaggerated rise in ammonia. Genetic testing for PYGM mutations is now the definitive method; muscle biopsy, once standard, shows absent myophosphorylase staining and subsarcolemmal accumulations of glycogen.

Treatment

There is no cure. Management centres on regular moderate aerobic exercise to improve oxidative capacity and the delivery of blood-borne fuels, avoidance of intense isometric and maximal exercise, and dietary measures; a diet rich in complex carbohydrates, and sucrose taken shortly before exercise, can improve exercise tolerance. Vitamin B6 supplementation has been reported to help some patients because pyridoxine is a cofactor for the residual enzyme.
