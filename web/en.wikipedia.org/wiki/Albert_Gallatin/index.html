Abraham Alfonse Albert Gallatin (29 January 1761 – 12 August 1849) was a Genevan-American politician, diplomat and ethnologist who served as the fourth and longest-serving United States Secretary of the Treasury, holding the office from 1801 to 1814 under Presidents Thomas Jefferson and James Madison.

Early life

Born into a wealthy Genevan family, Gallatin emigrated to the United States in 1780, settled in western Pennsylvania, and entered politics as an opponent of Federalist financial policies. He served in the U.S. House of Representatives, where he was a founder of the House Ways and Means Committee and a leading critic of Alexander Hamilton's fiscal system.

Secretary of the Treasury

As Treasury Secretary, Gallatin slashed the national debt even while financing the Louisiana Purchase, and championed internal improvements such as the National Road. The financial strain of the War of 1812 ended his tenure; in 1814 he was a key American negotiator of the Treaty of Ghent, which ended the war.

Later life

Gallatin afterwards served as minister to France and to Britain. In New York he helped found New York University in 1831 and devoted his later years to the ethnology of Native American peoples, earning recognition as a founder of American ethnology. He died in 1849, the last surviving member of Jefferson's cabinet.
