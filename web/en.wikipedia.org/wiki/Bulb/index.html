Bulb

In botany, a bulb is a short-stemmed underground storage organ consisting of a basal plate (a compressed, disc-like stem) from which roots grow, and layers of fleshy modified leaves (scales) that store food reserves. Bulbs allow the plant to survive an unfavourable season (such as winter cold or summer drought) in dormancy and to regrow rapidly when conditions improve. Plants that form true bulbs are a subset of geophytes.

Structure

A true bulb contains a miniature flower or shoot in an embryonic state, surrounded by fleshy scales. In tunicate bulbs (e.g. onion, tulip, daffodil, hyacinth), the outer scales are dry and papery, forming a protective tunic. In imbricate (scaly) bulbs (e.g. lily), the scales are loose and separate, without a common tunic. New bulbs (offsets or bulblets) form in the axils of the scales, allowing vegetative propagation.

Distinction from other storage organs

Bulbs are often confused with other underground storage organs: corms (solid stem tissue, e.g. crocus, gladiolus), tubers (swollen stems or roots, e.g. potato, dahlia), and rhizomes (horizontal underground stems, e.g. iris, ginger). The key diagnostic feature of a true bulb is its structure of layered fleshy leaf scales on a basal plate.

Cultivation and uses

Bulbs are of great horticultural importance: tulips, daffodils, lilies, hyacinths and crocuses are grown worldwide as ornamentals, supporting a major industry centred in the Netherlands. Edible bulbs include onions, garlic, shallots and leeks. Many bulbous plants require a period of chilling (vernalisation) before they will flower, which gardeners simulate by refrigerating bulbs before planting in warm climates.
