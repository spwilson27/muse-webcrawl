Fighting words

Fighting words are written or spoken words intended to incite hatred or violence, addressed directly to an ordinary person and likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction. In United States constitutional law, the "fighting words doctrine" is an exception to the First Amendment's protection of free speech.

Doctrine

The doctrine was established in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire (1942), in which the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the conviction of a man who had called a city marshal a "damned Fascist" and a "racketeer", defining fighting words as those "which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace". Later decisions greatly narrowed the doctrine: in Terminiello v. City of Chicago (1949), Cohen v. California (1971) and R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul (1992), the Court protected offensive speech and held that regulations may not discriminate based on the content of the fighting words. In practice, the Supreme Court has not upheld a fighting-words conviction since Chaplinsky itself.
