Helsinki Cathedral (Finnish: Helsingin tuomiokirkko, Suurkirkko; Swedish: Helsingfors domkyrka, Storkyrkan) is the Finnish Evangelical Lutheran cathedral of the Diocese of Helsinki, located in the neighbourhood of Kruununhaka at Senate Square, Helsinki, Finland. The church was originally built from 1830 to 1852 as a tribute to the Grand Duke of Finland, Tsar Nicholas I of Russia. It was also known as St Nicholas's Church (Finnish: Nikolainkirkko; Swedish: Nikolajkyrkan) until the independence of Finland in 1917. It is a major landmark of the city, with its tall central dome surrounded by four smaller domes, all painted green, and its white neoclassical façade.

History

The cathedral was designed by the German-born architect Carl Ludvig Engel, who was responsible for much of the monumental centre of Helsinki around Senate Square. Engel's design, in the neoclassical (Empire) style, formed a Greek cross plan with a high central dome. Construction began in 1830. Engel died in 1840 before the building was complete, and his successor, Ernst Bernhard Lohrmann, finished the work, adding the four smaller corner domes — which echo St Isaac's Cathedral in St Petersburg — and the zinc statues of the Twelve Apostles on the roof, cast from designs by the German sculptors August Wredow and Hermann Schievelbein and installed in 1849.

The church was consecrated in 1852. The crypt, completed in the 19th century, was converted in the 1980s into a space for exhibitions and concerts. The cathedral's earlier neighbour on the square, the smaller Ulrika Eleonora Church, had been demolished to make way for the monumental square.

Architecture

The cathedral is built in the form of a Greek cross — a square central area with four equal arms — and is entirely symmetrical. Its most distinctive features are the green domes: a tall central dome on a high drum, surrounded by four smaller domes at the corners. The building is painted brilliant white, which makes it stand out above the harbour and the surrounding cityscape. Above the porticoes stand the zinc statues of the Twelve Apostles, among the largest uniform sets of zinc sculpture in the world.

The interior is comparatively austere, in keeping with Lutheran tradition, seating about 1,300 people. The altarpiece, depicting the burial of Christ, was painted by Carl Timoleon von Neff and donated by Tsar Nicholas I. The three large chandeliers were also imperial gifts. The organ, built by the Danish firm Marcussen & Søn, dates from 1967.

Today

Helsinki Cathedral is one of the most popular tourist sights in Finland, receiving hundreds of thousands of visitors annually. It is used for regular worship, major state and university ceremonies, concerts, and exhibitions in its crypt. The cathedral is an emblem of Helsinki and appears widely in images of the city, dominating the skyline when viewed from the South Harbour.
