Landscape archaeology, also sometimes called total archaeology, is the study of the ways in which people in the past constructed and used the environment around them. Rather than focusing on a single excavation site, it treats the whole landscape — settlements, fields, roads, monuments, and the spaces between them — as the archaeological record. It is closely related to, but broader than, settlement archaeology and off-site archaeology.

History

The approach has roots in the work of antiquarian fieldworkers and in scholars such as O. G. S. Crawford, a pioneer of aerial archaeology in Britain, and W. G. Hoskins, whose The Making of the English Landscape (1955) popularised reading landscape features as history. From the 1970s, processual archaeology promoted systematic regional field survey (for example in the Mediterranean and the American Southwest), while post-processual approaches added phenomenological studies of how landscapes were experienced and imbued with meaning, influenced by Christopher Tilley's A Phenomenology of Landscape (1994).

Methods

Landscape archaeologists use fieldwalking and shovel-test surveys to map artefact scatters, aerial photography and satellite imagery to detect cropmarks and earthworks, airborne laser scanning (LiDAR) to reveal features under vegetation — famously used to map the Maya landscape at Caracol and the city of Angkor's suburbs — geophysical survey, and geographic information systems (GIS) for spatial analysis such as viewshed and least-cost path studies. Documentary, place-name, and oral evidence are commonly combined with the physical evidence.
