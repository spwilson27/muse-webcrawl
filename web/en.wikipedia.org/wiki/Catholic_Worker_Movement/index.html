Catholic Worker Movement

The Catholic Worker Movement is a collection of autonomous Christian communities founded in 1933 in New York City by Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin. Rooted in Catholic social teaching, personalism, pacifism and a commitment to voluntary poverty, the movement is best known for its houses of hospitality, which provide food, clothing and shelter to the poor and homeless.

History

The movement began with the newspaper The Catholic Worker, first published on 1 May 1933 and sold for a penny a copy — a price it famously still maintained for decades. From the newspaper grew houses of hospitality in New York and farming communes intended to embody Maurin's vision of a society in which it is "easier for people to be good": centred on the works of mercy, decentralism, and cultivation ("cult, culture and cultivation").

Principles and activity

Catholic Worker communities practise nonviolence and have been prominent in resistance to war, conscription, and nuclear weapons, as well as in advocacy on labour and racial justice. Each community is autonomous; there is no central headquarters or formal membership. By the twenty-first century more than two hundred communities existed in the United States and abroad, and Dorothy Day's cause for canonisation has been opened by the Catholic Church.
