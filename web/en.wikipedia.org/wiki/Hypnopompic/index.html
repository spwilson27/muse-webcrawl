Hypnopompic

The hypnopompic state (also hypnopompia) is the state of consciousness leading out of sleep, a term coined by the psychical researcher Frederic Myers. Its mirror is the hypnagogic state at sleep onset. In the hypnopompic state, the sleeper is transitioning from sleep to wakefulness and may experience vivid sensory phenomena, confusion, and lingering dream imagery.

Phenomena

Hypnopompic hallucinations are vivid visual, auditory, tactile, or kinetic experiences that occur upon awakening. Common forms include seeing geometric patterns, figures or animals in the room, hearing one's name called, or a sensation of floating or falling. They are reported by roughly 6–13% of the general population at some point. Hypnopompic hallucinations may accompany sleep paralysis, in which the muscle atonia of REM sleep persists briefly into wakefulness and the dreamer perceives an intruder or an oppressive presence in the room.

Clinical significance

Isolated hypnopompic phenomena are normal and are classified among the parasomnias. Frequent occurrences are associated with narcolepsy — where they form part of the classic tetrad together with excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, and sleep paralysis — as well as with sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedules, stress, and certain medications. Unlike the hallucinations of psychosis, hypnopompic hallucinations occur exclusively at the sleep–wake boundary and are usually recognized as unreal once full wakefulness is reached.

Etymology

The word derives from the Greek hypnos (sleep) and pompē (a sending forth, escort) — literally, being "sent forth" from sleep. Myers introduced the term in the late 19th century in the proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research.
