Binoculars

Porro prism binoculars, 8×42.

Binoculars or field glasses are two refracting telescopes mounted side-by-side and aligned to point in the same direction, allowing the viewer to use both eyes (binocular vision) when viewing distant objects. Most binoculars are sized to be held using both hands, although sizes vary widely from opera glasses to large mounted pedestal models.

Optical design

Unlike a (monocular) telescope, binoculars give users a three-dimensional image: each eyepiece presents a slightly different image to each eye, producing a perception of depth. Because they can be manufactured with correct (non-inverted) orientation, prisms are incorporated into the optical path. The two main prism designs are the Porro prism, in which the objective lenses are offset from the eyepieces giving a wide, three-dimensional view, and the more compact roof prism, in which the optical path folds in line.

Specifications

Binoculars are described by two numbers, such as 8×42: the first is the magnification, the second the diameter of the objective lenses in millimetres. These determine brightness and exit pupil (here 42 ÷ 8 = 5.25 mm). Common general-purpose sizes are 8×42 and 10×50; astronomers favour larger apertures, while compact models (8×25) suit travel. Anti-reflective lens coatings significantly improve light transmission.

History and uses

Binocular telescopes were being made within decades of the telescope's invention in the early 17th century, but practical handheld binoculars followed the introduction of Porro prisms in the 1850s and the manufacturing improvements of Carl Zeiss in the 1890s. Binoculars are used for birdwatching, hunting, astronomy, theatre, surveying and military observation.
