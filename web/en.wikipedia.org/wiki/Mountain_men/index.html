Mountain men

The mountain men were American explorers, trappers and traders who roamed the Rocky Mountains and the western United States from about 1810 to the 1880s, at their peak between 1820 and 1840. Working for fur companies or as free trappers, they hunted beaver for the felt-hat trade, mapped little-known country, and guided later emigrant parties westward.

The fur trade

Organisations such as William Ashley's Rocky Mountain Fur Company pioneered the rendezvous system, in which trappers and traders met each summer at an agreed valley to exchange furs for supplies. Famous mountain men include Jedediah Smith, Jim Bridger, Kit Carson, Hugh Glass, John Colter and Liver-Eating Johnson. The era declined after 1840 as silk replaced beaver felt in fashion and beaver populations were trapped out.

Life and legacy

Mountain men lived harsh, solitary lives amid grizzly bears, harsh winters and conflict with Native American nations, from whom many also learned survival skills and with whom some intermarried and traded. Their routes — over South Pass and along the Snake and Green Rivers — became the paths of the Oregon and California Trails, and their exploits fed a lasting frontier mythology in American literature and film.
