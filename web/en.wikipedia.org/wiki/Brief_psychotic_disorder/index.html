Brief psychotic disorder

Brief psychotic disorder is a mental disorder characterised by the sudden onset of psychotic symptoms — such as delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech or grossly disorganised behaviour — lasting at least one day but less than one month, followed by an eventual full return to the previous level of functioning. It is classified in the DSM-5 among the schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders.

Presentation

Symptoms appear suddenly, often in response to a marked stressor (when it may be termed brief reactive psychosis), or following childbirth (postpartum onset). The person may experience emotional turmoil, confusion and rapidly changing intense affect. By definition, the disturbance is not better explained by a mood disorder, schizophrenia, or the effects of a substance or medical condition.

Course and prognosis

The prognosis is generally good: most individuals recover fully within days to weeks, and recurrence is less common than in schizophrenia. Treatment typically involves short-term antipsychotic medication, supportive psychotherapy and management of any precipitating stressor. Estimates of frequency vary widely, and the condition is reported more often in low-income settings and in some cultural contexts, including the related French concept of bouffée délirante.

Classification

The ICD-10 and ICD-11 use the broader category of acute and transient psychotic disorders, which covers several similar conditions with acute onset and short duration.
