Attribution bias

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, an attribution bias (or attributional bias) is a cognitive bias that refers to the systematic errors made when people evaluate or try to find reasons for their own and others' behaviours. People constantly make attributions — judgements about the causes of events and behaviours — but these attributions are often distorted by systematic biases, especially when the judgements have motivational or emotional significance for the attributor.

Attributions weigh internal (dispositional) and external (situational) causes.

Origins

Attribution theory grew out of Fritz Heider's work in the 1940s and 1950s, especially his book The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958), which proposed that people act as "naïve scientists" inferring causes from behaviour. Later researchers, including Edward Jones, Keith Davis, Harold Kelley and Bernard Weiner, formalised the theory, and Lee Ross coined the term fundamental attribution error in 1977.

Common attribution biases

  • Fundamental attribution error (correspondence bias): the tendency to over-emphasise dispositional explanations for others' behaviour while under-emphasising situational ones.
  • Actor–observer asymmetry: people explain their own behaviour situationally but others' behaviour dispositionally.
  • Self-serving bias: attributing successes to one's own ability and failures to external factors.
  • Hostile attribution bias: interpreting ambiguous behaviour of others as hostile.
  • Defensive attribution hypothesis: attributing blame in ways that protect oneself from anxiety about being a victim or a perpetrator.
  • Just-world hypothesis: the belief that people get what they deserve, leading to victim-blaming.
  • Ultimate attribution error: attributing negative out-group behaviour dispositionally and positive out-group behaviour situationally.

Explanations

Proposed causes include cognitive heuristics (such as the availability heuristic), perceptual salience — the actor is figural against the situational ground — and motivational factors such as self-esteem maintenance and the need for control. Cultural differences have been found: individualist cultures tend to show stronger dispositional biases than collectivist cultures.
