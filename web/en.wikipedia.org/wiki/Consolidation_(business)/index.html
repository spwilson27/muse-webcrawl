Consolidation (business)

Consolidation in business is a form of corporate combination in which two or more companies merge into an entirely new legal entity, with all of the combining companies dissolving and transferring their assets, liabilities, and operations to the newly formed corporation. It is distinguished from a merger in the strict legal sense, in which one company survives and absorbs the others, and from an acquisition, in which one company purchases control of another that may continue to exist as a subsidiary. In everyday business language, however, "merger" and "consolidation" are often used interchangeably, including for combinations of near-equals.

Accounting usage

The term also refers to financial consolidation: the preparation of consolidated financial statements in which a parent company combines its accounts with those of its subsidiaries as if the group were a single economic entity, eliminating intercompany transactions and balances. Consolidation in this sense is required by accounting standards such as IFRS 10 and U.S. GAAP when one entity controls another, and the resulting group accounts additionally disclose non-controlling (minority) interests.

Motives and effects

Companies consolidate to achieve economies of scale, eliminate duplicated costs, combine complementary products or markets, increase market power, or rescue failing firms. Industry consolidation — a rising share of a market held by fewer, larger firms — is a recognized phase of industry life cycles and is scrutinized by competition authorities under antitrust law (in the United States, the Sherman and Clayton Acts; in the European Union, the Merger Regulation), which can block or condition combinations that substantially lessen competition.
