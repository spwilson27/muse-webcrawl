Engine control unit

An engine control unit (ECU), also called an engine control module (ECM), is an electronic control unit that manages a series of actuators on an internal combustion engine to ensure optimal engine performance. It reads values from sensors in the engine bay and interprets them using multidimensional look-up tables (fuel and ignition maps), adjusting fuel injection, ignition timing, idle speed, and variable valve timing in real time.

Functions

Key inputs include the throttle position, manifold air pressure or mass airflow, crankshaft and camshaft position, coolant temperature, and the exhaust oxygen (lambda) sensor, which allows closed-loop control of the air–fuel ratio for the catalytic converter. The ECU controls injector pulse width and spark advance, and also coordinates turbocharger boost, exhaust gas recirculation, evaporative emissions systems, and cooling fans.

History

Early electronic systems in the 1970s controlled single functions; the advent of affordable microprocessors around 1980 — driven by emissions and fuel-economy regulations such as the US Clean Air Act amendments — made integrated digital engine management standard. General Motors and Bosch's Motronic systems were early examples. Modern vehicles may contain dozens of networked ECUs, but the engine unit remains distinct from transmission, body, and chassis controllers.

Diagnostics and tuning

Since the 1990s, ECUs implement standardised on-board diagnostics (OBD-II in the US, EOBD in Europe), storing fault codes readable through a diagnostic port. Enthusiasts and workshops "remap" ECUs by altering stored calibration data to change performance characteristics, subject to emissions rules; manufacturers increasingly lock or encrypt calibrations.
