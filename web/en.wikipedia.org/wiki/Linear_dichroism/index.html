Linear dichroism

TechniqueSpectroscopy
MeasuresA∥ − A⊥ (difference in absorbance of parallel vs perpendicular polarised light)

Linear dichroism (LD) is the difference in absorption of light polarised parallel and perpendicular to an orientation direction, measured for a sample whose molecules are (at least partially) aligned. It is the linear counterpart of circular dichroism, and is used to study the orientation and conformation of molecules in oriented systems.

Principle

An electronic transition absorbs light most strongly when the electric vector of the light is aligned with the transition dipole moment. If the molecules in a sample are oriented — for instance DNA fibres, membrane proteins in lipid bilayers, polymer films, or filamentous assemblies stretched in flow — the absorbance for the two polarisations differs, and the LD spectrum reports both which transitions are present and at what angle their dipoles sit relative to the orientation axis.

Applications

Flow LD, in which long molecules such as DNA are aligned in a Couette flow cell, is used to study ligand binding geometry: intercalators, groove binders and proteins produce characteristic signals revealing how they sit on the DNA helix. LD is also applied to membrane peptides and proteins, amyloid and other fibres, carbon nanotubes, and liquid crystals. Because LD requires orientation, it complements circular dichroism, which works in isotropic solution but gives no orientational information.
