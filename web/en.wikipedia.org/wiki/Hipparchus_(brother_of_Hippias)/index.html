Hipparchus (Greek: Ἵππαρχος, Hipparkhos; died 514 BC) was the younger son of the Athenian tyrant Peisistratos and the brother of Hippias, who succeeded their father as ruler of Athens. Hipparchus shared in the family's cultural patronage but was not, according to Thucydides, the senior ruler — a point Thucydides stresses against the popular Athenian tradition.

Life and assassination

The Peisistratids patronised poets such as Anacreon and Simonides, and Hipparchus is associated with the recitation of the Homeric poems at the Panathenaea and with setting up inscribed herms throughout Attica. In 514 BC Hipparchus was assassinated by the lovers Harmodius and Aristogeiton during the Panathenaic procession. Ancient sources attribute the plot to a personal grievance: Hipparchus had made advances to Harmodius and, when rebuffed, publicly insulted Harmodius's sister by barring her from carrying a basket in the procession.

After the murder, Hippias's rule became harsher; he was expelled in 510 BC. In democratic Athens, Harmodius and Aristogeiton were celebrated as the "Tyrannicides," with bronze statues in the Agora and a famous drinking song, and were popularly (though inaccurately, as Thucydides insisted) credited with liberating Athens.
