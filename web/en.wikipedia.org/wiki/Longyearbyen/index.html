Longyearbyen (Norwegian pronunciation: [ˈlɔ̀ŋjɛrˌbyːən], "The Longyear Town") is the world's northernmost settlement with a population greater than 1,000, and the largest inhabited area of Svalbard, Norway. It stretches along the foot of the left bank of the Longyear Valley and along the shore of Adventfjorden. Longyearbyen is the administrative centre of Svalbard. In 2018 the town had a population of 2,368, with most of its residents being Norwegian and some being Russian and Ukrainian.

The town was founded in 1906 by the American mining engineer John Munro Longyear, whose Arctic Coal Company established the first major coal mine there. The settlement was destroyed by German warships in 1943 during the Second World War, and rebuilt after the war.

Geography and climate

Longyearbyen sits at 78 degrees north, only about 1,316 km (818 mi) from the North Pole. The sun does not set between mid-April and mid-August (midnight sun), and does not rise between late October and mid-February (polar night). Despite its extreme latitude, the climate is moderated by the North Atlantic Current, making Svalbard much warmer than other locations at the same latitude.

Economy and life

Coal mining was historically the main industry, operated by the state-owned Store Norske Spitsbergen Kulkompani; production has declined sharply in recent decades. Tourism, research and education have grown in importance: the town hosts the University Centre in Svalbard (UNIS), the Svalbard Museum, and the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in the nearby mountainside. Residents must take precautions against polar bears, and it is customary to carry a rifle when travelling outside the settlement.
