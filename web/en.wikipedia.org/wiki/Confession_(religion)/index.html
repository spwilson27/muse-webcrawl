Confession (religion)

Confession, in religion, is the acknowledgment of sin or wrongdoing, made to God, to a priest or minister, or to the community, as a step toward repentance and forgiveness. The term also denotes a formal statement of faith, as in the historic confessions of faith of Protestant churches, and by extension a religious denomination itself.

A confessional, where penitents confess their sins to a priest in the sacrament of penance.

In Christianity

In Roman Catholicism, confession is a central part of the sacrament of penance (also called reconciliation): the penitent confesses sins to a priest, expresses contrition, receives absolution and performs an assigned penance; the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required annual confession. The priest is bound by the absolute seal of confession. Eastern Orthodoxy likewise practises confession before a priest, usually standing before an icon. Lutheran churches retain private confession and absolution, while most other Protestant traditions practise confession corporately in worship or privately to God, rejecting obligatory auricular confession. Anglican practice summarises the position as "all may, some should, none must".

In other religions

Judaism emphasises vidui, the confession of sins, especially on Yom Kippur and in the daily liturgy, directed to God rather than to a priestly intermediary. In Islam, repentance (tawba) and seeking forgiveness (istighfar) are made directly to God. Buddhist monastic codes include regular confession of offences before the community at the uposatha observance.
