Absolute continuity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In calculus and real analysis, absolute continuity is a smoothness property of functions that is stronger than continuity and uniform continuity. The notion of absolute continuity allows one to obtain generalizations of the relationship between the two central operations of calculus — differentiation and integration. This relationship is commonly characterized in the framework of Riemann integration, but with absolute continuity it may be formulated in terms of Lebesgue integration.

Absolute continuity of functions

A function f on a compact interval [a, b] is absolutely continuous on [a, b] if for every positive number ε there is a positive number δ such that whenever a finite sequence of pairwise disjoint sub-intervals (xk, yk) of [a, b] satisfies the total length condition Σ|yk − xk| < δ, then Σ|f(yk) − f(xk)| < ε.

The collection of all absolutely continuous functions on [a, b] is denoted AC([a, b]). Every Lipschitz continuous function is absolutely continuous, and every absolutely continuous function is continuous and of bounded variation. The converse implications do not hold: the Cantor function is continuous and of bounded variation but not absolutely continuous, and the function f(x) = x2 sin(1/x2) (with f(0) = 0) is differentiable everywhere but not absolutely continuous on [0, 1].

Relation to the fundamental theorem of calculus

A Lebesgue integrable function f gives rise to an absolutely continuous indefinite integral. Conversely, if f is absolutely continuous on [a, b], then f is differentiable almost everywhere, its derivative f′ is Lebesgue integrable, and f(x) = f(a) + ∫ax f′(t) dt. Thus absolute continuity is exactly the property needed for the second part of the fundamental theorem of calculus to hold in the Lebesgue setting.

Absolute continuity of measures

Relatedly, a measure μ on a measurable space is said to be absolutely continuous with respect to a measure ν, written μ ≪ ν, if μ(A) = 0 for every measurable set A with ν(A) = 0. The Radon–Nikodym theorem gives conditions under which an absolutely continuous measure has a density with respect to another measure.
