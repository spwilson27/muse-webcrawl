Colourism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Colourism (also spelled colorism) is a form of prejudice or discrimination in which people with lighter skin tones are treated more favourably than people with darker skin tones. Unlike racism, colourism can occur within a single racial or ethnic group as well as between groups. The term was popularised by the writer Alice Walker, who used it in her 1982 essay collection In Search of Our Mothers' Gardens, defining it as "prejudicial or preferential treatment of same-race people based solely on their color".

Manifestations

Research in several countries has documented associations between skin tone and outcomes in employment, income, education, criminal sentencing and marriage markets, even after controlling for other variables. Sociologists have described historical mechanisms such as the "paper bag test" once used in some African-American institutions, and colonial hierarchies in Latin America (the casta system) and Asia that privileged proximity to European appearance.

By region

United States

During slavery, lighter-skinned enslaved people were sometimes assigned domestic work rather than field labour, embedding a hierarchy that persisted in social institutions after emancipation. Modern studies find skin-tone stratification in wages and sentencing, a phenomenon summarised in the phrase "light skin privilege".

South and East Asia

In India, colourism intersects with caste and with a large market for skin-lightening products; campaigns such as "Dark is Beautiful" have pushed back against advertising norms. Preference for pale skin is also documented in China, Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia, with roots in pre-colonial associations between fair skin and freedom from outdoor labour, reinforced by later Western influence.

Latin America and the Caribbean

Pigmentocracies — social orders stratified by skin shade — have been described across the region, alongside ideologies of blanqueamiento (whitening) and local gradations of shade vocabulary.

Media and beauty industries

Critics point to casting, lighting and retouching practices, and the global skin-lightening industry — worth billions of dollars — as sustaining colourist norms. Several major brands rebranded or discontinued "fairness" products in the 2020s following public criticism.
