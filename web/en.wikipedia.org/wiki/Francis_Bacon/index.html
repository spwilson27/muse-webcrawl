Francis Bacon, 1st Viscount St Alban (22 January 1561 – 9 April 1626) was an English philosopher, statesman, and essayist, often credited as the father of empiricism and of the scientific method. He argued that knowledge of nature should be gathered inductively from observation and experiment, free from the "Idols" — systematic sources of error he catalogued in his Novum Organum (1620).

Career

Bacon was the younger son of Sir Nicholas Bacon, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, and Gray's Inn, he sat in Parliament from 1584 and rose under James I to become Attorney General (1613) and Lord Chancellor (1618), being created Baron Verulam and later Viscount St Alban. In 1621 he was charged with taking bribes, confessed, and was fined and barred from office; his remaining years were devoted to writing and experiment — he died in 1626 after contracting pneumonia, reputedly while stuffing a chicken with snow to study refrigeration.

Works and legacy

His Essays (final edition 1625) are models of the form in English. The Advancement of Learning (1605) surveyed and classified knowledge; Novum Organum proposed a new logic of induction as the second part of his unfinished Instauratio Magna; the utopian fragment New Atlantis (1627) describes Salomon's House, a research institution that prefigured the Royal Society. Bacon's influence on the development of experimental science and on later philosophers from Locke to Kant is extensive; a fringe theory also attributes to him some of Shakespeare's works.
