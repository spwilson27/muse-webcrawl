Malva sylvestris, common mallow.

Malva is a genus of herbaceous annual, biennial and perennial plants in the family Malvaceae. It is one of several closely related genera in the tribe Malveae commonly known as mallows. The genus is widespread across temperate, subtropical and tropical regions of Africa, Asia and Europe.

Description

The leaves are alternate, palmately lobed, and the flowers have five pink, lilac, purple or white petals. The fruit is a disc-shaped schizocarp known colloquially as a "cheese" from its resemblance to a wheel of cheese segments.

Species

Well-known species include Malva sylvestris (common mallow), M. neglecta (dwarf mallow), M. moschata (musk mallow), M. parviflora, M. pusilla and M. verticillata (Chinese mallow).

Uses

Mallows have a long history as food and medicine: the leaves and young fruits are eaten in salads and soups (as in the Levantine dish khubeza and, in related species, Egyptian molokhia), and the mucilaginous leaves were used in herbal medicine as a demulcent. Several species are grown as garden ornamentals.

Etymology

The English word "mauve" derives, via French, from the Latin malva, after the colour of the flowers; the mallow gave its name to the first synthetic aniline dye, mauveine.
