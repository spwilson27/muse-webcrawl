Slum

A slum is a highly populated urban residential area consisting mostly of closely packed, decrepit housing units in a situation of deteriorated or incomplete infrastructure, inhabited primarily by impoverished persons. UN-Habitat defines a slum household as one lacking one or more of: durable housing, sufficient living space, improved water, improved sanitation, and secure tenure.

Scale and examples

UN estimates have put the global slum population at roughly one billion people, concentrated in the developing world. Well-known examples include Kibera in Nairobi, among Africa's largest; Dharavi in Mumbai, a dense settlement with a large informal economy of recycling and small manufacturing; the favelas of Brazil such as Rocinha in Rio de Janeiro; and Orangi Town in Karachi, among the world's largest informal settlements. Historically, the term emerged in 19th-century London and New York for districts such as the rookeries and Five Points.

Causes and conditions

Slums form where rapid urbanisation and rural-to-urban migration outrun formal housing supply, land markets and public services. Residents frequently work in the informal economy and face health burdens from poor water and sanitation, but settlements also sustain dense social networks, micro-enterprise and self-built housing that incremental upgrading can improve upon.

Policy responses

Approaches have shifted from clearance and eviction — criticised for destroying communities — toward in-situ slum upgrading: tenure regularisation, water and sewerage, paving and community participation, as promoted by UN-Habitat's programmes and the Sustainable Development Goal 11 target of ensuring adequate housing for all. Squatter settlements and shanty towns are closely related forms.
