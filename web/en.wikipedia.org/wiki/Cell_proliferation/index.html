Cell proliferation is the process by which a cell grows and divides to produce two daughter cells, leading to an exponential increase in cell number. It is a fundamental process in the growth and development of organisms, in tissue maintenance and repair, and — when uncontrolled — in cancer.

Regulation

Proliferation is driven by the cell cycle, whose progression through G1, S, G2 and M phases is controlled by cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases, and gated by checkpoints that monitor DNA damage and spindle assembly. External signals such as mitogens and growth factors (for example epidermal growth factor and platelet-derived growth factor) stimulate quiescent cells to re-enter the cycle through pathways including Ras–MAPK and PI3K–AKT–mTOR. Normal cells also exhibit contact inhibition, ceasing to divide when they form a confluent layer, and most somatic cells can divide only a limited number of times before reaching replicative senescence at the Hayflick limit.

Measurement

Proliferation is commonly assayed by direct cell counting, incorporation of labelled nucleotides such as BrdU or EdU into newly synthesised DNA, staining for the Ki-67 protein, or metabolic assays such as MTT. In histopathology, mitotic counts and Ki-67 indices are used to grade tumours.

Proliferation and cancer

Hallmarks of cancer include sustained proliferative signalling and evasion of growth suppressors: mutations in oncogenes such as MYC and RAS, or in tumour suppressors such as RB1 and TP53, free cells from normal controls. Many chemotherapy drugs act by damaging rapidly proliferating cells, which also accounts for side effects in gut lining and bone marrow.
