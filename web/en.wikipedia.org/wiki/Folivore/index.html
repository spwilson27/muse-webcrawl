Folivore

Folivore
DietLeaves (foliage)
ExamplesKoala, sloths, gorilla, howler monkeys, okapi, many caterpillars
AdaptationsEnlarged gut fermentation chambers, detoxification, slow metabolism

In zoology, a folivore is a herbivore that specializes in eating leaves. Mature leaves contain a high proportion of cellulose, which is hard to digest, relatively low energy content, and often plant defensive compounds such as tannins and alkaloids. Because of this, folivorous animals tend to have long digestive tracts, slow metabolic rates, and behavioral strategies—such as selective feeding and extensive resting—to cope with a low-quality diet.

Adaptations

Mammalian folivores rely on microbial fermentation to break down cellulose, either in a multi-chambered foregut (as in colobus monkeys, sloths, and kangaroos) or in an enlarged hindgut and cecum (as in koalas, gorillas, and hoatzins among birds). Many species practice coprophagy or cecotrophy to re-ingest partially digested food. Folivores also selectively eat young, protein-rich leaves and possess liver and salivary adaptations (such as tannin-binding proteins) to neutralize plant toxins. Large body size is advantageous because it increases gut capacity relative to energy requirements, though many folivores are small and simply tolerate low energy throughput.

Examples

Well-known folivores include the koala, which feeds almost exclusively on eucalyptus; tree sloths; gorillas and several leaf monkeys such as colobuses and langurs; howler monkeys; the okapi; and the hoatzin, a bird with foregut fermentation. Among invertebrates, many caterpillars, leaf beetles, and leafcutter ants (which farm fungus on leaf pulp) are folivorous.

Ecology

Because leaves are abundant but nutritionally poor, folivory is less common among mammals than grazing or frugivory, and folivores face limits on activity and reproduction imposed by their energy budget. Arboreal folivores are often solitary or live in small groups, spend much of the day resting, and have contributed to coevolutionary arms races between plants' chemical defenses and animal detoxification.
