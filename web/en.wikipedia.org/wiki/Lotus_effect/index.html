Lotus effect

The lotus effect refers to self-cleaning properties that are a result of ultrahydrophobicity as exhibited by the leaves of Nelumbo nucifera (the lotus flower). Dirt particles are picked up by water droplets due to the micro- and nanoscopic architecture of the surface, which minimises the droplets' adhesion to that surface. Ultrahydrophobicity and self-cleaning properties are also found in other plants, such as Tropaeolum (nasturtium), Opuntia (prickly pear), and Alchemilla, as well as on the wings of certain insects.

Explanation

The high water contact angle (typically greater than 150°) is caused by the surface structure: the leaf epidermis is covered with tiny bumps (papillae) roughly 10 micrometres high, themselves coated with hydrophobic wax crystals about 1 nanometre in diameter. A droplet resting on this rough, waxy topography sits mostly on air pockets trapped between the papillae — a state described by the Cassie–Baxter model — so it beads into a near-sphere and rolls off at very slight inclinations. As it rolls, it adheres to dirt particles more strongly than the leaf surface does, carrying them away: the mechanism of self-cleaning.

Research and discovery

Although the phenomenon was known in Asian traditions for centuries — the lotus being a symbol of purity — the underlying mechanism was investigated scientifically in the 1970s by botanists Wilhelm Barthlott and Christoph Neinhuis at the University of Bonn, who examined leaf surfaces with scanning electron microscopy. Their 1997 paper "Purity of the sacred lotus, or escape from contamination in biological surfaces" established the connection between surface roughness, reduced particle adhesion, and self-cleaning, and Barthlott coined the term "lotus effect", registering it as a trademark for commercial applications.

Applications

The principle has been applied in biomimetic products including self-cleaning paints (such as the facade paint Lotusan), treated glass, roofing materials, and textiles that resist wetting and soiling. Researchers continue to study durable superhydrophobic coatings, since the fine nanostructures that produce the effect are mechanically fragile compared with the self-repairing wax layer of a living leaf.
