Common tenrec

Common tenrec
Tenrec ecaudatus
Family: Tenrecidae
Conservation status: Least Concern

The common tenrec (Tenrec ecaudatus), also known as the tailless tenrec, is a mammal species in the family Tenrecidae, endemic to Madagascar. It is the largest living member of its family, reaching 25–39 cm in body length and weighing 1.5–2.5 kg. Its coarse fur is mixed with stiff spines, and its long, pointed snout bears prominent whiskers. The common tenrec has been introduced to the Comoros, Mauritius, Réunion, and the Seychelles.

Behaviour

The common tenrec is nocturnal and omnivorous, feeding on invertebrates such as earthworms, as well as frogs, small reptiles, and fruit. It forages by scent on the forest floor and can climb. During the dry season, when food is scarce and temperatures high, it may enter torpor or aestivate in burrows — an unusual strategy for a mammal. It is also notable for stridulation: it can produce sounds by rubbing together specialised quills on its back, one of the few mammals known to communicate this way.

Reproduction

The species is famous for its fecundity. Females have more teats (up to 29) than any other mammal, and litters of up to 32 young have been recorded, though 15–20 is more typical. Gestation lasts roughly 50–60 days, and the young develop rapidly on the ground among leaf litter, following their mother in a line.

Relationship with humans

In Madagascar the common tenrec is hunted as bushmeat and is culturally significant; it is widespread and listed as Least Concern by the IUCN. Its combination of primitive anatomical features and high reproductive output has made it a subject of evolutionary study.
