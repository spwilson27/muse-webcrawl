Warm-blooded

Warm-blooded is an informal term for animals that maintain a relatively constant internal body temperature largely through metabolic heat production, rather than relying on environmental heat. The technical terms are endothermy (heat generated internally) and homeothermy (temperature held roughly constant). Mammals and birds are the principal warm-blooded groups alive today.

Mechanisms

Endotherms generate heat as a by-product of a high metabolic rate, and regulate it with insulation (fur, feathers, fat), shivering, sweating, panting, and behavioural adjustments such as basking or seeking shade. A four-chambered heart and efficient lungs support the oxygen demand of sustained metabolism, enabling prolonged activity, including at night and in cold climates.

Costs and benefits

The main cost is food: a warm-blooded animal may need ten times as much energy as a similar-sized cold-blooded animal. The benefits include activity independent of ambient temperature, colonisation of cold environments, sustained aerobic exercise, and (in mammals) the ability to keep developing young warm.

Evolution

Endothermy evolved independently in the ancestors of mammals and birds, and possibly in some dinosaurs; evidence from bone growth patterns, respiratory structures and isotope studies continues to be debated. Some fish, such as tuna and lamnid sharks, and some insects are regionally endothermic, warming particular tissues.
