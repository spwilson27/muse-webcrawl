Suspension of judgment

The suspension of judgment is a cognitive process and rational state of mind in which one withholds judgments, particularly on the drawing of moral or ethical conclusions. The opposite is premature judgment or certainty. In philosophy, suspension of judgment is central to scepticism: the Greek term epoché (ἐποχή), "suspension", denotes the state in which the sceptic asserts neither the truth nor the falsehood of a proposition.

In ancient philosophy

For the Pyrrhonist sceptics described by Sextus Empiricus, epoché arises when opposing arguments and appearances are found to be of equal strength (isostheneia), leaving the mind unable to assent to either side. The Pyrrhonists held that this suspension was followed, as if by chance, by ataraxia — tranquillity of mind — which became the goal of the sceptical way of life. The Academic sceptics also practised suspension regarding matters that were not evidently certain.

Later uses

Descartes employed methodical doubt as a tool toward certainty rather than an end. In phenomenology, Husserl adapted epoché as the "bracketing" of the natural attitude to study experience itself. More loosely, suspension of judgment names the everyday discipline of deferring conclusions until evidence is sufficient — a cornerstone of scientific method and of fair adjudication.
