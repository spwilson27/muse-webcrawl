Apology (Plato)

AuthorPlato
LanguageAncient Greek
SubjectTrial of Socrates
Datec. 399–387 BC

The Apology of Socrates (Greek: Ἀπολογία Σωκράτους, Apología Sōkrátous) is a Socratic dialogue written by Plato. It purports to be the speech Socrates gave in his own defence at his trial in Athens in 399 BC, where he was charged with impiety (asebeia) and corrupting the youth of the city. It is generally regarded as one of the most reliable of Plato's early dialogues as an account of Socrates' thought, though it is a literary reconstruction rather than a transcript.

Structure

The speech falls into three parts corresponding to the phases of an Athenian trial. In the first and longest part, Socrates answers the formal charges brought by Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon. He explains that his reputation for wisdom began when the oracle at Delphi declared no one wiser than him, and that his questioning of reputed experts revealed their ignorance; this divine mission, he argues, explains both his poverty and the hostility against him. He famously declares that "the unexamined life is not worth living" and compares himself to a gadfly stinging the sluggish horse of the Athenian state.

The verdict and sentence

After the jury votes him guilty by a narrow margin, Socrates is asked to propose a counter-penalty to the prosecution's demand for death. He provocatively suggests he deserves free meals at the Prytaneum, an honour reserved for Olympic victors and public benefactors, before offering a small fine, later raised by his friends to thirty minae. The jury sentences him to death. In the final part, Socrates addresses those who voted for and against him, refusing to lament and speculating that death is either a dreamless sleep or a migration of the soul to another place where he could converse with the heroes of the past.

Legacy

The Apology has remained a foundational text of Western philosophy and a classic defence of free inquiry and intellectual integrity. Together with the Euthyphro, Crito, and Phaedo, it forms a tetralogy recounting the last days of Socrates. See also Socrates, Plato, and Crito.
