An eclipsing binary is a binary star system whose orbital plane lies nearly along the observer's line of sight, so that the two stars periodically eclipse each other as seen from Earth. The system's total brightness therefore varies with a period equal to the orbital period, dipping whenever one star passes in front of the other. Eclipsing binaries are a class of variable star and are of fundamental importance in astronomy because modelling their light curves — especially when combined with radial-velocity measurements — yields direct determinations of stellar masses, radii and temperatures, quantities obtainable in almost no other way.

Light curves and classification

When the brighter star is eclipsed (primary minimum) the brightness drop is usually deeper than when the fainter star is hidden (secondary minimum), because the two stars have different surface brightnesses. The traditional classification is based on the shape of the light curve:

  • EA (Algol type) — well-detached stars: the light is nearly constant between eclipses, and the minima are sharp and clearly delimited.
  • EB (Beta Lyrae type) — semi-detached systems: brightness varies continuously, so the start and end of eclipses cannot be pinpointed; the secondary minimum is noticeably shallower.
  • EW (W Ursae Majoris type) — contact binaries sharing a common envelope: periods typically under a day, with primary and secondary minima of nearly equal depth.

Scientific importance

From the durations of totality and eclipse, astronomers derive the stars' radii relative to the orbit; from the depths of the minima, the ratio of their surface brightnesses (and hence temperatures). Double-lined spectroscopic eclipsing binaries give absolute masses and radii to accuracies of about 1%, anchoring the theory of stellar structure and evolution. Eclipsing systems containing Cepheids or red giants also serve as precision distance indicators — the eclipsing binary method has measured the distance to the Large Magellanic Cloud to about 1% accuracy. Space missions such as Kepler and TESS have catalogued tens of thousands of eclipsing binaries and use the same transit signature to detect exoplanets.

Notable examples

The prototype, Algol (Beta Persei), dims visibly every 2.867 days; its regularity was recorded by John Goodricke in 1783, who proposed the eclipse explanation. Other famous systems include Beta Lyrae, W Ursae Majoris, Epsilon Aurigae (with its extraordinarily long 27-year period and mysterious eclipsing disc), and the bright southern star Spica (Alpha Virginis), a close ellipsoidal/eclipsing system.
