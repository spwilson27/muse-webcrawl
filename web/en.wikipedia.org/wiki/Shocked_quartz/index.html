Shocked quartz

Shocked quartz is a form of quartz that has a microscopic structure different from normal quartz. Under intense pressure (but limited temperature), the crystalline structure of quartz is deformed along planes inside the crystal known as planar deformation features (PDFs). These lamellae, visible under a microscope, form in sets with specific crystallographic orientations.

Formation

Shocked quartz is created by the extreme pressures of a meteorite impact event. Unlike volcanic and tectonic processes, which cannot generate the required shock pressures, the deformations in shocked quartz require pressures on the order of tens of gigapascals. Shocked quartz was first recognised in material from nuclear test sites and from impact craters such as Meteor Crater in Arizona, where the mineralogist Eugene Shoemaker and colleagues used it as diagnostic evidence of impact origin.

Under still higher pressures, quartz transforms into its high-pressure polymorphs coesite and stishovite, which likewise serve as impact indicators.

Significance

Because shocked quartz can be produced only by impact events (or human-made explosions), it is accepted as unambiguous evidence of a past impact. A worldwide layer of shocked quartz, together with an iridium anomaly, at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary provided key support for the hypothesis that a large asteroid impact caused the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago, and helped locate the buried Chicxulub crater in Mexico.
