Tagma (biology)

In biology, a tagma (Greek: τάγμα, plural tagmata) is a specialised grouping of multiple segments or metameres into a coherently functional morphological unit. Familiar examples are the head, thorax and abdomen of insects, and the cephalothorax and abdomen of spiders and other chelicerates.

Tagmosis

The evolutionary process of grouping segments into tagmata is called tagmosis. Within a tagma, segments may be fused, reduced or modified to perform a common function — for example, the head tagma of arthropods bears sensory organs and mouthparts, the thorax bears locomotor appendages, and the abdomen contains most of the viscera. The degree of tagmosis differs among arthropod groups: trilobites had three tagmata (cephalon, thorax and pygidium), while in mites the body is essentially a single undivided tagma.

Terminology

The individual tagmata of arthropods are further grouped in some taxa: in malacostracan crustaceans, for instance, the cephalon and thorax are often combined as a cephalothorax covered by a carapace. In anatomy of other segmented animals, such as annelids, tagmosis is far less pronounced, and the term is applied mainly to arthropods.
