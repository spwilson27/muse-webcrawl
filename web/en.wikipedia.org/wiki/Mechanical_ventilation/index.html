Mechanical ventilation

Mechanical ventilation
SpecialtyIntensive care medicine, pulmonology, anaesthesiology
UsesRespiratory failure, general anaesthesia, airway protection
ApproachesInvasive (endotracheal tube, tracheostomy); non-invasive (mask)

Mechanical ventilation is a method of artificially supporting or replacing spontaneous breathing using a machine called a ventilator. It assists or controls the flow of air into and out of the lungs of a patient who is unable to breathe adequately on their own. Mechanical ventilation is a mainstay of intensive care, emergency medicine and anaesthesia.

A ventilator delivering positive-pressure breaths through an endotracheal tube.

Indications

Ventilation is instituted for acute or chronic respiratory failure, which may be hypoxaemic (failure of oxygenation, as in ARDS or pneumonia) or hypercapnic (failure of ventilation, as in exacerbations of COPD), for airway protection in coma or after cardiac arrest, and routinely during general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation.

Methods

Invasive ventilation delivers gas via an endotracheal tube or a tracheostomy. Non-invasive ventilation uses a tight-fitting nasal or face mask and includes CPAP and bilevel support. Modern ventilators operate in numerous modes: volume-controlled or pressure-controlled mandatory ventilation, assist-control, synchronised intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV), pressure-support ventilation, and newer adaptive modes. Key settings include tidal volume, respiratory rate, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Complications

Positive-pressure ventilation can itself injure the lung (ventilator-induced lung injury, including barotrauma and pneumothorax), predispose to ventilator-associated pneumonia, cause diaphragm atrophy, and produce haemodynamic effects such as reduced venous return and hypotension. "Lung-protective" strategies—low tidal volumes (about 6 mL/kg predicted body weight), limited plateau pressures, and prone positioning in severe ARDS—have been shown to reduce mortality. Historically, negative-pressure devices such as the iron lung were used extensively during the polio epidemics of the mid-20th century before positive-pressure techniques predominated.

Weaning

As the underlying condition resolves, ventilatory support is progressively withdrawn ("weaning"), commonly assessed with spontaneous breathing trials, before the tube is removed (extubation). Some patients require prolonged ventilation and rehabilitation in specialist units.
