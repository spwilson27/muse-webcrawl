Mehmed II (30 March 1432 – 3 May 1481), known as Mehmed the Conqueror, was the Ottoman sultan who conquered Constantinople on 29 May 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire, and went on to expand Ottoman rule across the Balkans, Anatolia and the Crimea. He first reigned briefly in 1444–46 and definitively from 1451 to 1481.

For the Constantinople campaign Mehmed built the Rumeli Hisarı fortress on the Bosphorus, assembled a great fleet, and commissioned giant bombards from the founder Urban, whose guns battered the Theodosian Walls during the 53-day siege. After the conquest he repopulated and rebuilt the city — Topkapi Palace, the Grand Bazaar, mosques and colleges — made it his capital, styled himself Kayser-i Rûm (Caesar of the Romans), and granted the Orthodox Patriarchate a recognised place under Ottoman rule. He later conquered Serbia, Bosnia, the Morea, Trebizond and Crimea, and fought Venice and Hungary; he died in 1481 while campaigning, possibly preparing an invasion of Italy after the landing at Otranto.

Legacy

Mehmed is regarded as one of the great Ottoman sultans: a polyglot patron of Renaissance art and learning (he sat for Gentile Bellini) as well as a relentless military campaigner. Istanbul's Fatih district and the Fatih Sultan Mehmet Bridge are named for him — 'Fatih' means 'the Conqueror'.
