Toyo Miyatake

Toyo Miyatake (宮武東洋, 1895–1979)
Born: 1895, Kagawa, Japan
Died: 1979, Los Angeles, California, U.S.
Occupation: Photographer
Known for: Photographs of the Manzanar incarceration camp

Toyo Miyatake (宮武東洋, Miyatake Tōyō; 1895–1979) was a Japanese-American photographer best known for documenting the incarceration of Japanese Americans at the Manzanar camp during World War II, using a camera he smuggled into the camp and operated in defiance of a ban on photography by internees.

Miyatake in his Little Tokyo studio.

Early life and career

Miyatake was born in Kagawa, Japan, and immigrated to the United States in 1909. He studied photography and opened a studio in the Little Tokyo district of Los Angeles, where he became part of the city's artistic community, photographing dancers, actors, and visiting celebrities such as Michio Ito. In the 1920s he was associated with Edward Weston, who encouraged his work and became a friend.

Manzanar

Following the attack on Pearl Harbor and Executive Order 9066, Miyatake and his family were forcibly removed to the Manzanar War Relocation Center in the Owens Valley. Cameras were prohibited for internees, but Miyatake smuggled in a lens and film holder and built a camera body from wood with the help of a camp carpenter. He photographed camp life clandestinely at first, focusing his camera before asking a Caucasian friend to trip the shutter; eventually camp director Ralph Merritt, recognising the value of the documentation, permitted him to take photographs openly and set up a studio in the camp.

Miyatake's images — of barracks, guard towers, weddings, funerals, and everyday life behind barbed wire — provide one of the few records of the incarceration made by an internee. When Ansel Adams photographed Manzanar in 1943, Miyatake assisted him.

Later life and legacy

After the war, Miyatake returned to Los Angeles and reopened his studio in Little Tokyo, which his family continued to operate after his death in 1979. His Manzanar photographs have been widely exhibited and published, and the Rafu Shimpo newspaper building and his studio remain landmarks of Japanese American history. In 2009, a documentary about his life, Toyo Miyatake: Infinite Shades of Gray, was produced. His archive is held in part by the Japanese American National Museum.
