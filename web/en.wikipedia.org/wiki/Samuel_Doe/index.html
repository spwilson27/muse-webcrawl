Samuel Kanyon Doe (1951–1990) was a Liberian military officer and politician who ruled Liberia from 1980 until his death in 1990. A master sergeant in the Armed Forces of Liberia, he led the 1980 coup d'état that overthrew President William Tolbert and ended more than a century of political dominance by Americo-Liberian settlers. Doe was the first Liberian head of state of indigenous (non-Americo-Liberian) descent.

Rise to power

Doe, an ethnic Krahn from Grand Gedeh County, enlisted in the army as a young man and rose to master sergeant. On 12 April 1980 he led a group of non-commissioned officers who stormed the Executive Mansion in Monrovia and killed President Tolbert. The People's Redemption Council (PRC), chaired by Doe, took power; thirteen officials of the old regime were publicly executed on a Monrovia beach, an event broadcast worldwide.

Rule

The PRC initially promised reform and a return to civilian rule, and Doe cultivated close relations with the United States, which substantially increased aid to Liberia during the Cold War. A new constitution was adopted in 1984; Doe's National Democratic Party of Liberia won the 1985 elections amid widespread allegations of fraud, and he was sworn in as president in January 1986. His government became increasingly authoritarian and favoured his Krahn group, provoking a failed coup by Thomas Quiwonkpa in November 1985, followed by violent reprisals against the Gio and Mano peoples of Nimba County — deepening the ethnic divisions that later fuelled civil war.

Civil war and death

In December 1989 Charles Taylor's National Patriotic Front of Liberia invaded from Côte d'Ivoire, beginning the First Liberian Civil War. By mid-1990 Doe controlled little more than central Monrovia, contested by Taylor's forces and a breakaway faction under Prince Johnson. On 9 September 1990 Doe was captured at the headquarters of the West African peacekeeping force ECOMOG and was tortured and killed by Johnson's men; video of his interrogation and mutilation circulated internationally. His death was followed by years of civil war that devastated Liberia.
