The Redshirts were the volunteer followers of Giuseppe Garibaldi in the wars of Italian unification.

The Redshirts (Italian: Camicie Rosse), also known as Garibaldini, were the volunteer troops led by Giuseppe Garibaldi during the wars of the Italian unification (the Risorgimento). They took their name from the red shirts they wore instead of a full military uniform.

Origins

The red shirt originated in Garibaldi's exile in South America. In the 1840s his Italian Legion, fighting in the Uruguayan Civil War, adopted red shirts — according to the traditional account, a consignment of cheap red woollen shirts originally intended for slaughterhouse and salting-plant workers in Buenos Aires, whose colour would disguise bloodstains. The garment became the legion's emblem and travelled with Garibaldi back to Italy in 1848, worn by his volunteers in defence of the Roman Republic in 1849.

Expedition of the Thousand

The Redshirts won their greatest fame in the Expedition of the Thousand (Spedizione dei Mille) of 1860. About a thousand volunteers sailed from Genoa aboard two steamers and landed at Marsala in Sicily on May 11, 1860. Despite being outnumbered, Garibaldi's force won at Calatafimi, took Palermo, crossed to the mainland, and swept up the peninsula, collapsing the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. At the meeting at Teano on October 26, 1860, Garibaldi handed the conquered south to King Victor Emmanuel II, enabling the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy the following year.

Later campaigns and legacy

Redshirt volunteers fought again in the Third Italian War of Independence (1866), in Garibaldi's campaign against the Papal States ending at Mentana (1867), and in 1870–71 an Army of the Vosges under Garibaldi fought for France in the Franco-Prussian War. The red shirt became an enduring symbol of patriotic volunteerism; Garibaldi's descendants later led a legion in French service in 1914, and the Garibaldi Battalions of the Spanish Civil War and Italian partisans of the Second World War consciously echoed the tradition.
