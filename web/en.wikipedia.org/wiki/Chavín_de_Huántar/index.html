Chavín de Huántar is an archaeological site in the Ancash Region of Peru, containing the ruins and artifacts constructed by the pre-Inca Chavín culture. The site, located in the valley of the Mosna River at an altitude of about 3,180 metres, was the ceremonial and administrative centre of the Chavín religious movement, one of the earliest widespread cultural horizons in the Andes.

History

Construction at the site began around 1200 BCE and continued, in several phases, for roughly seven centuries. The complex consists of two main temple platforms — the Old Temple and the New Temple (Castillo) — surrounded by sunken circular and rectangular plazas, terraces, and an elaborate network of underground galleries and drainage channels.

The interior galleries of the Old Temple contain the Lanzón, a granite monolith carved with the image of the principal Chavín deity, still in its original position in a cruciform chamber. Other famous carvings include the Raimondi Stela and the Tello Obelisk.

Significance

Chavín de Huántar served as a pilgrimage and oracle centre whose religious iconography — combining jaguars, caymans, raptorial birds, and San Pedro cactus imagery — spread across much of the northern and central Andes. The site was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985. In 1996–97 the name became widely known internationally when the Japanese ambassador's residence in Lima, seized by MRTA guerrillas, was retaken in "Operation Chavín de Huántar", named after the site's underground tunnels.
