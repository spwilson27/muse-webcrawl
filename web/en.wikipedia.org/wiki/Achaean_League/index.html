The Achaean League (Greek: Κοινὸν τῶν Ἀχαιῶν, "League of the Achaeans") was a Hellenistic-era confederation of Greek city-states on the northern and central Peloponnese. Re-founded in 280 BC, it grew into one of the two great federal states of late Hellenistic Greece, rivalling the Aetolian League, before its destruction by Rome in 146 BC.

History

A loose league of twelve Achaean towns existed from the 5th century BC. The Hellenistic league was refounded in 280 BC by Dyme, Patrae, Tritaea and Pharae. Under Aratus of Sicyon, who joined the league in 251 BC and served as strategos many times, it expanded aggressively — capturing Corinth (243 BC) and bringing in Argos, Megalopolis and other cities, often positioning itself against Macedon. The historian Polybius, himself an Achaean statesman, chronicled its rise in his Histories.

In the Cleomenic War the league, under pressure from Sparta's Cleomenes III, allied with Macedon's Antigonus III Doson, who crushed Sparta at Sellasia (222 BC). The statesman Philopoemen later reformed the league's army and finally brought Sparta itself into the league (192 BC). Relations with Rome deteriorated after the Third Macedonian War; in 146 BC the league's war with Rome (the Achaean War) ended in defeat at the Battle of Corinth, the sack of Corinth by Lucius Mummius, and the dissolution of the league — Greece passed under direct Roman rule.

Government

The league combined federal and local citizenship, common coinage and measures, and federal assemblies meeting at Aegium. Its executive was the strategos, elected annually and not immediately re-eligible, assisted by a council (boule) and board of ten damiourgoi. The league's federal structure was later studied as a model of federation, including by the authors of the American Federalist Papers.
