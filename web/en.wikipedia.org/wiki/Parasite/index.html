Parasite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A parasite is an organism that lives on or inside another organism (its host) and benefits by deriving nutrients at the host's expense. Parasitism is one of the most successful lifestyles on Earth: parasites occur in nearly every animal group, and a large fraction of all species may be parasitic. They range from microscopic single-celled organisms (such as the malaria parasite Plasmodium) and bacteria-like organisms, through worms (helminths) and lice, fleas and ticks, to parasitic plants like mistletoe and even a few vertebrates (the cuckoo, in the related strategy of brood parasitism, and the vampire bat).

Types and life cycles

Ectoparasites live on the host's surface (lice, ticks, fleas); endoparasites live inside, in the gut, blood or tissues. Many have complex life cycles involving several hosts — Plasmodium, for instance, alternates between humans and Anopheles mosquitoes — and they manipulate host behaviour to complete transmission: the rabies virus promotes aggression, and some fungi drive infected ants to climb and bite vegetation before killing them. Hosts evolve defences — above all the immune system — and parasites evolve countermeasures, in an evolutionary arms race.

Impact on humans

Parasitic diseases remain a major global health burden: malaria, schistosomiasis, soil-transmitted worm infections, river blindness (onchocerciasis) and Chagas disease together affect billions, mostly in tropical regions, and are targets of World Health Organization programmes against "neglected tropical diseases". In wealthy countries, food- and water-borne parasites (such as Giardia and Toxoplasma) still circulate quietly. Conversely, the near-absence of parasites in sanitised environments is hypothesised to contribute to rising allergy and autoimmune disease — the "old friends" hypothesis.
