Twelve-string guitar

The twelve-string guitar is a steel-string guitar with 12 strings in six courses, which produces a thicker, more ringing tone than a standard six-string guitar. Typically, the strings of the lower four courses are tuned in octaves, with the highest two courses tuned in unison; the strings are usually played as pairs ("courses") rather than individually.

Sound and tuning

Because of the small difference in frequency between the paired strings — and the shimmering chorus-like beating effect they produce — the twelve-string has a bright, rich, naturally chorused sound, with greater sustain and volume than a comparable six-string. In standard tuning the courses run E–A–D–G–B–E, with each of the four bass courses pairing a normal string with one an octave higher. To reduce the very high tension on the neck, many players tune the instrument a semitone or whole tone below standard pitch, sometimes using a capo to return to concert pitch.

History

The origins of the twelve-string are obscure; instruments with doubled courses are centuries old (as in the mandolin family and the Spanish vihuela), and the modern twelve-string guitar emerged in the United States around the turn of the 20th century, associated with makers such as Oscar Schmidt's Stella brand. It became a hallmark of American folk and blues through Lead Belly, who played a Stella twelve-string and called it the "king of the twelve-string guitar players of the world."

In the 1960s the electric twelve-string — above all the Rickenbacker 360/12, played by George Harrison of the Beatles and Roger McGuinn of the Byrds — defined the jangle of folk rock on songs such as "Mr. Tambourine Man" and "Turn! Turn! Turn!". The acoustic twelve-string remained prominent in folk and rock, in the hands of players such as Pete Seeger, Gordon Lightfoot, Leo Kottke, and John Denver, and in recordings by Led Zeppelin, Pink Floyd, and the Eagles ("Hotel California").
