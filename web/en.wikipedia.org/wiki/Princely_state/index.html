A princely state (also called a native state, feudatory state or Indian state) was a nominally sovereign entity of the British Indian Empire that was not directly governed by the British, but rather by an Indian ruler under a form of indirect rule, subject to a subsidiary alliance and the suzerainty or paramountcy of the British Crown. At the time of the partition of British India in 1947, there were 565 recognised princely states.

History

The system developed as the East India Company expanded across India in the 18th and 19th centuries, concluding treaties and subsidiary alliances with local rulers who accepted British supremacy in exchange for protection and internal autonomy. The states ranged enormously in size and importance: from Hyderabad, comparable to a large European country, and Jammu and Kashmir, Mysore, Baroda and Gwalior, down to small estates of a few square miles. Rulers received gun salutes graded by rank, from 21 guns for the most senior down to 9. The Chamber of Princes, established in 1920, gave the larger states a consultative forum.

Accession and integration

With independence in 1947 the British paramountcy lapsed, and the states were advised to accede to either India or Pakistan on the subjects of defence, external affairs and communications. Under Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V. P. Menon, nearly all states within India's borders signed Instruments of Accession and were progressively merged into the Indian Union; the holdouts Hyderabad (annexed in 1948 after a police action) and Kashmir (whose accession triggered the Indo-Pakistani War of 1947–48) became lasting disputes. The rulers retained privy purses and titles until the 26th Amendment to the Indian Constitution abolished their recognition in 1971.
