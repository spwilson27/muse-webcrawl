Gulf

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A gulf is a large inlet from an ocean or sea into a landmass, typically larger and more deeply indented than a bay. Gulfs are formed as a result of plate tectonics and, like bays, are often important centres of human activity — fishing, trade and shipping — because of the sheltered waters they provide.

Terminology

Geographical terms for inlets — gulf, bay, sound, fjord, bight and sea — are used inconsistently around the world and often reflect historical usage rather than size alone. The Gulf of Mexico (about 1.6 million km²), the largest gulf in the world, is considerably bigger than Hudson Bay, which is classed as a bay, while the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of California are sometimes called seas.

Notable gulfs

Among the world's best-known gulfs are the Gulf of Mexico, the Persian Gulf (a focus of world oil trade), the Gulf of California (Sea of Cortés), the Gulf of Alaska, the Gulf of Guinea, the Gulf of Thailand, the Gulf of Carpentaria in Australia, the Gulf of Bothnia and the Gulf of Finland in the Baltic, and the Gulf of Aqaba and Gulf of Suez at the head of the Red Sea.

Formation

Many gulfs arise where the crust has thinned and subsided in rift valleys — as with the Red Sea gulfs and the Gulf of California, which is actively widening — or where rising post-glacial seas drowned river valleys and lowlands, as in the Gulf of Mexico basin and the Persian Gulf.
