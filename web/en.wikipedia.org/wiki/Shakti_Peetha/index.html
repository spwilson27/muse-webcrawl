Shakti Peetha

The Shakti Pithas, also rendered Shakti Peethas (Sanskrit: शक्ति पीठ, "seats of Shakti"), are significant shrines and pilgrimage destinations in Shaktism, the goddess-focused tradition of Hinduism. According to tradition, they are the places where parts of the body of the goddess Sati fell to earth as Shiva carried her charred body across the world in grief. The number of Peethas varies by text and tradition — commonly 51 (or 52), arranged symbolically on the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet — while early texts enumerate lists of 4, 18 (Maha Shakti Pithas), 51, 64 or 108.

The legend

By the standard Puranic account, Sati, daughter of Daksha and wife of Shiva, immolated herself at her father's sacrifice after he insulted Shiva. Maddened with grief, Shiva carried her body through the universe, performing the destructive dance of Tandava, until Vishnu — to restore cosmic order — dismembered the body with his Sudarshana Chakra (in other versions, the body decayed and fell piece by piece). Wherever each part landed became a sacred seat of the goddess, each paired with a form of Shiva as Bhairava.

Principal shrines

Four shrines are often ranked as pre-eminent (Adi Shakti Pithas): Kamakhya in Assam, where the goddess's yoni fell and which is famed for the Ambubachi Mela; the Vimala/Kalighat complex — Kalighat in Kolkata, where toes of the right foot fell; Taratarini in Odisha; and Dakshina Kalika or the shrines of Jwalamukhi and Hinglaj in Balochistan, Pakistan, where the crown/head portion fell. Other celebrated Peethas include Ambaji (Gujarat), Vaishno Devi's region in Jammu (the tongue tradition), Chamundeshwari (Mysuru), Sharada Peeth (Kashmir, now in Pakistan-administered territory), and the Naina Devi shrine in Himachal Pradesh (the eyes).

Distribution and practice

The Peethas are spread across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal and Sri Lanka (with one attributed to Tibet), mapping sacred geography onto the subcontinent: devotees conceive the land itself as the goddess's body. Each shrine has its own festival cycle; most draw enormous crowds during Navaratri. The concept is closely linked to goddess theology in the Devi Bhagavata Purana and Kalika Purana, and to Tantric traditions that treat the sites as nodes of power (pitha) analogous to sacred centres in the yogic body.
