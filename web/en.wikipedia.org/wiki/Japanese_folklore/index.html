Japanese Folklore

Japanese folklore (日本民話) encompasses the folk traditions, tales, legends, and beliefs of Japan, shaped by Shinto and Buddhist worldviews and by the oral storytelling of rural communities. It features a rich cast of spirits and beings — kami, yōkai (supernatural creatures), yūrei (ghosts), and heroic or trickster figures — and classic tales such as Momotarō, Urashima Tarō, and the story of the grateful crane.

Origins and sources

Early myths were recorded in the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720), which recount the creation of the Japanese islands, the sun goddess Amaterasu, and her brother Susanoo. Medieval setsuwa collections such as the Konjaku Monogatari preserved Buddhist and secular tales, while painters depicted yōkai in picture scrolls like the Hyakki Yagyō Emaki. In the modern era Kunio Yanagita founded academic folklore studies in Japan, collecting legends in works such as The Legends of Tōno (1910).

Common beings

Oni are horned ogres, often red or blue, wielding iron clubs; tengu are bird-like mountain goblins associated with martial arts; kappa are water imps with a dish of water on the head; kitsune (foxes) and tanuki (raccoon dogs) are shapeshifting tricksters; and yūrei, pale ghosts in white with long black hair, anchor Japan's ghost-story tradition.

Folktale types

Classic mukashibanashi include Momotarō, the boy born from a peach who defeats the oni of Onigashima; Urashima Tarō, the fisherman who visits the Dragon Palace and returns centuries later; Kaguya-hime of The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter; and animal-gratitude tales such as the crane wife (Tsuru no Ongaeshi). Festivals such as Setsubun, with bean-throwing to expel oni, keep folklore alive in practice.
