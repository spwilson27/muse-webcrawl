Nocturne

A nocturne is a musical composition that is inspired by, or evocative of, the night. The form was developed principally for the solo piano in the early 19th century, most famously by Frédéric Chopin, though the term was first applied to piano pieces by the Irish composer John Field. The word derives from the Latin nocturnus, "of the night", and in earlier usage referred to a part of the Catholic night office of prayers.

History

In the 18th century, "notturno" denoted a light multi-movement serenade-like work for evening performance, used by composers such as Haydn and Mozart. John Field (1782–1837) transformed the term: his 18 nocturnes for piano (from about 1812) established the characteristic form — a lyrical, melancholy melody over a broken-chord accompaniment, in free, song-like (bel canto) style, often in ternary form.

Chopin's nocturnes

Chopin composed 21 nocturnes for solo piano between 1827 and 1846, which are considered the most significant contribution to the genre. They range from serene and lyrical pieces to dramatic works with turbulent middle sections, such as the Nocturne in C minor, Op. 48 No. 1. Perhaps the most famous is the Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9 No. 2; the posthumously published Nocturne in C-sharp minor has also become widely known through film and popular culture.

Later composers

Later composers of piano nocturnes include Fauré (13 nocturnes), Scriabin, Satie, and Poulenc. The form was also adapted for orchestra, as in Fauré's and Debussy's works — Debussy's three orchestral Nocturnes (1899) were inspired by Whistler's paintings of the same name. In visual art, James McNeill Whistler titled many of his night paintings "nocturnes".
