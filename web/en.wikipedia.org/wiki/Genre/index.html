Genre

EtymologyFrench genre, "kind, sort", from Latin genus
Applies toLiterature, film, music, art, games
ExamplesTragedy, comedy, science fiction, western, hip hop

Genre (from French genre, meaning "kind" or "sort") is any style or form of communication in any medium — literature, film, music, television, or other art forms — with a developed set of conventions. Genres are formed by sets of conventions that change over time as new genres are invented and old ones fall out of use; works frequently cross genre boundaries, borrowing and modifying the conventions of the genres they inhabit.

History

The concept descends from ancient Greek classification of literature: Plato divided poetry into the dithyramb, lyric and epic, and Aristotle's Poetics defined the genres of tragedy and comedy, which remained foundational to literary theory into the Renaissance. Modern genre systems expanded with print culture and the entertainment industries; film genres such as the western, film noir and the musical, and popular music genres such as rock, jazz and hip hop, are largely products of the twentieth century.

Function and theory

Genres organise audiences' expectations and guide marketing, shelving and recommendation. Genre theorists debate whether genres are fixed taxonomies or fluid agreements between creators, industries and audiences. Subgenres refine categories (for example, cyberpunk within science fiction), and hybrid works combine conventions from several genres at once.
