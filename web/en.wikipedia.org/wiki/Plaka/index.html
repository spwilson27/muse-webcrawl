Plaka

A street in Plaka, Athens

Plaka (Greek: Πλάκα) is the old historical neighbourhood of Athens, Greece, clustered around the northern and eastern slopes of the Acropolis. It incorporates the labyrinthine streets and neoclassical architecture for which it is sometimes called the "Neighbourhood of the Gods".

History

Plaka was developed mostly around the ruins of the Ancient Agora of Athens, in an area continuously inhabited since antiquity. During the years of Ottoman rule it was the Turkish quarter of the city. After the Greek War of Independence and the establishment of Athens as the capital of the new Greek state in 1834, the district retained its residential character, built largely in the neoclassical style of the 19th century.

The name "Plaka" was in common use by the end of the 19th century; its origin is uncertain, and several derivations have been proposed, including one from a large flat slab (plaka) associated with the church of Saint George of the Rock.

Today

Plaka is visited by hundreds of thousands of tourists every year, drawn by its pedestrian streets, tavernas, cafés, souvenir shops, and museums, including the Museum of Greek Folk Art and the Jewish Museum of Greece. The district of Anafiotika, built on the slopes of the Acropolis by islanders from Anafi in the 19th century, preserves the whitewashed character of a Cycladic village within the city.

Strict zoning regulations introduced in the 1980s helped preserve the neighbourhood's fabric, converting many buildings to museums, restaurants, and shops while banning some of the noisier nightlife uses.
