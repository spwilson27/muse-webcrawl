Uranium-235 (235U or U-235) is an isotope of uranium making up about 0.72% of natural uranium. Unlike the predominant isotope uranium-238, it is fissile: it can support a nuclear chain reaction, splitting when struck by a slow (thermal) neutron and releasing energy together with two or three further neutrons. It is the principal fuel of nuclear reactors and the fissile material of the first atomic bomb used in warfare.

Properties and decay

U-235 has a half-life of about 704 million years and decays by alpha emission into thorium-231, heading the actinium decay series. Each fission releases about 202.5 MeV of energy, mostly as the kinetic energy of the fission fragments. A sphere of pure U-235 has a critical mass of roughly 52 kg.

Enrichment and uses

Because natural uranium contains too little U-235 for most reactors and for weapons, its proportion is increased by uranium enrichment — today chiefly by gas centrifuges. Low-enriched uranium (3–5% U-235) fuels most power reactors; highly enriched uranium (above 20%, typically about 90%) is used in weapons and some research and naval reactors. U-235 powered the "Little Boy" gun-type bomb dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945; its fission was also demonstrated in the Chicago Pile-1 reactor of 1942.

History

The isotope was identified in 1935 by Arthur Jeffrey Dempster. Following the discovery of nuclear fission in 1938, U-235 was recognised as the isotope responsible for slow-neutron fission, which the Manhattan Project exploited through enormous enrichment plants at Oak Ridge.
