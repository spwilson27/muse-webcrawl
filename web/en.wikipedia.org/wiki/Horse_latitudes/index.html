Horse latitudes

The horse latitudes are the latitudes about 30 to 35 degrees north and south of the equator. The region, also known as the subtropical highs or subtropical ridges, is characterized by high atmospheric pressure, calm or light variable winds, and predominantly sunny, dry weather. The world's great deserts, such as the Sahara, Arabian, Australian, and Kalahari deserts, lie largely under these belts.

Meteorology

The horse latitudes lie beneath the descending branches of the Hadley cells: air that rose in the equatorial doldrums (the Intertropical Convergence Zone) travels poleward aloft and sinks around 30° latitude, warming and drying as it descends. The resulting subtropical high-pressure systems, such as the Azores High, dominate the weather of the region. The belts shift seasonally, moving poleward in summer and toward the equator in winter.

The name

The origin of the name is uncertain. A popular explanation holds that sailing ships carrying horses from Europe to the Americas or the West Indies were sometimes becalmed in these latitudes for so long that water ran short and horses died or were thrown overboard. Another proposed derivation connects the name with the "dead horse" ceremony held by sailors, or with a corruption of Spanish terms for the Gulf winds. The Oxford English Dictionary records the term in use by the late 18th century while treating its etymology as unknown.
