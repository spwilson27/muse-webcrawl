Alchemy

Alchemy (from Arabic al-kīmiyā) is an ancient branch of natural philosophy, a philosophical and protoscientific tradition practised throughout Europe, Africa, China, and throughout Asia. Alchemists sought the transmutation of base metals into gold, the discovery of a universal solvent, and the creation of the philosopher's stone — believed to grant immortality and convert lead into gold.

History

Western alchemy emerged in Greco-Roman Egypt, in figures such as Zosimos of Panopolis (c. AD 300), and developed substantially in the medieval Islamic world, where Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) and al-Razi systematised experiment, laboratory apparatus, and chemical processes including distillation. Translated into Latin from the 12th century, alchemy flourished in medieval and early modern Europe; Paracelsus redirected it toward medicine, founding iatrochemistry.

Though often dismissed as pseudoscience, alchemy developed laboratory techniques — distillation, crystallisation, sublimation — and discovered substances including mineral acids, alcohol, and phosphorus. Isaac Newton wrote extensively on alchemy. During the 17th and 18th centuries, the experimental legacy of alchemy fed into modern chemistry, while its chrysopoeia (gold-making) claims were progressively abandoned; Robert Boyle and Antoine Lavoisier are key figures in the transition.

Goals and symbolism

Beyond the philosopher's stone, alchemists pursued the elixir of life and the alkahest. Their writings are famously allegorical, using symbols such as the ouroboros, the hermaphrodite, and the union of sulfur and mercury. Chinese waidan and neidan and Indian rasayana traditions pursued longevity through elixirs and internal practices.

Legacy

Alchemy influenced art, literature, and psychology — Carl Jung interpreted alchemical imagery as a map of individuation. Nuclear physics has since achieved genuine transmutation of elements, though at impractical cost.
