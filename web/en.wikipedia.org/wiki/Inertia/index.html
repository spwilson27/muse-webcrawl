Inertia

Inertia is the tendency of objects in motion to stay in motion, and objects at rest to stay at rest, unless a force causes their speed or direction to change. It is one of the fundamental principles of classical mechanics, described by Isaac Newton in his first law of motion in the Principia (1687): every body perseveres in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by forces impressed on it.

History

The concept developed from medieval theories of impetus through the work of Galileo Galilei, who recognised that a body moving on a frictionless horizontal plane would continue indefinitely, and René Descartes, who stated laws of nature based on the conservation of motion. Newton built on these ideas, and the notion was refined further in Einstein's theories, where inertial motion follows the geometry of spacetime.

Mass and rotational inertia

The mass of an object is a measure of its inertia: the greater the mass, the greater the force needed to change its motion. For rotating bodies, the corresponding quantity is the moment of inertia, which depends on how mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation.
