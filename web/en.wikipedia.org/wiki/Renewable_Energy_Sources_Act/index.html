Renewable Energy Sources Act

The Renewable Energy Sources Act (German: Erneuerbare-Energien-Gesetz, EEG) is a German law that was originally adopted in 2000 to encourage the generation of renewable electricity. It is regarded as the central policy instrument of the Energiewende, Germany's transition to a low-carbon energy supply. The EEG introduced a system of feed-in tariffs: guaranteed payments to renewable electricity generators for a fixed period, with priority grid connection and purchase obligations for network operators.

History

The EEG 2000 built on the earlier Electricity Feed-in Act of 1991. Major revisions followed in 2004, 2009, 2012, 2014, 2017, 2021, and 2023. The EEG 2014 and EEG 2017 moved the system toward competitive auctions for larger installations and introduced market premiums. The EEG 2023 set a target of at least 80% of electricity consumption from renewables by 2030.

Effects

Under the EEG, the share of renewable electricity in Germany rose from about 6% in 2000 to over 40% by the early 2020s. Costs were funded mainly through an EEG surcharge on electricity consumers until mid-2022, when the surcharge was abolished and funding shifted to the federal budget and carbon pricing revenues.
