Tripartite System

The Tripartite System was the structure of state secondary education in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland from the Education Act 1944 (the "Butler Act") until the introduction of comprehensive schools from the 1960s. At age eleven, children took the eleven-plus examination and were allocated to one of three types of school, intended to suit different aptitudes:

  • Grammar schools for an academic curriculum leading to examinations and university;
  • Secondary technical schools for science, engineering, and commerce;
  • Secondary modern schools for a more general, practical education to the leaving age.

Operation and criticism

In practice technical schools were few — only a small percentage of pupils attended them — so the system worked mainly as a bipartite division between grammar and secondary modern schools. The system was criticised for entrenching class divisions, for the unreliability of selection at eleven, and for poorer funding and status of secondary moderns, despite the official claim of "parity of esteem". Labour governments from 1965 pressed local authorities to adopt comprehensive education, and most of England and Wales did so in the 1970s, though selection by eleven-plus survives in areas such as Kent and Buckinghamshire, and Northern Ireland retains a largely selective system.
