Wiggers diagram

A Wiggers diagram is a standard diagram used in cardiovascular physiology, named after the American physiologist Carl J. Wiggers (1883–1963). It plots several measurements of the cardiac cycle against a common time axis (usually with the electrocardiogram as the reference), so that the mechanical and electrical events of a single heartbeat can be related to one another.

Contents of the diagram

Aligned vertically against time on the x-axis, a typical Wiggers diagram shows:

  • Pressures — aortic pressure, left ventricular pressure and left atrial pressure (in mmHg), often with the ventricular pressure curve expanded in a separate inset so its small changes can be seen beside the much larger aortic swings.
  • Ventricular volume — showing filling during diastole and ejection during systole, with end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes.
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG) — usually lead II, marking the P wave (atrial depolarisation), QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation) and T wave (ventricular repolarisation).
  • Phonocardiogram — the heart sounds S1 and S2 (and sometimes S3 and S4), recorded from the chest wall.
  • Sometimes also aortic blood flow and valve events, marking the opening and closing of the mitral and aortic valves.

Events shown

The diagram divides the cycle into systole and diastole. Atrial contraction follows the P wave; ventricular contraction begins after the QRS complex, closing the mitral valve (producing S1) and starting isovolumetric contraction. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens and ejection occurs. As the ventricle relaxes, pressure falls below aortic pressure, the aortic valve closes — producing the dicrotic notch in the aortic trace and the second heart sound S2 — and isovolumetric relaxation follows until the mitral valve opens and filling resumes. The atrial trace shows its characteristic a, c and v waves.

Use

The Wiggers diagram remains a staple of medical and physiology teaching because it integrates electrocardiography, pressure physiology and valve mechanics in one picture. Variants are used to teach the effects of valve lesions such as aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation, in which the pressure and sound traces change in predictable ways.
