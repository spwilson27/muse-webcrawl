Curie–Weiss law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Curie–Weiss law
DescribesMagnetic susceptibility of a ferromagnet above its Curie temperature
Named afterPierre Curie, Pierre-Ernest Weiss
Key parameterWeiss constant θ (Curie–Weiss temperature)

The Curie–Weiss law describes the magnetic susceptibility χ of a ferromagnet in the paramagnetic region above the Curie temperature:

χ = C / (T − θ)

where C is the material-specific Curie constant, T is the absolute temperature, and θ is the Weiss constant (Curie–Weiss temperature). For many materials θ is close to the Curie temperature TC, though it may differ somewhat because of the details of the interactions between magnetic moments.

Derivation

Pierre-Ernest Weiss proposed in 1907 that neighboring magnetic moments in a ferromagnet interact through an internal "molecular field" proportional to the magnetization. Adding this mean field to Pierre Curie's simpler law (χ = C/T) shifts the temperature at which susceptibility diverges from zero to θ, producing the Curie–Weiss form. A plot of 1/χ against T is a straight line with slope 1/C, and its intercept on the temperature axis gives θ.

Behavior and exceptions

A positive θ indicates ferromagnetic interactions; a negative θ points to antiferromagnetic correlations, as in ferrimagnets and antiferromagnets measured above their ordering temperature. The law fails close to the critical temperature, where fluctuations become important and mean-field theory breaks down; the susceptibility then follows a power law with a critical exponent γ ≈ 1.3 rather than the mean-field value 1.
