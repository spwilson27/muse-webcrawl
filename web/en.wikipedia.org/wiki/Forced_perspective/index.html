Forced perspective

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forced perspective is a technique that uses optical illusion to make an object appear farther away, closer, larger or smaller than it actually is. By carefully controlling scale, position and the viewer's single viewpoint, artists, architects, photographers and filmmakers create convincing false relationships of size and distance.

In architecture and art

Renaissance artists and architects used forced perspective deliberately: Borromini's colonnade in the Palazzo Spada in Rome appears about 37 metres long but is only about 9 metres, achieved with shrinking columns and a sloping floor; the false choir of Bramante's Santa Maria presso San Satiro compresses a painted and modelled apse into less than a metre of depth. Stage sets have used the same principles for centuries.

In photography and tourism

Playful photographs — a tourist "holding up" the Leaning Tower of Pisa, "pinching" the sun, or holding a distant building in the palm — rely on aligning a near object with a far one from a single camera position. The illusion collapses if the viewer moves or uses both eyes at close range.

In filmmaking

Before digital compositing, forced perspective was a staple of special effects: actors placed at different distances from the camera on scaled sets appear as giants or diminutive creatures in the same shot. It was used extensively in The Lord of the Rings films to make hobbits appear smaller than Gandalf in shared scenes, with moving camera rigs ("moving forced perspective") synchronised so the alignment survived camera motion. Theme parks use the technique too — Disney's main streets and castles use progressively smaller upper storeys to appear grander.
