An AMBER Alert (also rendered Amber Alert) is a child abduction emergency alert issued by law enforcement in many countries, most prominently the United States, to solicit the public's help in finding abducted children. The system originated in Texas in 1996 after the abduction and murder of nine-year-old Amber Hagerman of Arlington, Texas; the name was later given the backronym "America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response".

How it works

Alerts are issued by state or local police when a case meets criteria such as: a confirmed abduction of a child aged 17 or younger, a risk of serious bodily harm or death, and enough descriptive information about the child, abductor or vehicle. Alerts interrupt radio and television broadcasts via the Emergency Alert System, appear on electronic highway signs, and since 2013 are pushed to mobile phones as Wireless Emergency Alerts.

Effectiveness and criticism

The U.S. Department of Justice reports that hundreds of children have been recovered as a direct result of AMBER Alerts — the programme credits over 1,200 recoveries cumulatively. Critics note that most alerts involve family abductions or misunderstandings rather than stereotypical stranger kidnappings, that a large share of recoveries would have occurred anyway, and that overuse risks public desensitisation. Similar systems have been adopted in Canada, Mexico, Australia and several European countries (as "child alert" systems).
