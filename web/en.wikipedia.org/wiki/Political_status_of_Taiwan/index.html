Political status of Taiwan

The political status of Taiwan is a long-running dispute over whether Taiwan and its surrounding islands are part of China, and under which government. Taiwan is governed by the Republic of China (ROC), which retreated to the island in 1949 after losing the Chinese Civil War to the Communists who founded the People's Republic of China (PRC).

Background

Qing-dynasty China ceded Taiwan to Japan in the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki. After Japan's defeat in 1945 the island was placed under ROC administration. In 1949 the ROC government relocated to Taipei while the PRC was proclaimed in Beijing; both governments initially claimed to be the sole legitimate government of all China.

The dispute

The PRC regards Taiwan as a province to be "reunified", by force if necessary, and insists on its One China principle. The ROC has democratized since the late 1980s; its position has evolved from claiming the mainland to emphasizing that Taiwan is already independent under the ROC name. Most states maintain formal relations with Beijing while keeping unofficial ties with Taipei. UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 (1971) transferred China's UN seat from the ROC to the PRC; Taiwan has been excluded from the UN system since.

Views within Taiwan

Opinion in Taiwan ranges from support for eventual unification to support for formal independence, with polls consistently showing the largest share favouring some version of the status quo. The term "status quo" itself is defined differently by the parties involved. Cross-strait relations remain a central issue in regional security, with the United States practising a policy of "strategic ambiguity" under the Taiwan Relations Act.
