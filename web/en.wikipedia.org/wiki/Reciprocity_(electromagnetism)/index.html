Reciprocity (electromagnetism)

In classical electromagnetism, reciprocity refers to a family of theorems relating the sources and the resulting fields in linear, time-invariant media. The best known is the Lorentz reciprocity theorem: for two sets of sources (J1, M1) and (J2, M2) oscillating at the same frequency in the same environment, the fields they produce satisfy a symmetric relation — loosely, the reaction of source 1 on the field of source 2 equals the reaction of source 2 on the field of source 1.

An antenna's transmitting and receiving properties are linked by reciprocity.

Lorentz reciprocity theorem

Derived from Maxwell's equations, the theorem in its integral form states that, under appropriate conditions,

∫ (E1 × H2 − E2 × H1) · dS = ∫ (E2 · J1 − E1 · J2) dV

(with corresponding terms for magnetic currents). For sources in a region bounded at infinity, the surface term vanishes, giving the familiar reaction equality ⟨1,2⟩ = ⟨2,1⟩. The theorem requires the media to be linear with symmetric permittivity and permeability tensors; gyrotropic or magnetised materials (such as ferrites in a static magnetic field) violate reciprocity, which is exploited in isolators and circulators.

Applications

Reciprocity underlies antenna theory: a good transmitting antenna is a good receiving antenna, with identical radiation patterns and impedance in both roles. It is also used in circuit theory (the reciprocity theorem for networks), acoustics, seismology, and scattering analysis, and its breakdown defines the field of nonreciprocal devices.

Related results

Other reciprocity statements include the Rayleigh–Carson reciprocity theorem of electrical network theory and Green's reciprocity in electrostatics. Analogous theorems exist in elastodynamics and in quantum scattering theory, the latter connected with time-reversal symmetry.
