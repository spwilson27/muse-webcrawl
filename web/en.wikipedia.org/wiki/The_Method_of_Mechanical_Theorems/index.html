The Method of Mechanical Theorems (Greek: Περὶ μηχανικῶν θεωρημάτων πρὸς Ἐρατοσθένη ἔφοδος), also called The Method, is one of the major surviving works of the ancient Greek mathematician and inventor Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287 – c. 212 BC). Written in the form of a letter to his colleague Eratosthenes of Cyrene, the head of the Library of Alexandria, it describes a heuristic technique — the "method" of the title — by which Archimedes discovered many of his most famous results about areas and volumes.

The mechanical method

Archimedes explains that he first arrived at his theorems by a mechanical argument: he imagined geometric figures as if they were physical objects with weight, sliced into infinitely many infinitely thin strips, and balanced on a lever against figures whose areas or volumes he already knew. Using the law of the lever — a result he had himself proved in On the Equilibrium of Planes — he could compute the unknown quantity from the balance condition. Because the argument reasons about indivisible slices, Archimedes regarded it as a method of discovery rather than a rigorous proof; the results so found were afterward to be established by the rigorous Greek technique of exhaustion. The work is nonetheless striking to modern readers for its close anticipation of integral calculus, nearly two thousand years before Newton and Leibniz.

Contents

The treatise states fourteen propositions. Among the results obtained by the method are:

  • The area of a parabolic segment is 4/3 the area of the inscribed triangle with the same base and height — the result also proved rigorously in Quadrature of the Parabola.
  • The volume of a sphere is four times the volume of the cone with base equal to a great circle of the sphere and height equal to its radius, whence the volume of a sphere is two-thirds that of the circumscribing cylinder — the discovery Archimedes prized most, commemorated, according to Cicero, by a sphere and cylinder on his tomb.
  • The centre of gravity of a hemisphere, of a segment of a sphere, and of a paraboloid of revolution.

Survival and rediscovery

The Method was thought lost for centuries. It survives in a single manuscript, the Archimedes Palimpsest: a 10th-century Byzantine copy of Archimedes' works whose parchment was scraped and overwritten in the 13th century with a prayer book. The Danish philologist Johan Ludvig Heiberg identified the underwriting in Constantinople in 1906 and recognised The Method among the texts, transforming modern understanding of Archimedes' thought. The palimpsest resurfaced at auction in 1998 and was subsequently studied at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore, where multispectral imaging recovered much of the erased text, including parts of The Method unreadable to Heiberg.
