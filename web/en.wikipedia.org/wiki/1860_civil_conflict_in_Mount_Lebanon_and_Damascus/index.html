1860 civil conflict in Mount Lebanon and Damascus

The 1860 civil conflict in Mount Lebanon and Damascus, also known as the 1860 Syrian civil war, was a conflict between Maronite Christians and Druze in Ottoman Syria, culminating in massacres of Christians in Mount Lebanon and Damascus in the summer of 1860. Thousands of Christians were killed, and the violence prompted a major European intervention and a reorganisation of Ottoman government in Lebanon whose confessional arrangements echo into the present day.

Background

Mount Lebanon was home to intertwined Maronite and Druze communities under Ottoman rule. Tensions grew after the Egyptian occupation of the 1830s armed Christian peasants, and the subsequent "double Qaimaqamate" administration divided the mountain into Maronite and Druze districts. In 1858 Maronite peasants in Kisrawan rose against their own feudal lords under Tanyus Shahin, raising sectarian fears among the Druze. Fighting broke out in May 1860 and spread across the mixed districts, with Maronite towns such as Deir al-Qamar, Hasbaya and Zahle attacked and large numbers of Christians killed.

Damascus massacres

In July 1860 violence spread to Damascus, where mobs attacked the Christian quarter over several days. Estimates of the dead in the city run into the thousands. The Algerian exile Emir Abd al-Qadir famously sheltered and protected many Christians in Damascus, an act celebrated internationally. The Ottoman government, under pressure from the European powers, prosecuted and executed a number of officials and rioters held responsible.

Aftermath

France landed an expeditionary force in 1860–61, the first major European humanitarian intervention of its kind. In 1861 the powers and the Porte agreed the Règlement Organique, which created the Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate, an autonomous district governed by a Christian Ottoman governor with a sectarian administrative council — a system that endured until the First World War and laid foundations for Lebanon's confessional political order.
