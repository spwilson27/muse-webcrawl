Roman censor

The censor was a magistrate in ancient Rome who was responsible for maintaining the census, supervising public morality, and overseeing certain aspects of the government's finances. The office, traditionally established in 443 BC, was regarded as the culmination of a distinguished political career; only former consuls were usually elected to it.

Duties

Two censors were elected at a time, originally for a term connected with the census cycle of five years (the lustrum), though their active tenure was later limited to 18 months. The censors kept the rolls of citizens and their property, assigned citizens to voting tribes and centuries, supervised the membership of the Senate — removing those judged unworthy by a mark of disgrace, the nota censoria — and let out public contracts, including tax farming and public works.

History

The most famous censor was Cato the Elder, whose strict supervision of public morals in 184 BC made "censorious" conduct proverbial. Under the Empire the censorship declined as emperors assumed its powers themselves; Domitian made himself censor for life, and the office fell into abeyance.
