Fitness (biology)

Fitness (often denoted w in population genetics models) is the quantitative representation of an individual's reproductive success. It is equal to the average contribution to the gene pool of the next generation that is made by individuals of a specified genotype or phenotype. Fitness measures how well an organism passes on its genes; it is a central concept in evolutionary biology and in the modern formulation of natural selection.

Definition

The fitness of a genotype may be modelled as the probability of survival to reproductive age multiplied by the average fecundity of survivors. Because fitness is a rate-like quantity, it is often expressed in relative terms: in relative fitness, the fittest genotype in a population is assigned the value 1 and all others are expressed as fractions of it. The complement of relative fitness is the selection coefficient, written s, so that w = 1 − s.

"Survival of the fittest" — the phrase coined by Herbert Spencer and adopted by Darwin — refers not to physical strength but to this reproductive measure: organisms "fit" to their environment leave more descendants.

Components of fitness

Fitness is usually decomposed into components such as viability (survival to reproductive age), mating success, fecundity, and fertility. These components may trade off against one another: an organism that invests heavily in early reproduction may have a shorter lifespan, for example. Because selection acts on the whole life cycle, lifetime reproductive success is the usual empirical proxy for fitness.

Inclusive fitness

Inclusive fitness, developed by W. D. Hamilton in 1964, extends the concept to include the copies of an individual's genes present in relatives. An allele that causes an organism to help kin reproduce can spread even at a cost to its own reproduction, provided the benefit to relatives, discounted by their relatedness, exceeds the cost — a condition known as Hamilton's rule. Inclusive fitness provides the standard explanation for the evolution of altruism and eusociality.

Measurement and complications

Fitness is environment-dependent: a trait that is advantageous in one habitat may be neutral or harmful in another. It can also be frequency-dependent, when the success of a strategy depends on how common it is in the population. In finite populations, genetic drift may overwhelm weak selection, so higher fitness does not guarantee that a genotype will increase in frequency.
