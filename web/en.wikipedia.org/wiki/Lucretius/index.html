Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99 – c. 55 BCE) was a Roman poet and philosopher, known for his didactic poem De rerum natura ("On the Nature of Things"), the fullest surviving exposition of Epicureanism. Almost nothing is known of his life; Jerome's chronicle reports, probably unreliably, that he was driven mad by a love potion and wrote in lucid intervals before taking his own life.

De rerum natura

The poem, in six books of Latin hexameters addressed to the politician Memmius, explains Epicurean physics and ethics: that everything is atoms and void; that atoms occasionally swerve (the clinamen), breaking deterministic chains and allowing free will; that the soul is material and mortal; that the gods do not intervene; and that death is nothing to fear. It opens with a famous invocation to Venus and includes celebrated passages on the plague of Athens, with which the unfinished poem ends.

Influence

The poem narrowly survived: rediscovered in a German monastery by Poggio Bracciolini in 1417, it became a conduit for atomism into Renaissance and early modern thought, influencing Gassendi, Newton-era corpuscular philosophy and later scientific materialism — a transmission popularised in Stephen Greenblatt's The Swerve (2011).
