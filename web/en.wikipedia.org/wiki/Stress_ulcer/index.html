Stress ulcer

A stress ulcer is a mucosal defect of the stomach or duodenum (stress-related mucosal damage) that occurs in the setting of severe physiological stress, such as major surgery, trauma, burns, sepsis, or critical illness. Unlike ordinary peptic ulcers, stress ulcers are usually multiple, superficial, and located in the fundus and body of the stomach.

Pathophysiology

Critical illness reduces blood flow to the gastric mucosa (splanchnic hypoperfusion), impairing the mucus–bicarbonate barrier and prostaglandin synthesis, so that normal amounts of acid and pepsin injure the mucosa. Named variants include Curling's ulcers, associated with severe burns, and Cushing's ulcers, associated with head injury and raised intracranial pressure, which increase acid secretion via vagal stimulation.

Clinical significance and prevention

The main danger is gastrointestinal bleeding, which increases mortality in intensive care patients. Risk factors for clinically important bleeding include mechanical ventilation for more than 48 hours and coagulopathy. Stress ulcer prophylaxis with proton pump inhibitors or H2-receptor antagonists is given to high-risk ICU patients, although the benefits are balanced against risks such as pneumonia and Clostridioides difficile infection.
