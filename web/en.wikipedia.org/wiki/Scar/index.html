Scar


Illustration placeholder: a mature scar, lighter and smoother than surrounding skin

A scar (or cicatrix) is an area of fibrous tissue that replaces normal skin after an injury. Scars result from the biological process of wound repair in the skin, as well as in other organs and tissues of the body. Thus, scarring is a natural part of the healing process. With the exception of very minor lesions, every wound (e.g., after accident, disease, or surgery) results in some degree of scarring. An exception is an animal with complete regeneration, such as a salamander, which regrows tissue including skin without scarring.

Mechanism

Scar tissue is composed of the same protein (collagen) as the tissue it replaces, but the fibre composition differs: instead of the random basketweave formation of normal skin, collagen in scars is aligned in a single direction, producing tissue that is typically of inferior functional quality. Scarred skin is less resistant to ultraviolet radiation, and sweat glands and hair follicles do not grow back. Formation proceeds through the phases of wound healing — hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation (granulation tissue and myofibroblast-driven contraction), and remodelling — over weeks to more than a year.

Types

  • Hypertrophic scars — raised, red scars confined to the original wound margins, common after burns and surgery; they often regress partly over time.
  • Keloid scars — overgrowths of scar tissue that extend beyond the original wound, more common in darker skin types; they rarely regress and may recur after excision.
  • Atrophic scars — sunken scars left by acne or chickenpox, where collagen loss pulls the surface below the surrounding skin.
  • Stretch marks (striae) — dermal scars of rapid stretching in pregnancy, growth, or weight change.
  • Contractures — scars, especially across joints after burns, that tighten and restrict movement.

Treatment

Most scars are cosmetic only, but raised and contracted scars may itch, hurt, or limit motion. Treatments include silicone gel sheeting, pressure garments, intralesional corticosteroid injections, laser therapy, cryotherapy, dermabrasion, and surgical revision; prevention emphasises wound care, tension-free closure, and sun protection of healing wounds. Complete scar removal is not possible, though many scars fade substantially over one to two years.
