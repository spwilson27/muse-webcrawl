Christian mysticism

Christian mysticism refers to the pursuit of direct, experiential awareness of the presence of God within Christianity, and to the texts and practices that prepare for, describe and result from such experience. Mystical theology in the tradition distinguishes a threefold path: purgation, illumination and union (the via triplex).

History

Roots lie in the New Testament (Paul's vision in 2 Corinthians 12; the Johannine writings) and early monasticism. Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500) founded apophatic ("negative") theology, while Eastern Orthodoxy developed the hesychast tradition of the Jesus Prayer and the doctrine of theosis (deification), defended by Gregory Palamas. In the Latin West, figures such as Bernard of Clairvaux, Hildegard of Bingen, Meister Eckhart, Julian of Norwich, Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross shaped a rich literature of contemplative prayer; John of the Cross's "dark night of the soul" became a classic description of spiritual purgation.

Practices and themes

Practices include contemplative prayer, lectio divina, meditation on Scripture, fasting and silence. Common themes are divine darkness and unknowing (as in the anonymous The Cloud of Unknowing), the soul's bridal union with Christ, and the distinction between created grace and uncreated divine presence — debated between Eastern and Western theology.

Modern engagement

Interest revived in the 20th century through writers such as Evelyn Underhill, Thomas Merton and Karl Rahner, and through the centering prayer movement. Mysticism is studied alongside psychology of religion, with scholars debating whether mystical experiences share a common core across traditions.
