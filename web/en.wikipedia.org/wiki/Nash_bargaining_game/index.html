Nash bargaining game

Nash bargaining game
Field: Game theory, economics
Introduced by: John Forbes Nash Jr. (1950)
Key concept: Nash bargaining solution maximizing the product of players' gains over the disagreement point

The Nash bargaining game is a simple two-player game in cooperative game theory used to model bargaining interactions. Two players demand a portion of some good (often money). If the total amount demanded is less than or equal to the available amount, both players receive their demand; if the total exceeds the amount available, neither receives anything. John Forbes Nash Jr. studied the problem in his 1950 paper "The Bargaining Problem."

The bargaining problem

Formally, a two-person bargaining problem consists of a feasible set F of pairs of utilities (u₁, u₂) that the players can achieve by agreement, and a disagreement point d = (d₁, d₂) representing the utilities each receives if negotiations break down. A bargaining solution is a rule that selects a single outcome from F for every such problem.

The Nash bargaining solution

Nash proposed four axioms that a reasonable solution should satisfy: invariance to equivalent utility representations, Pareto optimality, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and symmetry. He proved that exactly one solution satisfies all four: the outcome that maximizes the product (u₁ − d₁)(u₂ − d₂) of the players' gains over disagreement. This is known as the Nash bargaining solution.

Extensions and applications

Asymmetric versions drop the symmetry axiom and weight the players' gains according to their relative bargaining power. The solution has been applied to wage negotiations, international trade, the division of assets in divorce, and climate agreements. Non-cooperative foundations were later developed through alternating-offer models, notably by Ariel Rubinstein, in which the Nash outcome emerges as the unique subgame perfect equilibrium as the players' time discounting vanishes.
