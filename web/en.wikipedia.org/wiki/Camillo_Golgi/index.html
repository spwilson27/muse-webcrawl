Camillo Golgi

Camillo Golgi
Born: 7 July 1843, Corteno, Italy
Died: 21 January 1926, Pavia, Italy
Known for: Golgi apparatus, black reaction stain
Award: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1906)

Camillo Golgi (1843–1926) was an Italian biologist and pathologist at the University of Pavia, celebrated for pioneering methods to stain nervous tissue and for discovering the cellular structure now called the Golgi apparatus. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Santiago Ramón y Cajal — ironically, his great scientific rival.

The black reaction

In 1873, working in a small kitchen laboratory, Golgi developed the reazione nera ("black reaction"), a silver chromate stain that impregnates a small, random fraction of neurons in their entirety, making the full branching of individual nerve cells visible against a translucent background for the first time. The method revolutionised neuroanatomy and is still used.

Neuron doctrine debate and later work

Golgi used his stain to argue for the reticular theory — that the nervous system is a continuous network — while Cajal's work with the same stain supported the neuron doctrine of discrete cells. In 1898 Golgi identified the intracellular apparatus (the Golgi apparatus) in nerve cells. He also described tendon sensory organs (Golgi tendon organs) and studied the malaria parasite's life cycle, distinguishing the tertian and quartan fevers.
