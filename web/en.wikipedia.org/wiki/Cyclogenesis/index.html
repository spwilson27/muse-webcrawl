Cyclogenesis

Cyclogenesis is the development or strengthening of cyclonic circulation in the atmosphere — the process by which cyclones form and intensify. It is the opposite of cyclolysis, the weakening of a cyclone. The term applies to several distinct processes at different scales, from extratropical cyclones spanning thousands of kilometres down to mesoscale systems.

Extratropical cyclogenesis

Mid-latitude (extratropical) cyclones typically form along frontal boundaries where contrasting air masses meet, as described by the Norwegian cyclone model developed by the Bergen School after the First World War. A wave on the front deepens as warm air advances poleward on its eastern side (warm front) and cold air pushes equatorward behind it (cold front). Upper-level support — divergence ahead of a trough, jet stream dynamics, and vorticity advection — allows surface pressure to fall and the system to mature, eventually occluding as the cold front overtakes the warm front.

Explosive cyclogenesis

When central pressure falls by at least 24 hectopascals in 24 hours (adjusted for latitude), the process is called explosive cyclogenesis, and the resulting storm is popularly called a "bomb cyclone". Such systems commonly develop over warm ocean currents such as the Gulf Stream and Kuroshio in winter.

Tropical cyclogenesis

The formation of tropical cyclones requires warm sea surface temperatures (generally above 26.5 °C), moist air, low vertical wind shear, sufficient Coriolis force, and a pre-existing disturbance. Only a small fraction of tropical disturbances develop into named storms. Agencies such as the U.S. National Hurricane Center monitor development and issue designations such as "Invest" for systems under investigation.

Other forms

Lee cyclogenesis occurs downwind of mountain ranges, as in the Colorado lows east of the Rockies and Genoa lows south of the Alps. Mesoscale cyclogenesis can produce polar lows and mesoscale convective vortices.
