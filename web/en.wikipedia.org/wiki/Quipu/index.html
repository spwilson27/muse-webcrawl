Quipu

A quipu (Quechua: khipu, "knot"), sometimes called a "talking knot", was a recording device fashioned from strings used in the Andean civilizations of South America, above all the Inca Empire. A quipu usually consists of cotton or camelid-fibre cords in various colours, spun and plied, from which pendant strings hang, carrying knots of different types — single knots, long knots, and figure-eight knots — positioned to encode numbers in a base-ten positional system.

An Inca quipu

Use

Quipus were the empire's principal accounting tool. Trained record-keepers, the quipucamayoc, maintained them to track census figures, taxes paid in labour (mit'a), warehouse inventories, and military resources. Spanish chroniclers reported that quipus could also record other kinds of information, and modern research has suggested some may have encoded phonetic or other non-numerical data, though the extent of this remains debated.

More than a thousand quipus survive, many in museums. After the Spanish conquest, quipus were suppressed as idolatrous and their use declined, though knotted-cord records persisted in some Andean villages into the 20th century as pastoral records.
