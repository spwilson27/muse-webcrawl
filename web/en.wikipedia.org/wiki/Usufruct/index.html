Usufruct

Usufruct (from Latin usus et fructus, "use and fruit") is a legal right accorded to a person or party that confers the temporary right to use and derive income or benefit from someone else's property, provided that the property is not damaged or destroyed. The property owner is known as the naked owner or bare owner, and the holder of the usufruct is the usufructuary.

History

Usufruct has its origins in Roman law, where it was a type of personal servitude (servitus personae) giving the usufructuary the right to use another's property and take its fruits while preserving its substance (salva rerum substantia). It remains a central institution of civil law systems — including those of France, Louisiana, Quebec, and Scotland — but has no exact equivalent in common law, where similar arrangements are achieved through life estates and trusts.

Scope and obligations

The usufructuary holds two of the three classic rights of ownership: usus (the right to use the thing) and fructus (the right to take its fruits, such as crops, rents, or interest), but not abusus (the right to dispose of or destroy it). The usufructuary must maintain the property, pay ordinary charges and taxes on it, and return it intact at the end of the usufruct, which typically ends at the usufructuary's death or after a fixed term.

A related concept is the quasi-usufruct, which applies to consumable things such as money or grain: the usufructuary may consume them but must return an equivalent quantity or value.
