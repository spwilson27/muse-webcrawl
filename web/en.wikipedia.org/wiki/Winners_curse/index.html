Winner's curse

For the behavioural phenomenon in auctions generally, see Auction theory.

The winner's curse is a tendency in common-value auctions for the winning bid to exceed the item's intrinsic value. Because each bidder estimates the value with some error, the bidder with the most optimistic — and on average most overestimated — valuation tends to win. If bidders do not account for this selection effect, the winner systematically overpays and "wins" an item worth less than the price paid, making the victory a curse.

Explanation

Consider an oil lease whose true value is the same for all firms but unknown. Each firm estimates the value; estimates scatter around the truth. The highest estimate tends to be far above the true value, so naive bidding based on one's own estimate leads the winner to lose money on average. Rational bidders in the theory of auctions "shade" their bids downward to compensate, and the required adjustment grows with the number of bidders, since more bidders make the maximum estimate still more extreme.

Evidence

The phenomenon was first described in 1971 by petroleum engineers Capen, Clapp and Campbell, who observed disappointing returns on Gulf of Mexico oil leases. It has since been documented in laboratory experiments, corporate takeover bidding (where acquiring firms' shareholders often lose value), free-agent signings in professional sports, initial public offerings, and online auctions. In experimental settings, inexperienced bidders fall prey to the curse readily, while experienced bidders in repeated markets learn to shade their bids, though often incompletely.

Related concepts

The winner's curse is connected to the regression fallacy and to adverse selection. In private-value auctions, where each bidder's value is independent, the curse does not arise in the same way.
