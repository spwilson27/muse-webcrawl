Naga Panchami

Naga Panchami is a day of traditional worship of nāgas (serpent deities) or snakes observed by Hindus, Jains and Buddhists throughout India, Nepal and other countries where adherents of these religions live. The festival falls on the fifth day (panchami) of the bright half of the lunar month of Shravana (roughly July–August).

Worship

On this day devotees offer milk, sweets, flowers and lamps to images of serpents, to anthills and snake burrows believed to be their dwellings, and in some places to live cobras handled by snake charmers. Women pray for the well-being of their brothers and families, and in parts of India swings are set up and fairs held. Worshippers abstain from digging the earth on this day, out of respect for the snakes living within it.

Mythology

Several legends are associated with the festival. In the Mahabharata, the sage Astika halted King Janamejaya's great snake sacrifice (sarpa satra) undertaken to avenge his father Parikshit's death from a snakebite. Another tale links the day to Krishna's victory over the serpent Kaliya in the Yamuna River. Regional folk tales, such as that of a farmer whose plough accidentally killed young snakes and whose family was saved through a daughter's devotion, are recited as the festival's katha (story).

Regional observances

The festival is marked with special fervour in Maharashtra (notably at the village of Shirala, famous for its live-cobra processions), West Bengal (where the goddess Manasa is worshipped), Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat and Nepal. In South India, clay or silver serpent idols are worshipped at home and in temples.
