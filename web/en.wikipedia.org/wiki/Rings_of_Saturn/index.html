Rings of Saturn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Saturn with its broad bright rings seen at an angle, bands of cream and gold on the planet's globe]

The rings of Saturn are the most extensive and conspicuous ring system of any planet in the Solar System. They consist of countless small particles, almost all water ice with traces of rocky material, ranging from dust grains to chunks metres across, orbiting Saturn above its equator.

The main rings span from about 7,000 km to 80,000 km above the equator (the bright A, B and C rings), yet are astonishingly thin — locally perhaps only about ten metres thick in places. Gaps such as the Cassini Division are shaped by resonances with Saturn's moons.

Discovery and exploration

Galileo first saw something odd about Saturn in 1610 with his telescope; Christiaan Huygens correctly identified a ring in 1655. The Pioneer 11, Voyager and especially Cassini spacecraft (2004-2017) revealed the rings' fine structure of ringlets and waves.

Age

Whether the rings are as old as Saturn or far younger (perhaps ~100 million years, from a shattered icy moon or comet) is an active scientific debate informed by Cassini data.
