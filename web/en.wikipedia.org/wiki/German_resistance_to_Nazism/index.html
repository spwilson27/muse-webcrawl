German resistance to Nazism (German: Widerstand gegen den Nationalsozialismus) refers to the opposition of individuals and groups in Germany to the regime of Adolf Hitler between 1933 and 1945. The resistance was ideologically diverse and never a single unified movement: it ranged from communists and socialists driven underground in 1933, through church dissent and student protest, to conservative officers and officials who concluded that Hitler had to be removed by force.

Early opposition

The first resisters were the parties of the left. After the Reichstag Fire of February 1933 the Communist Party (KPD) was smashed and its activists imprisoned in the first concentration camps; the Social Democrats (SPD) voted against the Enabling Act and maintained exile and underground networks. Trade unionists, Jehovah's Witnesses (who refused the Hitler salute and conscription), and dissident clergy of the Confessing Church — such as Martin Niemöller and the theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer — also resisted on religious grounds. Georg Elser, a lone carpenter, came within minutes of killing Hitler with a bomb in Munich's Bürgerbräukeller on 8 November 1939.

Civilian and youth resistance

The White Rose group at Munich University, centred on the siblings Hans and Sophie Scholl and Professor Kurt Huber, distributed leaflets denouncing Nazi crimes and calling for passive resistance in 1942–43; its leading members were executed in February 1943. The loose intelligence and discussion networks the Gestapo called the "Red Orchestra" (Rote Kapelle) passed information to the Soviet Union. Working-class youth gangs such as the Edelweiss Pirates defied the Hitler Youth and aided deserters and escaped prisoners.

Military and conservative resistance

Within the armed forces and the Abwehr intelligence service, officers including Hans Oster and Admiral Wilhelm Canaris shielded conspirators and warned foreign governments of Hitler's plans. The civilian Kreisau Circle around Helmuth James Graf von Moltke met to plan a just post-Nazi Germany. These strands converged in the assassination attempts against Hitler, culminating in the 20 July 1944 plot: Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at the Wolf's Lair headquarters, and the conspirators launched Operation Valkyrie, a coup using the Reserve Army. The bomb failed to kill Hitler; the coup collapsed, and thousands of suspects were arrested — around 200 were executed, many after show trials before Roland Freisler's People's Court, and others, including Bonhoeffer and Canaris, were murdered in the regime's final weeks.

Legacy

In the early postwar years many Germans regarded the resisters as traitors, but the Federal Republic gradually embraced them as moral exemplars; the Bendlerblock in Berlin, where Stauffenberg and his fellow conspirators were shot, now houses the German Resistance Memorial Center, and 20 July is commemorated annually.
