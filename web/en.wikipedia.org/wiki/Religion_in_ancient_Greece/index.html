Religion in ancient Greece

Religion in ancient Greece encompasses the collection of beliefs, rituals, and mythology originating in Greece in the form of both popular public religion and cult practices. These groups varied enough for it to be possible to speak of Greek religions or "cults" in the plural, though most of them shared similarities. Many Greeks recognised the major gods and goddesses — Zeus, Poseidon, Hades, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Ares, Dionysus, Hephaestus, Athena, Hermes, Demeter, Persephone, and Hera — though philosophies such as Stoicism and some forms of Platonism used language that seems to assume a single transcendent deity.

Beliefs

Greek religion was polytheistic and had no central text or creed comparable to a scripture; instead, myth, epic poetry (above all Homer and Hesiod), and local cult practice transmitted religious knowledge. The gods were anthropomorphic and immortal, subject to fate and to their own passions, and governed domains of nature and human life.

Practices

Central practices included prayer, sacrifice (especially animal sacrifice at outdoor altars), votive offerings, libations, festivals, and processions. Temples housed cult statues but were not congregational places of worship. Oracles, most famously at Delphi and Dodona, mediated divine guidance, and mystery religions such as the Eleusinian Mysteries offered initiates hopes for the afterlife.

Sanctuaries and festivals

Panhellenic sanctuaries such as Olympia, Delphi, Nemea and Isthmia hosted athletic and artistic competitions. In Athens, festivals like the Panathenaea and the Dionysia structured the civic calendar and fused religious celebration with civic identity.

Decline

Greek religion persisted into the Roman period, increasingly intertwined with Roman practice, and declined as Christianity spread in the late Roman Empire; public pagan cult was suppressed in the 4th century CE under Christian emperors.
