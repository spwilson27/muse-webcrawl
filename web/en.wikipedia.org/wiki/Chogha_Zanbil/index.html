Overview

Chogha Zanbil is an ancient Elamite religious complex in Khuzestan Province, southwestern Iran, about 40 kilometres southeast of Susa. Founded around 1250 BCE by King Untash-Napirisha as the city of Dur Untash, it preserves the best-surviving ziggurat outside Mesopotamia and was among the first Iranian sites inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, in 1979.

Description

At its centre stands a five-storey ziggurat, originally about 52 metres high and dedicated to the god Inshushinak, built of mudbrick faced with baked brick, some bricks inscribed in Elamite and Akkadian cuneiform. Three concentric walls enclose temples to numerous Elamite and Mesopotamian deities, palaces, and a funerary complex with underground royal tombs. A remarkable hydraulic system brought water from the Karkheh River by canal.

History and excavation

The city was never fully completed and was largely abandoned after the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal's campaigns in the seventh century BCE, though occupation continued for centuries. Rediscovered in the 1930s during oil exploration, it was excavated by Roman Ghirshman between 1951 and 1962. The name Chogha Zanbil means 'basket mound' in the local Luri dialect, describing the ziggurat's profile.
