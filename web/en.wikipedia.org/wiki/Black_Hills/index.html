Black Hills

The Black Hills are a small, isolated mountain range rising from the Great Plains in western South Dakota and northeastern Wyoming. Sacred to the Lakota Sioux, who call them Pahá Sápa, the hills are the site of Mount Rushmore, Crazy Horse Memorial, Wind Cave, and Custer State Park. Their name comes from the dark appearance of their pine-covered slopes from a distance.

Geology

The hills formed by a dome uplift some 70 million years ago; erosion exposed a core of Precambrian granite and schist — among the oldest rocks in North America, around 2 billion years old — ringed by sedimentary layers including limestone hosting Wind Cave and Jewel Cave.

History

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 granted the Black Hills to the Sioux in perpetuity as part of the Great Sioux Reservation. After Custer's 1874 expedition confirmed gold, miners flooded in, and the US seized the hills in 1877 following the Great Sioux War. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980), the Supreme Court ruled the taking illegal and awarded compensation, now worth over a billion dollars with interest; the Sioux have refused the money, demanding the land's return, making it one of the longest-running Indigenous land disputes in US history.

Tourism

Attractions include the carved faces of Mount Rushmore, the still-growing Crazy Horse Memorial, the annual Sturgis Motorcycle Rally, the historic mining town of Deadwood, and wildlife in Custer State Park.
