Human rights in China

The state of human rights in China is a subject of significant international debate. The government of the People's Republic of China maintains that it protects human rights with "Chinese characteristics", emphasising subsistence, development and social stability, while critics and international organisations document extensive restrictions on speech, religion, assembly and minority rights.

Legal framework

The Constitution of the People's Republic of China guarantees freedoms of speech, press, assembly and religion, but these are qualified by provisions prohibiting acts detrimental to the state, and China has no independent constitutional court. China signed but has never ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

Civil and political rights

The Chinese Communist Party maintains a monopoly on political power. Independent media are prohibited, the internet is censored through the "Great Firewall", and dissidents and human-rights lawyers have been imprisoned — notably in the 2015 "709 crackdown". The 1989 Tiananmen Square protests remain a taboo subject domestically.

Ethnic and religious minorities

The treatment of Uyghurs in Xinjiang — where the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights reported in 2022 that serious violations, possibly crimes against humanity, had occurred during mass detention — of Tibetans, and of unregistered churches and Falun Gong practitioners, draws sustained international criticism, which Beijing rejects as interference.

International response

Western governments, the European Union and NGOs such as Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch regularly criticise China's record, while China points to its poverty-reduction achievements and rallies support among developing states at the UN Human Rights Council.
