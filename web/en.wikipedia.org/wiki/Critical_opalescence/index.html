Critical opalescence

Critical opalescence is a phenomenon that occurs in the region of a continuous phase transition, where a normally transparent liquid or gas becomes cloudy and milky in appearance. It is most famously observed at the liquid–gas critical point, where the distinction between liquid and vapour disappears.

Cause

Near a critical point, fluctuations in density occur over ever-larger length scales and relax ever more slowly (critical slowing down). When these fluctuations reach sizes comparable to the wavelength of visible light, they scatter light strongly in all directions, so the fluid takes on a milky, opalescent whiteness. The effect is a direct visual manifestation of the divergence of the correlation length and of the isothermal compressibility at the critical point.

History and theory

The phenomenon was noted in the nineteenth century in experiments on carbon dioxide near its critical temperature (31.0 °C). Marian Smoluchowski (1908) and Albert Einstein (1910) explained it theoretically using the statistical mechanics of fluctuations, Einstein's treatment of critical opalescence being an important confirmation of the reality of molecular fluctuations and hence of atoms. Modern theory places the effect within the framework of critical phenomena and universality classes, where the scaling behaviour near the critical point is the same for fluids and for model systems such as the Ising model.

Critical opalescence also occurs in liquid mixtures near a consolute point and in other systems with a second-order phase transition; the scattering of light (and of neutrons and X-rays) near critical points remains a standard experimental probe of critical behaviour.
