Cream

Cream is a dairy product composed of the higher-butterfat layer skimmed from the top of milk before homogenization. In un-homogenized milk, fat globules, being less dense than the aqueous phase, gradually rise to the top; this layer can be skimmed off, or cream can be separated much faster using centrifugal separators. Cream produced from cow's milk has a pale yellowish-white color from carotenoids in the fat.

Types

Grades of cream are defined by butterfat content, which varies by country. In the United States, "half and half" contains roughly 10.5–18% fat; light cream about 18–30%; light whipping cream 30–36%; and heavy (whipping) cream at least 36%. In the United Kingdom, single cream is about 18% fat, whipping cream about 35%, and double cream about 48%. Clotted cream, made in Devon and Cornwall by heating cream so a crust forms, reaches about 55–65% fat. Cultured products include crème fraîche and sour cream. Cream with a high fat content whips into stable foam because the fat globules partially coalesce around air bubbles; this is the basis of whipped cream.

Uses and production

Cream is used in coffee, sauces, soups, desserts, butter and ice cream manufacture, and baking. World production runs to millions of tonnes annually, dominated by major dairy-producing countries. Most cream sold today is pasteurized or ultra-high-temperature (UHT) treated. Non-dairy "creamers" and creams made from coconut or soy are sold as alternatives.
