Instrumental and intrinsic value

Instrumental value is the value something has as a means to a desired end, whereas intrinsic value is the value something has in itself, for its own sake. The distinction is a central topic in ethics and axiology, the philosophical study of value.

Definitions

Money is a classic example of something with instrumental value: it is valuable because of what it can buy. By contrast, states such as happiness or pleasure are often held to have intrinsic value — they are desired for their own sake, not for the sake of anything else. Philosophers debate which things, if any, are intrinsically valuable, and whether intrinsic value depends on valuers or exists independently.

Environmental ethics

The distinction plays a major role in environmental ethics. Conservation arguments may rest on nature's instrumental value (ecosystem services) or on claims that species and ecosystems have intrinsic value independent of human use.

History

The distinction traces to Plato and Aristotle, who separated goods pursued for their own sake from goods pursued for the sake of others, and was developed by Kant, who held that rational beings have dignity rather than mere price, and by G. E. Moore in his work on ethics.
