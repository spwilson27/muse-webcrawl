Coordination polymer

Schematic of a one-dimensional coordination polymer chain.

A coordination polymer is an inorganic or organometallic polymer structure containing metal cation centers linked by ligands. More formally, a coordination polymer is a coordination compound with repeating coordination entities extending in one, two, or three dimensions. Many such materials are studied for applications in luminescence, conductivity, magnetism, and gas storage.

History and synthesis

Coordination polymers have been known since the early twentieth century; Prussian blue, a mixed-valence iron cyanide solid, is an early example whose structure was clarified in the 1970s. Modern work, driven by crystal engineering, uses designed bridging ligands — such as dicarboxylates, bipyridines, and cyanides — combined with metal ions under solvothermal or diffusion conditions to produce extended networks with predictable topologies.

Relation to metal–organic frameworks

Porous three-dimensional coordination polymers are commonly called metal–organic frameworks (MOFs). While some authors use the terms interchangeably, IUPAC recommendations treat metal–organic frameworks as a subset of coordination polymers that are potentially porous coordination networks with organic ligands. Not all coordination polymers are porous: many are dense chains or layers.

Properties and applications

  • Gas storage and separation: porous frameworks can adsorb hydrogen, methane and carbon dioxide.
  • Catalysis: metal sites and functional ligands can act as heterogeneous catalysts.
  • Sensing: luminescent coordination polymers change emission in response to guests or stimuli.
  • Magnetism and conductivity: exchange interactions and conjugated bridges can yield magnetic or electrically conducting materials.
