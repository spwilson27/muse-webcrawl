Ratification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ratification is the process by which a state (or other international actor) confirms its consent to be bound by a treaty it has signed, completing the treaty's entry into force for it after deposit of the instrument of ratification. It is the second step of the classic two-stage process — signature (authenticating the text, and obliging the signatory not to defeat the treaty's object and purpose pending ratification) followed by ratification — which separates the executive's negotiation from the state's definitive commitment.

Domestic procedures

Ratification's domestic mechanics are constitutional law: the US President ratifies after Senate advice and consent by two-thirds (the bottleneck that diverted practice into executive agreements and left the US a signatory-but-non-party to many codifications, VCLT included); parliamentary systems increasingly require legislative approval before ratification (the UK's Ponsonby Rule, codified in 2010); the EU's mixed agreements require member-state ratifications — each an occasion for referendum drama (Ireland on Lisbon, 2008–09) or regional blockage (Wallonia nearly stopping CETA, 2016). Related acts perform equivalent consent without prior signature (accession, acceptance, approval), and provisional application (VCLT Article 25) lets treaties operate pending ratification — as with the Energy Charter Treaty, whose withdrawals show provisional application's long tail.
