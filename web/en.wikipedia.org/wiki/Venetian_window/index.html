Venetian window
TypeTripartite arched window
Also calledSerlian window, Palladian window
Associated withAndrea Palladio, Venetian architecture

A Venetian window (also called a Serlian window or, when used by Andrea Palladio, a Palladian window; the arrangement is the serliana) is a large window divided into three parts: a tall central opening with a semicircular arch, flanked by two shorter, flat-topped side openings separated from the centre by columns or pilasters. The central arch springs from entablature segments carried over the side lights.

History

The motif descends from ancient Roman triumphal arches. It was codified in Sebastiano Serlio's influential treatise on architecture (1537), from which it takes the name "Serlian," and was taken up enthusiastically by Palladio, most famously in the arcades of the Basilica Palladiana in Vicenza (begun 1549), where the motif solves the problem of spanning bays of different widths. In Venice itself, tripartite windows — often grouped in the central hall (portego) of a palazzo — became a hallmark of Gothic and Renaissance palaces on the Grand Canal.

Later use

Through Palladio's Quattro Libri (1570) the window spread across Europe and became a signature element of Palladianism in Britain — used by Inigo Jones and extensively in 18th-century country houses — and of colonial and Federal architecture in America. Variants with a blind or glazed centre appear in countless Georgian and neoclassical buildings.
