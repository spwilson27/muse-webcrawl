Budō (Japanese: 武道, "martial way") is a Japanese term describing modern Japanese martial arts — disciplines derived from the older feudal combat techniques (bujutsu) but re-formed, especially from the Meiji period onward, as paths of self-cultivation, character development and sport. Where a bujutsu name ends in -jutsu ("technique"), the corresponding budō art typically ends in -dō ("way/path"): thus jūjutsu gave rise to judo, kenjutsu to kendo, and aikijujutsu informed aikido.

Philosophy

The concept of dō reflects the influence of Zen Buddhism, Shinto and Confucian ethics on modern martial training: mastery of technique is pursued together with discipline, etiquette (reigi), humility and the perfection of character. The modern formulation of budō was consolidated in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by figures such as Kanō Jigorō (judo) and Funakoshi Gichin (karate), and institutionally by the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai (1895). After the Second World War the Allied occupation briefly banned budō, after which the arts were revived largely as sports and means of education; the Nippon Budokan (built 1964) became their national centre, and the Japanese Budo Association codified a "Budo Charter" in 1987.

Modern gendai budō

The nine arts recognised by the Japanese Budo Association are judo, kendo, kyūdō (archery), sumo, karate-dō, aikidō, shōrinji kempō, naginata and jūkendō. Budō training typically combines kata (pre-arranged forms), free practice (randori or sparring), etiquette rituals and graded ranks (kyū and dan).
