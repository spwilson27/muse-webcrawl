Euthyphro

Euthyphro (Greek: Εὐθύφρων, Euthýphrōn) is a Platonic dialogue written by the ancient Greek philosopher Plato. It depicts a conversation between Socrates and Euthyphro, a self-proclaimed expert in religion and piety, shortly before Socrates' trial for impiety and corrupting the youth in 399 BC. The dialogue's central question — whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods — gives rise to the famous Euthyphro dilemma, which remains a key problem in the philosophy of religion and meta-ethics.

Setting and characters

The dialogue takes place outside the court of the archon basileus (king archon) in Athens. Socrates is there to respond to the indictment brought against him by Meletus. He meets Euthyphro, who is prosecuting his own father for murder: the father had bound a labourer who had killed a slave and left him in a ditch, where he died of exposure while the father awaited advice from a religious exegete. Euthyphro's relatives consider the prosecution impious, but Euthyphro claims superior knowledge of piety and divine law justifies his action.

The dialogue

Socrates, with characteristic irony, asks Euthyphro to teach him what piety (to hosion) is, so that he may defend himself against the charge of impiety. Euthyphro offers a series of definitions, each of which Socrates dismantles:

  1. Piety is prosecuting wrongdoers — rejected as a mere example, not a general definition.
  2. Piety is what is loved by the gods — problematic because the gods disagree, so the same act could be both pious and impious.
  3. Piety is what is loved by all the gods — this refined version prompts Socrates' famous question: "Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?" Euthyphro concedes that the gods love the pious because it is pious, which means being loved by the gods is a consequence, not the essence, of piety.
  4. Piety is the part of justice concerned with the care of the gods — pressed further, "care" collapses into service to the gods, and Euthyphro falls back on defining piety as knowledge of prayer and sacrifice — "a kind of trading skill between gods and men" — which circles back to what pleases the gods, the definition already refuted.

Realising the argument has gone in a circle, Euthyphro hastily departs, claiming he is in a hurry, and Socrates is left without the definition he sought. The dialogue is thus aporetic — it ends in perplexity (aporia) without a settled answer.

The Euthyphro dilemma

The question Socrates poses has been adapted by later philosophers against divine command theory: if actions are good merely because God commands them, morality appears arbitrary; but if God commands them because they are good, then goodness exists independently of God's commands. The dilemma, in monotheistic form, continues to be debated by philosophers and theologians.

Classification

The Euthyphro is generally classed among Plato's early "Socratic" dialogues, characterised by the search for definitions and an inconclusive ending.
