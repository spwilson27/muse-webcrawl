Head cheese

Head cheese or brawn is a cold cut terrine or meat jelly that originated in Europe. It is made with flesh from the head of a calf or pig (less commonly a sheep or cow), and often set in aspic. The parts of the head used are sometimes limited to the fleshy parts, though the dish's reputation rests on its traditional use of the whole head.

Despite the name, head cheese is not a dairy cheese. It is usually eaten cold as a luncheon meat or sliced on sandwiches.

Preparation

The head is cleaned, the brain (and in many preparations the eyes and ears) removed, and the remaining head simmered for hours with vegetables and seasonings until the meat is tender. Natural gelatin from the bones and skin makes the cooking liquid set into a firm jelly as it cools. The meat is picked from the bones, chopped, combined with the strained broth, seasoned — commonly with parsley, pepper, allspice, bay, or vinegar — and pressed into a mould or loaf pan to set.

Varieties

Many regional versions exist. In Germany, Sülze or Presskopf is often sour, set with vinegar. In Britain it is known as brawn; in France as fromage de tête; in Italy as coppa di testa; in Poland as salceson; in Russia and Ukraine as saltison, often including garlic. Creole cooking in Louisiana produced a spicy version known as hog's head cheese, seasoned with cayenne and green onions. A related but distinct dish, souse, is pickled in vinegar. In Scandinavia, sylteflesk is a Christmas staple.

History

Head cheese belongs to the tradition of using the whole animal after slaughter; medieval and early modern cookery texts describe brawn made from boar's and pig's heads. It was a practical way to preserve less prized cuts, the aspic acting as a protective seal. Commercial production continues today, though the dish has declined in popularity in many countries as tastes shifted away from offal and whole-head preparations.
