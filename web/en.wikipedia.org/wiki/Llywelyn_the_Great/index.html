Llywelyn ab Iorwerth (Welsh: [ɬəˈwɛlɪn ab ˈjɔrwɛrθ], c. 1173 – 11 April 1240), also known as Llywelyn Fawr (Welsh for "Llywelyn the Great"), was a Welsh prince who became the de facto ruler of most of Wales during the early 13th century. Prince of Gwynedd from 1195, he extended his authority over the other Welsh kingdoms through a combination of warfare and diplomacy, and by the end of his reign was recognised as the dominant figure in native Wales, styling himself Prince of Aberffraw and Lord of Snowdon.

Early life

Llywelyn was the grandson of Owain Gwynedd, king of Gwynedd. His father, Iorwerth ab Owain, died or was sidelined early, and Llywelyn grew up in exile, possibly in England. By 1194 he had returned to Gwynedd and, with his cousins Gruffudd and Maredudd, defeated his uncle Dafydd ab Owain Gwynedd at the Battle of Aberconwy, seizing control of eastern Gwynedd. By 1200 he was sole ruler of Gwynedd.

Rise to power

Llywelyn built a close relationship with King John of England, marrying John's illegitimate daughter Joan in 1205. In 1211, after relations soured, John invaded Gwynedd and forced Llywelyn to surrender territory east of the River Conwy. Llywelyn recovered by allying with the other Welsh princes and with the English barons in revolt against John; the Welsh clauses of Magna Carta (1215) reflected his gains. In 1216 a council at Aberdyfi recognised him as overlord of the other Welsh princes, and the Treaty of Worcester (1218) with the regents of the young Henry III confirmed his position.

Rule

Llywelyn campaigned repeatedly in south Wales, taking castles including Carmarthen and Cardigan, and dominated the politics of Powys and Deheubarth. He founded the Cistercian abbey of Aberconwy and worked to secure the succession of his son Dafydd, born to Joan, over his elder son Gruffudd — a departure from Welsh custom that required the backing of both the English crown and the Church. He was the grandfather, through Dafydd and later through Gruffudd's son Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, of the last native Prince of Wales.

Death and legacy

In his last years Llywelyn withdrew increasingly to religious life; he died on 11 April 1240 at Aberconwy Abbey, where he was buried, having taken the Cistercian habit. He is remembered as one of the most successful Welsh rulers of the Middle Ages — hence his epithet "the Great", shared in Welsh history only with Rhodri Mawr. His court poet was Llywarch ap Llywelyn (Prydydd y Moch).
