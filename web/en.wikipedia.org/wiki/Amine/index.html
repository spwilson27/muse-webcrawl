Amine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amines are organic compounds and functional groups that contain a basic nitrogen atom with a lone pair. Amines are formally derivatives of ammonia (NH3), wherein one or more hydrogen atoms have been replaced by a substituent such as an alkyl or aryl group. Depending on the number of organic groups attached to nitrogen, amines are classified as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°); a nitrogen bearing four groups is a quaternary ammonium cation.

Properties

Lower amines are gases or volatile liquids with characteristic fishy or ammoniacal odours. The nitrogen lone pair makes amines basic and nucleophilic; in water they establish equilibrium with alkylammonium ions. Most amines have pKa values of the conjugate acid around 9–11.

Occurrence and applications

Important amines include amino acids, biogenic amines such as histamine and serotonin, and the alkaloids. Industrially, amines are used in dyes, pharmaceuticals, gas treatment (absorbing CO2 and H2S), and as building blocks for polymers such as nylon, whose monomer hexamethylenediamine is a diamine. Aniline, an aromatic amine, is a major industrial precursor.

Synthesis

Amines are prepared by alkylation of ammonia, reduction of nitriles, nitro compounds and amides, reductive amination of carbonyl compounds, and the Gabriel synthesis for primary amines.
