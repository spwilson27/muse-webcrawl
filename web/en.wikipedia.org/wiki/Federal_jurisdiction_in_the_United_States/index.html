Federal jurisdiction in the United States

Federal jurisdiction in the United States refers to the legal authority of the federal courts to hear and decide cases. Unlike state courts, which are courts of general jurisdiction, the federal courts of the United States are courts of limited jurisdiction: under Article III of the Constitution and statutes enacted by Congress, they may adjudicate only certain categories of cases.

Sources of jurisdiction

Article III, Section 2 of the Constitution extends the judicial power of the United States to cases arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States; cases affecting ambassadors and consuls; admiralty and maritime cases; controversies to which the United States is a party; controversies between two or more states; controversies between citizens of different states (diversity jurisdiction); and controversies between citizens of the same state claiming lands under grants of different states, among others.

Subject-matter jurisdiction

Federal question jurisdiction

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331, the district courts have original jurisdiction over civil actions "arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States." The Supreme Court's decision in Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley (1908) established the well-pleaded complaint rule: the federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff's properly pleaded complaint.

Diversity jurisdiction

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1332, district courts may hear cases between citizens of different states (or between citizens of a state and foreign citizens) where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000. Under Strawbridge v. Curtiss (1806), complete diversity is required: no plaintiff may share state citizenship with any defendant. A corporation is a citizen of both its state of incorporation and the state of its principal place of business.

Other grants

Congress has conferred jurisdiction over specific subjects, including bankruptcy (28 U.S.C. § 1334), patent and copyright cases (§ 1338), admiralty (§ 1333), cases involving federal officers, and claims against the United States under the Tucker Act and the Federal Tort Claims Act. Federal courts also exercise supplemental jurisdiction (§ 1367) over state-law claims closely related to claims within their original jurisdiction, and removal jurisdiction (§ 1441) allows defendants to transfer qualifying cases from state to federal court.

Personal jurisdiction and venue

In addition to subject-matter jurisdiction, a federal court must have personal jurisdiction over the parties, governed by the Due Process Clauses and state long-arm statutes (incorporated in federal court by Rule 4(k) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure). Venue is generally proper where a defendant resides or where a substantial part of the events giving rise to the claim occurred (28 U.S.C. § 1391).

Appellate jurisdiction

The courts of appeals review final decisions of the district courts under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction is principally discretionary, exercised through writs of certiorari under § 1254 and § 1257.
