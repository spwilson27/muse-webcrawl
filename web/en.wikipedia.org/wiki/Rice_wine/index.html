Rice wine

TypeAlcoholic beverage
Alcohol by volumeTypically 10–20%
IngredientsRice, water, fermentation starter (yeast and mould)
Region of originEast and Southeast Asia

Rice wine is an alcoholic beverage fermented from rice, traditionally consumed in East Asia and Southeast Asia. Unlike grape wine, which is made by fermenting fruit sugars directly, rice wine requires the starch in rice first to be converted to sugar — a process performed by moulds, typically Aspergillus oryzae (koji) or mixed starter cultures — while yeast simultaneously or subsequently converts the sugar to alcohol. The resulting drinks range from clear and dry to sweet, milky and unfiltered.

Varieties by country

  • China — huangjiu ("yellow wine"), including the celebrated Shaoxing wine used both for drinking and cooking; mijiu, a clearer, sweeter rice wine; and baijiu, a distilled spirit usually made from sorghum but also from rice.
  • Japan — sake, brewed from polished rice with koji; and mirin, a sweet rice wine used in cooking. Distilled rice spirits include shōchū.
  • Korea — makgeolli, a milky, lightly sparkling unfiltered wine; cheongju, a clear refined wine; and soju, traditionally distilled from rice wine.
  • Vietnam — rượu gạo and rượu cần, the latter drunk communally through straws from a jar.
  • Philippines — tapuy, a clear wine from the Cordilleras, and basi made from sugarcane.
  • Thailand and Laos — sato and lao hai (jar alcohol).

Production

Production typically begins with washing and steaming rice, then inoculating it with a starter containing mould spores. The mould's enzymes saccharify the starch; yeast then ferments the sugars. In the Japanese parallel-fermentation method for sake, saccharification and fermentation proceed simultaneously in the same tank, allowing alcohol concentrations of up to about 20% — among the highest of any undistilled fermented beverage. Rice wines may be filtered, pasteurised and aged, or consumed young and cloudy.

History

Fermented rice beverages are ancient in China: chemical analysis of pottery from the Neolithic village of Jiahu (c. 7000–6600 BC) found residues of a fermented drink made from rice, honey and fruit — among the earliest evidence of alcoholic fermentation anywhere. Rice wine became central to Chinese ritual, cuisine and poetry, and spread with rice culture throughout Asia.
