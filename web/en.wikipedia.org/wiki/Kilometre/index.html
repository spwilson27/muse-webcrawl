The kilometre (SI symbol: km; American spelling kilometer) is a unit of length in the International System of Units (SI), equal to one thousand metres, the SI base unit of length. It is the standard unit for expressing road distances, geographic scales and speed limits (kilometres per hour) in most of the world.

Definition and equivalence

Since the metre is defined by fixing the speed of light in vacuum at 299,792,458 metres per second, the kilometre equals exactly 1,000 of those metres. In imperial and US customary terms, one kilometre is approximately 0.621371 miles, 1,093.61 yards, or 3,280.84 feet. Conversely, one international mile is exactly 1.609344 kilometres. A square kilometre (km²) equals 100 hectares or about 0.386 square miles; a cubic kilometre measures large volumes such as lakes or ice sheets.

History

The kilometre arose with the French metric system of the 1790s, in which the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator along the Paris meridian — making the pole-to-equator distance, by design, about 10,000 km. The name combines the SI prefix kilo- (from Greek chilioi, "thousand") with metre. Through the 19th and 20th centuries the metric system was adopted worldwide; most countries changed road signs to kilometres during metrication programmes in the 1960s–1980s. Notable exceptions to everyday use include the United States and (for road distances) the United Kingdom, which retain miles, though kilometres are used in science, sport and many official contexts there as well.

Usage and pronunciation

The kilometre is used for road and rail distances, marathon and race descriptions (e.g. a "10K" run), aviation and maritime contexts in some countries, and in derived units such as km/h for speed and km/L for fuel economy. The standard SI pronunciation stress differs by country: both KIL-o-mee-ter and kil-OM-eter are heard in English. Informally, "klick" is military slang for a kilometre.
