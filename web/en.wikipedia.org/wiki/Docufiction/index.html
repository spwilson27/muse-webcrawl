Docufiction

Docufiction (or docu-fiction) is the cinematographic combination of documentary and fiction, a genre that attempts to capture reality as closely as possible while introducing unreal elements or fictional situations in narration in order to strengthen the representation of reality.

History

The term was coined by Jean Rouch in the early 1960s, but the practice reaches back to Robert J. Flaherty's Nanook of the North (1922), in which staged scenes were used to portray Inuit life. Rouch's own films, such as Moi, un noir (1958) and Chronicle of a Summer (1961, with Edgar Morin), developed the approach into "ethnofiction", in which real people improvise fictional versions of their own lives.

Characteristics

Docufiction often employs non-professional actors playing themselves or versions of themselves, real locations, and a narrative structure that blurs the boundary between observed fact and constructed story. It is related to, but distinct from, the docudrama, which re-enacts documented events with actors.
