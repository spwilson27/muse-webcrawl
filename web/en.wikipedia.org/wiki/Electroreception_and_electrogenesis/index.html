Electroreception and electrogensis


The ampullae of Lorenzini give sharks their electrical sense

Electroreception is the biological ability to perceive natural electrical stimuli. It has been observed almost exclusively in aquatic or amphibious animals, since water is a much better conductor of electricity than air. Electroreception is used mainly for electrolocation — detecting nearby objects and prey by the distortions they cause in an electric field — and for communication. Electrogenesis, the generation of electricity by an animal, usually accompanies the sense: weakly electric fish generate fields to sense their surroundings, while a few species, like the electric eel, produce discharges strong enough to stun prey and deter predators.

Passive electroreception

Sharks and rays detect the weak bioelectric fields of prey using the ampullae of Lorenzini, gel-filled canals opening as pores on the head; they are among the most sensitive electroreceptors known. Paddlefish, some catfish, monotremes such as the platypus, and even the Guiana dolphin also possess passive electroreception.

Active electroreception

Two groups of freshwater fishes — the Gymnotiformes of South America and the Mormyridae of Africa — generate weak electric organ discharges and sense distortions of the resulting field caused by objects of differing conductivity. These fish also communicate by modulating their discharge patterns, encoding species, sex, and social signals.

Electric organs

Electric organs are made of stacked modified muscle or nerve cells called electrocytes; their synchronized discharge adds in series and parallel. Strongly electric fishes include the electric eel, electric catfish, torpedo rays, and electric skates, with the largest discharges reaching hundreds of volts.
