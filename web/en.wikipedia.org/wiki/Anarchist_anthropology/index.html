Anarchist anthropology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anarchist anthropology is a body of theory and ethnographic research that draws on anthropology's cross-cultural record to examine how human societies have organised themselves without states, rulers, or permanent hierarchies, and what that record implies for political theory. The field is associated above all with Pierre Clastres, Harold Barclay, and David Graeber, whose 2004 pamphlet Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology gave the approach its best-known manifesto.

Origins

The roots of the approach reach back to Peter Kropotkin's Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902), which used ethnographic and historical evidence of cooperation in stateless societies to argue against social-Darwinist accounts of competition. Early anthropologists such as Franz Boas documented egalitarian peoples, though usually without drawing anarchist conclusions. In the mid-twentieth century, E. E. Evans-Pritchard's account of the Nuer as an "ordered anarchy" of segmentary lineages, and the comparative volume African Political Systems (1940), catalogued societies that governed themselves without centralised authority.

Key works

Pierre Clastres's Society Against the State (1974) argued that many Amazonian societies were not "pre-state" but actively organised to ward off the emergence of coercive power — chiefs were given prestige but no power to command. Harold Barclay's People Without Government: An Anthropology of Anarchy (1982) surveyed stateless societies worldwide. David Graeber's Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology (2004) proposed studying the "implicit" political philosophy of movements and communities avoiding the state, and criticised academia's marginalisation of anarchist thought. Charles Macdonald's Uncultural Behavior (2020) and work by Brian Morris, David Wengrow, and James C. Scott (The Art of Not Being Governed, 2009) have continued the themes.

Themes

  • The diversity of non-state political forms: band societies, segmentary lineages, councils, consensus assemblies.
  • Mechanisms that suppress hierarchy: ridicule of would-be chiefs, fission, seasonal alternation between centralised and dispersed organisation.
  • The critique of evolutionary narratives that treat the state as inevitable.
  • Applications to contemporary movements using consensus decision-making and horizontal organisation.

Criticism

Critics caution against romanticising stateless societies, noting that the absence of a state does not guarantee the absence of domination by age, gender, or kinship, and that some "stateless" peoples developed their forms in interaction with — or flight from — neighbouring states rather than in pristine isolation.
