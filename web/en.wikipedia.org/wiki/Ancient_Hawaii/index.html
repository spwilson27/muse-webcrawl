Ancient Hawaii

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Ancient Hawaii is the period of Hawaiian history preceding the unification of the Kingdom of Hawaii by Kamehameha the Great in 1795, and before sustained contact with Europeans following Captain James Cook's arrival in 1778. It encompasses roughly a millennium of settlement and development by Polynesian voyagers and their descendants.

Settlement

Polynesian voyagers from the Marquesas and later the Society Islands settled the Hawaiian Islands in double-hulled voyaging canoes, navigating by stars, winds and currents. Archaeological and radiocarbon evidence suggests settlement by around AD 1000–1200. Settlers brought "canoe plants" such as taro, sweet potato, banana, coconut and breadfruit, along with pigs, chickens and dogs.

Society

Ancient Hawaiian society was stratified into aliʻi (chiefs and nobility), kahuna (priests and experts), makaʻāinana (commoners) and kauwā (outcasts). Land was organised in ahupuaʻa, wedge-shaped divisions running from the mountains to the sea that gave each community access to varied resources. Religion centred on gods such as Kū, Lono, Kāne and Kanaloa, worshipped at stone temple platforms called heiau, and social life was governed by the kapu system of sacred prohibitions. Irrigated taro terraces (loʻi) and fishponds supported dense populations, estimated at several hundred thousand before contact.

Late period and unification

By the 18th century, powerful chiefs ruled the major islands and competed for supremacy. Kamehameha of Hawaii island, aided by Western weapons and advisers after 1790, conquered Maui and Oahu (culminating at the Battle of Nuʻuanu in 1795) and received the submission of Kauai in 1810, completing the unification of the kingdom. The old religion was abolished in 1819, shortly before the arrival of American Protestant missionaries in 1820.
