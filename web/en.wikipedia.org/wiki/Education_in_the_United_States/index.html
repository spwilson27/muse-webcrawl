Education in the United States

Education in the United States is provided mainly by the public sector, with control and funding coming from state and local governments; the federal government, through the Department of Education, supplies a minority of funding and sets broad policy conditions. Schooling is compulsory in every state, generally from about age 5–7 to 16–18. American education follows a broadly standard pattern: elementary school (kindergarten through grade 5 or 6), middle or junior high school, and high school (to grade 12), followed by post-secondary education at colleges and universities. The United States spends more per student than most OECD countries, and its universities dominate global rankings, though outcomes vary widely by state, district and income.

Structure and governance

Public schools are run by locally elected or appointed school districts — roughly 13,000 of them — funded mainly by local property taxes and state appropriations, which produces large disparities in resources between districts. Private schools educate about 10% of students, and a small but growing share are homeschooled or attend publicly funded charter schools and magnet schools. National assessments include the NAEP ("the Nation's Report Card"); most states administer standardised tests required under federal law (now the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015, successor to No Child Left Behind). The Common Core State Standards, adopted by most states in the 2010s, set shared expectations in mathematics and English language arts.

Higher education

The United States has over 4,000 degree-granting institutions, including public flagship research universities, liberal arts colleges, and a large system of two-year community colleges offering associate degrees and transfer routes. Four-year bachelor's degrees are the standard undergraduate qualification; graduate and professional education (master's, PhD, MD, JD) follows. Admission to selective colleges typically considers grades, SAT or ACT scores (increasingly optional), essays and extracurricular activities. Tuition costs — especially at private institutions — and student loan debt (exceeding $1.6 trillion nationally) are persistent policy concerns.

History

Public "common schools" spread in the 19th century under reformers such as Horace Mann; compulsory attendance laws were universal by 1918. Landmark developments include the land-grant universities (Morrill Acts, 1862/1890), the GI Bill (1944), the Supreme Court's school-desegregation ruling in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), and federal special-education guarantees (IDEA, 1975).
