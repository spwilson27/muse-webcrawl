Cricothyrotomy

The incision is made through the cricothyroid membrane, between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages.

A cricothyrotomy (also called cricothyroidotomy) is an emergency incision made through the skin and the cricothyroid membrane of the neck to establish an airway in a patient who cannot be ventilated or intubated by other means. It is a procedure of last resort in the "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" scenario, used in severe facial trauma, airway obstruction, anaphylaxis or massive swelling where endotracheal intubation is impossible.

Technique

The operator identifies the cricothyroid membrane — the soft depression between the thyroid cartilage (Adam's apple) and the cricoid cartilage — makes a vertical skin incision and a horizontal incision through the membrane, then inserts a small cuffed tube or a purpose-made cannula, commonly using a scalpel–bougie technique or a Seldinger wire-guided kit. A needle cricothyrotomy with a wide-bore cannula and high-pressure oxygen jet ventilation is an alternative, particularly in children, in whom the surgical procedure is relatively contraindicated under about 10–12 years of age because of the risk of subglottic stenosis.

Complications

Complications include bleeding, tube misplacement or displacement, damage to the vocal cords or cartilages, tracheal stenosis, infection and oesophageal perforation. Because the tube is small and temporary, cricothyrotomy is usually converted to a formal tracheostomy once the patient is stabilised. It differs from a tracheostomy, which is a more deliberate procedure, usually performed lower in the trachea in an operating theatre.
