Sanger sequencing

Sanger sequencing
Also known as: chain-termination method, dideoxy sequencing
Developed by: Frederick Sanger and colleagues, 1977
Typical read length: up to ~1,000 base pairs

Sanger sequencing is a method of DNA sequencing based on the selective incorporation of chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides by DNA polymerase during in vitro DNA replication. Developed by Frederick Sanger and colleagues in 1977, it was the dominant sequencing technology for roughly four decades and was the method used to produce most of the Human Genome Project's sequence.

Method

In the classic method, a single-stranded DNA template is combined with a primer, DNA polymerase, the four ordinary deoxynucleotides (dNTPs), and small amounts of the four dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs), which lack the 3′-hydroxyl group needed to extend a growing DNA strand. Whenever a ddNTP is incorporated, synthesis stops, producing a set of fragments of every possible length, each ending at a position where that base occurs. The fragments are separated by size and the base at each terminal position is read off in order.

Automation and modern use

Automated versions label each ddNTP with a different fluorescent dye and separate the fragments by capillary electrophoresis, with a laser reading the colour sequence to produce an electropherogram. Although high-throughput "next-generation" methods have displaced Sanger sequencing for whole-genome work, it remains the gold standard for verifying individual DNA fragments, sequencing PCR products and plasmids, and clinical confirmation of variants, owing to its accuracy and read lengths of up to about 1,000 bases.
