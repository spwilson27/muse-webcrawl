Romance novel

A romance novel or romantic novel is a genre fiction novel that primary focuses on the relationship and romantic love between two people, typically with an emotionally satisfying and optimistic ending. The genre is one of the best-selling in publishing, particularly in North America, and encompasses numerous subgenres including contemporary, historical, paranormal, romantic suspense and Regency romance.

Definition

The Romance Writers of America defined the genre by two elements: a central love story and an emotionally satisfying, optimistic ending. Works failing the second criterion — such as Wuthering Heights or Romeo and Juliet — are generally classed as love stories or tragedies rather than genre romance.

History

Precursors include Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740) and the novels of Jane Austen, whose works established enduring courtship plots. The modern genre took shape in the 20th century: Mills & Boon in Britain and Harlequin in Canada popularised short category romances, while Kathleen E. Woodiwiss's The Flame and the Flower (1972) launched the longer, more sensual historical romance. Authors such as Nora Roberts, Georgette Heyer (for Regency romance) and E. L. James reshaped the market in successive decades.

Subgenres

  • Category romance — short novels published in numbered monthly lines.
  • Historical romance — set before the mid-20th century, including Regency, medieval and Western settings.
  • Contemporary romance — set in the present day.
  • Paranormal romance — involving vampires, shapeshifters, magic or time travel.
  • Romantic suspense — combining a love story with mystery or thriller plots.

Market and readership

Romance has consistently accounted for a large share of mass-market fiction sales; readers are predominantly women. The rise of e-books and self-publishing in the 2010s greatly expanded the genre, and "romantasy" crossovers became bestsellers in the 2020s.
