Soft energy path

The soft energy path is an energy policy concept introduced by physicist and energy analyst Amory Lovins in a landmark 1976 Foreign Affairs article, "Energy Strategy: The Road Not Taken?". It contrasts with the "hard path" of ever-expanding centralised energy supply from fossil fuels and nuclear power.

Principles

The soft path emphasises energy efficiency and end-use matching, reliance on renewable energy sources that are diverse, decentralised, and matched in scale and quality to their end uses, and technologies that are flexible, resilient, and sustainable. Lovins argued that efficiency improvements and renewables could meet energy needs more cheaply and safely than expanding large power stations and fuel supply chains.

Influence

The concept helped found the field of energy policy analysis focused on demand-side management and became a foundational text for the modern clean-energy movement. Lovins elaborated the argument in his 1977 book Soft Energy Paths: Toward a Durable Peace and through the Rocky Mountain Institute, which he co-founded in 1982. Critics at the time questioned the feasibility of large-scale renewables, though subsequent cost declines in wind and solar power have vindicated many of its economic claims.
