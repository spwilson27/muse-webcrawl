A fief (from Latin feudum) was the central element of feudalism in medieval Europe. It consisted of heritable property or rights granted by an overlord to a vassal who held it in return for a form of feudal allegiance and service, usually military service and attendance at the lord's court. The fees were often lands — the largest landholder in a fief was the lord — but could also be revenues, offices or other rights.

Investiture

The grant of a fief was made in a ceremony of homage and fealty: the vassal knelt before the lord, placed his joined hands between the lord's hands, and swore an oath of loyalty. The lord then invested the vassal with the fief, symbolised by handing over a clod of earth, a banner or a staff. The relationship was reciprocal: a lord who failed to protect his vassal could forfeit the bond.

Development

Fiefs developed from the late Roman and Carolingian beneficia — grants of land for service that were originally for life. From the 10th and 11th centuries fiefs increasingly became hereditary, passing to the vassal's heirs on payment of a relief. Subinfeudation allowed vassals to grant parts of their fiefs to their own vassals, creating a hierarchy of tenure culminating in the king. Services owed included military host service, castle guard, aids (ransom, knighting of the lord's son, marriage of his daughter) and counsel.

Decline

From the later Middle Ages, money rents and professional armies eroded the military basis of the fief, and feudal tenures were progressively abolished by early modern states; in France feudal dues were famously abolished on the night of 4 August 1789 during the Revolution.
