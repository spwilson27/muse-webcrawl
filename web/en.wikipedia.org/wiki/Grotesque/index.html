Grotesque

Grotesque is an adjective describing something in a comically or repulsively ugly, distorted or unnatural manner: incongruous, fantastic, bizarre. The word derives from the Italian grottesca, from grotta (a cave or grotto), referring to the style of ornament discovered in the buried rooms of Nero's Domus Aurea in Rome in the late 15th century, which Renaissance artists such as Raphael imitated.

In art and ornament

In ornament, "grotesques" are decorative arrangements of interlaced arabesques, strapwork, and fantastic hybrid human and animal figures, often in a light, fanciful manner. The style flourished in the Renaissance and was revived in the 18th century. In architecture, carved stone figures on buildings—often confused with gargoyles—are also called grotesques.

In literature

In literature, the grotesque is a mode characterized by distorted, incongruous, and exaggerated depictions of people and the world, frequently mixing the comic and the frightening. Writers associated with the grotesque include Nikolai Gogol, Edgar Allan Poe, Franz Kafka, and, in the Southern Gothic tradition, Flannery O'Connor. The theorist Mikhail Bakhtin analyzed the "grotesque body" in the work of François Rabelais, linking it to carnival, exaggeration, and regeneration.

In typography

In typography, "grotesque" (or "grotesk") refers to a family of 19th-century sans-serif typefaces, such as Akzidenz-Grotesk, characterized by relatively uniform stroke weights and closed apertures; the term was applied partly because the letterforms were once considered bizarre.
