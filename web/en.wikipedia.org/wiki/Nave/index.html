Nave

The nave of a Gothic cathedral, viewed toward the chancel.

The nave (from Latin navis, "ship") is the main body of a church building, the central approach to the high altar, and the principal space occupied by the congregation. In traditional Western church architecture, the nave extends from the entrance (often at the west end) to the chancel, and is flanked by side aisles separated from it by rows of columns or piers forming an arcade. Above the arcade may run a triforium and a clerestory, whose windows light the tall central vessel.

Etymology and symbolism

The word derives from the Latin navis, ship — the same root that gives "navy" and "navigate". The comparison of the church to a ship, carrying the faithful through the storms of the world, is ancient; the congregation seated in the nave was also associated with the Latin navis through medieval symbolism in which the church building represented both the ark of salvation and the body of the Christian community.

Architecture

The nave developed from the Roman basilica, a long colonnaded hall adopted by early Christians for worship. In Romanesque and Gothic churches the nave is usually divided into bays by its supports, covered by a wooden roof or by stone rib vaulting, and proportioned to dominate the building's interior. The longest medieval nave in England is that of St Albans Cathedral; the nave of Winchester Cathedral is the longest of any Gothic cathedral in Europe. In cruciform churches the nave meets the transepts at the crossing, beyond which lies the chancel or choir, reserved historically for clergy and singers.

Use

Traditionally the nave belonged to the laity, while the chancel was the responsibility of the clergy — an arrangement reflected in English parish churches in the division of maintenance costs between parishioners and rector. Since the liturgical reforms of the twentieth century, worship has in many churches been centred on an altar brought forward into the nave or crossing, and nave altars are common in cathedrals for large services.
