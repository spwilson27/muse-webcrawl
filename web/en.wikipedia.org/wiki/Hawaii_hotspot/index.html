The Hawaii hotspot is a volcanic hotspot located near the Hawaiian Islands in the northern Pacific Ocean. It is responsible for the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain, a trail of more than 80 volcanoes spanning some 6,200 kilometres (3,900 mi) from the Aleutian Trench in the northwest Pacific to the young Lōʻihi seamount southeast of the Island of Hawaii.

Mantle plume theory

The hotspot is explained by the mantle plume theory, proposed by J. Tuzo Wilson in 1963: a relatively fixed upwelling of hot material from deep in the Earth's mantle melts through the Pacific Plate as the plate moves over it, producing a chain of volcanoes that grow older with distance from the plume. Each island is active only while it sits above the plume and becomes extinct as the plate carries it away.

The bend

The chain shows a prominent bend of about 60 degrees, formed roughly 47 million years ago, long attributed to a sharp change in the direction of Pacific Plate motion. More recent research suggests the plume itself drifted southward during the chain's early history, so the bend reflects a combination of plate motion and plume movement.

Present activity

Today the hotspot feeds the volcanoes of the Island of Hawaii — Kīlauea, Mauna Loa, Hualālai, and Mauna Kea — while the submarine Lōʻihi seamount, still nearly a kilometre below the surface, is regarded as the next Hawaiian island in formation. Seismic tomography images a broad low-velocity zone in the deep mantle beneath Hawaii, consistent with a plume rooted near the core–mantle boundary.
