The Thoroughbred is a horse breed developed for horse racing. Although the word thoroughbred is sometimes used to refer to any breed of purebred horse, it technically refers only to this breed. Thoroughbreds are considered "hot-blooded" horses, known for their agility, speed and spirit. The breed was developed in 17th- and 18th-century England, when native mares were crossbred with imported Oriental stallions of Arabian, Barb and Turkoman breeding.

History

All modern Thoroughbreds trace their pedigrees to three foundation sires imported into England around the turn of the 18th century: the Byerley Turk, the Darley Arabian and the Godolphin Arabian. The breed's stud book, the General Stud Book, was first published in 1791 and has been maintained ever since; only horses descended from recorded stock may be registered as Thoroughbreds. Almost all modern Thoroughbreds — about 95% — descend in the direct male line from the Darley Arabian.

Characteristics

Thoroughbreds typically stand 15.2 to 17.0 hands high, with a refined head, long neck, deep chest, short back and long legs. They are bred for speed over distances from sprint races of five furlongs to staying races beyond two miles, and are also raced over obstacles in National Hunt racing. The breed has been used to improve many other breeds and is the foundation of the Standardbred, American Quarter Horse and numerous sport horse registries.

Racing and health

Thoroughbred racing is a major global industry centred in Britain, Ireland, France, the United States, Australia, Japan and elsewhere. Breeding for speed has been accompanied by concerns about soundness: Thoroughbreds suffer relatively high rates of musculoskeletal injury and exercise-induced pulmonary haemorrhage, and the ethics of racing, wastage of slow horses and the use of medication are subjects of continuing debate.
