Smilax

Smilax is a genus of about 300–350 species of flowering plants in the family Smilacaceae, found throughout tropical and temperate regions of the world. Its members are commonly known as greenbriers, catbriers, or smilaxes; several tropical species provide the drug and flavouring sarsaparilla.

Description

Most Smilax species are climbing or scrambling vines with tough, wiry stems often armed with prickles, though the genus also includes some herbs and shrubs. The leaves are alternate and leathery, typically with three prominent veins and a pair of tendrils arising from the petiole, which the plant uses to climb. Plants are dioecious, with separate male and female individuals bearing small greenish flowers; the fruit is a red, blue, or black berry eaten by birds, which disperse the seeds. Many species have stout, tuberous rhizomes.

Distribution and ecology

The genus is pantropical with extensions into temperate zones: species are numerous in the Americas, East and Southeast Asia, and Australasia. In North America, greenbriers such as Smilax rotundifolia and Smilax laurifolia are characteristic plants of thickets and forest edges, forming dense tangles that provide cover for wildlife; deer and other animals browse the shoots and foliage.

Uses

The roots and rhizomes of several Central and South American species (including Smilax aristolochiifolia and Smilax ornata) yield sarsaparilla, long used in traditional medicine and as a flavouring for soft drinks. In East Asia, Smilax china (China root) is an established medicinal plant. Shoots and young leaves of some species are eaten as vegetables, and in the southeastern United States the tender tips of greenbrier are foraged as a wild food. Extracts of Smilax species are also used in dietary supplements, though medicinal claims remain largely unverified.
