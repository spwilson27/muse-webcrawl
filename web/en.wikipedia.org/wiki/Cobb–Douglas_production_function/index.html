Cobb–Douglas production function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, the Cobb–Douglas production function is a particular functional form of a production function, widely used to represent the technological relationship between the amounts of two or more inputs (typically physical capital and labour) and the amount of output that can be produced by those inputs. It takes the form:

Y = A · Lβ · Kα

where Y is total production, L is labour input, K is capital input, A is total factor productivity, and α and β are the output elasticities of capital and labour respectively.

A Cobb–Douglas production surface: output rises with both capital and labour.

History

The function was developed by economist Paul Douglas and mathematician Charles Cobb, who fitted it to United States manufacturing data for 1899–1922 in their 1928 paper "A Theory of Production". They found α ≈ 0.25 and β ≈ 0.75, matching the observed shares of national income going to capital and labour — a regularity that became one of the "stylised facts" of growth economics.

Properties

If α + β = 1 the function exhibits constant returns to scale; greater than 1, increasing returns; less than 1, decreasing returns. The exponents equal the factor income shares under perfect competition. The function has an elasticity of substitution of exactly one, which is both its convenience and a common criticism; generalisations such as the CES production function relax this. Taking logarithms yields a linear equation, making the function easy to estimate econometrically.

Uses and criticism

Cobb–Douglas functions underpin the Solow growth model, much empirical growth accounting, and countless applied studies. Critics from Joan Robinson onward have questioned the meaning of an aggregate "capital stock", and some argue the good statistical fit is partly an artefact of the accounting identity linking output to factor payments.
