Carnivorous plant

Carnivorous plants are plants that derive some or most of their nutrients from trapping and consuming animals or protozoans, typically insects and other arthropods, and occasionally small mammals and birds. They have adapted to grow in waterlogged sunny places where the soil is thin or poor in nutrients, especially nitrogen, such as acidic bogs. They can be found on all continents except Antarctica, as well as many Pacific islands.

Carnivorous plants are widely believed to have evolved independently at least a dozen times across five different orders of flowering plants, and are represented by more than a dozen genera. At least 583 species attract, trap, and kill prey, absorbing the resulting available nutrients. Over 300 species of protocarnivorous plants in several taxa also show some but not all of these characteristics.

Trapping mechanisms

Five basic trapping mechanisms are found in carnivorous plants:

  • Pitfall traps (pitcher plants) — prey fall into a deep cavity filled with digestive enzymes or bacteria, as in Nepenthes, Sarracenia, and Cephalotus.
  • Flypaper traps — use a sticky mucilage to bind prey, as in sundews (Drosera) and butterworts (Pinguicula).
  • Snap traps — use rapid leaf movements, as in the Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula) and the waterwheel plant (Aldrovanda vesiculosa).
  • Bladder traps — suck prey into a bladder that generates an internal vacuum, as in bladderworts (Utricularia).
  • Lobster-pot traps — force prey in a direction from which escape is difficult, owing to inward-pointing bristles, as in corkscrew plants (Genlisea).

Digestion and nutrient uptake

Once trapped, prey is digested either by enzymes secreted by the plant itself or by bacteria and commensal organisms living in the trap fluid; the resulting amino acids, ammonium ions, phosphates, and other nutrients are absorbed through the leaf surface. Despite being able to catch animals, all carnivorous plants remain photosynthetic and obtain their energy from sunlight like other plants — carnivory supplies mineral nutrients, above all nitrogen and phosphorus, rather than energy.

Evolution

Carnivory in plants is thought to have evolved as an adaptation to nutrient-poor, bright, wet habitats, where the benefit of additional nitrogen outweighs the cost of building traps. Charles Darwin's 1875 book Insectivorous Plants was the first major scientific treatment of the phenomenon and established that plants such as the Venus flytrap genuinely digest insects.

Cultivation

Carnivorous plants are popular in cultivation, with the Venus flytrap, tropical pitcher plants, and sundews widely grown as houseplants and in bog gardens. Most require consistently moist, nutrient-poor, acidic soil and pure water low in dissolved minerals.
