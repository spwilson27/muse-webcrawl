The term glycan describes any sugar chain — formally a compound consisting of a number of monosaccharides linked glycosidically — and is used especially for the carbohydrate portion of a glycoconjugate such as a glycoprotein, glycolipid or proteoglycan. Glycans may be free polysaccharides or attached to proteins and lipids, where they decorate cell surfaces and secreted proteins.

Structure and diversity

Unlike nucleic acids and proteins, glycans are often branched, and their monosaccharide units can be joined at several positions and in two anomeric configurations (α or β). This gives glycan structures enormous combinatorial diversity: a small number of building blocks can generate vastly more distinct structures than an equivalent number of amino acids or nucleotides. Glycan synthesis is not template-driven; instead it is controlled by the repertoire of glycosyltransferase enzymes expressed in a cell.

Classes

N-linked glycans attach to the nitrogen of asparagine side chains in proteins, sharing a common core structure. O-linked glycans attach to the oxygen of serine or threonine residues. Glycosaminoglycans are long, often sulfated chains found in proteoglycans of the extracellular matrix and cell surfaces, including heparin and hyaluronic acid.

Function

Glycans mediate cell–cell recognition, immune responses, protein folding and stability, and host–pathogen interactions. The ABO blood group antigens are glycans, and the hemagglutinin of influenza virus binds sialic-acid-containing glycans on host cells. Altered glycosylation is a hallmark of many cancers, and glycan analysis is an active area of biomarker research.
