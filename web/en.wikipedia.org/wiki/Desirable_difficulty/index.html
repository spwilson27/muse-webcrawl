Desirable difficulty

A desirable difficulty is a learning task that requires a considerable but answerable amount of effort, thereby improving long-term performance. The term was coined by the psychologist Robert A. Bjork in the 1990s. While a difficult task slows down learning in the short term, it improves retention and transfer over the long term — the opposite of conditions that make learning feel easy and fluent but produce poor retention.

Conditions that introduce desirable difficulties often depress performance during study but raise it on delayed tests.

Examples

  • Spaced practice: distributing study over time rather than massing it (cramming).
  • Interleaving: mixing problems of different types within a practice session instead of blocking them by type.
  • Retrieval practice (the testing effect): recalling information from memory rather than re-reading it.
  • Generation: producing answers or solutions oneself before being shown them.
  • Varied conditions: practising in varied contexts and formats.

Conditions and limits

A difficulty is "desirable" only if the learner has the background knowledge to overcome it; if the task is too far beyond the learner, the difficulty is undesirable and impedes learning. The framework fits within a broader view that distinguishes performance during instruction from durable learning, and it has influenced evidence-based study advice — with the caveat that learners often misjudge effective strategies as ineffective because they feel harder.
