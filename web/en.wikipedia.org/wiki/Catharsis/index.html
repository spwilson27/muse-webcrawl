Catharsis

Catharsis
Greekκάθαρσις (katharsis)
Literally"Purification", "cleansing", "clarification"
Key sourceAristotle, Poetics ch. 6; Politics VIII
Related conceptsMimesis, hamartia, tragedy

Catharsis is from the Ancient Greek word κάθαρσις, katharsis, meaning "purification" or "cleansing". In literature and drama it refers to the effect of tragedy on its audience: in his Poetics, Aristotle states that tragedy, "through pity and fear, effects the catharsis of such emotions." Few terms in literary criticism have generated more debate, since Aristotle uses the word only once in the surviving Poetics without defining it.

Interpretations

Scholars have read catharsis in three main ways: as purgation, a quasi-medical discharge of pent-up emotion (the reading favoured by the classicist Jacob Bernays, who influenced his nephew-by-marriage Sigmund Freud's early theory); as purification, a moral refinement of the emotions so that they are felt toward the right objects; and as clarification, an intellectual understanding of the universal pattern revealed by the plot, in which pity and fear are resolved into comprehension. In the Politics, Aristotle also applies the term to the effect of certain kinds of music that relieve religious frenzy.

Use in psychology and culture

Breuer and Freud adopted the "cathartic method" in Studies on Hysteria (1895): symptoms were treated by re-experiencing and discharging repressed affect (abreaction). The word has since entered general use for any emotional release brought about by art, confession, or intense experience. Plays such as Oedipus Rex and The Persians are the standard test cases for the theory: an audience's grief and terror, whether for a fallen king or for a defeated enemy, is somehow transformed by the drama into pleasure and understanding.
