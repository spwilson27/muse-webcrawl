Kepler orbit

Placeholder image: elements of an elliptical orbit.

In celestial mechanics, a Kepler orbit is the motion of one body relative to another as an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola, which forms a two-dimensional orbital plane in three-dimensional space. It is the idealised solution of the two-body problem under Newtonian gravity, named after Johannes Kepler, whose laws of planetary motion describe it.

Orbital elements

A Keplerian orbit can be specified by six orbital elements: the semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis, and the position of the body along the orbit (true anomaly) at a given epoch. Together these define the size, shape, and orientation of the conic-section trajectory.

Kepler's equation

The position of the orbiting body is found by solving Kepler's equation, M = E − e sin E, relating the mean anomaly M to the eccentric anomaly E and eccentricity e. Because the equation is transcendental, it is solved numerically in practice.

Applications and limitations

Kepler orbits underlie spacecraft trajectory design and ephemeris computation, but real orbits deviate from the ideal because of perturbations from third bodies, non-spherical gravity fields, atmospheric drag, and relativistic effects.
