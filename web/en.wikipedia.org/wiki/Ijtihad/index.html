Ijtihad

Ijtihad (Arabic: اجتهاد, ijtihād, "effort, exertion") is an Islamic legal term referring to independent reasoning by an expert in Islamic law, or the thorough exertion of a jurist's mental faculty in finding a solution to a legal question. A jurist qualified to perform ijtihad is called a mujtahid. Ijtihad is contrasted with taqlid, the following of precedent and the rulings of earlier scholars without independent inquiry.

Role in Islamic law

In classical legal theory (usul al-fiqh), ijtihad is applied where the primary sources — the Quran and the Sunnah — do not give an explicit ruling, using techniques such as analogy (qiyas) and consideration of public welfare. The mujtahid was required to master Arabic, the revealed texts, consensus (ijma), and the methods of legal reasoning.

"Closing of the gate"

A long-standing scholarly debate concerns whether the "gate of ijtihad" was closed around the 10th century CE, after which jurists supposedly confined themselves to taqlid within the established schools (madhhabs). Many modern historians argue the picture was more varied, and reformers from the 18th century onward — including figures associated with the Salafi movement and modernists such as Muhammad Abduh — called for renewed ijtihad to meet new circumstances. In Twelver Shi'ism, ijtihad remained a living institution, with mujtahids (ayatollahs) issuing rulings followed by lay believers.
