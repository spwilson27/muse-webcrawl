Familial adenomatous polyposis

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant inherited condition in which numerous adenomatous polyps form mainly in the epithelium of the large intestine. While these polyps start out benign, malignant transformation into colon cancer occurs if they are left untreated. It is caused by a mutation in the APC gene, a tumour suppressor located on chromosome 5.

Specialty:Gastroenterology, oncology, genetics
Symptoms:Hundreds to thousands of colonic polyps, often asymptomatic at first
Cause:Mutation in the APC gene
Inheritance:Autosomal dominant
Treatment:Surveillance, prophylactic colectomy

Signs and symptoms

Polyps typically begin to develop during the teenage years, and in classic FAP the colon may be carpeted with hundreds to thousands of them. Most patients are asymptomatic until polyps grow large or cancer develops. Without treatment, colorectal cancer is essentially inevitable, with a mean age of diagnosis around 40 years. Extracolonic features may include polyps of the stomach and duodenum, desmoid tumours, osteomas of the jaw, dental abnormalities, epidermoid cysts, and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE). The combination of FAP with extracolonic manifestations was historically called Gardner syndrome.

Genetics

FAP results from germline mutations in the APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) gene. Loss of APC function removes a brake on Wnt signalling and cell proliferation, initiating polyp formation. In an attenuated form of the condition (AFAP), fewer polyps develop and cancer onset is later. A recessively inherited variant, MUTYH-associated polyposis, is caused by mutations in the MUTYH base-excision repair gene.

Management

Management centres on regular endoscopic surveillance beginning in adolescence, genetic testing of at-risk family members, and preventive surgery — typically a colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis or a proctocolectomy with ileal pouch — performed before cancer develops. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as sulindac and celecoxib can reduce polyp burden but do not replace surgery.
