Swamp blues
Swamp blues: sparse, echoing Louisiana blues of the 1950s–60s
Stylistic originsLouisiana blues, Delta blues, rhythm and blues, Cajun and Creole music
Cultural origins1950s, Baton Rouge and Crowley, Louisiana
Key figuresSlim Harpo, Lightnin' Slim, Lazy Lester, Lonesome Sundown, Silas Hogan
Key label/producerExcello Records; producer J. D. "Jay" Miller

Swamp blues is a subgenre of blues music that developed in Louisiana in the 1950s, centred on Baton Rouge and the recording studio of producer J. D. "Jay" Miller in Crowley. Released largely on the Excello label, swamp blues is characterised by a laid-back, loping groove, sparse instrumentation, heavy echo and reverb, prominent harmonica, and a humid, bayou atmosphere that distinguishes it from the harder Chicago blues of the same era.

Style and history

Miller recorded local musicians including Lightnin' Slim, whose "Rooster Blues" (1959) was an early success, Slim Harpo — the genre's biggest star, with "I'm a King Bee" (1957) and the US R&B number-one "Baby Scratch My Back" (1966) — Lazy Lester, Lonesome Sundown, Katie Webster and Silas Hogan. The sound drew on Delta blues, New Orleans rhythm and blues and the region's Cajun and Creole dance music; its relaxed rhythms also shaded into "swamp pop" ballads by artists such as Cookie and the Cupcakes.

Influence

Swamp blues records were prized in 1960s Britain: the Rolling Stones recorded "I'm a King Bee", and the Yardbirds, Animals and other British R&B groups covered Slim Harpo and Howlin' Wolf-influenced Excello material. The swampy aesthetic later resurfaced in the term "swamp rock" for Creedence Clearwater Revival and Tony Joe White, and swamp blues remains celebrated through Baton Rouge's blues festivals and reissue programmes.
