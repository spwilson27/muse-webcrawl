Scots law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scots law is the legal system of Scotland. It is a mixed legal system, containing elements of both civil law (from its Roman-Dutch and feudal heritage and the institutional writers — Stair's Institutions, 1681, is its classic authority) and common law (absorbed through centuries of contact with England). The Acts of Union 1707 preserved Scotland's courts, law and church; the Court of Session (1532) remains the supreme civil court, the High Court of Justiciary the supreme criminal court, sitting in Edinburgh.

Distinctive features include the fifteen-member jury in criminal trials deciding by simple majority, the third verdict “not proven” alongside guilty and not guilty (long debated, with abolition proposed in recent Scottish legislation), the requirement of corroboration in criminal cases, a separate property and succession law, no doctrine of consideration in contract, and delict rather than tort. Since 1998 the Scottish Parliament legislates on most domestic matters, producing growing divergence from England (university tuition, land reform, criminal procedure). Civil appeals reach the UK Supreme Court; criminal appeals end in Scotland.
