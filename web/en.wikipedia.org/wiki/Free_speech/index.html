Free speech

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · For freedom of speech as a legal right, see Freedom of speech.

Free speech is the principle that individuals and communities should be able to articulate their opinions and ideas without fear of retaliation, censorship, or legal sanction. It underlies the legal right of freedom of speech, recognised in Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and in instruments such as the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, but the principle is broader than any single law: it also describes a social and political norm valued in democratic societies as a check on power and a means of discovering truth.

Justifications

Classical defences of free speech include the argument from truth, articulated by John Stuart Mill in On Liberty (1859): suppressing an opinion robs humanity whether the opinion is true (losing the chance to exchange error for truth) or false (losing the clearer perception of truth produced by its collision with error). Other justifications stress self-government — citizens cannot govern themselves without open discussion — individual autonomy and self-fulfilment, and tolerance as a civic virtue.

History

The ideal has roots in ancient Athens, where parrhesia (frank speech) and isegoria (equal right to address the assembly) were prized, though limited to citizens. Early modern landmarks include John Milton's Areopagitica (1644) against press licensing, the English Bill of Rights' protection of parliamentary speech (1689), and the First Amendment (1791), which bars the U.S. Congress from abridging "the freedom of speech, or of the press". The twentieth century saw the principle entrenched in international human-rights law while remaining contested in practice worldwide.

Limits and debates

Almost no legal system treats speech as absolutely protected. Common exceptions include incitement to imminent violence, defamation, obscenity, true threats, and fraudulent commercial claims; many democracies also prohibit certain forms of hate speech, a category the United States largely protects. Contemporary debates concern speech on privately owned online platforms — which are generally not bound by constitutional free-speech rules written for governments — disinformation, and the tension between free expression and equality or public health. The "marketplace of ideas" metaphor remains influential, as do critiques that formal freedom of speech can coexist with large inequalities in whose speech is actually heard.
