Mantineia (Greek: Μαντίνεια) was an ancient Greek city in Arcadia in the Peloponnese, situated on a plain north of modern Tripoli. Formed by the union (synoecism) of five villages around 500 BC, it became the leading city of eastern Arcadia and the scene of two of the most famous hoplite battles of classical Greece.

History

Mantineia was generally democratic in constitution and frequently opposed to Sparta, though geography constrained its policy. At the First Battle of Mantineia in 418 BC, during the Peloponnesian War, Sparta defeated a coalition of Argos, Mantineia, Elis and Athens in the largest land battle of the war. In 385 BC Sparta punished the city by breaking it back into its constituent villages (dioecism); the Mantineians refounded the city after Sparta's defeat at Leuctra in 371 BC.

At the Second Battle of Mantineia in 362 BC, the Theban general Epaminondas defeated a Spartan-led coalition using his oblique phalanx tactics, but was killed in the moment of victory, leaving Greece, in Xenophon's phrase, in still greater confusion. In 222 BC the city was sacked by the Macedonian king Antigonus III Doson and refounded as Antigoneia; the emperor Hadrian later restored its ancient name.

Archaeology

Excavations have revealed the elliptical city walls, the agora, and a theatre. The site's most famous artwork, the Mantineia Base — three relief panels attributed to the school of Praxiteles, depicting Apollo, Marsyas and a Muse — is now in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.
