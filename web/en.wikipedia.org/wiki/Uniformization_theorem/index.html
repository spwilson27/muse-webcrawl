Uniformization theorem

Every Riemann surface is a quotient of the sphere, the plane, or the hyperbolic disc.

In mathematics, the uniformization theorem states that every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally equivalent to one of three Riemann surfaces: the open unit disc, the complex plane, or the Riemann sphere. It follows that every Riemann surface admits a complete Riemannian metric of constant curvature, and that every Riemann surface is a quotient of one of the three simply connected surfaces by a free, properly discontinuous group action.

Statement and classification

The theorem generalises the Riemann mapping theorem from conformal maps of simply connected domains in the plane to abstract Riemann surfaces. A compact Riemann surface of genus zero is conformally the Riemann sphere; a surface of genus one (a torus) is a quotient of the complex plane by a lattice; and every surface of genus two or more is a quotient of the unit disc — equivalently the hyperbolic plane — by a Fuchsian group, and carries a hyperbolic metric. The three geometries correspond to positive, zero and negative curvature.

History

The theorem was conjectured in the 19th century and proved independently in 1907 by Henri Poincaré and Paul Koebe, following Felix Klein's and Poincaré's earlier work on automorphic functions. It became a cornerstone of complex analysis and Riemann surface theory. In the 21st century, William Thurston's geometrization programme — completed by Grigori Perelman's proof of the Poincaré conjecture using Ricci flow — is often described as a three-dimensional analogue of uniformization.
