Citroën Méhari

The Citroën Méhari is a lightweight recreational and utility vehicle manufactured by the French automaker Citroën from 1968 to 1988. Based on the chassis and mechanicals of the Citroën Dyane 6, itself derived from the 2CV, the Méhari had bodywork made of ABS plastic, making it cheap, light and resistant to minor damage. About 144,953 units were built. The name méhari refers to a fast-running dromedary camel used by the French camel corps in North Africa.

The Méhari, a plastic-bodied leisure vehicle based on the Dyane.

Design and production

The Méhari was designed by former French Air Force officer Count Roland de La Poype's company SEAB, which developed the plastic body. Citroën adopted the design and launched the car in May 1968. It used the 602 cc air-cooled flat-twin engine of the Dyane 6, front-wheel drive and the characteristic interconnected suspension of the 2CV family. The open body had a folding soft top and could be hosed out; payload was about 400 kg. A four-wheel-drive version was produced from 1979, distinguished by a spare wheel mounted on the bonnet, and a limited run served with the French army and the Irish Defence Forces.

Variants and markets

The Méhari was sold in numerous countries, assembled locally in some, and was popular as a beach car in southern France and Spain. In the United States it was briefly marketed (1969–1970) as the Citicar-adjacent leisure import before tightening safety rules ended imports. A van-bodied sibling, the Méhari's platform also underpinned the FAF (Facile à Fabriquer) models for developing markets.

Legacy

Citroën revived the name for the battery-electric E-Méhari convertible in 2016, styled after the Cactus concept. The original Méhari has a strong enthusiast following, and reproduction plastic body panels keep many in use.
