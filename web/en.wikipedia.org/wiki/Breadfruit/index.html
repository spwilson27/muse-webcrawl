Breadfruit

Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis) is a species of flowering tree in the mulberry and jackfruit family (Moraceae) believed to have originated in New Guinea and the Indo-Malay region. Its large, starchy fruit has been a staple food in Oceania for millennia and was carried by Austronesian voyagers across the Pacific. Breadfruit is closely related to breadnut and jackfruit, and a mature tree can yield 200 or more fruits per season, making it one of the highest-yielding food plants in the world.

Description

Breadfruit trees grow to a height of more than 26 metres, with large, glossy, deeply lobed leaves. All parts contain a milky latex. The fruit is a multiple fruit developing from the whole inflorescence; individual fruits are round to oval, 10–30 cm in diameter, with a rough green to yellow-green skin and pale, starchy flesh. Most cultivated varieties in the Pacific are seedless; seeded forms are often called breadnut. The name derives from the texture of the moderately ripe cooked fruit, which resembles freshly baked bread and has a potato-like flavour.

Cultivation and cuisine

Breadfruit is propagated mainly from root suckers and root cuttings. It thrives in tropical lowlands with high rainfall. The fruit is eaten roasted, baked, boiled, fried, or fermented — in Polynesia a fermented paste (such as Samoan masi or Marquesan mā) was stored in pits as a famine reserve. In the Caribbean, where breadfruit was introduced in the 1790s, it is a staple in dishes such as oil down, the national dish of Grenada. Breadfruit is rich in complex carbohydrates and gluten-free; it is increasingly milled into flour.

History

Austronesian peoples spread breadfruit throughout the Pacific as one of their "canoe plants". Europeans first described it in the 17th century. In 1787 HMS Bounty, commanded by William Bligh, was sent to Tahiti to collect breadfruit plants intended as a cheap food for enslaved labourers in the British West Indies — the voyage that ended in the famous mutiny of 1789. Bligh succeeded on a second voyage in 1793. Interest in breadfruit as a climate-resilient food-security crop has revived in the 21st century.
