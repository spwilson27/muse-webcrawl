Flux

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics and applied mathematics, flux is the amount of a physical quantity that passes through a surface per unit time. In the general mathematical formulation, the flux of a vector field F through a surface S is the surface integral Φ = ∬S F · dA, and by the divergence theorem it equals the volume integral of the divergence of F over the enclosed region.

Types of flux

Common examples include magnetic flux (measured in webers), the quantity underlying Faraday's law of induction and Gauss's law for magnetism; electric flux, appearing in Gauss's law; mass flux and volumetric flux in fluid dynamics; heat flux in thermodynamics; radiant flux and luminous flux in radiometry and photometry; and neutron flux in nuclear engineering. In transport phenomena, flux densities (such as Fick's law flux for diffusion) describe the rate of transfer per unit area.

Terminology

The word comes from the Latin fluxus, "flow". Isaac Newton and later James Clerk Maxwell developed the concept in connection with fluid analogies for fields; Maxwell's treatment of lines of force passing through surfaces made flux central to classical electromagnetism. Distinct from the physics concept, flux also denotes substances used to promote melting or joining in metallurgy and soldering.
