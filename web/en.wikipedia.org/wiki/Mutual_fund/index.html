Mutual fund

[Image: a pie-chart graphic showing a diversified fund portfolio split across shares, bonds and cash]

A mutual fund is a pooled Investment vehicle that collects money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of securities — shares, bonds or money-market instruments. Investors own units priced daily at net asset value (NAV); the fund is run by a management company for a fee (the expense ratio), under rules on diversification, disclosure and custody.

Types and significance

Funds divide into actively managed funds, whose managers pick securities aiming to beat a benchmark, and index funds, which track a market index such as the S&P 500 at very low cost. Research consistently finds most active funds underperform their benchmark after fees over long periods, fuelling the index-fund growth pioneered by Vanguard (founded by John Bogle, first retail index fund 1976). Mutual funds dominate retirement saving in the United States (401(k) plans) and, with their exchange-traded cousins (ETFs), hold tens of trillions of dollars worldwide. Closed-end funds, money-market funds and target-date funds are important variants; conduct and fees are regulated by bodies such as the SEC under the Investment Company Act of 1940.
