Music of Tuva

The music of Tuva, a Russian republic in southern Siberia bordering Mongolia, is best known internationally for throat singing (Tuvan: khöömei), a technique in which a single vocalist produces two or more distinct pitches simultaneously. Tuvan music reflects the nomadic herding culture of the Tuvan people, the sounds of the steppe landscape, and the region's shamanic and Buddhist traditions.

Throat singing

Khöömei is the general Tuvan term for throat singing, encompassing several distinct styles. In sygyt, a clear, flute-like harmonic melody floats above a sustained drone. Kargyraa produces a deep, growling undertone, generated with the ventricular folds. Other styles include borbangnadyr (a warbling, stream-like effect), ezengileer (imitating the rhythm of horse stirrups), and chylandyk. Traditionally, singers performed alone outdoors, imitating the sounds of wind, water, birds, and animals — a practice with roots in animist beliefs that natural sounds carry spiritual significance.

Instruments

Traditional instruments include the doshpuluur, a two- or three-stringed long-necked lute; the igil, a two-stringed horse-head fiddle played with a bow; the byzaanchy, a four-stringed spike fiddle; and the khomus (Jew's harp). Storytelling epics were traditionally accompanied by these instruments, and the horse — central to Tuvan life — is celebrated in both the carving of the igil's scroll and in song lyrics.

Modern developments

During the Soviet period Tuvan music was promoted through state folk ensembles. After 1991, groups such as Huun-Huur-Tu (founded 1992) and Yat-Kha brought throat singing to world-music audiences, while the ensemble Alash and vocalist Sainkho Namtchylak fused traditional techniques with jazz, rock, and experimental music. Throat singing is inscribed on UNESCO-related heritage lists and remains a strong emblem of Tuvan identity; the republic's capital, Kyzyl, hosts festivals and a monument marking the geographic centre of Asia.
