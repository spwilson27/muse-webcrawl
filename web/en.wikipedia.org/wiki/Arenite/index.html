Arenite

Arenite (from Latin arena, "sand") is a sedimentary rock composed of sand-sized grains — between 0.0625 and 2 mm in diameter — with little or no fine matrix. In the widely used classification of F. J. Pettijohn, arenites are sandstones containing less than 15 per cent matrix (mud-grade material between the grains); sandstones with more matrix are called wackes or greywackes. The term separates sandstones whose pore spaces are filled with cement from those clogged with detrital mud.

Types

Arenites are subdivided by the composition of their framework grains. Quartz arenites contain more than 90–95 per cent quartz, are usually white or light-coloured, and represent the most compositionally mature sandstones, formed by prolonged weathering, transport, and reworking that destroyed less stable minerals — they are typical of stable continental interiors and passive margins, and make excellent aquifers and petroleum reservoirs. Arkoses (arkosic arenites) contain abundant feldspar, especially potassium feldspar, derived from granitic source rocks with limited transport. Lithic arenites are rich in fragments of other rocks and are common near active mountain belts and volcanic terrains.

Formation

Arenites accumulate in environments where fine mud is winnowed away by currents — beaches, dunes, braided rivers, shallow marine bars, and turbidity-current channels. During burial, compaction and the precipitation of cements such as quartz, calcite, or iron oxides lithify the sand into rock, while the low matrix content preserves comparatively high porosity and permeability.

Significance

Because grain composition records the tectonic setting and climate of the source area, arenites are a primary tool of provenance studies in sedimentary geology. Quartz arenites of great antiquity, such as those of the North American craton, testify to long periods of tectonic stability, and thick arenite sequences host major groundwater and hydrocarbon reserves.
