Emperor Wu of Han

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Maoling tomb mound of Emperor Wu, near Xi'an]

Emperor Wu of Han (156 – 29 March 87 BC), born Liu Che, was the seventh Han emperor of China, reigning 141–87 BC for 54 years — one of the longest and most consequential reigns in Chinese history. Under Wu the Han turned from consolidation to expansion: the Xiongnu confederation was broken by the campaigns of Wei Qing and Huo Qubing, the Hexi Corridor and Tarim Basin opened (the Silk Road, launched by Zhang Qian's missions), Northern Vietnam (Nanyue) and Korea (Wiman Joseon) annexed, and the empire's borders approached their greatest pre-modern extent. At home he enthroned Confucianism as state ideology (with Dong Zhongshu), founded the Imperial University, nationalised salt, iron and coinage, and left the treasury strained by war — prompting his late-life repentance (the Luntai Edict, 89 BC) and the succession settlement under the regent Huo Guang.

The great expansion

Wu inherited full granaries and a policy of appeasing the Xiongnu with tribute and princesses; he ended it. From 133 BC (the failed Mayi ambush) the war ran for decades: Wei Qing's reconquest of the Ordos (127 BC), Huo Qubing's seizure of the Hexi Corridor (121 BC) and the annihilating Mobei campaign (119 BC) that drove the Xiongnu court beyond the Gobi. Zhang Qian's journeys (138–126, 119–115 BC) — sent to ally with the Yuezhi, returning instead with the world's map rewritten — opened Central Asia: Ferghana's 'heavenly horses' were won by expedition (101 BC), protectorates planted in the Tarim, and silk began its road west.

Costs and repentance

The price was paid at home: currency manipulation, salt and iron monopolies, sale of offices, crushing campaigns (the 103 BC disaster, Li Guangli's surrender), and the witchcraft terror of 91 BC that killed the crown prince Liu Ju and tens of thousands. The Luntai Edict of 89 BC — rejecting a new Central Asian garrison, confessing the burdens — is read as the great self-correction; Wu died in 87 BC leaving the child Zhao Di and Huo Guang's regency. His tomb, the Maoling pyramid-mound, is the grandest of the Han imperial tombs, and his era remains the template of Chinese imperial greatness and its limits.
