Mistake of fact

A mistake of fact is a legal defence in criminal law in which the defendant, because of an erroneous belief about a factual circumstance, lacked the mental state (mens rea) required for the offence. It is contrasted with mistake of law, which generally is no excuse, under the maxim ignorantia juris non excusat.

AreaCriminal law
EffectNegates mens rea where the belief, if true, makes the act lawful
ContrastMistake of law

Principles

If a person takes another's umbrella honestly believing it is their own, the intent to steal is absent and theft is not committed. For offences requiring a specific intent or knowledge, even an unreasonable honest belief may suffice; for negligence-based or strict liability offences, the mistake usually must be reasonable, or is no defence at all. In common-law jurisdictions, classic authorities include R v Tolson (1889) on bigamy and DPP v Morgan (1976) on honest belief in consent, the latter since modified by statute in England and Wales.

Applications

Mistake of fact arises in cases of mistaken identity of property, consent in assault and sexual offences, self-defence based on a mistaken belief of attack, and possession offences where the defendant did not know the nature of an item. US law treats claim of right in theft and "mistake of age" inconsistently across states.

Related doctrines

In contract and property law, "mistake" also refers to errors in formation that may make agreements voidable — a distinct civil doctrine. See mens rea, mistake of law and strict liability.
