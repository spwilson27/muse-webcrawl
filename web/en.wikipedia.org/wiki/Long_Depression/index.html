Long Depression

Period1873–1896 (dates vary by country)
TriggerPanic of 1873
Regions affectedEurope and North America
Key featuresDeflation, falling prices and profits, railway overbuilding

The Long Depression was a worldwide price and economic depression that began with the Panic of 1873 and lasted, depending on the country and the metric used, until about 1896. It was most severe in Europe and the United States, which had been experiencing strong economic growth fuelled by the Second Industrial Revolution and the completion of major railway networks. At the time it was called the "Great Depression", a name later transferred to the downturn of the 1930s.

Course

The panic began in Vienna in May 1873 and spread to the United States, where the failure of the banking house Jay Cooke and Company in September triggered bank runs and the collapse of a speculative railway boom. The New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days. In the United States the National Bureau of Economic Research dates a contraction from October 1873 to March 1879 — at 65 months the longest on record, exceeding even the Great Depression of the 1930s.

Character

Unlike later depressions, the Long Depression was characterised less by falling output — industrial production continued to grow in many countries — than by persistent deflation: prices fell steadily, squeezing profits and raising the real burden of debts. Agriculture suffered acutely as grain prices collapsed in the face of competition from the American prairies and Russia, prompting demands for protective tariffs and, in the United States, the free silver movement. Waves of further crises followed, including the Panic of 1884 and the Panic of 1893. Historians debate how far the period deserves the name of a single depression, but agree on its deflationary character and its role in driving the first great wave of globalisation-era protectionism and imperial expansion.
