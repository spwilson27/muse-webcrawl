Four-stroke engine

Four-stroke engine
TypeInternal combustion engine
StrokesIntake, compression, power, exhaust
InventorNicolaus Otto (practical form, 1876)

A four-stroke (also four-cycle) engine is a type of internal combustion engine in which the piston completes four separate strokes while turning the crankshaft twice. A stroke is the full travel of the piston along the cylinder in one direction.

The four strokes

Intake: the intake valve opens and the piston descends, drawing in the air–fuel mixture (or air alone in a diesel). Compression: both valves close and the piston rises, compressing the charge. Power: the mixture is ignited — by a spark plug in a petrol engine, or by compression heat in a diesel — and the expanding gases drive the piston down. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens and the piston rises, pushing out the burnt gases.

History

The four-stroke principle was described by Alphonse Beau de Rochas in 1862 and put into commercially successful practice by Nicolaus Otto in 1876. The Otto cycle engine became the standard powerplant for automobiles, motorcycles, aircraft and small machinery throughout the following century.

Comparison with two-stroke engines

Because each cylinder fires only once every two revolutions, a four-stroke engine delivers power less frequently than a two-stroke of the same size, but it burns fuel more completely, runs more cleanly and quietly, and separates lubrication from the fuel supply — advantages that have made it dominant in road vehicles.
