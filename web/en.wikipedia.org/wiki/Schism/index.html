Schism

A schism is a formal division within a religious body

A schism (pronounced /ˈsɪzəm/ or /ˈskɪzəm/) is a split or division between people, usually members of an organisation, movement, or religious denomination. The word derives from the Greek schisma, "a cleft, split", from schizein, "to split".

In Christianity

In Christian theology, a schism is a breach of communion between churches or within a church. It is traditionally distinguished from heresy: heresy concerns doctrine, whereas schism concerns communion and obedience — schismatics may hold orthodox doctrine while refusing communion with the larger body. Major historical schisms include:

  • The East–West Schism (1054), which divided Christianity between the Roman Catholic Church in the West and the Eastern Orthodox Church in the East.
  • The Western Schism (1378–1417), during which rival popes at Rome, Avignon, and Pisa claimed authority.
  • The divisions of the Protestant Reformation in the 16th century, often described as schisms by the Roman Catholic Church.

Other uses

By extension, the term is used for splits in political parties, movements, and other organisations, and in Islam for the historic Sunni–Shia division, which arose from a dispute over succession to Muhammad.
