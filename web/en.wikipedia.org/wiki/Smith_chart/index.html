Smith chart

The Smith chart (sometimes also called the Smith diagram by Anglophone users) is a graphical calculator or nomogram designed for electrical and electronics engineers specialising in radio frequency (RF) engineering to assist in solving problems with transmission lines and matching circuits. It was independently invented by Phillip H. Smith of Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1939 (with a similar chart published by Tōsaku Mizuhashi in Japan in 1937).

Description

The Smith chart is a plot of the complex reflection coefficient within a unit circle, overlaid with a grid representing normalised impedance. Circles of constant resistance and arcs of constant reactance form the characteristic pattern. Because it plots a normalised quantity, the same chart works for any characteristic impedance. Points can be rotated around the chart's centre to represent movement along a transmission line, with one full revolution corresponding to half a wavelength.

Uses

Engineers use the Smith chart to determine input impedance of a line terminated in a given load, to design impedance-matching networks with stubs and lumped components, to read standing-wave ratio directly from the radius of a constant-SWR circle, and to design and stabilise RF amplifiers. Although modern network analysers and simulation software compute these quantities numerically, the chart remains a standard teaching tool and many instruments can display measurements in Smith-chart form.
