Consensus

For decision-making by a group, see Consensus decision-making. For agreement in science, see Scientific consensus.
Consensus emerges when members of a group converge on a shared position after deliberation.

Consensus is a state of general agreement among members of a group or community, or the process by which such agreement is reached. The word derives from Latin consensus, "agreement, accord", from consentire, "to feel together". A consensus may be explicit and unanimous, or it may denote a broad convergence of opinion in which dissenters accept the prevailing view as legitimate or workable.

Forms

Consensus decision-making is a formal process used by groups from Quaker meetings to activist networks and open-source projects: proposals are discussed and modified until no participant maintains a principled objection, though options such as "standing aside" allow members to register disagreement without blocking. This differs from unanimity (everyone's first preference) and from majority voting, which can leave a large minority overruled. International bodies such as the WTO and UN climate conferences often operate by consensus, giving each member an effective veto.

In technical standard-setting the Internet Engineering Task Force famously works by "rough consensus" — a substantial majority with minority concerns addressed where possible — a phrase enshrined in the dictum "rough consensus and running code".

Other senses

  • Scientific consensus: the collective position of a scientific community on a question where evidence has converged, e.g. on evolution or anthropogenic climate change; consensus here results from evidence and peer review rather than negotiation.
  • Medicine: consensus statements and conferences (such as NIH Consensus Development Conferences) synthesise expert agreement to guide practice.
  • Distributed computing: the consensus problem asks how a set of processes or nodes agree on a value despite failures, solved by protocols such as Paxos and Raft and by the proof-of-work mechanism of blockchains.
  • Historical/political: "consensus history" and the post-war "consensus politics" of mid-20th-century Britain describe eras in which the main parties shared broad policy ground.

Criticism

Consensus processes are praised for inclusiveness and durable outcomes but criticised for slowness, for pressure toward conformity ("groupthink"), and for empowering vetoes; some political theorists, such as Chantal Mouffe, defend adversarial ("agonistic") politics against an ideal of consensus that suppresses legitimate conflict.
