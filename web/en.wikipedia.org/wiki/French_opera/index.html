French opera

The Palais Garnier, home of the Paris Opera since 1875.

French opera is the tradition of opera composition in France and in the French language, one of the major national operatic traditions of Europe. From its origins at the court of Louis XIV it developed distinctive conventions — a five-act structure, prominent ballet, spectacle and declamatory recitative shaped by the rhythms of French verse — that set it apart from Italian opera and repeatedly brought the two traditions into rivalry.

Baroque origins

Jean-Baptiste Lully founded the French operatic tradition with his tragédies en musique, beginning with Cadmus et Hermione (1673), written with librettist Philippe Quinault. Jean-Philippe Rameau renewed the genre in the 1730s–50s with works such as Hippolyte et Aricie, Castor et Pollux and Les Indes galantes, while the rival opéra comique genre — spoken dialogue mixed with music — flourished at the fair theatres. Christoph Willibald Gluck's Paris operas of the 1770s, including Iphigénie en Tauride, reformed the tradition and influenced the young Mozart.

19th century

The 19th century saw the rise of grand opéra — vast five-act historical spectacles by composers such as Giacomo Meyerbeer (Les Huguenots), Auber and Halévy — and the more intimate opéra lyrique of Gounod (Faust) and Bizet, whose Carmen (1875), an opéra comique, became the most famous French opera of all. Massenet (Manon, Werther) and later Debussy, whose Pelléas et Mélisande (1902) opened a modernist path, carried the tradition into the 20th century, followed by Ravel, Poulenc (Dialogues des Carmélites) and Messiaen (Saint François d'Assise).
