Diploidisation

FieldEvolutionary genetics
Related conceptsPolyploidy, whole genome duplication, fractionation

Diploidisation is the evolutionary process by which a polyploid genome — one containing more than two complete sets of chromosomes, usually following a whole genome duplication — gradually returns to behaving like a diploid genome. Over time, duplicated genes are lost or diverge in function, chromosomes rearrange, and meiosis stabilises so that chromosomes pair as bivalents rather than multivalents.

Mechanism

After a whole genome duplication, most duplicate gene copies are redundant. Many are lost through a process called fractionation, in which deletions and disabling mutations remove one copy, often biased toward one of the two subgenomes. Surviving duplicates may be retained because of dosage constraints or because they have partitioned the original gene's functions (subfunctionalisation) or evolved new ones (neofunctionalisation).

Examples

Diploidisation is well documented in flowering plants, whose genomes record multiple ancient duplications, and in yeasts: the genome of Saccharomyces cerevisiae descends from a duplication about 100 million years ago, after which most duplicate genes were lost. Evidence of ancient diploidised duplications also exists in vertebrates, including the two rounds of whole genome duplication early in vertebrate evolution.
