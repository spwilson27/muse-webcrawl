Sectarianism in Glasgow

An Old Firm derby in Glasgow

Sectarianism in Glasgow refers to the long-standing religious and political division, principally between Catholics and Protestants, that has shaped parts of the social and cultural life of Glasgow, Scotland. The divide is most visibly expressed through the rivalry between the city's two dominant football clubs, Celtic and Rangers — the "Old Firm" — but historically also touched employment, housing, education and marching traditions.

Origins

Large-scale Irish immigration to Glasgow in the nineteenth century, much of it Catholic and concentrated during and after the Great Famine, met an established Protestant Scottish population. Competition for jobs and housing, Irish nationalism and Ulster unionism, and separate Catholic schooling sustained distinct communal identities. Celtic was founded in 1887 among the Irish Catholic community of the East End, while Rangers became identified with the Protestant, unionist community.

Manifestations

Football matches between the two clubs have periodically been accompanied by violence, sectarian singing and banners, and disputes over songs and symbols — including the Union Flag and the Irish tricolour — have drawn in politicians and the media. Orange Order parades, particularly around the Twelfth of July, and Irish republican marches are another focus of tension and policing attention.

Responses and decline

Anti-sectarian initiatives have included the charity Nil by Mouth, founded in 1995, school twinning programmes, and legislation: the Offensive Behaviour at Football and Threatening Communications (Scotland) Act 2012, repealed in 2018, and provisions of the Hate Crime and Public Order (Scotland) Act 2021. Researchers and commentators debate how deep sectarian discrimination remains in modern Glasgow, with many pointing to generational decline alongside persistent flashpoints around football and parades.
