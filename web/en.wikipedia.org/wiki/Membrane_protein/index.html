Membrane protein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Membrane proteins are proteins that interact with, or are part of, biological membranes. They include integral membrane proteins permanently anchored within the lipid bilayer, and peripheral membrane proteins that are only temporarily attached to the membrane or to integral proteins. Membrane proteins constitute a significant fraction of the proteome — roughly 20–30% of all genes in most genomes encode them — and they are the targets of over half of all modern medicinal drugs.

Categories

Integral (intrinsic) membrane proteins have one or more segments embedded in the bilayer. Transmembrane proteins span the membrane, usually with one or more alpha-helices of hydrophobic amino acids, or with beta-barrels (common in bacterial outer membranes, e.g. porins). Other integral proteins are anchored by lipid modifications or a single leaflet-spanning helix.

Peripheral (extrinsic) membrane proteins attach to membrane surfaces through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds, or associations with integral proteins, and can often be released by changes in pH or ionic strength.

Functions

Membrane proteins perform most of the specific work of membranes:

  • Transport — channels, carriers and pumps (e.g. ion channels, aquaporins, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase).
  • Reception and signalling — receptors such as G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), which bind hormones and neurotransmitters.
  • Enzymatic activity — e.g. enzymes of the respiratory chain in mitochondria and of photosynthesis in chloroplasts.
  • Adhesion and recognition — cell-adhesion molecules and antigen-presenting proteins such as MHC molecules.

Study

Membrane proteins are notoriously difficult to crystallise because they must be extracted from the bilayer with detergents. Advances in X-ray crystallography, electron crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy have greatly expanded the number of known membrane protein structures, beginning with the photosynthetic reaction centre (1985) and the potassium channel KcsA (1998).
