German Rentenmark

Unit: Rentenmark (RM)
Introduced: 15 October 1923
Issuing authority: Deutsche Rentenbank
Replaced: Papiermark
Exchange rate: 1 RM = 1012 Papiermark

The Rentenmark (German: [ˈʁɛntn̩maʁk]) was a currency issued by the Deutsche Rentenbank in October 1923 to stop the hyperinflation then devastating the German economy. It replaced the Papiermark, which had become effectively worthless, at a rate of one trillion (1012) Papiermark to one Rentenmark.

History

After World War I, Germany's finances collapsed under war debts, reparations under the Treaty of Versailles, and large budget deficits financed by printing money. By the autumn of 1923 the Papiermark's value had fallen so far that prices doubled within days. Finance minister Hans Luther and currency commissioner Hjalmar Schacht devised the Rentenmark as a stabilisation measure.

Backing and stabilisation

The Rentenmark was nominally backed by a mortgage (Rente) on German agricultural land and industrial assets, valued at 3.2 billion gold marks. Although this backing could not realistically be redeemed, it restored public confidence because the quantity issued was strictly limited. The new currency was pegged at 4.2 Rentenmark to the US dollar, the pre-war gold parity. Prices stabilised almost immediately, an outcome sometimes called the "miracle of the Rentenmark."

Successor

In August 1924 the Rentenmark was joined — and in practice superseded — by the Reichsmark, issued under the Dawes Plan at par with the Rentenmark, which continued to circulate alongside it until 1948.
