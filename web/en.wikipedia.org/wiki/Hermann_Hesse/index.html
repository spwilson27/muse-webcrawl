Hermann Hesse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hermann Hesse in 1926, pipe in hand at Montagnola: the novelist in the years of Steppenwolf]

Hermann Karl Hesse (2 July 1877 – 9 August 1962) was a German-Swiss poet, novelist and painter, awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1946. His novels of self-seeking — Demian (1919), Siddhartha (1922), Steppenwolf (1927), Narcissus and Goldmund (1930) and The Glass Bead Game (1943) — made him a prophet to the counter-culture of the 1960s, which rediscovered the writer who had spent his life staging the quarrel between bourgeois order and the artist's soul.

Born in Calw to Pietist missionaries (his mother was born in India), Hesse rebelled through boarding schools and a suicide attempt to a bookseller's apprenticeship and slow self-education; Peter Camenzind (1904) freed him to write. The First World War — his pacifism denounced in Germany, his personal crises deepened by his father's death and his wife's illness — drove him to Jungian analysis and to Demian, published under a pseudonym and hailed as the voice of returning soldiers.

The mature novels

Siddhartha, from his 1911 India journey and his family's missionary past, follows a Brahmin's son to enlightenment by a river; Steppenwolf splits Harry Haller between wolf and man in the jazz age; The Glass Bead Game, his last and longest novel, imagines a future province of the mind, Castalia, and its game's master Knecht — the work of his Swiss years at Montagnola, where Thomas Mann and a procession of pilgrims visited.

Reception

Dismissed mid-century in Germany, Hesse was canonised by American youth culture (the band Steppenwolf took its name from the novel) and remains among the most translated German authors — his books' promise that the self can be remade speaks anew to each generation of the disaffected young.
