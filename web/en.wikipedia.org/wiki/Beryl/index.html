Beryl

Beryl
FormulaBe3Al2(SiO3)6
Crystal systemHexagonal
Hardness7.5–8 (Mohs)
VarietiesEmerald, aquamarine, morganite, heliodor, goshenite, red beryl
SourcesGranitic pegmatites; Colombia, Brazil, Russia, USA

Beryl is a mineral composed of beryllium aluminium silicate, Be3Al2(SiO3)6. Well-known varieties of beryl include the green emerald and the blue aquamarine, both prized gemstones. Pure beryl is colourless, and its varieties are coloured by trace impurities: chromium and vanadium give emerald's green, iron gives aquamarine's blue and heliodor's yellow, and manganese gives the pink of morganite and the rare red beryl.

Occurrence

Beryl occurs most commonly in granitic pegmatites, where crystals can reach enormous sizes — specimens several metres long have been found in Maine and South Dakota — and also in mica schists and, for emerald, in hydrothermal deposits such as those of Colombia. It is the principal ore of the metal beryllium, although most industrial beryllium today comes from bertrandite.

Varieties

  • Emerald — deep green, coloured by chromium and/or vanadium.
  • Aquamarine — blue to blue-green; the March birthstone.
  • Morganite — pink to peach, named for banker J. P. Morgan.
  • Heliodor / golden beryl — yellow to golden.
  • Goshenite — colourless beryl.
  • Red beryl (bixbite) — a very rare raspberry-red variety from Utah.

History

Beryl has been valued since antiquity: the Ptolemaic emerald mines of Egypt supplied the ancient world, and lenses of beryl were used in early spectacles — the word "beryl" ultimately derives, through Latin and Greek bēryllos, from a term for a pale sea-green gemstone.
