The Cordeliers, officially the Society of the Friends of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, was a radical political club of the French Revolution. Founded in Paris in 1790 and named after the Cordeliers district — and the former Franciscan ("Cordelier") convent where it met — the club championed direct democracy, universal male suffrage, and the right of insurrection, and admitted members for a small subscription, making it far more socially open than the Jacobin Club.

The Cordeliers met in a former Franciscan convent on the Left Bank.

History

The club grew out of the Cordeliers district assembly, which had protected journalist Jean-Paul Marat from arrest. As a club it campaigned against the veto and the distinction between "active" and "passive" citizens, petitioned for the king's deposition after the Flight to Varennes (1791), and was implicated in the Champ de Mars petition that ended in a massacre of demonstrators. Cordelier militants, including Georges Danton, played leading roles in the insurrection of 10 August 1792 that overthrew the monarchy.

During the National Convention, Cordeliers such as Danton and Camille Desmoulins were prominent Montagnards, while the club's left wing — the Hébertists around Jacques-René Hébert and the newspaper Le Père Duchesne — pushed for dechristianisation and harsher revolutionary measures. In the power struggles of spring 1794, the Hébertists were arrested and executed in March; days later Danton, Desmoulins, and their allies (the "Indulgents") followed them to the guillotine, and the club, its leadership destroyed, faded from existence.

Ideology and significance

The Cordeliers articulated a politics of popular sovereignty, suspicion of representatives, and social grievance — price controls, punishment of hoarders — that made them a conduit between the Parisian sections and the revolutionary government, and an enduring reference point for later democratic and socialist movements.
