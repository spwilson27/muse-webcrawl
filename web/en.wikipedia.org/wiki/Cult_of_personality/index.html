Cult of personality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cult of personality arises when a regime uses mass media, propaganda, spectacle and repression to create an idealised, heroic and worshipful image of a leader, presented as uniquely wise, virtuous and indispensable. The term was popularised by Nikita Khrushchev's “Secret Speech” of 25 February 1956, On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences, denouncing the cult of Joseph Stalin.

Stalin's Soviet Union, Hitler's Germany, Mao's China, Kim Il-sung's North Korea and Saddam Hussein's Iraq built elaborate cults: ubiquitous portraits and statues, honorific titles, rewritten histories, ritual applause and the leader's name attached to cities, prizes and institutions. North Korea's Kim family cult, extending across three generations and embedded in state ideology, is the most complete surviving example. Hannah Arendt and others treated the leader cult as a functional element of totalitarian rule, atomising society while binding it to the leader; milder forms of image-management exist in democracies, though the term is usually reserved for systems without free media or opposition.

Functions and decline

Cults legitimise personal rule above institutions, signal the costs of disloyalty, and coordinate elites around a single point. They are brittle: they rarely transfer intact to successors (Khrushchev's de-Stalinisation; the post-Mao reaction against “blind faith”), and their collapse can discredit the whole order they glorified.
