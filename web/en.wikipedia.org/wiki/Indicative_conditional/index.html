Indicative conditional

An indicative conditional is a conditional sentence in the indicative mood, typically of the form "If A, then B" — for example, "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did". Indicative conditionals contrast with subjunctive (counterfactual) conditionals such as "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have", and their semantics is a major topic in the philosophy of language and philosophical logic.

The material conditional debate

Classical logic treats "if" as the material conditional, which is automatically true whenever the antecedent is false. Philosophers such as H. P. Grice defended this account, attributing the oddity of such cases to pragmatics rather than semantics. Others, following Frank Jackson and Robert Stalnaker in different ways, have proposed that indicative conditionals express something about probability or about the world as it is under the supposition of the antecedent.

Ernest Adams's thesis

A widely discussed view, associated with Ernest W. Adams, holds that the assertability of an indicative conditional goes by the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent: "If A, then B" is acceptable when P(B|A) is high. This thesis explains why intuitively compelling inferences involving conditionals (such as certain forms of modus ponens in everyday reasoning) can still fail in designed counterexamples.

Gibbard's example

Allan Gibbard's famous riverboat poker example showed apparently inconsistent conditionals both acceptable to different observers with different information, fuelling debate between expressivist, relativist, and epistemic accounts. The study of indicative conditionals overlaps with the psychology of reasoning, where the "suppression effect" and other phenomena challenge purely logical treatments.
