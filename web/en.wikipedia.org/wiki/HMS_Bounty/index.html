HMS Bounty

Launched1784 (as the collier Bethia)
Acquired26 May 1787, by the Royal Navy
Commanded byLieutenant William Bligh
FateBurned by mutineers, 23 January 1790

HMS Bounty, also known as HM Armed Vessel Bounty, was a small merchant vessel purchased by the Royal Navy in 1787 for a botanical mission: to transport breadfruit plants from Tahiti to the British colonies in the West Indies as a cheap food source for enslaved workers. She is famous for the mutiny that occurred aboard her in the South Pacific on 28 April 1789.

The breadfruit voyage

Under the command of Lieutenant William Bligh, Bounty sailed from England in December 1787, reached Tahiti in October 1788 after a gruelling passage, and spent five months collecting breadfruit plants while the crew lived ashore and formed relationships with the islanders. The ship departed in April 1789 heavily laden with plants.

The mutiny

On 28 April 1789, near Tofua in the Friendly Islands, master's mate Fletcher Christian and a group of crewmen seized the ship and set Bligh and 18 loyalists adrift in the ship's open launch. Bligh navigated the launch some 3,600 nautical miles to Timor without charts — one of the great feats of seamanship — while the mutineers returned to Tahiti. Christian and a small party, with Tahitian companions, eventually settled on remote Pitcairn Island, where they burned Bounty in January 1790 to conceal her. Their descendants still live on Pitcairn, and several mutineers who remained on Tahiti were later captured by HMS Pandora and tried in England.

Legacy

The mutiny on the Bounty has inspired numerous books, plays and films, and remains the most famous mutiny in naval history. The ship's remains in Bounty Bay, Pitcairn Island, have been partially salvaged and studied.
