Tidal bore

Labelled placeholder: a tidal bore on a funnel-shaped estuary.

A tidal bore, often simply given as bore in context, is a tidal phenomenon in which the leading edge of the incoming tide forms a wave (or waves) of water that travels up a river or narrow bay, reversing the direction of the river's current. It is a strong tide that pushes up the river, against the current, and is caused by the funnel shape of a bay or estuary combined with a large tidal range.

Conditions

Bores occur in relatively few locations worldwide, where the tidal range exceeds about six metres and the incoming tide is funnelled into a shallow, narrowing river. The wave may be a single breaking front or an undular train of waves, and can travel at speeds of 10–40 km/h.

Notable bores

  • The Qiantang River bore in China, the world's largest, sometimes reaching 9 metres.
  • The Severn bore in England, ridden by surfers on big spring tides.
  • The Pororoca on the Amazon in Brazil.
  • Bores in the Bay of Fundy system in Canada, and the Petitcodiac River.
  • The mascaret of the Seine and Dordogne in France.

Surfing

Some bores are surfed for many kilometres; the phenomenon has given rise to river-bore surfing on the Severn, the Amazon's Pororoca, and Alaska's Turnagain Arm.
