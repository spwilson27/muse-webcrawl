Plasmin

Plasmin degrades fibrin, the protein mesh of a blood clot, in the process of fibrinolysis.

Plasmin is an important enzyme (EC 3.4.21.7) present in blood that degrades many blood plasma proteins, including fibrin clots. The degradation of fibrin is termed fibrinolysis. In humans, the plasmin protein (in the zymogen form of plasminogen) is encoded by the PLG gene.

Function

Plasmin is a serine protease that acts to dissolve fibrin blood clots. Apart from fibrinolysis, plasmin proteolyses proteins in various other systems: it activates collagenases, some mediators of the complement system, and weakens the wall of the Graafian follicle, leading to ovulation. Plasmin cleaves fibrin, fibronectin, thrombospondin, laminin, and von Willebrand factor. Plasmin, like trypsin, belongs to the family of serine proteases.

Plasmin is released as a zymogen called plasminogen from the liver into the systemic circulation. Two major glycoforms of plasminogen exist in humans, differing in their degree of glycosylation. Plasminogen is converted to active plasmin by a variety of enzymes, including tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA), urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA), kallikrein, and factor XII (Hageman factor). Fibrin is a cofactor for plasminogen activation by tissue plasminogen activator, which is why therapeutic t-PA acts mainly at the clot.

Regulation and clinical significance

Free plasmin in the circulation is rapidly inactivated by α2-antiplasmin, while plasminogen activators are inhibited by plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1). Deficiency of plasminogen may lead to impaired wound healing and ligneous conjunctivitis. Drugs that promote plasmin formation — alteplase and other thrombolytics — are used to dissolve clots in myocardial infarction and stroke, while antifibrinolytic drugs such as tranexamic acid inhibit plasminogen activation and are used to reduce bleeding.
