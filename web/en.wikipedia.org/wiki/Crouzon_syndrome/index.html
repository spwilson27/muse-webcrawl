Crouzon syndrome

Crouzon syndrome is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterised by the premature fusion of certain skull bones (craniosynostosis). This early fusion prevents the skull from growing normally and affects the shape of the head and face. It was first described by the French neurologist Louis Edouard Octave Crouzon in 1912.

Signs and symptoms

Features include a short, broad or tall skull, bulging and wide-set eyes (proptosis) due to shallow eye sockets, an underdeveloped midface, a beak-like nose, and dental crowding from a small upper jaw. Unlike some related syndromes, the hands and feet are typically normal, and intelligence is usually unaffected, though raised intracranial pressure can occur.

Genetics and treatment

The condition is caused by mutations in the FGFR2 gene (and rarely FGFR3), which encode fibroblast growth factor receptors. Management involves a multidisciplinary craniofacial team; surgery to remodel the skull and advance the midface may be performed in childhood to protect vision, breathing and appearance. The incidence is estimated at about 1 in 60,000 births.
