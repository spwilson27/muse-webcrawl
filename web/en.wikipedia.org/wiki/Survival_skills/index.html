Survival skills

Fire-making is a foundational survival skill

Survival skills are techniques that a person may use in a dangerous situation — such as a natural disaster, being lost in the wilderness, or a vehicle breakdown in a remote area — to save themselves or others. They are distinct from the fictional treatment of survival in survival fiction, though the two overlap in popular culture.

Priorities

Survival training is organised around a small set of urgent needs, often summarised by the "rule of threes": a person can survive roughly three minutes without air, three hours without shelter in harsh conditions, three days without water, and three weeks without food. Instruction therefore prioritises signalling for rescue, shelter from exposure, water procurement and purification, and fire.

Core techniques

Key skills include building shelters from natural materials or a tarp; making fire by matches, ferrocerium rods, or friction methods such as the bow drill; locating and disinfecting water by boiling, filtration, or chemical treatment; navigation by map, compass, and natural signs; first aid; knot-tying; and foraging or trapping where rescue is delayed. Modern preparedness also emphasises the survival kit — the "ten essentials" promoted by mountaineering organisations — and the psychological discipline summarised in military manuals such as the US Air Force's SERE (Survival, Evasion, Resistance and Escape) training and the mnemonic SURVIVAL.

Bushcraft and preparedness culture

The recreational practice of wilderness living skills is called bushcraft, popularised by instructors such as Mors Kochanski and Ray Mears. A broader preparedness ("prepper") movement applies survival skills to long-term self-sufficiency.
