High-intensity interval training

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is a form of interval training and cardiovascular exercise strategy alternating short periods of intense or explosive anaerobic exercise with brief recovery periods until exhaustion. HIIT workouts alternate between maximal or near-maximal efforts and low-intensity recovery, and typically last under 30 minutes.

Regimens

Well-known protocols include the Tabata method (eight rounds of 20 seconds of ultra-intense exercise at about 170% of VO2max with 10 seconds of rest, developed from research by Professor Izumi Tabata on Japanese Olympic speed skaters), the Gibala regimen (repeated 30-second "Wingate-style" all-out sprints with several minutes of recovery), and various work-to-rest combinations such as 30/30 or 4×4-minute intervals popularised by Norwegian researchers.

Physiological effects

Research has found that HIIT can improve VO2max, insulin sensitivity, blood pressure and cardiovascular health in less time than moderate-intensity continuous training. Mechanisms include increased mitochondrial density, improved fat oxidation and excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (the "afterburn" effect). Benefits have been demonstrated in recreational athletes, sedentary adults and in cardiac rehabilitation settings, though the high intensity carries an increased risk of injury and overtraining if not properly progressed.
