Emil Fischer

Hermann Emil Louis Fischer (9 October 1852 – 15 July 1919) was a German chemist and 1902 recipient of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. He discovered the Fischer esterification. He also developed the Fischer projection, a symbolic way of drawing asymmetric carbon atoms. He is considered the founder of biochemistry for his work on sugars and proteins.

Early life and career

Fischer was born in Euskirchen, near Cologne, the son of a successful businessman. He studied chemistry at the University of Bonn and the University of Strasbourg, receiving his doctorate in 1874 under Adolf von Baeyer. He held professorships at Munich, Erlangen, and Würzburg before succeeding August Wilhelm von Hofmann at the University of Berlin in 1892, where he remained until his death.

Scientific work

Fischer determined the structures and stereochemistry of the sugars, synthesizing glucose, fructose, and numerous other carbohydrates, and explained their configurations using the projection method that bears his name. His studies of the purines led to the synthesis of caffeine and related compounds, and formed a foundation for work on nucleic acids. He also investigated enzymes—proposing the "lock and key" model of enzyme-substrate specificity in 1894—and proteins, synthesizing peptides of up to 18 amino acids.

Honors and legacy

Fischer received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1902 "in recognition of the extraordinary services he has rendered by his work on sugar and purine syntheses." The Fischer projection and Fischer esterification remain standard tools of organic chemistry, and the Emil Fischer Medal is awarded by the German Chemical Society.
