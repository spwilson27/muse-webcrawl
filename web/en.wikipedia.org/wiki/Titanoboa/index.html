Titanoboa

Titanoboa (meaning "titanic boa") is an extinct genus of giant boid snake that lived during the Paleocene epoch, approximately 60 to 58 million years ago, in what is now La Guajira in northeastern Colombia. The only known species, Titanoboa cerrejonensis, is the largest snake ever discovered, surpassing the previous record holder, Gigantophis of Egypt.

Discovery

Fossils of Titanoboa were recovered from the Cerrejón coal mine, one of the world's largest open-pit coal mines, in sediments of the Cerrejón Formation — the same deposits that yielded an exceptionally rich early rainforest fossil flora and fauna. The snake was formally described in 2009 by an international team led by Jason Head of the University of Toronto and Jonathan Bloch of the Florida Museum of Natural History, working with the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute. Vertebrae from at least 28 individuals were found.

Size and lifestyle

By comparing the size and shape of its vertebrae with those of living boas and anacondas, researchers estimated Titanoboa at 12.8 metres (42 feet) long, possibly up to 14.3 metres, with a mass of about 1,135 kilograms (2,500 lb) — as heavy as a small car. Like its modern relatives it was a constrictor, and associated fossils of giant turtles and crocodylians suggest it hunted in the rivers and swamps of the ancient rainforest, likely preying on large fish, turtles, and dyrosaurid crocodiles.

Climate implications

Because snake body size is strongly temperature-dependent — as ectotherms they can only grow as large as ambient heat allows — Titanoboa's enormous size implies that the neotropics of the Paleocene were significantly hotter than today, with mean annual temperatures of roughly 30–34 °C. The fossil has thus become an important line of evidence in debates about past "hothouse" climates and how tropical ecosystems may respond to future warming.
