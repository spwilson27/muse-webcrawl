Karl Wilhelm Otto Lilienthal (23 May 1848 – 10 August 1896) was a German pioneer of aviation who became known as the "flying man" and the "Glider King". He was the first person to make well-documented, repeated, successful flights with gliders, and his systematic measurements of lift on cambered wings — following the principles first set out by George Cayley — provided the data on which later pioneers, including the Wright brothers, founded their work in aerodynamics.

Research on bird flight

With his brother Gustav, Lilienthal studied the flight of birds, especially storks, from childhood. A trained mechanical engineer, he ran a successful factory in Berlin making small steam engines and invented a toy brick (the Anker stone blocks) with his brother. His book Birdflight as the Basis of Aviation (1889) published the polar diagrams of lift and drag for cambered airfoils that became standard reference data.

Gliding flights

From 1891 Lilienthal flew a series of hang gliders of his own design, launching from an artificial hill (the Fliegeberg) he built near Berlin and from hills at Rhinow and Stölln. He controlled his machines by shifting his body weight. By 1896 he had made some 2,000 flights, covering distances of up to about 250 metres, and photographs of him in the air — widely published in newspapers and magazines around the world — established the public image of human flight and inspired experimenters everywhere.

Death and legacy

On 9 August 1896 his glider stalled and crashed at Stölln; Lilienthal broke his spine and died the next day in Berlin. His reputed last words were "Opfer müssen gebracht werden!" ("Sacrifices must be made!"). The Wright brothers credited his published data and example as a principal inspiration. Original Lilienthal gliders survive in museums in Munich, Washington, London and Moscow, and his grave in Berlin's Lankwitz cemetery bears the epitaph of his sacrifice.
