Seven Pillars of Wisdom

Seven Pillars of Wisdom is the autobiographical account by T. E. Lawrence ("Lawrence of Arabia") of his experiences serving as a British liaison officer with Arab rebel forces during the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in the First World War, from 1916 to 1918. First published in a private edition in 1926, it is regarded as a classic of war literature and travel writing.

Title

The title is drawn from Proverbs 9:1: "Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars." Lawrence had originally used it for a planned book about seven great cities of the Middle East, a manuscript he abandoned; he reused the title for his war memoir.

Composition and publication

Lawrence claimed to have lost the first manuscript of the book, written in 1919, while changing trains at Reading railway station, and rewrote it largely from memory. A draft was printed at Oxford in 1922 (the "Oxford text"). The famous 1926 "subscriber's edition" was lavishly produced in a limited run, illustrated with portraits by artists including Eric Kennington, and sold at a price below its production cost. After Lawrence's death in 1935, a trade edition was published and became a bestseller. An abridged version, Revolt in the Desert (1927), had earlier reached a wide audience.

Content

The narrative follows Lawrence's work with the forces of the Hashemite Arabs under Emir Faisal, describing guerrilla operations against the Hejaz Railway, the capture of Aqaba in 1917, the campaign into Syria, and the fall of Damascus in 1918. It combines military detail with reflections on guerrilla strategy, Arab politics, British imperial policy, and Lawrence's own moral conflicts, including over the Sykes–Picot Agreement and his role at Deraa.

Legacy

The book formed the principal basis for David Lean's film Lawrence of Arabia (1962). Its literary merit and its historical reliability have both been debated; scholars treat it as a work of literature shaped by memory and self-presentation as much as a factual record.
