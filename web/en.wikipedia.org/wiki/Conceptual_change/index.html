Conceptual change

Conceptual change is the process by which a person's or a community's concepts—the mental structures used to understand a domain—are substantially revised. The term is used both in the philosophy and history of science, to describe episodes such as the replacement of Aristotelian physics by Newtonian mechanics, and in education research, to describe how students replace naive misconceptions with scientifically accepted ideas.

In science education

Research beginning in the 1970s and 1980s showed that students often hold robust intuitive theories—for example, that heavier objects fall faster, or that heat is a substance—which resist ordinary instruction. In an influential 1982 paper, Posner, Strike, Hewson and Gertzog proposed conditions under which learners accommodate a new conception: dissatisfaction with the existing one, and a replacement that is intelligible, plausible, and fruitful.

Models

Several theoretical frameworks describe the process. "Cold" models emphasise rational restructuring of beliefs; "warm" models (such as Pintrich, Marx and Boyle's) add motivation, emotion, and classroom context. Other scholars, including Vosniadou, describe gradual enrichment and revision of framework theories, while diSessa's "knowledge in pieces" view holds that novices' knowledge is fragmented rather than theory-like, so change consists in reorganising many small elements.

In the history of science

Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions, in which one paradigm replaces another, is often read as a theory of large-scale conceptual change, though Kuhn himself stressed the partial incommensurability of successive paradigms.
