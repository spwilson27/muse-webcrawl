Compressed sensing in MRI

Compressed sensing (also known as compressive sensing) is a signal processing technique for efficiently acquiring and reconstructing a signal by exploiting its sparsity. Its application to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allows images to be reconstructed accurately from far fewer k-space samples than required by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, substantially shortening scan times.

Principles

MRI images are compressible: they are sparse in a suitable transform domain, such as the wavelet domain, or have sparse spatial gradients (finite differences). Compressed sensing MRI therefore acquires data with an incoherent, typically random or variable-density, undersampling pattern in k-space, and reconstructs the image by solving a constrained optimisation problem that balances data consistency with sparsity, for example by minimising the total variation or the l1-norm of the transform coefficients.

Clinical impact

Faster acquisitions reduce motion artefacts, improve patient comfort, and make feasible applications such as high-resolution cardiac imaging within a breath-hold, dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, and paediatric scans without sedation. Compressed sensing products have been cleared for clinical use by major scanner manufacturers, and the approach is increasingly combined with parallel imaging and, more recently, deep-learning-based reconstruction.
