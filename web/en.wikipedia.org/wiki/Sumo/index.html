Sumo (Japanese: 相撲, Hepburn: sumō) is a form of competitive full-contact wrestling where a rikishi (wrestler) attempts to force his opponent out of a circular ring (dohyō) or into touching the ground with any body part other than the soles of his feet (usually by throwing, shoving or pushing him down). Sumo originated in Japan, the only country where it is practised professionally, where it is considered the national sport.

History

Sumo originated as a Shinto ritual dance praying for good harvests; wrestling matches at shrines and the imperial court (sekitori ceremonies recorded in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki) developed through the Heian and Kamakura periods. In the Edo period (1603–1868) sumo became professional entertainment, with ranking charts (banzuke), stables (heya) and rules largely recognisable today. The Japan Sumo Association now runs six 15-day grand tournaments (honbasho) each year, in Tokyo (January, May, September), Osaka (March), Nagoya (July) and Fukuoka (November).

Rules and techniques

A bout begins after ritual preliminaries — including leg-stomping (shiko), salt purification thrown into the ring, and the stylised crouch-and-glare (shikiri) — and is decided in seconds once the wrestlers charge (tachiai). Winning techniques (kimarite) number 82 officially, the most common being yorikiri (force out), oshidashi (push out) and hatakikomi (slap down). Striking with a closed fist, hair-pulling and attacks to the eyes are forbidden.

Ranks and life

Wrestlers progress through six divisions, from jonokuchi up to the top makuuchi division. Its elite ranks are, in ascending order, komusubi, sekiwake, ōzeki and the highest, yokozuna — a rank from which there is no demotion; a fading yokozuna is expected to retire. In total, only 73 men had been promoted to yokozuna by the early 2020s. Sekitori (the top two divisions) receive salaries; all wrestlers live communally in heya under an oyakata (stablemaster), following a strict hierarchy, eating the protein-rich stew chankonabe and wearing the oichō topknot in public.

Foreign wrestlers

From the late twentieth century, foreign-born wrestlers — especially Hawaiians such as Akebono, the first foreign-born yokozuna (1993), and a succession of Mongolian champions including Asashōryū and Hakuhō — came to dominate the top ranks. Hakuhō, who retired in 2021, holds the record with 45 top-division championships.
