A glass cockpit is an aircraft cockpit that features electronic (digital) flight instrument displays, typically large LCD screens, rather than traditional analog dials and gauges. The term derives from the glass of cathode-ray tubes first used for the displays. A glass cockpit includes an Electronic Flight Instrument System (EFIS), usually with a primary flight display (PFD) and a navigation display, plus an engine-indicating and crew-alerting system (EICAS or ECAM) that monitors aircraft systems.

History

Glass displays were developed for military aircraft in the 1970s and entered airliner service with the Boeing 757 and 767 in the early 1980s. The Airbus A320 (1988) combined a full glass cockpit with fly-by-wire controls and side-sticks. Research at NASA and elsewhere showed that integrated electronic displays improved situational awareness and reduced pilot workload. From the early 2000s, systems such as the Garmin G1000 brought glass cockpits to light general-aviation aircraft, and glass panels are now standard in nearly all new aircraft.

Advantages and concerns

Electronic displays can present more information, tailored to the phase of flight, with integrated checklists, moving maps, traffic, weather, and synthetic vision. They reduce the number of electromechanical instruments and their maintenance burden. However, critics note the risk of "automation complacency" and mode confusion, and pilots must be trained to manage failures and revert to standby instruments. Accidents such as Air France Flight 447 prompted renewed emphasis on manual flying skills alongside glass-cockpit automation.
