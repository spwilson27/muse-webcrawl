In business, economics, and finance, liquidity is the degree to which an asset can be quickly bought or sold in the market at a price reflecting its intrinsic value — that is, converted into cash without significantly affecting its price. Cash itself is the most liquid asset, while tangible items such as real estate and fine art are relatively illiquid.

Market liquidity

A market is liquid if transactions can occur rapidly and in large volume with little price impact. Measures include the bid–ask spread, market depth, and trading volume. Shares of large companies listed on major exchanges are highly liquid; the shares of small companies and thinly traded securities may sell at a liquidity discount. Loss of market liquidity — as in the financial crisis of 2007–2008, when mortgage-backed securities became nearly unsaleable — can cascade into a systemic crisis, prompting central banks to act as lenders of last resort and supply emergency liquidity.

Accounting and funding liquidity

In accounting, liquidity refers to a firm's ability to meet its short-term obligations; common measures are the current ratio and the quick ratio. Funding liquidity is the ability of a borrower to raise cash or roll over debt as it falls due. A solvent institution can still fail through a liquidity crisis if it cannot convert assets to cash quickly enough — the mechanism of bank runs.

Liquidity preference

John Maynard Keynes's theory of liquidity preference explains the demand for money as held for transactions, precautionary, and speculative motives, linking liquidity to interest-rate determination.
