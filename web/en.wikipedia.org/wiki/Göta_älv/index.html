The Göta älv ("River of the Geats") is a river in western Sweden that drains Lake Vänern, the largest lake in the European Union, into the Kattegat at the city of Gothenburg. Although only about 93 kilometres long, it carries more water than any other river in Scandinavia, with a mean discharge of roughly 570 cubic metres per second. Together with the Klarälven, which flows into Vänern, it forms Sweden's longest river system, around 720 kilometres.

Course

The river leaves Lake Vänern at Vänersborg and flows south-west past Trollhättan, where it descends about 32 metres through the famous Trollhättan Falls. The falls were once one of Sweden's great natural spectacles; today most of the water is diverted through hydroelectric power stations and the locks of the Trollhätte Canal, and the falls are released only on special "Falls Days" and for brief daily periods in summer. Below Trollhättan the river continues past Lilla Edet and the historic town of Kungälv, where it divides into two branches around the island of Hisingen: the main Göta älv branch to the south and the Nordre älv to the north. The southern branch flows through Gothenburg, Sweden's largest port, and reaches the sea in the Gothenburg archipelago.

Navigation

The Göta älv is the seaward section of the waterway linking Gothenburg with Lake Vänern and, via the Göta Canal, with the Baltic Sea. The present Trollhätte Canal, with locks opened in 1916, allows vessels of up to about 88 metres in length to pass the falls. Shipping on the river (Vänern traffic, Vänertrafik) carries several million tonnes of freight annually, especially timber products, oil and bulk goods, and the river is of strategic importance as an inland transport route.

Water supply and landslides

Gothenburg draws most of its drinking water from the Göta älv, supplemented by water from the lakes Delsjön and Rådasjön when river water quality falls. The river valley is cut through deep deposits of post-glacial clay, including quick clay, and has a long history of destructive landslides; the Tuve landslide of 1977, on Hisingen, killed nine people and destroyed dozens of houses, and landslide risk along the river is a major concern in Swedish land-use planning, aggravated by climate change.

History

The river was the historic heartland of the Geats and a contested frontier between Sweden, Norway and Denmark for centuries. Fortresses such as Bohus at Kungälv guarded its mouth, and Gothenburg was founded at its estuary in 1621 to give Sweden a secure port on the west coast. The Göta älv valley remains one of the most densely populated and industrialised corridors in Sweden.
