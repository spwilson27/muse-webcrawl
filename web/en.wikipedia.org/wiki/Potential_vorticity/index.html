Potential vorticity


Labelled image placeholder: isentropic potential vorticity chart

In fluid mechanics, potential vorticity (PV) is a quantity proportional to the dot product of vorticity and stratification. It is a conserved quantity in adiabatic, frictionless flow and is therefore a useful tracer of the motion of air and water parcels in the atmosphere and oceans.

Definition

The Ertel potential vorticity is defined as PV = (1/ρ) ζa · ∇θ, where ζa is the absolute vorticity vector, θ is potential temperature, and ρ is density. In meteorology, PV is commonly measured in potential vorticity units (PVU), where 1 PVU = 10−6 K m2 kg−1 s−1.

Conservation

The conservation of potential vorticity follows from the work of Carl-Gustaf Rossby (1936) for shallow-water flow and Hans Ertel (1942) for the general stratified case. Because PV is conserved following the motion under adiabatic and frictionless conditions, fluid parcels carry their PV with them, which makes PV maps a powerful tool for analysing atmospheric dynamics.

Applications

Potential vorticity thinking, developed notably by Brian Hoskins and collaborators, underlies much of modern dynamical meteorology. Upper-level PV anomalies are associated with surface cyclogenesis; the tropopause can be characterised by a PV threshold of about 2 PVU; and stratospheric PV structures help explain phenomena such as sudden stratospheric warmings and blocking. In oceanography, PV conservation governs the behaviour of currents crossing latitude lines and topography.
