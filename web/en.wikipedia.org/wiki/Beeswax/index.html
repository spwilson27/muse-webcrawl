Beeswax (Latin: cera alba) is a natural wax produced by honey bees of the genus Apis. The wax is formed into scales by eight wax-producing glands in the abdominal segments of worker bees, which discard it in or at the hive. The hive workers collect and use it to form cells for honey storage and larval and pupal protection within the beehive. Chemically, beeswax consists mainly of esters of fatty acids and various long-chain alcohols.

Production

Worker bees secrete wax scales from glands on their abdomens; the scales are nearly colourless when first produced and turn yellow to brown as they incorporate pollen oils and propolis. Bees use roughly 3.5–4 kg of honey to produce 0.5 kg of wax. Beekeepers harvest surplus wax when extracting honey from comb and by rendering old comb and cappings; the wax is melted, strained, and cast into blocks for sale.

Composition

Beeswax is a complex mixture of more than 300 compounds, principally monoesters (about 35%), hydrocarbons, free fatty acids, and hydroxyesters. Its composition varies somewhat with bee subspecies and diet, but its physical properties are notably consistent, making it a reliable material for industry.

Uses

Beeswax has been used since prehistory — in lost-wax casting, as a lubricant and waterproofing agent, and in encaustic painting. Modern uses include candles (notably liturgical candles), cosmetics and lip balms, pharmaceuticals as a stiffening agent and coating (food additive E901), food wraps, polishes for furniture and leather, and the production of honeycomb foundation for beekeeping frames.
