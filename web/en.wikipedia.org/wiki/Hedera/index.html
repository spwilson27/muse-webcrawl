Hedera

Hedera, commonly called ivy, is a genus of 12–15 species of evergreen climbing or ground-creeping woody plants in the family Araliaceae, native to western, central and southern Europe, Macaronesia, northwestern Africa and Asia.

Hedera, commonly called ivy (particularly H. helix, common ivy or English ivy), is a genus of evergreen climbing or ground-creeping woody plants in the family Araliaceae. Ivies climb by means of aerial rootlets bearing adhesive pads that secrete a powerful glue-like substance of nanoparticles, allowing them to scale trees, cliffs and walls to heights of 30 metres or more.

Description

Ivies show striking leaf dimorphism: juvenile climbing stems bear three- to five-lobed leaves, while mature, flowering stems in full light produce unlobed, ovate leaves and umbels of greenish-yellow flowers in autumn. The flowers are rich in nectar and are an important late-season food for bees and other insects; the black berries ripen in late winter and are eaten by birds, though they are poisonous to humans. The genus is native across Europe and Asia; some species, especially H. helix and H. hibernica, have become invasive in parts of North America and Australia, where they smother forest understorey and add weight and wind resistance to trees.

Cultivation and symbolism

Hundreds of cultivars are grown as ornamental ground cover, for wall clothing, and as houseplants; variegated forms are especially popular. Ivy has long symbolised fidelity and eternity — it was sacred to Bacchus/Dionysus, and ivy leaves crown poets and newlyweds in classical tradition. American universities of the "Ivy League" take their name from the ivy traditionally planted on their buildings.
