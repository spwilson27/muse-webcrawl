Volcker Rule

The Volcker Rule is Section 619 of the Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010), a United States federal regulation that generally prohibits banks from conducting certain investment activities with their own accounts and limits their dealings with hedge funds and private equity funds — so-called proprietary trading. It is named after former Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker, who proposed it in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis.

Provisions

The rule bars deposit-taking banking entities from proprietary trading — buying and selling securities, derivatives and other instruments principally for short-term profit — and from acquiring ownership interests in, or sponsoring, hedge funds and private equity funds, subject to exemptions for market making, underwriting, risk-mitigating hedging, and trading in US government obligations. Banks must maintain compliance programmes, and the largest must report quantitative trading metrics to regulators.

History

Volcker, then chairman of President Obama's Economic Recovery Advisory Board, proposed separating speculative trading from commercial banking, echoing the repealed Glass–Steagall Act. The final implementing rule was adopted jointly by five agencies in December 2013. In 2018 the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act exempted banks with under $10 billion in assets, and in 2019–2020 regulators simplified the rule's compliance requirements, changes supporters called streamlining and critics called gutting.
