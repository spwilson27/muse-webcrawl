Copper Kings

The Copper Kings were three industrialists — Marcus Daly, William A. Clark, and F. Augustus Heinze — who battled for control of the copper mining industry in Butte, Montana, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Their rivalry, sometimes called the War of the Copper Kings, shaped Montana politics and produced some of the most spectacular fortunes of the Gilded Age.

Background

Butte began as a gold and silver mining camp, but its enormous copper deposits — needed for the new electrical industry — made it one of the richest mining districts on earth, often described as "the richest hill on Earth." Copper mining required heavy capital for smelters, railroads, and timber, and the men who controlled those assets fought bitterly for supremacy.

The three kings

Marcus Daly, an Irish immigrant, developed the Anaconda mine and built the smelter town of Anaconda; his enterprise grew into the Anaconda Copper Mining Company, backed by eastern financiers including George Hearst. William Andrews Clark, a banker and merchant turned miner, amassed a vast fortune and used his newspapers and political influence against Daly. F. Augustus Heinze, the youngest of the three, arrived later, exploited Montana's apex law through litigation, and for a time paralyzed Amalgamated Copper's operations in Butte.

War of the Copper Kings

The feud between Daly and Clark dominated Montana politics in the 1890s, culminating in Clark's bribery-tainted election to the United States Senate in 1899. Heinze's courtroom battles with the Amalgamated (Anaconda) interests ended in 1906 when he sold his Butte properties for some $12 million. The eventual victor was not any individual king but the consolidated Anaconda Company, which dominated Montana's economy and politics for decades as "the Company."

Legacy

The Copper Kings left mansions in Butte and Helena, the Daly Mansion near Hamilton, and a legacy of industrial development, labour conflict, and environmental damage — notably the Berkeley Pit — that Montana still lives with.
