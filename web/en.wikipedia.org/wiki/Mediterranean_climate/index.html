Mediterranean climate

A Mediterranean climate (also called a dry summer temperate climate) is a climate type characteristic of the lands surrounding the Mediterranean Sea and of certain regions on the western coasts of continents between roughly 30° and 45° latitude. It is defined by dry summers and mild, rainy winters, corresponding to the Köppen climate classifications Csa (hot-summer) and Csb (warm-summer).

Distribution

Besides the Mediterranean Basin, Mediterranean climates occur in five relatively small regions: coastal California, central Chile, the southwestern tip of South Africa (the Cape region), and southwestern and southern Australia (around Perth and Adelaide). Together these regions are famous as biodiversity hotspots with distinctive sclerophyll vegetation — maquis, chaparral, matorral, fynbos, and mallee.

Causes

The pattern results from the seasonal migration of subtropical high-pressure cells: in summer the highs expand poleward and suppress rainfall, while in winter they retreat equatorward, allowing mid-latitude cyclones and frontal rain to reach the region. Most rain falls in the cooler months, and summer drought of three to five months is typical.

Characteristics

Summers are hot or warm, sunny, and dry; winters are mild with frost uncommon in coastal areas. Annual rainfall typically ranges from about 350 to 900 mm. The climate supports characteristic agriculture: olives, grapes, citrus fruits, figs, almonds, and wheat, and evergreen oak woodland and shrubland vegetation adapted to summer drought and periodic fire.

Köppen subtypes

  • Csa — hot-summer Mediterranean climate: hottest month averaging above 22 °C; typical of inland Mediterranean coasts, e.g. Athens, Los Angeles.
  • Csb — warm-summer Mediterranean climate: hottest month below 22 °C; e.g. San Francisco, Cape Town, Porto.
  • Csc — cold-summer Mediterranean climate: rare, found in high-elevation coastal areas.
