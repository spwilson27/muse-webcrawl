Vault (architecture)


Rib vaulting in a Gothic cathedral

In architecture, a vault is a self-supporting arched form, usually of stone or concrete, serving to cover a space with a ceiling or roof. Like an arch, a vault directs the weight of the roof outward and downward along its curve, generating lateral thrust that must be resisted by thick walls, buttresses, or ties.

History

Vaulting was known in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, but it was the Romans who exploited it on a monumental scale in concrete, roofing baths, basilicas, and warehouses with barrel and groin vaults. Early Christian and Byzantine builders developed domes and pendentives, while Romanesque architects revived stone barrel vaults for churches. Gothic builders combined pointed arches, ribs, and flying buttresses to create soaring, light-filled vaults of great span. Renaissance and Baroque architects returned to classical forms; in the modern era, thin-shell concrete and steel revived vaulting in structures such as railway stations and market halls.

Types of vault

Barrel vault

The simplest vault: an arch extruded along a straight line, forming a tunnel-like half-cylinder. It exerts continuous lateral thrust along both walls.

Groin vault

Formed by two barrel vaults intersecting at right angles, producing a bay with four curved triangular surfaces (groins). Thrust is concentrated at the corners, allowing openings in the walls between supports.

Rib vault

A framework of stone ribs — diagonal, transverse, and wall ribs — supporting thin masonry webbing between them. Characteristic of Gothic architecture, rib vaults span rectangular bays and channel loads to the corners.

Fan vault

A late Gothic (especially English Perpendicular) form in which ribs radiate from a point like a fan, forming conoid surfaces, as at King's College Chapel, Cambridge.

Other forms

These include the annular (ring) vault, cloister vault, cross vault, domical vault, and sail vault, among many regional and historical variants such as the Catalan (timbrel) vault built of flat tiles.

Construction

Traditional masonry vaults were built over temporary timber centring. Key structural concerns are the line of thrust, the stability of abutments, and settlement during construction. Modern shells use reinforced or prestressed concrete, sometimes only a few centimetres thick relative to their span.
