Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a group of gram-positive bacteria that are genetically distinct from other strains of Staphylococcus aureus. MRSA is responsible for several difficult-to-treat infections in humans. It caused more than 100,000 deaths attributable to antimicrobial resistance in 2019. MRSA is any strain of S. aureus that has developed (through natural selection) or acquired (through horizontal gene transfer) resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics, which include the penicillins (methicillin, oxacillin, dicloxacillin, nafcillin, etc.) and the cephalosporins.

Mechanism of resistance

Resistance is conferred by the mecA gene, carried on a mobile staphylococcal cassette chromosome element (SCCmec). The gene encodes an alternative penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a, which has a very low affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics and continues cell-wall synthesis when the native PBPs are inactivated. MRSA thus resists essentially all beta-lactams, with the exception of the later-generation cephalosporins ceftaroline and ceftobiprole, which were designed to bind PBP2a.

Epidemiology and disease

MRSA was first identified in 1961, shortly after methicillin entered clinical use. For decades it was primarily a healthcare problem (HA-MRSA), infecting hospitalised and immunocompromised patients through wounds, catheters and ventilators. From the 1990s, genetically distinct community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) strains — notably USA300 in North America — emerged in healthy people, typically causing skin and soft-tissue infections such as abscesses and boils, and occasionally severe necrotizing pneumonia or sepsis. A third group, livestock-associated MRSA, is linked to intensive animal farming. Infections range from mild skin lesions to endocarditis, osteomyelitis, bacteremia and toxic shock syndromes; invasive MRSA carries a substantial mortality rate.

Prevention and treatment

Control rests on hand hygiene, contact precautions, environmental cleaning, screening and decolonisation of carriers (for example with nasal mupirocin and chlorhexidine bathing), and antimicrobial stewardship to reduce the selective pressure favouring resistance. Treatment depends on severity: simple abscesses may need only incision and drainage; systemic infections are treated with agents such as vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or ceftaroline, guided by susceptibility testing.
