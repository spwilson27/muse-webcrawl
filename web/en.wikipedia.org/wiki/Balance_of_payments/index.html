Balance of payments

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The balance of payments (BOP) of a country is the record of all international economic transactions between its residents and those of other countries over a period. It comprises the current account (trade in goods and services, primary income such as investment earnings and remittances, and transfers) and the capital and financial account (investment flows, lending and reserve changes). By accounting construction the overall balance is zero: a current-account deficit is financed by a financial-account surplus (net inflows), and vice versa.

Persistent imbalances are politically charged: the United States has run current-account deficits for most years since the early 1980s, financed by foreign purchases of dollar assets (the dollar's “exorbitant privilege”), while China, Germany, Japan and oil exporters have run large surpluses at various times. Under fixed exchange rates, deficits drain reserves and can force devaluation or IMF programmes; under floating rates they are cushioned but not painless. Economists debate when deficits signal strength (investment opportunities, safe-asset demand) or vulnerability (borrowing for consumption, sudden-stop risk, as in the Asian crisis of 1997–98).
