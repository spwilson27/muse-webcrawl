Driving under the influence (DUI) is the offense of driving, operating, or being in control of a vehicle while impaired by alcohol or drugs (including recreational drugs and those prescribed by physicians) to a level that renders the driver incapable of operating a motor vehicle safely. It is called driving while intoxicated (DWI) in some jurisdictions, and drink-driving in British English. In the United States, alcohol-impaired driving fatalities accounted for 13,524 deaths in 2022, about 32 percent of all traffic fatalities.

Blood alcohol content

Most jurisdictions criminalize driving with a blood alcohol content (BAC) above a statutory limit, regardless of observable impairment—a per se offense. All U.S. states except Utah set the limit at 0.08 g/dL; Utah lowered its limit to 0.05 in 2018, matching most of Europe, where limits range from 0.00 (e.g., Czechia, Hungary) to 0.05, with 0.08 retained in England and Wales. Lower limits usually apply to commercial drivers (0.04 in the U.S.) and to novice or underage drivers under zero-tolerance laws.

Detection and testing

Officers typically rely on driving behavior, standardized field sobriety tests (horizontal gaze nystagmus, walk-and-turn, one-leg stand), and preliminary breath tests. Evidential breath testing uses instruments such as the Intoxilyzer or DataMaster; blood draws provide the most direct measurement. Under "implied consent" laws, licence holders are deemed to have consented to chemical testing, and refusal brings administrative suspension. Drug impairment, including cannabis, is harder to standardize; drug recognition experts (DREs) apply a 12-step evaluation, and many jurisdictions set per se limits for THC in blood.

Penalties

Sanctions typically escalate with prior offenses and aggravating factors (very high BAC, a child passenger, injury or death). They include fines, licence suspension or revocation, mandatory alcohol education, vehicle impoundment, and imprisonment; felony DUI or vehicular homicide charges apply in serious cases. Many jurisdictions require ignition interlock devices—breathalyzers wired to the starter—for offenders.

Prevention

Advocacy by Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD), founded in 1980 by Candy Lightner after her daughter was killed by a repeat offender, helped drive the U.S. National Minimum Drinking Age Act (1984) and the nationwide 0.08 standard (2000). Sobriety checkpoints (upheld in Michigan Dept. of State Police v. Sitz, 1990), administrative licence revocation, designated-driver campaigns, and rideshare services are credited with cutting U.S. drunk-driving deaths by roughly half since the early 1980s.
