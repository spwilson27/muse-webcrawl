End of history

The end of history is a political and philosophical concept that supposes a particular political, economic, or social system may develop that would constitute the end-point of humanity's sociocultural evolution and the final form of human government. The concept is most associated with the American political scientist Francis Fukuyama, whose 1989 essay "The End of History?" and 1992 book The End of History and the Last Man argued that the worldwide spread of liberal democracy and free-market capitalism might signal the end point of humanity's ideological evolution.

Fukuyama's thesis

Fukuyama argued, drawing on the philosophy of Hegel as interpreted by Alexandre Kojève, that history should be understood as an evolutionary process driven by ideological struggle, and that with the end of the Cold War and the collapse of the Soviet Union, liberal democracy had emerged as the final form of government for all nations, having defeated its rivals of fascism and communism. He did not claim that events would cease, or that all countries would become successful liberal democracies in practice, but that the ideological evolution of humanity had reached its conclusion.

Criticism

The thesis has been widely criticised. Samuel P. Huntington's "clash of civilizations" thesis proposed that cultural and religious conflict would dominate the post-Cold War world instead. Critics have also pointed to the rise of authoritarian capitalism, Islamist movements, and populism, and to the resilience of authoritarianism in countries such as China, as evidence against the claim. Fukuyama himself has since qualified aspects of the argument, writing on the challenges of political decay and identity politics within liberal democracies.
