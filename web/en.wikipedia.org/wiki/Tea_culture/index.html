Tea culture


Tea preparation as ceremony: utensils, setting, and gesture are central to many tea cultures

Tea culture is the culture surrounding the consumption of tea — the ways it is prepared, served, and enjoyed, and the social, artistic, and spiritual practices that have grown up around it. Tea originated in southwest China, where it was used medicinally, and spread across East Asia and, later, the rest of the world, acquiring distinctive local forms wherever it took root.

East Asia

China is the homeland of tea. The Tang-dynasty scholar Lu Yu's Classic of Tea (8th century) codified its preparation and appreciation. The Gongfu tea ceremony of Fujian and Chaozhou emphasises skill and small, repeated infusions of oolong tea, while whisked powdered tea of the Song dynasty influenced later Japanese practice.

Japan developed the tea ceremony (chanoyu, sadō, or chadō) around powdered green tea (matcha), shaped by Zen Buddhism and masters such as Sen no Rikyū (16th century), who emphasised the aesthetics of wabi — rustic simplicity — and the principles of harmony, respect, purity, and tranquillity.

Korea maintains its own tea ceremony (darye) and a long tradition of temple teas. Tibet drinks butter tea (po cha), churned with yak butter and salt; Mongolia has a similar salted milk tea, süütei tsai.

South and Central Asia, and the Middle East

In India, masala chai — black tea simmered with milk, sugar, and spices — became a national drink in the 20th century, sold by chai wallahs at every railway station. Iran and Turkey are among the world's highest per-capita tea consumers: Turkish çay is brewed strong in a double teapot (çaydanlık) and served in tulip glasses; Iranian tea houses (chaikhaneh) are old social institutions. In the Arab world mint tea is central to hospitality, most famously in Morocco, where green tea with mint is poured from a height into small glasses.

Europe and the Americas

Tea reached Europe in the 17th century through Portuguese and Dutch traders. In Britain it became the national drink: afternoon tea, credited to Anna, Duchess of Bedford, in the 1840s, added sandwiches and cakes to the ritual, and "builder's tea" remains a workplace staple. Russia developed the samovar tradition and strong tea served with lemon or jam. In the southern United States, sweet iced tea predominates.

Social role

Across cultures, tea functions as a marker of hospitality, a pause in the working day, a medium of business and diplomacy, and an object of connoisseurship comparable to wine, with its own vocabularies of terroir, season, and craftsmanship.
