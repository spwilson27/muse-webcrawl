Jean-François Champollion (French: [ʒɑ̃ fʁɑ̃swa ʃɑ̃pɔljɔ̃]; 23 December 1790 – 4 March 1832), also known as Champollion le jeune ("the Younger"), was a French scholar, philologist and orientalist, known primarily as the decipherer of Egyptian hieroglyphs and a founding figure in the field of Egyptology. Partially raised by his brother, the scholar Jacques Joseph Champollion-Figeac, Champollion was a child prodigy in philology, giving his first public paper on the decipherment of Egyptian scripts as a teenager.

Champollion announced the key principles of his decipherment in his Lettre à M. Dacier of September 1822 and set out the full system in his Précis du système hiéroglyphique (1824). He made a major expedition to Egypt in 1828–29, was appointed the Louvre's first curator of Egyptian antiquities, and in 1831 became the first professor of Egyptology at the Collège de France. His Grammaire égyptienne and Dictionnaire égyptien were published posthumously.

Early life

Champollion was born in Figeac in southwestern France, the youngest of seven children of a bookseller. From childhood he displayed an extraordinary gift for languages: by his teens he had mastered Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Chaldean, Persian, and Coptic, the last of which — the liturgical language of Egyptian Christians, a late form of the ancient Egyptian language — proved decisive for his later work. In 1801 he joined his brother Jacques Joseph in Grenoble, and at sixteen presented a paper arguing that Coptic was the late descendant of the language of the pharaohs.

After study in Paris under Silvestre de Sacy and others, Champollion was appointed assistant professor of history at Grenoble in 1810, aged nineteen. His linguistic work during the following decade, including an early Coptic grammar and dictionary manuscript, steadily prepared the ground for the decipherment.

Decipherment of hieroglyphs

The Rosetta Stone, discovered by French soldiers in 1799, bore the same decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek scripts, but scholars had been unable to read either Egyptian script. Building on the work of Silvestre de Sacy, Johan David Åkerblad, and Thomas Young — who had correctly identified several values in cartouches — Champollion's breakthrough was to demonstrate that hieroglyphic writing was a complex mixture of phonetic, logographic, and determinative signs, rather than purely symbolic.

Working with cartouches of Ptolemy and Cleopatra from the Philae obelisk and Abu Simbel, and applying his knowledge of Coptic, in September 1822 Champollion confirmed the phonetic values of a series of signs and reportedly rushed to his brother's office crying "Je tiens mon affaire!" ("I've got it!") before collapsing. His letter to Bon-Joseph Dacier, read to the Académie des Inscriptions on 27 September 1822, announced the decipherment. In the Précis (1824) he extended the system from Greco-Roman era names to the inscriptions of the pharaonic period, proving the script's continuity over millennia.

Priorities and the respective contributions of Young and Champollion were debated during and after their lifetimes; Young's identifications were important but preliminary, while Champollion produced the complete working system by which Egyptian texts could actually be read.

Career and expedition to Egypt

In 1826 Champollion was appointed curator of the new Egyptian collection of the Louvre, which he organised and catalogued. From July 1828 to late 1829 he led a joint Franco-Tuscan expedition up the Nile with Ippolito Rosellini, verifying his system against thousands of inscriptions at sites including Dendera, Karnak, Luxor, and Abu Simbel, and protesting against the destruction and export of monuments. On his return he was elected to the Académie des Inscriptions and, in 1831, appointed to the newly created chair of Egyptian antiquities at the Collège de France.

Death and legacy

Worn out by the Egyptian expedition, Champollion died in Paris on 4 March 1832, aged 41, probably of a stroke. His brother published his Egyptian grammar (1836–41) and dictionary (1841–43) from his manuscripts. Champollion is regarded as the founder of scientific Egyptology; the museum at his birthplace in Figeac, the Musée Champollion, and monuments in Egypt commemorate him. The recurring claim in French tradition that he exclaimed upon solving the script remains one of the most famous anecdotes of decipherment, alongside the work of Michael Ventris on Linear B.
