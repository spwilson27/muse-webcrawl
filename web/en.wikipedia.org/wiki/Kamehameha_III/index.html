Kamehameha III

Kamehameha III, born Kauikeaouli (11 August 1813 – 15 December 1854), was the third king of the Kingdom of Hawaii, reigning from 1825 to 1854 — the longest reign of any Hawaiian monarch. His rule transformed Hawaii into a constitutional monarchy with written laws, secured international recognition of Hawaiian independence, and saw the Great Māhele land reform.

Early reign

Kauikeaouli succeeded his brother Kamehameha II as a child in 1825, under the regency of Queen Kaʻahumanu and, after her death in 1832, of Kīnaʻu. He was educated by Protestant missionaries, and after a rebellious youth asserted his authority in the 1830s. In 1837 he married Kalama, who became his queen.

Constitutional government

In 1839 Kamehameha III promulgated a Declaration of Rights, followed in 1840 by Hawaii's first written constitution, establishing a legislature with an elected house of representatives. When a British captain, Lord George Paulet, unilaterally seized the kingdom in 1843, the king temporarily ceded sovereignty under protest; five months later Rear Admiral Richard Thomas restored Hawaiian independence. The king's words on the restoration, Ua Mau ke Ea o ka ʻĀina i ka Pono ("The life of the land is perpetuated in righteousness"), became Hawaii's state motto. Britain and France formally recognised Hawaiian independence in November 1843, followed by the United States.

Great Māhele

The Great Māhele of 1848 divided land among the king, chiefs, and government, and the Kuleana Act of 1850 allowed commoners to claim fee-simple title to plots they cultivated. Although intended to secure Hawaiian landholding, the new system — together with the legalisation of land sales to foreigners — ultimately facilitated large-scale alienation of land from Native Hawaiians.

Death

Kamehameha III moved the capital from Lahaina to Honolulu in 1845. He died in 1854 and was succeeded by his nephew and adopted son, Kamehameha IV.
