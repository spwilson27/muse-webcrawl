New Dutch Waterline

The New Dutch Waterline (Dutch: Nieuwe Hollandse Waterlinie) was a line of defensive works in the Netherlands built between 1815 and 1940, replacing the older Dutch Waterline of the 17th century. It ran from the Zuiderzee near Muiden south to the Biesbosch, protecting the cities of Holland — including Amsterdam and Utrecht — behind a controlled system of inundations.

Principle

The line's defence rested on flooding: a chain of polders could be deliberately inundated to a depth of roughly 30 centimetres — too deep for infantry and vehicles to cross easily, yet too shallow for boats. Forts, batteries and casemates guarded the dikes, locks, roads and other points where the water barrier could be crossed.

History

Planned under King William I and engineered by Cornelis Krayenhoff, the line was progressively strengthened with brick and later concrete forts throughout the 19th century. It was mobilized during the Franco-Prussian War and the First World War, but by 1940 aircraft and modern artillery had made it obsolete, and it played no decisive role in the Second World War. In 2021 the line was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the Dutch Water Defence Lines.
