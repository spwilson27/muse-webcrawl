Coronary angiography

Coronary angiography is a diagnostic imaging procedure that uses X-ray angiography to visualise the coronary arteries, the blood vessels supplying the heart muscle. It is the reference standard ("gold standard") for diagnosing coronary artery disease and is performed as part of cardiac catheterisation.

Procedure

A catheter is inserted into an artery — most commonly the radial artery at the wrist, or the femoral artery in the groin — and advanced under fluoroscopic guidance to the openings of the coronary arteries. An iodinated contrast agent is injected, and rapid X-ray images (cineangiograms) record the arterial lumen in several projections. Narrowings (stenoses), blockages and congenital anomalies can be identified and measured. The same access permits intervention, such as angioplasty and stenting, or pressure and flow measurements within an artery.

Indications and risks

Indications include evaluation of chest pain, positive stress tests, unstable angina, heart attack (myocardial infarction), and assessment before heart surgery. Risks include bleeding at the access site, contrast allergy, kidney injury from contrast, arrhythmias, and rarely stroke, arterial dissection or heart attack; overall complication rates are low, on the order of 1–2 percent, with death below 0.1 percent in diagnostic procedures.

History

Selective coronary angiography was pioneered by Mason Sones at the Cleveland Clinic in 1958–1962, building on Werner Forssmann's self-catheterisation experiments of 1929. It laid the foundation for coronary artery bypass surgery and percutaneous coronary intervention.
