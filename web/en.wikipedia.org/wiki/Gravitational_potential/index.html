Gravitational potential

In classical mechanics, the gravitational potential is a scalar potential associated with the gravitational force. It is the gravitational potential energy of a particle divided by its mass, i.e. the work done per unit mass to move an object from a reference point (usually infinity) to a given point in a gravitational field.

Definition

For a point mass M, the gravitational potential at distance r is

V(r) = −GM/r,

where G is the gravitational constant. The gravitational field is the negative gradient of the potential, g = −∇V. By the principle of superposition, the potential of a distribution of masses is the sum (or integral) of the potentials of its parts, and in terms of the mass density ρ it satisfies Poisson's equation, ∇²V = 4πGρ.

Properties

The potential is conventionally taken to be zero at infinity and negative everywhere else, reflecting the attractive nature of gravity: work must be done on a particle to move it out of a potential well. The concept simplifies many calculations because it is a scalar rather than a vector quantity. Surfaces of constant potential are called equipotential surfaces; near the Earth these correspond approximately to the surface defined by the geoid.

In general relativity

In general relativity, gravity is described by the curvature of spacetime rather than by a potential, but in the weak-field limit the time-time component of the metric tensor plays a role analogous to the Newtonian potential.
