Temporal rangeOrdovician – Late Devonian (c. 470–360 million years ago)
Groups includedCephalaspidomorphi (osteostracans, galeaspids), Pteraspidomorphi (heterostracans) and others

Ostracoderms (from Greek ὄστρακον, "shell", and δέρμα, "skin") are the armoured jawless fish of the Palaeozoic Era. The term embraces a paraphyletic assemblage of primitive, mostly bottom-dwelling vertebrates whose heads — and often much of the body — were encased in a shield of dermal bone.

Anatomy

Ostracoderms lacked jaws and (with a few exceptions) paired fins, moving by undulating a largely naked tail. Most were filter feeders or grazers that sucked up food from the sediment. Groups include the heterostracans (such as Pteraspis), the osteostracans (such as Cephalaspis, with a distinctive horseshoe-shaped head shield), the galeaspids of China and Vietnam, and the anaspids.

Evolutionary significance

Ostracoderms were the first vertebrates with true bone, and their sensory-line systems and inner ears (with two semicircular canals) foreshadow later fish anatomy. They flourished from the Ordovician through the Devonian and declined as jawed fishes (gnathostomes) diversified; modern analyses place various ostracoderm groups along the stem leading to jawed vertebrates.

Decline

By the end of the Devonian all ostracoderm lineages were extinct. Their only surviving jawless relatives today are the lampreys and hagfish, which lack the bony armour entirely.
