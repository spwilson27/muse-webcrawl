Superionic conductor

A superionic conductor, also called a fast ion conductor, is a solid material in which certain ions move almost as freely as they do in a liquid, giving ionic conductivities comparable to those of molten salts or strong electrolyte solutions. Typically one ion sublattice becomes effectively "molten" within a rigid framework of the remaining ions.

Mechanism

In an ordinary ionic crystal, ions vibrate about fixed lattice sites, and conduction requires rare hops into vacancies or interstitial sites. In superionic conductors, the mobile ion sublattice possesses a large number of accessible, interconnected sites with low activation barriers, so ions diffuse rapidly through the structure. The phenomenon was first noted by Michael Faraday in heated silver sulfide and lead(II) fluoride in the 19th century.

Examples

The archetypal example is alpha-phase silver iodide (α-AgI), which above 146 °C attains conductivities around 1 S/cm as its silver ions become highly mobile. Other important materials include β-alumina (a sodium-ion conductor used in sodium–sulfur batteries), NASICON-type phosphates, garnet-type lithium lanthanum zirconium oxide (LLZO), sulfide glasses and crystals such as Li10GeP2S12, and stabilised zirconia, an oxide-ion conductor at high temperature.

Applications

Superionic conductors are central to solid-state batteries, where a ceramic or glassy electrolyte promises improved safety over flammable liquids, and also appear in fuel cells (solid oxide fuel cells use zirconia-based electrolytes), gas sensors, electrochromic devices and supercapacitors.
