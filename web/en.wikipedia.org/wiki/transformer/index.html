Transformer

A transformer is a passive component that transfers electrical energy from one electrical circuit to another circuit, or multiple circuits. A varying current in any coil of the transformer produces a varying magnetic flux in the transformer's core, which induces a varying electromotive force (EMF) across any other coils wound around the same core. Electrical energy can be transferred between separate coils without a metallic (conductive) connection between the two circuits.

Basic principles

Transformers work on Faraday's law of induction. When an alternating current flows in the primary winding, it creates a varying magnetic field that links the secondary winding and induces a voltage in it. The ratio of secondary to primary voltage is approximately equal to the ratio of the numbers of turns in the windings, so transformers can "step up" or "step down" alternating voltages.

Applications

Transformers are essential to the transmission and distribution of alternating-current electric power: generators' output is stepped up to high voltages for efficient long-distance transmission and stepped down again for distribution and use. Smaller transformers are used in electronic equipment, audio systems, instrumentation and impedance matching. See also electromagnetic induction.

History

Induction coils were developed in the 1830s–1870s following Faraday's discovery of induction in 1831. Practical transformers were developed in the 1880s by engineers including Lucien Gaulard, John Gibbs, William Stanley Jr., and the Zipernowsky–Bláthy–Déri team in Budapest, making AC power systems feasible.
