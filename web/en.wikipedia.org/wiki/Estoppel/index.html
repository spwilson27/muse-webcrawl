Estoppel

Estoppel is a common law doctrine that prevents a person from taking a position in litigation that is contrary to a position previously taken, or from denying facts they previously asserted, when another party has reasonably relied on that position or assertion to their detriment.

Estoppel
Field: Common law, Equity
Related: Laches, Waiver, Res judicata

Overview

Estoppel operates as a rule of evidence and as a substantive doctrine in equity. Its purpose is fairness: a party should not be allowed to mislead another and then benefit from the inconsistency.

Types

Estoppel by representation

Arises where a person makes a clear representation of fact, intending another to act on it, and that other does so to their detriment.

Promissory estoppel

Prevents a promisor from going back on a promise on which the promisee has relied, even without consideration. It is famously illustrated by Central London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947) and in American law by Section 90 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts.

Proprietary estoppel

Concerns rights over property, where an assurance about property rights leads another to act detrimentally in reliance.

Issue estoppel / Res judicata

Prevents relitigation of issues or claims already decided by a competent court. See Res judicata.

Elements

Courts generally require a clear representation or promise, reasonable reliance, detriment, and that it would be unconscionable or unjust to allow the party to depart from the representation.
