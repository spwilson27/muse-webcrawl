Aesculus hippocastanum, the horse chestnut, is a species of flowering plant in the soapberry and lychee family Sapindaceae. It is a large deciduous tree, commonly known as the horse chestnut or conker tree. Its glossy brown seeds, known as conkers, are widely used in the children's game of the same name, particularly in Britain and Ireland.

Description

The horse chestnut grows to about 39 metres (128 ft) tall, with a domed crown of stout branches. The leaves are palmately compound, with five to seven leaflets radiating from a central point; each leaflet is 13–30 cm long, making the whole leaf up to 60 cm across. The sticky winter buds are large and distinctive, and the leaf scars left on twigs resemble a horseshoe complete with nail holes.

Flowers appear in spring in upright panicles 10–30 cm tall, white with a small red or yellow blotch at the base of each petal. The fruit is a green, spiky capsule containing one (rarely two or three) large, shiny brown seeds — the conkers.

Distribution and uses

The species is native to a small area in the Pindus Mountains mixed forests and Balkan mixed forests of southeast Europe, but has been widely planted across temperate Europe and North America as a street and park tree since its introduction to western Europe in the early 17th century. Extracts of the seed, standardised for the compound aescin, are used in herbal medicine for chronic venous insufficiency. The seeds are mildly toxic to humans and livestock if eaten raw.

Conkers

The game of conkers involves threading a conker on a string and striking an opponent's conker in turn until one breaks. The World Conker Championships are held annually in Northamptonshire, England. A persistent folk belief holds that conkers placed in houses repel spiders, though there is little scientific evidence for the claim.

Pests and diseases

Since the 1980s European populations have been badly affected by the horse-chestnut leaf miner (Cameraria ohridella), a moth whose larvae tunnel through the leaves, and by bleeding canker, a bacterial disease caused by Pseudomonas syringae pv. aesculi. The species was assessed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List in 2019 because of declines in its small native range.
