Creatinine

Creatinine
Formula: C₄H₇N₃O
Molar mass: 113.12 g/mol
Source: breakdown of creatine phosphate in muscle
Excretion: kidneys, in urine

Creatinine is a waste product formed by the spontaneous, non-enzymatic breakdown of creatine and creatine phosphate in skeletal muscle. It is produced at a fairly constant rate proportional to muscle mass, released into the blood, and excreted almost entirely by the kidneys through glomerular filtration. Because of this, the concentration of creatinine in the blood is the most widely used indicator of kidney function.

Clinical use

Serum creatinine is measured as part of routine blood tests (commonly alongside blood urea nitrogen). Typical reference ranges are about 0.6–1.2 mg/dL in adult men and 0.5–1.1 mg/dL in adult women, varying with muscle mass, age, diet, and assay method. A rising creatinine suggests a fall in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), as occurs in acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease. Clinicians usually convert the creatinine value into an estimated GFR (eGFR) using equations such as the CKD-EPI formula, which adjust for age and sex. Creatinine clearance, estimated from urine and serum measurements or from formulae such as Cockcroft–Gault, was historically used for the same purpose.

Measurement and limitations

The classical assay is the Jaffé reaction, in which creatinine reacts with picric acid in alkaline solution to form a coloured complex; enzymatic assays are also widely used. Creatinine is an imperfect marker: it is influenced by muscle mass, meat intake, and some drugs (such as trimethoprim and cimetidine, which block its tubular secretion), and substantial kidney function can be lost before the serum level rises markedly. Another filtration marker, cystatin C, is sometimes used to supplement it.
