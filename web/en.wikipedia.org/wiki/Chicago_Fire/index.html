The Great Chicago Fire was a conflagration that burned in the city of Chicago, Illinois, from October 8 to October 10, 1871. The fire began in or around a small barn belonging to the O'Leary family on DeKoven Street on the city's near West Side, and, driven by a strong southwest wind through a city built largely of wood after a long dry spell, it jumped the Chicago River twice and devastated the downtown and North Side before rain helped extinguish it on the night of October 9–10.

Course of the fire

The blaze was first noticed around 9 p.m. on Sunday, October 8. A watchman misdirected firefighters to the wrong location, losing critical time, and the exhausted fire department — which had fought another large fire the previous night — could not contain it. Flames consumed the wooden sidewalks, roads and buildings; a whirl of superheated air ("fire devils") carried burning debris across the river. The courthouse, the Tribune building, hotels, theatres and the waterworks all burned; when the pumping station's roof collapsed, the city lost its water supply and the fire burned unchecked until it exhausted the fuel at the city's northern edge and rain began to fall.

Damage and casualties

Approximately 300 people died, though bodies were never fully accounted for. More than 17,500 buildings were destroyed, some 73 miles (117 km) of streets and 120 miles (190 km) of sidewalks were ruined, and about 100,000 of Chicago's 300,000 residents were left homeless. Famous survivors included the O'Leary house itself at the fire's origin, the Chicago Water Tower and Pumping Station on Michigan Avenue, and St. Michael's Church in Old Town.

Cause and the O'Leary legend

Legend holds that Mrs. Catherine O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern in the barn — a story a reporter later admitted inventing, and the city council formally exonerated O'Leary in 1997. The true cause was never determined; other theories include sparks from a chimney, a dropped cigar or pipe by neighbours gambling in the barn, or embers from the previous night's fire. The same dry, windy night saw other catastrophic fires around Lake Michigan, including the Peshtigo Fire in Wisconsin — the deadliest wildfire in American history, which killed over a thousand people but was overshadowed in the press by Chicago.

Rebuilding

Chicago rebuilt rapidly with the help of nationwide and international relief. Stricter building codes pushed construction toward brick, stone, iron and terracotta, and the rebuilt city became a laboratory for modern architecture — within fifteen years it produced the first steel-frame skyscrapers of the Chicago School. The disaster is commemorated today by one of the four stars on Chicago's municipal flag.
