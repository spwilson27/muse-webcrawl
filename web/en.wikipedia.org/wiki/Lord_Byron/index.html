Lord Byron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Lord Byron in Albanian dress (1813) by Thomas Phillips: the poet in gorgeous embroidered costume, the portrait he loved best]

George Gordon Byron, 6th Baron Byron (22 January 1788 – 19 April 1824), known as Lord Byron, was an English poet, peer and revolutionary, the defining celebrity of Romanticism. His fame — Europe-wide, scandal-fed — rested on Childe Harold's Pilgrimage (1812–18), the verse tales, Manfred (1817) and the great comic epic Don Juan (1819–24), left unfinished at his death fighting for Greek independence.

'I awoke one morning and found myself famous', he said of Childe Harold's success. The Byronic hero — brooding, defiant, exiled by secret guilt — stepped from his poems into European culture; Byron's own life fed the type: the glamorous lameness (a club foot), the rumoured incest with his half-sister Augusta Leigh, the separation scandal of 1816, and exile among the Shelleys, Polidori (whose Vampyre grew from Byron's fragment) and Italian carbonari.

Exile and Don Juan

Driven from England in 1816, Byron settled in Venice and Ravenna, wrote Don Juan in ottava rima — a form he turned into the most flexible comic instrument in English verse, swinging from satire to tenderness mid-stanza — and fathered, with Claire Clairmont, the daughter Allegra, whose death in a convent school at five darkened his last years.

Greece and death

In 1823 Byron sailed to join the Greek war of independence, spent his fortune refitting the fleet at Missolonghi, and died there of fever in April 1824, aged 36 — Greeks mourned him as a national hero, and his heart (by tradition) stayed in Greece. Back in England, Westminster Abbey refused him burial; the Poets' Corner memorial came only in 1969.
