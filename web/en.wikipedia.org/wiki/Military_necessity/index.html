Military necessity

Military necessity operates alongside the principles of humanity, distinction, and proportionality.

Military necessity is a legal concept in the law of armed conflict (international humanitarian law) under which a belligerent may apply the degree of force necessary to achieve a legitimate military objective, provided the acts are not otherwise prohibited by law. Together with humanity, it forms one of the foundational principles from which the modern laws of war are derived.

Definition

International humanitarian law defines military necessity as permitting measures that are actually necessary to accomplish a legitimate military purpose and are not otherwise forbidden. The concept emerged in codified form in the Lieber Code of 1863, issued during the American Civil War, which stated that military necessity admits of direct destruction of life or limb of armed enemies and of other persons whose destruction is incidentally unavoidable.

Limits

Military necessity is not a justification for acts prohibited by specific rules: it cannot excuse torture, attacks directed against civilians, or the use of prohibited weapons. It is balanced by the principles of distinction (combatants must distinguish civilian objects from military objectives), proportionality (expected civilian harm must not be excessive in relation to the anticipated military advantage), and humanity (unnecessary suffering must be avoided).

Kriegsraison

The older German doctrine of Kriegsraison — that "necessity in war overrules the manner of warfare" — was rejected at the Nuremberg trials, which held that military necessity cannot override explicit prohibitions of the laws of war.
