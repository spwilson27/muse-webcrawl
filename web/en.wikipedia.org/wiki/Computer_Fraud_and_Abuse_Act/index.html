The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) is a United States cybersecurity law enacted in 1986 as an amendment to the first federal computer crime statute. Codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1030, it criminalises accessing a computer without authorisation, or exceeding authorised access, and has been amended many times as computing has changed.

Provisions

The Act prohibits, among other things: obtaining national security information by unauthorised access; accessing financial or government computers without authorisation; computer fraud; damaging a protected computer (including transmitting malware); trafficking in passwords; and extortion involving threats to computers. It also provides a civil cause of action for victims.

Controversy and case law

The CFAA has been criticised for vague language — particularly "exceeds authorized access" — that critics say can criminalise ordinary terms-of-service violations and chill security research. The prosecution of Aaron Swartz became a prominent focus of reform campaigns. In Van Buren v. United States (2021), the Supreme Court narrowed the statute, holding that "exceeds authorized access" covers entering off-limits areas of a system, not merely using information one is entitled to access for an improper purpose.
