Satyajit Ray

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Satyajit Ray behind the camera on location in rural Bengal in the 1950s, lining up a shot for the Apu films]

Satyajit Ray (2 May 1921 – 23 April 1992) was an Indian film director, screenwriter, author, illustrator, graphic designer and film critic, regarded as one of the greatest filmmakers of the twentieth century. His first film Pather Panchali (1955), made with an amateur crew and borrowed money, won the inaugural Best Human Document award at Cannes and opened Indian cinema to the world.

Ray directed 36 films: the Apu trilogy (Pather Panchali, Aparajito, 1956; Apur Sansar, 1959), Jalsaghar (1958), Devi (1960), Charulata (1964, his own favourite), the Calcutta films and the popular Goopy–Feluda children's and detective works. He designed his posters, composed his scores from the sixties onward, and wrote bestselling detective and science fiction in Bengali.

Pather Panchali and the trilogy

Born in Calcutta to a family of printers and writers (his father Sukumar Ray is Bengali children's literature's Lewis Carroll), Ray worked in advertising and book design, scripted on the London trip where he saw Bicycle Thieves ninety-nine times over (as he put it), and shot Pather Panchali on weekends over three years. The trilogy's humanism — poverty without miserabilism, time as the true subject — announced a new Indian cinema against the Bombay musical mainstream.

Honours and influence

Ray received an Honorary Academy Award in 1992, accepted from his hospital bed weeks before his death, India's Bharat Ratna, the Legion of Honour, and retrospectives everywhere. Akira Kurosawa's verdict — that not to have seen Ray's cinema is to have lived without seeing the sun or moon (in the form usually cited) — stands as his profession's judgement. Wes Anderson's The Darjeeling Limited is dedicated to him; Martin Scorsese and the world's archivists champion his restorations.
