Ethylene tetrafluoroethylene

Ethylene tetrafluoroethylene (ETFE) is a fluorine-based plastic, a copolymer of ethylene and tetrafluoroethylene. It was designed to have high corrosion resistance and mechanical strength over a wide temperature range. ETFE is best known in its foil form as a lightweight, transparent alternative to glass in large-span architecture, where it is used in inflated cushion systems.

Properties

ETFE has a melting point of about 260 °C and retains useful properties from cryogenic temperatures up to roughly 150 °C in continuous service. It is resistant to ultraviolet radiation, weathering, and most chemicals, and it does not degrade or yellow significantly outdoors over decades. The film transmits a high proportion of visible light and can be printed or fritted to control solar gain. ETFE foil is only about 1% of the weight of glass of equivalent area, greatly reducing the load on supporting structures.

Architectural use

In buildings, ETFE foil is fabricated into pneumatic cushions of two or more layers, kept inflated by low-pressure air. The cushions are clamped into aluminium extrusions supported on a lightweight structure. Famous examples include the Eden Project in Cornwall, England (2001), whose biomes are clad in hexagonal ETFE cushions; the Beijing National Aquatics Centre (the "Water Cube", 2008), whose bubble-like façade is made entirely of ETFE; and the Allianz Arena in Munich, whose inflated panels can be illuminated in different colours.

Other uses

Beyond architecture, ETFE is used as a wire and cable insulation in aerospace and industrial applications because of its toughness, radiation resistance, and low flammability. It also serves as a release film and as a protective covering for solar cells.
