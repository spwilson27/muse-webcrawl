François Mansart

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

François Mansart (23 January 1598 – 23 September 1666) was a French architect credited with introducing classicism into Baroque architecture in France. He is regarded as one of the greatest European architects of the 17th century, and his name survives in the mansard roof, the steep double-pitched roof with windows that he popularised (though he did not invent it).

Life and career

Born in Paris, the son of a master carpenter, Mansart was trained in the workshop of Salomon de Brosse, the leading French architect of the previous generation. He worked under the patronage of Gaston, Duke of Orléans, for whom he rebuilt much of the Château de Blois (1635–38) in a classical style that broke decisively with older French practice. Unlike many contemporaries he never travelled to Italy, forming his classicism from prints, drawings, and the French classical tradition.

Major works

His masterpiece is the Château de Maisons (1642–51, now Maisons-Laffitte), built for the financier René de Longueil — a perfectly symmetrical country house of exceptional refinement that became a model for French classical architecture. In Paris he designed the church of the Val-de-Grâce (begun 1645, though he was dismissed before completion), the Hôtel de La Vrillière, and the Sainte-Marie de la Visitation chapel. In his later years he fell from favour as Louis XIV's taste and Colbert's administration favoured other hands, and many of his Parisian hôtels were demolished or altered, so that his reputation rests heavily on Maisons and Blois.

Legacy

Mansart's rigorous, understated classicism shaped the French tradition carried forward by his great-nephew Jules Hardouin-Mansart, architect of Versailles. The mansard roof — used by Mansart extensively at Maisons and Blois — took his name in both French and English.
