The Estates General of 1789 was a general assembly representing the French estates of the realm: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and the commoners (Third Estate). Summoned by King Louis XVI, it was the first meeting of the Estates General since 1614. It opened on 5 May 1789 at Versailles and marked the opening phase of the French Revolution. After weeks of deadlock over voting procedure, the Third Estate proclaimed itself the National Assembly, and the assembly was dissolved in its original form on 27 June 1789 when the King ordered the other estates to join it.

Background

By the 1780s the French monarchy faced a severe fiscal crisis. War debts, including the cost of supporting the American Revolution, an inefficient tax system riddled with exemptions for the privileged orders, and failed reform attempts by ministers such as Turgot, Calonne and Necker left the crown effectively bankrupt. The Assembly of Notables of 1787 refused to sanction new taxes, insisting that only the Estates General could approve fundamental fiscal change. On 8 August 1788 Louis XVI announced that the Estates General would meet the following spring, and Necker was recalled to direct the preparations.

Elections and cahiers

Elections were held in early 1789 across France. The Third Estate was granted double representation — roughly twice as many deputies as each of the other orders — although the crucial question of whether voting would be by order or by head was left unresolved. In total about 1,139 deputies were elected: some 291 clergy, 270 nobility and 578 commoners. Assemblies at every level drafted cahiers de doléances, lists of grievances and demands that reveal widespread support for constitutional government, fiscal equality and freedom of the press, alongside local complaints about taxes, feudal dues and grain prices.

The deadlock over voting

The Estates General opened with great ceremony on 5 May 1789 in the Hôtel des Menus-Plaisirs at Versailles. Almost immediately it stalled over the verification of credentials: the Third Estate demanded that verification be held in common and that votes be counted by head, which, with sympathetic members of the clergy and nobility, would give reformers a majority. Voting by order, by contrast, allowed the two privileged estates to outvote the Third two to one. Six weeks of negotiation produced no compromise.

National Assembly and Tennis Court Oath

On 17 June 1789 the deputies of the Third Estate, joined by a few parish priests, voted to rename themselves the National Assembly, claiming to represent at least 96 percent of the nation. The King responded by closing their meeting hall, ostensibly for preparations for a royal session. On 20 June the deputies reassembled in a nearby tennis court and swore the Tennis Court Oath, pledging not to disperse until France had a constitution. At the royal session of 23 June Louis XVI proposed reforms but ordered the estates to deliberate separately; the Assembly refused to disperse. On 27 June the King capitulated and instructed the clergy and nobility to join the National Assembly, effectively ending the Estates General as a body of separate orders.

Legacy

The Estates General of 1789 was the last ever held in France. Its transformation into the National Assembly transferred sovereignty, in practice, from the crown to representatives of the nation, and set in motion the events — the storming of the Bastille on 14 July, the abolition of privileges on 4 August, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen — that destroyed the ancien régime.
