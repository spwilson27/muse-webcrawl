Magnetic dipole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A magnetic dipole is the limit of either a closed loop of electric current or a pair of magnetic poles as the size of the source is reduced to zero while keeping the magnetic moment constant. It is the magnetic analogue of the electric dipole, but the analogy is not perfect: isolated magnetic monopoles have never been observed, so magnetic dipoles in nature arise from current loops and from the intrinsic spin of elementary particles.

Field of a dipole

Far from the source, the magnetic field of a dipole with moment m at position r falls off as 1/r3 and has the characteristic form B ∝ (μ0/4πr3)[3(m·r̂)r̂ − m]. Along the axis of the dipole the field is parallel to m and twice as strong as in the equatorial plane at the same distance, where it is antiparallel.

Examples

The Earth's magnetic field is approximated to first order by a dipole tilted with respect to the rotation axis. Elementary particles such as the electron possess intrinsic magnetic dipole moments associated with their spin; the anomalous magnetic dipole moment of the electron and muon provides one of the most precise tests of quantum electrodynamics.

Forces and torques

A dipole in an external field B experiences a torque τ = m × B tending to align it with the field, and in a non-uniform field a net force F = ∇(m·B). Dipole–dipole interactions govern phenomena from the behaviour of compass needles to magnetic resonance imaging contrast and the ordering of magnetic materials.
