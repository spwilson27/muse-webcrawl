Council of Florence

Ecumenical council, 1431–1449; sessions at Basel, Ferrara, Florence, and Rome.

The Council of Florence is the seventeenth ecumenical council recognised by the Roman Catholic Church, held between 1431 and 1449. It was originally convoked at Basel by Pope Martin V, transferred to Ferrara in 1438 and then to Florence in 1439, where it achieved a decree of union with the Byzantine Greek Church. A rump council continued at Basel in opposition, prolonging the conciliarist controversy.

Background

The council was summoned in the aftermath of the Council of Constance, which had ended the Western Schism but whose decree Frequens required regular future councils. Delegates at Basel clashed with Pope Eugene IV, who attempted to dissolve the council; the majority refused and proceeded to assert conciliar supremacy, eventually electing the antipope Felix V—the last historical antipope.

Union with the Greeks

Seeking Western military aid against the advancing Ottoman Turks, the Byzantine emperor John VIII Palaiologos travelled to Italy with Patriarch Joseph II and some 700 delegates. After months of theological negotiation at Ferrara and Florence, the decree of union, Laetentur Caeli, was solemnly proclaimed in Florence Cathedral on 6 July 1439. The Greeks accepted the Latin positions on the procession of the Holy Spirit (Filioque), purgatory, and papal primacy, while retaining their own liturgical rites. The union was repudiated by much of the clergy and populace in Constantinople and was never effectively implemented before the city's fall in 1453.

Other unions and legacy

The council also negotiated decrees of union with the Armenians (1439) and the Copts of Egypt (1442), and issued the bull Cantate Domino. By outlasting the rival assembly at Basel, which dissolved in 1449, the council marked the practical defeat of conciliarism and the restoration of papal authority, though many of its union decrees proved ephemeral.
