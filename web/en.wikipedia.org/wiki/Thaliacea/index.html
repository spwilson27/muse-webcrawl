Thaliacea

The Thaliacea are a class of marine animals within the subphylum Tunicata (the tunicates). Unlike their bottom-dwelling relatives the ascidians (sea squirts), thaliaceans are free-floating (planktonic) throughout their lives. The class comprises three orders: the salps, the doliolids, and the pyrosomes. They are found in warm and temperate seas worldwide and are notable for their transparent, gelatinous bodies and, in many species, complex life cycles involving alternation of generations.

Characteristics

Thaliaceans have barrel- or tube-shaped bodies surrounded by a cellulose-like tunic. Bands of muscle encircle the body; their rhythmic contraction pumps water through the animal, simultaneously propelling it by jet propulsion and driving a feeding current across a mucus net that traps phytoplankton and other small particles. This combined swimming-and-filtering system makes salps among the most efficient filter feeders in the ocean, able to strain particles as small as bacteria.

Life cycle

Salps and doliolids exhibit an alternation of generations. A solitary asexual generation (the oozoid) buds off chains of the sexual generation (blastozooids); each individual in the chain is a hermaphrodite that produces a single egg, fertilised internally, which develops into a new solitary individual. This cycle allows populations to explode rapidly when phytoplankton blooms — salp swarms can appear within weeks. Pyrosomes form free-floating colonies of hundreds to thousands of tiny zooids embedded in a common tunic; many are bioluminescent, and some colonies grow several metres long.

Ecology

Because they package tiny phytoplankton into large, fast-sinking faecal pellets and carcasses, thaliaceans play an outsized role in the ocean's biological carbon pump, exporting carbon to the deep sea. They are eaten by fish, sea turtles, birds, and other predators, and their blooms can clog fishing nets and power-plant intakes.
