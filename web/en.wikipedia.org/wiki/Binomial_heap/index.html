In computer science, a binomial heap is a data structure that acts as a priority queue while also supporting fast merging of two heaps. It was introduced by Jean Vuillemin in 1978. A binomial heap is implemented as a collection of binomial trees, each of which satisfies the heap property, and it can be viewed as the heap analogue of the binary number system: just as a binary number has at most one 1-bit per position, a binomial heap has at most one binomial tree of each order.

A binomial heap: a set of heap-ordered binomial trees, at most one of each order.

Binomial trees

A binomial tree of order 0 is a single node. A binomial tree of order k is formed by linking two binomial trees of order k−1, making the root of one the child of the other. Such a tree has exactly 2k nodes, and the number of nodes at each depth follows the binomial coefficients — hence the name. The root of a tree of order k has k children, which are the roots of binomial trees of orders 0 through k−1.

Operations

Merging two heaps proceeds like binary addition: trees of equal order are linked into one tree of the next order, with the smaller root on top to preserve the heap property. Insertion is a merge with a one-node heap, costing O(1) amortized time. Finding the minimum requires scanning the O(log n) tree roots. Deleting the minimum removes its tree, promotes that tree's children (themselves a binomial heap) into the collection, and merges — all in O(log n). Decrease-key bubbles a key upward through its tree, also in O(log n).

Comparison

Binomial heaps support the full priority-queue operation set with amortized bounds comparable to binary heaps, while adding an efficient union operation that binary heaps lack. The Fibonacci heap can be seen as a "lazy" generalization of the binomial heap that improves decrease-key to amortized constant time, at the price of more complex bookkeeping.
