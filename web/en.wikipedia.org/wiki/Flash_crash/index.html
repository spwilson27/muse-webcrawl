Flash crash

For the specific 2010 event, see Flash Crash.

DefinitionVery rapid, deep, volatile fall in security prices within an extremely short time, followed by a quick recovery
Associated withAlgorithmic and high-frequency trading

A flash crash is a very rapid, deep and volatile fall in security prices occurring within an extremely short time period, followed by a comparatively rapid recovery. Flash crashes are most often associated with electronic and algorithmic trading, in which automated strategies can cascade: falling prices trigger further automated selling, market makers withdraw, and liquidity briefly vanishes.

Notable events

6 May 2010

The original and most famous flash crash struck U.S. equity markets: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 9 per cent intraday before recovering much of the loss within minutes. See Flash Crash.

Later events

Subsequent flash events include the U.S. Treasury market turmoil of 15 October 2014, in which the 10-year yield moved by an unusually large amount in minutes; the sterling flash crash of 7 October 2016, when the pound fell about 6 per cent against the dollar in Asian trading; a flash crash in Japanese yen crosses on 3 January 2019; and repeated mini flash crashes in individual stocks, which occur far more frequently than market-wide events.

Mechanisms

Contributing mechanisms identified by researchers and regulators include feedback loops among execution algorithms, "hot potato" passing of inventory among high-frequency firms, sudden liquidity withdrawal, erroneous ("fat-finger") orders, and deliberate manipulation such as spoofing. Fragmentation of trading across many venues can hamper price discovery during stress.

Mitigation

Exchanges and regulators responded with circuit breakers, limit up–limit down bands, kill switches, order-to-trade ratio limits, and requirements for market makers to maintain continuous quotes.
