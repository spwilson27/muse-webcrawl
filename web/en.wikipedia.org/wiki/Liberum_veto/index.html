Liberum veto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The liberum veto (Latin for "free veto") was a parliamentary device in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth under which any single deputy to the Sejm could nullify an entire legislative session by shouting Nie pozwalam! ("I do not allow!"). Because legislation required unanimity, one objection dissolved the Sejm and invalidated all acts it had passed during that session.

History

The practice derived from the principle of unanimity in the Commonwealth's noble democracy and from instructions binding deputies to their regional sejmiks. A veto was first used to dissolve a Sejm in 1652 by the deputy Władysław Siciński. Thereafter use of the veto grew; during the reign of Augustus III (1734–1763) nearly every Sejm was broken up, foreign powers — especially Russia and Prussia — routinely bribed deputies to veto reforms, and the Commonwealth became effectively ungovernable, contributing to its partitions by neighbouring empires.

Abolition and legacy

The Constitution of 3 May 1791 abolished the liberum veto in favour of majority voting, one of the last major reforms before the state's destruction. The term survives as a byword for the paralysis that an absolute unanimity requirement can produce, and is still invoked in debates about unanimity rules in international bodies.
