Judea (Latin: Iudaea; Hebrew: יהודה, Yehuda; Greek: Ἰουδαία) is the historical and geographical name given to the mountainous southern part of the region of Palestine/the Land of Israel. The name originates with the biblical tribe of Judah and the associated Kingdom of Judah. Judea was the heartland of Jewish life in antiquity, the setting of the Hasmonean and Herodian kingdoms, and a province of the Roman Empire famous for the Jewish–Roman wars.

Geography

Judea proper is a rugged limestone hill country running from the Shephelah foothills in the west down to the wilderness of the Dead Sea in the east, and from the hills around Beit El in the north to the vicinity of Beersheba in the south. Jerusalem, Hebron and Bethlehem lie along its central watershed. Eastward the land drops steeply to the Jordan Valley and the Dead Sea, the lowest land on earth; the semi-arid eastern slopes are known as the Judean Desert, home in antiquity to fortresses such as Masada and Herodium and to the sectarian community at Qumran.

History

Biblical and Persian periods

According to the biblical account, Judea was the territory of the tribe of Judah and formed the Kingdom of Judah after the division of the united monarchy around 930 BCE. Babylonian forces destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in 586 BCE and exiled much of the population. Under Persian rule the region was reconstituted as the province of Yehud, the Second Temple was rebuilt, and the returning exiles under Ezra and Nehemiah re-established Jerusalem as the centre of Jewish worship.

Hellenistic and Hasmonean periods

After Alexander the Great, Judea passed between the Ptolemaic and Seleucid kingdoms. The Maccabean revolt (from 167 BCE) against Seleucid suppression of Judaism produced the independent Hasmonean kingdom, which expanded to rule most of the country until Pompey captured Jerusalem in 63 BCE.

Roman period

Rome ruled first through client kings — above all Herod the Great (37–4 BCE), who rebuilt the Temple on a monumental scale — and from 6 CE through prefects and procurators in the province of Judaea, among them Pontius Pilate. The Great Revolt (66–73 CE) ended with the destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple in 70 CE and the fall of Masada. After the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE), Hadrian renamed the province Syria Palaestina, and the centre of Jewish life shifted to Galilee.

Later usage

"Judea" remained a literary and ecclesiastical term through the Byzantine, Islamic and Ottoman periods. In modern usage it is applied to the southern part of the West Bank, often in the combined administrative phrase "Judea and Samaria", while geographers use it for the historical hill country itself.
