The heliocentric system from Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543).

Heliocentrism is the astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun at the centre of the universe. Historically, it stood opposed to geocentrism, which placed Earth at the centre. The idea was first proposed by Aristarchus of Samos in the 3rd century BC, but it was Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543), published the year of his death, that presented a complete mathematical model.

Development

Copernicus retained circular orbits and epicycles, so his system was not decisively more accurate than Ptolemy's; its appeal was conceptual — it explained retrograde motion naturally and ordered the planets by period. Johannes Kepler replaced circles with ellipses and derived his three laws of planetary motion (1609–1619) from Tycho Brahe's data, and Galileo Galilei's telescopic discoveries — the phases of Venus, Jupiter's moons — undermined the Ptolemaic model, provoking his trial by the Roman Inquisition in 1633. Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) supplied the physical mechanism, and direct observational proofs accumulated with Bradley's stellar aberration (1729) and Bessel's measurement of stellar parallax (1838).

Significance

The Copernican Revolution is a central episode of the Scientific Revolution and a classic case of a paradigm shift: it displaced humanity from the cosmic centre and reshaped philosophy as much as astronomy. Modern cosmology goes further — the Sun itself is an ordinary star, and contemporary cosmology assumes no centre at all.
