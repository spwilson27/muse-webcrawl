Germ cell tumor

A germ cell tumor (GCT) is a neoplasm derived from germ cells — the cells that normally give rise to sperm or eggs. Germ cell tumors most often arise in the gonads (testis or ovary) but can also occur outside them, along the midline of the body, because of abnormal migration of germ cells during embryonic development. They may be benign or malignant and occur in both children and adults.

Classification

The major division is between seminomas (called dysgerminomas in the ovary and germinomas in the central nervous system) and nonseminomas. Nonseminomatous tumors include embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac (endodermal sinus) tumor, choriocarcinoma, and teratoma. Teratomas contain tissues derived from multiple germ layers and are often benign when mature, especially in the ovary (dermoid cysts); immature teratomas can be malignant. Mixed tumors containing several components are common.

Presentation and diagnosis

Testicular germ cell tumors — the most common solid cancer in young men — usually present as a painless testicular lump. Ovarian tumors may cause abdominal pain or swelling, and sacrococcygeal teratomas are the most common tumors of newborns. Diagnosis combines imaging, biopsy or orchiectomy pathology, and serum tumor markers: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), produced by yolk sac elements, and human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG), produced by choriocarcinoma and some seminomas. Elevated AFP rules out a pure seminoma.

Treatment and prognosis

Germ cell tumors are among the most curable of cancers. The introduction of cisplatin-based combination chemotherapy in the 1970s, pioneered by Lawrence Einhorn, transformed metastatic testicular cancer from a usually fatal disease into one cured in the great majority of cases. Treatment combines surgery (radical inguinal orchiectomy for testicular tumors), chemotherapy regimens such as BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin), and in some settings radiotherapy for seminoma. Even patients with widespread metastases frequently achieve complete remission, and overall survival for testicular germ cell tumors exceeds 95 percent in many series.

Extragonadal tumors

Extragonadal germ cell tumors occur in the mediastinum, retroperitoneum, pineal and suprasellar regions of the brain, and the sacrococcygeal area. Mediastinal nonseminomatous tumors carry a poorer prognosis and are associated with Klinefelter syndrome, which also increases germ cell tumor risk overall.
