Carbonic anhydrase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The carbonic anhydrases (or carbonate dehydratases) form a family of enzymes that catalyse the rapid interconversion of carbon dioxide and water to bicarbonate and protons (and vice versa): CO2 + H2O ⇌ HCO3− + H+. This reaction proceeds slowly on its own but is essential to CO2 transport in blood, acid–base balance, respiration, bone resorption, and the formation of aqueous humour and cerebrospinal fluid.

Kinetics and mechanism

Carbonic anhydrases are among the fastest enzymes known: a single molecule of human carbonic anhydrase II can hydrate roughly a million molecules of CO2 per second, a rate limited mainly by diffusion. Most animal carbonic anhydrases are metalloenzymes containing a zinc ion in the active site, coordinated by three histidine residues. The zinc activates a bound water molecule to hydroxide, which attacks CO2 to form bicarbonate; a histidine "proton shuttle" then regenerates the active site. A cadmium-containing form found in marine diatoms is a notable exception, showing that the metal centre can vary.

Families and isoforms

At least seven genetically distinct carbonic anhydrase families (α, β, γ, δ, ζ, η and θ) have evolved independently — a textbook example of convergent evolution. Humans possess α-class enzymes: sixteen isoforms (CA I–XV) differing in tissue distribution, subcellular location and catalytic activity, including cytosolic, membrane-bound, mitochondrial and secreted forms.

Clinical significance

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide and dorzolamide are used to treat glaucoma (by reducing aqueous humour production), altitude sickness, epilepsy and, as weak diuretics, oedema. Deficiency of CA II causes a syndrome of osteopetrosis with renal tubular acidosis and cerebral calcification.
