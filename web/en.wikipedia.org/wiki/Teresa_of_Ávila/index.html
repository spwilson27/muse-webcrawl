Teresa of Ávila

BornTeresa Sánchez de Cepeda y Ahumada, 28 March 1515, Ávila, Spain
Died4 October 1582, Alba de Tormes, Spain
Canonised12 March 1622, by Pope Gregory XV
Feast15 October
HonoursDoctor of the Church (1970)

Teresa of Ávila (born Teresa Sánchez de Cepeda y Ahumada; 28 March 1515 – 4 October 1582), also called Saint Teresa of Jesus, was a Spanish Carmelite nun, mystic, and religious reformer. She was a leading figure of the Counter-Reformation, the founder — with Saint John of the Cross — of the Discalced Carmelites, and the author of classics of spiritual literature including her autobiography, The Interior Castle, and The Way of Perfection. In 1970 she became the first woman named a Doctor of the Church.

Life

Teresa entered the Carmelite Convent of the Incarnation in Ávila in 1535, at the age of twenty. After years of conventional religious life marked by serious illness, she underwent from the mid-1550s a profound conversion to a life of intense mental prayer, experiencing visions and ecstasies that she described with celebrated literary vividness — including the "transverberation", in which an angel pierced her heart with a golden dart, later immortalised in Bernini's sculpture The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa.

Convinced that the Carmelite rule had grown lax, she founded in 1562 the small Convent of Saint Joseph in Ávila, devoted to strict enclosure, poverty, and contemplative prayer. Despite opposition from civic and ecclesiastical authorities — and scrutiny from the Inquisition, to which her writings were denounced — she went on to found seventeen convents across Spain, travelling widely in gruelling conditions, and with John of the Cross extended the reform to the friars.

Writings

Teresa wrote under obedience to her confessors, in a direct, conversational Castilian style. The Life of Teresa of Jesus (written 1562–1565) is her spiritual autobiography. The Way of Perfection instructs her nuns in prayer. Her masterpiece, The Interior Castle (1577), describes the soul as a castle of seven mansions through which the pray-er journeys toward union with God at its centre. Her writings are considered peaks of both Spanish Golden Age literature and Christian mystical theology. She died at Alba de Tormes on the night of 4 October 1582 — the night the Gregorian calendar reform took effect in Spain, so that her death is also dated 15 October, the date of her feast.
