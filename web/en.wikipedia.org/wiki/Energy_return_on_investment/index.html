Energy return on investment

Energy return on investment (EROI or ERoEI), also called energy returned on energy invested (EROEI), is the ratio of the energy delivered by a source to the energy used to obtain it:

EROI = Energy delivered ÷ Energy invested

When the EROI of a resource is at or below 1, it becomes an energy sink rather than a net source. The concept, developed in energy ecology by researchers including Howard T. Odum and Charles A. S. Hall, is used to compare fuels, judge energy transitions, and estimate the surplus energy available to support economic activity.

Typical values

Estimates vary with methodology and boundaries, but commonly cited ranges include: early 20th-century oil and gas above 50:1, declining as fields deplete (modern conventional oil often estimated at 15–30:1); coal high (often above 30:1 at the mine mouth); hydropower frequently above 40:1; wind turbines around 15–25:1; solar photovoltaics around 8–15:1 and rising with technology; nuclear power estimates vary widely; corn ethanol near or below 1.5:1. Unconventional sources such as tar sands are typically estimated lower than conventional oil.

Interpretation and debate

Advocates argue a complex society needs an average EROI of at least roughly 5–10:1 to maintain infrastructure, health care, and education. Critics note definitional inconsistencies — what counts as "invested" energy, where to set the system boundary, and whether to adjust for energy quality (electricity versus heat) — and that EROI alone says nothing about power density, intermittency, or environmental damage.
