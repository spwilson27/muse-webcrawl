Forced labour under German rule during World War II

Forced labour under German rule during World War II involved the coercive use of millions of foreign civilians and prisoners of war in the German war economy. By September 1944, some 7.9 million foreign civilian workers and prisoners of war — roughly a quarter of Germany's workforce — were employed in the Reich, in addition to concentration camp inmates and others compelled to work under brutal conditions.

Recruitment and deportation

Early in the war, workers were recruited from occupied countries with promises of wages, but as demand grew, recruitment became systematic coercion: mass roundups and deportations brought workers from Poland, the occupied Soviet territories (the Ostarbeiter), France and elsewhere. Fritz Sauckel, appointed General Plenipotentiary for Labour Deployment in 1942, organised the programme and was later convicted at Nuremberg for war crimes and crimes against humanity.

Conditions

Conditions varied by nationality, reflecting the Nazi racial hierarchy: Western Europeans generally received better treatment, while Poles and Soviet workers faced discriminatory decrees, starvation rations, confinement in camps and violent discipline. Prisoners in concentration camps were also hired out to German industry under the policy of "extermination through labour" for some categories of prisoner.

Aftermath

After 1945, surviving labourers faced repatriation, often coerced in the case of Soviet citizens. Compensation largely came decades later: in 2000 Germany established the Foundation "Remembrance, Responsibility and Future", funded jointly by the state and German industry, which paid out more than €4 billion to former forced labourers.
