Adaptive radiation

Adaptive radiation
FieldEvolutionary biology
DefinitionRapid diversification of a single ancestral lineage into many species occupying different ecological niches

In evolutionary biology, adaptive radiation is a process in which organisms diversify rapidly from an ancestral species into a multitude of new forms, particularly when a change in the environment makes new resources available, alters biotic interactions or opens new ecological niches. The phenomenon is a major source of biodiversity: a single lineage can, over geologically short periods, produce a wide array of species adapted to different diets, habitats and ways of life.

Characteristics

Adaptive radiations are commonly recognised by four features: a common ancestry of the component species; a correlation between phenotype and environment (species' traits match the niches they occupy); trait utility — the traits confer a performance advantage; and rapid speciation compared with background rates.

Examples

The classic case is Darwin's finches of the Galápagos Islands, whose beaks diversified for different foods after colonisation from the South American mainland. Other celebrated radiations include the Hawaiian honeycreepers and silversword alliance, the cichlid fishes of Lakes Victoria, Tanganyika and Malawi (which produced hundreds of species), Caribbean Anolis lizards, and the diversification of mammals and birds into niches vacated by the non-avian dinosaurs after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction 66 million years ago.

Causes

Radiations are typically triggered by ecological opportunity: colonisation of an island or lake with few competitors, the extinction of incumbents, or the evolution of a "key innovation" — such as the pharyngeal jaw of cichlids or the nectar-feeding apparatus of honeycreepers — that opens access to previously unavailable resources.
