Pope Boniface VIII

Pope Boniface VIII (Latin: Bonifatius VIII; born Benedetto Caetani, c. 1230 – 11 October 1303) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 24 December 1294 until his death in 1303. A trained canon lawyer from a powerful Roman baronial family, he is remembered for his sweeping claims of papal authority — expressed in the bull Unam Sanctam (1302) — for proclaiming the first Roman Jubilee in 1300, and for his ruinous conflict with King Philip IV of France, which ended with his humiliation at Anagni and foreshadowed the Avignon Papacy.

Early life and election

Benedetto Caetani studied law at Bologna and served as a papal notary and diplomat, becoming cardinal deacon in 1281 and cardinal priest in 1291. He succeeded Pope Celestine V, the elderly hermit-pope whose abdication in December 1294 — which Caetani, as his adviser, had helped engineer — shocked Christendom. Boniface had Celestine imprisoned in the castle of Fumone, where he died in 1296, a fact that fed hostile legends about the new pope.

Pontificate

Boniface worked to restore order in Rome and the Papal States, crusaded in Sicily, and waged a savage feud against the rival Colonna family, whose cardinals he deposed and whose stronghold of Palestrina he razed. In 1300 he proclaimed the first Holy Year Jubilee, drawing immense crowds of pilgrims to Rome and greatly enriching the Church; Dante, who despised Boniface, depicts the Jubilee crowds in the Inferno. His bull Clericis laicos (1296) forbade clerical taxation by lay rulers, provoking conflict with Edward I of England and, above all, Philip IV of France. After a temporary compromise, the quarrel escalated: the arrest of the bishop of Pamiers (1301), the bull Ausculta Fili, and finally Unam Sanctam (18 November 1302), which declared that "it is altogether necessary to salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman Pontiff."

Outrage of Anagni and death

Philip IV responded by denouncing Boniface as a heretic and simoniac and sending his agent Guillaume de Nogaret to Italy. On 7 September 1303, Nogaret and Sciarra Colonna seized the pope at Anagni — the "Outrage of Anagni" — where the aged pontiff, in full regalia, refused to flee. Freed by the townspeople after three days, Boniface returned to Rome a broken man and died on 11 October 1303. Dante placed him in the eighth circle of Hell among the simoniacs, yet the pope's legal and institutional legacy — including the Liber Sextus of canon law (1298) and the founding of the University of Rome La Sapienza (1303) — was substantial.
