Hoof

A hoof (plural hooves or hoofs) is the tip of a toe of an ungulate mammal, strengthened by a thick and horny keratin covering. Artiodactyls are even-toed ungulates, such as cattle, deer, pigs, and sheep, whose weight is borne equally by the third and fourth toes, giving the familiar cloven (split) hoof. Perissodactyls are odd-toed ungulates, such as horses and rhinoceroses, whose weight falls mainly on one central toe enclosed in a single hoof.

Structure

The hoof wall grows continuously from the coronary band at its top, like a human fingernail—to which the hoof is homologous. Within the wall sit the sole, the V-shaped frog (in horses), and the sensitive laminae that bond the wall to the coffin bone (third phalanx) inside. The hoof acts as a shock absorber: as the horse's weight bears down, the wall expands slightly and the frog pumps blood back up the leg, an arrangement sometimes called a "second heart."

Evolution and adaptation

Hooves evolved as grazing mammals adapted to open grasslands, trading grasping feet for speed and endurance. Different lineages suit different terrain: the sharp-edged hooves of mountain goats grip rock; camels have broad, padded toes for sand; and reindeer spread their cloven hooves to walk on snow and swim.

Care

Domestic horses require regular trimming by a farrier every six to eight weeks, because hooves confined or worked on hard surfaces wear unevenly. Horseshoes of metal (and nowadays sometimes plastic or glue-on materials) protect the wall but are not needed by all horses. Diseases and disorders of the hoof include laminitis (inflammation of the laminae, which can cause the coffin bone to rotate), thrush, abscesses, and navicular syndrome; in cattle, foot rot and digital dermatitis are major economic problems in dairy farming.
