Nucleophilic substitution

In organic chemistry, a nucleophilic substitution is a class of reaction in which a nucleophile — a reagent species rich in electrons — selectively bonds with or attacks the positive or partially positive charge of an atom or group of atoms, replacing a leaving group. The atom or group receiving the nucleophile is called the electrophile; the atom bearing the leaving group is usually carbon, and the leaving group departs as a stable species, often the conjugate base of a strong acid.

Unimolecular substitution (SN1)

In the SN1 mechanism the leaving group departs first, in the rate-determining step, forming a planar carbocation intermediate that the nucleophile then attacks from either side. The result is typically racemisation of a stereocentre, and the reaction is favoured by tertiary (or resonance-stabilised) substrates and polar protic solvents. Rearrangements of the carbocation may occur.

Bimolecular substitution (SN2)

In the SN2 mechanism the nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group ("backside attack") in a single concerted step, so the rate depends on both reactants. The geometry passes through a trigonal-bipyramidal transition state and the configuration at carbon is inverted — Walden inversion. The reaction is favoured by methyl and primary substrates and is hindered by steric bulk.

Competing reactions and factors

Substitution competes with elimination (E1/E2), and the outcome depends on substrate structure, nucleophile strength and basicity, leaving-group ability, solvent, and temperature. Related processes include nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) on electron-poor aromatic rings, and acyl substitution at carbonyl carbons.
