Thrust-specific fuel consumption

High-bypass turbofans achieve low thrust-specific fuel consumption.

Thrust-specific fuel consumption (TSFC) is the fuel consumption of an engine divided by the thrust it produces. It is the standard measure of the fuel efficiency of jet engines and other reaction engines, expressing how much fuel must be burned per unit time to sustain a given thrust. A lower TSFC indicates a more efficient engine at producing thrust.

TSFC = ṁf / F

where ṁf is the mass flow rate of fuel and F is the thrust. Common units are kilograms of fuel per newton-second (kg/(N·s)) in SI, or pounds of fuel per pound-force-hour (lb/(lbf·h)) in engineering usage. TSFC varies with flight speed, altitude and throttle setting, so quoted figures specify test conditions.

Relation to specific impulse

TSFC is the reciprocal of specific impulse expressed in weight-flow terms: an engine with a high specific impulse has a low TSFC. For air-breathing engines, TSFC at cruise largely determines aircraft range through the Breguet range equation, so airliner engines are optimised for the lowest possible TSFC at the cruise condition rather than at take-off. Comparing engines by TSFC alone ignores differences in weight and frontal area, which designers must trade against efficiency.

Brake-specific fuel consumption

For engines producing shaft power rather than jet thrust — piston engines, turboprops and turboshafts — the analogous measure is brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC), the fuel flow per unit of shaft power output, because thrust cannot be measured directly at the engine.
