Russellian monism

Russellian monism is a theory in the philosophy of mind, named after Bertrand Russell, holding that consciousness and the physical world are grounded in a single (monistic) kind of reality. It is inspired by Russell's argument, developed in works such as The Analysis of Matter (1927), that physics describes matter only in terms of its structure and relations, leaving its intrinsic nature unknown.

The view

Russellian monists argue that the intrinsic properties of fundamental physical entities — sometimes called quiddities — may be phenomenal (experiential) or protophenomenal. Consciousness is thereby located within the basic fabric of nature rather than treated as something wholly separate from the physical, allowing the theory to avoid both reductive physicalism and substance dualism. Depending on how the intrinsic properties are understood, versions of the view shade into panpsychism or panprotopsychism.

Reception

The theory has been developed by contemporary philosophers including David Chalmers, Galen Strawson, and Philip Goff, and remains controversial: critics raise the "combination problem" of how micro-level experiences could combine into a unified human consciousness, a difficulty first pressed by William James.
