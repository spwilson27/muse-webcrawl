A blind audition is a method of evaluating musical performance in which the candidate plays behind a screen so that the judging committee cannot see them. Adopted widely by American symphony orchestras from the 1970s onward, the practice is intended to ensure that hiring rests on sound alone rather than on the performer's sex, appearance, or personal connections. Candidates are typically identified by number, and some orchestras lay carpet to muffle the sound of footsteps and heels.

Evidence and debate

A widely cited study by economists Claudia Goldin and Cecilia Rouse, published in the American Economic Review in 2000, analysed audition records and personnel files from major orchestras and estimated that the use of screens substantially increased the likelihood that women would advance in auditions and be hired, accounting for a significant part of the rise in the female share of new orchestra members. Later commentators have debated the size of the effect, and some musicians note that final rounds are often unblinded, so screens chiefly govern preliminary and semi-final rounds.

Wider use

The success of the practice in music popularised the general idea of blind evaluation — anonymised grading, blind manuscript review, and identity-blind first stages in recruitment — as a tool against unconscious bias. Popular television singing competitions such as The Voice borrowed the imagery of judges facing away from auditioning singers.
