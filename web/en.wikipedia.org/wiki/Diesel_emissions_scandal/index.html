Diesel emissions scandal

Volkswagen emissions scandal
DateExposed September 2015
Also known asDieselgate
ParticipantsVolkswagen Group (VW, Audi, Porsche); other manufacturers later investigated
OutcomeOver $38 billion in fines, settlements and buybacks; criminal convictions; accelerated shift to electric vehicles

The diesel emissions scandal, also known as Dieselgate, erupted in September 2015 when the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued a notice of violation to the Volkswagen Group, finding that the company had installed "defeat device" software in about 11 million diesel cars worldwide, including some 580,000 in the United States.

The defeat device

The software detected when a car was undergoing emissions testing — from steering position, speed, engine duration and barometric pressure — and engaged full emissions controls so the car would pass nitrogen oxide (NOx) limits. On the road, the controls were reduced and the cars emitted up to 40 times the legal limit of NOx. The cheating was uncovered after researchers at West Virginia University, working with the International Council on Clean Transportation, found large discrepancies between laboratory and real-world emissions of Volkswagen diesels.

Consequences

Volkswagen's chief executive Martin Winterkorn resigned within days. The company set aside tens of billions of euros; total costs exceeded €35 billion (over $38 billion), including a $14.7 billion settlement in the United States covering buybacks and compensation, and a $2.8 billion criminal fine after pleading guilty in 2017. Executives and engineers were prosecuted in the US and Germany; Audi CEO Rupert Stadler was arrested in 2018 and later convicted in Germany. Investigations spread to other manufacturers, and regulators worldwide tightened real-driving emissions testing. The scandal badly damaged the reputation of diesel technology in passenger cars and accelerated Volkswagen's pivot to electric vehicles.

Health and environmental impact

Studies estimated that the excess emissions caused dozens of premature deaths in the United States and over a thousand in Europe, owing to the respiratory and cardiovascular effects of nitrogen oxides and resulting particulate pollution.
