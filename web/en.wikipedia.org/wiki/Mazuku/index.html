Mazuku

[Image: Depressions in the volcanic fields north of Lake Kivu where mazuku collect]
The volcanic region near Goma, Democratic Republic of the Congo

Mazuku (Swahili for "evil wind") are pockets of carbon-dioxide-rich air that accumulate in depressions and low-lying ground in volcanically active regions, most famously around Lake Kivu and the city of Goma in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, near the Nyiragongo and Nyamuragira volcanoes.

Carbon dioxide (CO2) seeps from magma and volcanic fractures in the ground. Because CO2 is denser than air, on calm days it pools invisibly in hollows, gullies and pits, displacing oxygen. People and animals entering a mazuku can be asphyxiated without warning, since the gas is colourless and odourless; victims simply lose consciousness and suffocate. Mazuku are locally feared and are blamed for periodic deaths of livestock and, occasionally, of people — especially children, who are closer to the ground where concentrations are highest.

Hazard and mitigation

The phenomenon is related to, but distinct from, the catastrophic limnic eruptions at Lake Monoun (1984) and Lake Nyos (1986) in Cameroon, in which dissolved CO2 burst from lake water and killed more than 1,700 people. Around Goma, where mazuku occur within a densely populated city rebuilt on young lava flows after Nyiragongo's 2002 eruption, volcanologists of the Goma Volcano Observatory map the dangerous depressions, mark them with warning signs, and educate residents. Proposed mitigation includes venting wells and avoiding construction in the mapped hollows. Children have been taught simple tests for the gas, such as observing whether a candle or match held low to the ground goes out.
