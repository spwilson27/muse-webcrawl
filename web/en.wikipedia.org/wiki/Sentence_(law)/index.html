Sentence (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, a sentence is the punishment for a crime ordered by a trial court after a defendant is convicted, following a guilty plea or a verdict at trial. Sentences may include imprisonment, probation, fines, community service, restitution, or, in a shrinking number of jurisdictions, death. In most systems the sentence is imposed at a dedicated sentencing hearing, at which the court considers aggravating and mitigating factors, victim impact statements, and pre-sentence reports.

Types

Custodial sentences may be determinate (a fixed term), indeterminate (a range, with release determined by a parole board), or life sentences, with or without the possibility of parole. Multiple sentences for separate offences may run concurrently (at the same time) or consecutively (one after another). Courts may suspend part or all of a sentence, and mandatory minimum sentences remove judicial discretion for specified offences, a practice much debated for its effects on proportionality and prison populations.

Purposes

Sentencing theory identifies several purposes: retribution (deserved punishment), deterrence of the offender and others, incapacitation of dangerous offenders, rehabilitation, and restoration to victims and communities. Sentencing guidelines — such as the US federal guidelines and the Sentencing Council guidelines for England and Wales — aim to promote consistency while preserving some individualised discretion. Appeals against sentence are common, and sentences found to be unduly lenient may in some systems be referred upward by prosecutors.
