Trauma (medicine)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In medicine, trauma is any injury caused by an external force — physical, chemical, or thermal — ranging from minor wounds to life-threatening multisystem injuries. Major trauma is injury that has the potential to cause prolonged disability or death. Worldwide, trauma is a leading cause of death in people under about 45 years of age; road traffic collisions, falls, and interpersonal violence are the dominant mechanisms.

Classification

Injuries are broadly classified by mechanism as blunt (for example, from vehicle collisions and falls) or penetrating (gunshot and stab wounds), and also burns, blast injury, and environmental exposures such as hypothermia. Severity is graded with scoring systems such as the Abbreviated Injury Scale and the composite Injury Severity Score. The pattern of deaths after major trauma is often described as trimodal: immediate deaths from catastrophic brain or vascular injury; early deaths, within the "golden hour", from haemorrhage and airway problems that rapid treatment can prevent; and late deaths from sepsis and multiple organ failure.

Assessment and management

Modern trauma care follows structured protocols such as Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS). The primary survey addresses, in order: Airway with cervical spine protection, Breathing and ventilation, Circulation with haemorrhage control, Disability (neurological status), and Exposure and environmental control. Sometimes summarised as treating the greatest threat to life first, the approach uses techniques such as tourniquets and direct pressure, pelvic binders, chest drains, tranexamic acid, balanced blood-product transfusion, and permissive hypotension before definitive surgical control — the strategy of "damage control resuscitation" and "damage control surgery". Imaging centres on focused ultrasound (FAST) and whole-body CT in stable patients. Care is delivered by trauma teams in designated trauma centres, with triage systems designed to get the most seriously injured to the right facility fastest.

Complications

Major trauma can produce the "lethal triad" of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy, and later complications including infection, venous thromboembolism, acute respiratory distress syndrome, compartment syndrome, and multiple organ dysfunction. Psychological sequelae, including post-traumatic stress disorder, are common among survivors.

Prevention

Trauma is also a public-health field: seat-belt and helmet laws, drink-driving enforcement, speed management, fall-prevention programmes, violence-interruption schemes, and safe product design have all reduced injury rates where applied. Epidemiologist William Haddon's matrix remains a standard framework for analysing injury countermeasures before, during, and after the injurious event.
