Tone sandhi

Tone sandhi
FieldPhonology
DefinitionChange in the pronunciation of a tone caused by neighbouring tones
Well-known examplesMandarin Chinese, Taiwanese Hokkien, Wu Chinese

Tone sandhi is a sound change in tonal languages, in which the tones assigned to individual words or morphemes change based on the pronunciation of the words that surround them (in a similar way to external sandhi). It is usually encountered in languages of East and Southeast Asia (especially Chinese languages), sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of the Americas. When the changed tone is still recognizable as a variant of the underlying tone, linguists distinguish it from the "citation tone" a syllable carries in isolation.

Mandarin Chinese

The best-known example is the third-tone sandhi of Standard Mandarin: a third tone (dipping, 214) followed by another third tone is pronounced as a second tone (rising, 35). Thus nǐ hǎo ("hello", underlyingly 3+3) is pronounced ní hǎo (2+3). Other Mandarin tone changes are lexicalized: the words bù ("not", fourth tone) becomes second tone bú before another fourth tone, and yī ("one", first tone) changes to yí or yì depending on the following tone.

Other languages

Taiwanese Hokkien (Southern Min) has an extensive system in which nearly every tone changes to a different tone in non-final position within a phrase — a "tone circle" of substitutions. Shanghainese and other Wu varieties exhibit complex left-dominant sandhi, where the tone of the first syllable spreads across the whole polysyllabic word. Tone sandhi is also important in Thai, Vietnamese, and many Otomanguean languages of Mexico, such as Mixtec, where sandhi behaviours can be intricate and morphologically conditioned.

Theoretical issues

Tone sandhi poses classic problems for phonological theory: whether changes apply cyclically or in phrases, whether resulting tones are "neutralized" or distinct, and how sandhi interacts with syntax and the lexicon. It figured prominently in debates leading to autosegmental phonology, which represents tones on their own tier, allowing them to spread, delink, and reassociate.
