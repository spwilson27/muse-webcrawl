Tile

Labelled placeholder: decorative glazed ceramic tiles.

Tiles are usually thin, square or rectangular coverings manufactured from hard-wearing material such as ceramic, stone, metal, baked clay, or even glass. They are generally fixed in place in an array to cover roofs, floors, walls, edges, or other objects such as tabletops. Alternatively, tile can sometimes refer to similar units made from lightweight materials such as perlite, wood, and mineral wool, typically used for wall and ceiling applications.

Materials and manufacture

Ceramic tiles are made from clay or other inorganic raw materials, shaped while soft, then dried and fired in a kiln at high temperature. They may be glazed, with a vitrified liquid layer fused to the surface, or unglazed, as in terracotta. Porcelain tiles are a denser, finer-grained form of ceramic fired at higher temperatures. Natural stone tiles are cut from granite, marble, slate, travertine and other stones.

History

Decorative tilework has a long history: glazed brick reliefs adorned Mesopotamian temples, the Ishtar Gate of Babylon being the most famous example; fired tiles were produced in the Indus Valley and in ancient Egypt. Islamic architecture developed tile mosaic (zellij) and underglaze-painted tiles (such as İznik ware) into major art forms, and painted tin-glazed tiles — Delftware in the Netherlands, azulejos in Portugal and Spain — spread through early modern Europe.

Uses

  • Flooring: durable, water-resistant surfaces for kitchens and bathrooms.
  • Roof tiles: clay or concrete shingles overlapping to shed rain.
  • Wall tiles: splashbacks, showers, and decorative façades.
  • Specialised uses: heat-shield tiles on spacecraft, and acoustic ceiling tiles.
