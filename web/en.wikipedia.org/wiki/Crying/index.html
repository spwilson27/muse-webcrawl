Crying

Crying is the production and shedding of tears in response to an emotional state or physical pain. Emotional crying — sobbing accompanied by lacrimation — is considered a distinctly human behavior, though other animals produce tears for lubrication and vocal distress calls. Crying typically involves the lacrimal glands, controlled by the parasympathetic nervous system via the facial nerve, together with characteristic facial expressions, altered breathing, and vocalizations.

Types of tears

Three kinds of tears are distinguished: basal tears, secreted continuously to lubricate and protect the cornea; reflex tears, produced in response to irritants such as onion vapors, smoke, or bright light; and emotional (psychic) tears, triggered by feelings including sadness, grief, joy, frustration, and relief. Emotional tears have a somewhat different chemical composition, containing higher levels of protein-based hormones such as prolactin and adrenocorticotropic hormone and the natural analgesic leucine enkephalin — a fact sometimes cited for the commonly reported feeling of relief or calm after a good cry, although the evidence for a cathartic effect is mixed.

Development and social aspects

Infants cry as a primary means of communication; human babies are born able to produce tears visibly within the first weeks of life. Adults in all cultures cry, with large differences in frequency by sex emerging around puberty — attributed to both hormonal factors (prolactin may promote tearing, testosterone inhibit it) and social norms discouraging male crying in many societies. Crying serves social functions: it signals distress and helplessness, elicits empathy and comfort, and can strengthen social bonds. Charles Darwin discussed crying in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872), dismissing any purpose for tears themselves; modern researchers such as Ad Vingerhoets have treated crying as an attachment behavior and a form of nonverbal communication.
