Angiogenesis

FieldDevelopmental biology, oncology
Key factorsVEGF, FGF, angiopoietins, HIF-1α
Related processVasculogenesis, lymphangiogenesis

Angiogenesis is the physiological process through which new blood vessels form from pre-existing vessels, distinct from vasculogenesis, in which vessels arise de novo from endothelial progenitor cells during embryogenesis. Angiogenesis continues the growth of the vasculature mainly by sprouting and intussusceptive (splitting) processes.

Mechanism

In sprouting angiogenesis, low oxygen levels stabilise hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), which drives expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Endothelial "tip cells" degrade the basement membrane and lead a sprout toward the signal, followed by proliferating "stalk cells"; the sprout connects to another vessel, pericytes are recruited, and flow begins. The balance between pro-angiogenic factors and inhibitors such as angiostatin and endostatin keeps adult angiogenesis largely quiescent except in wound healing and the female reproductive cycle.

Medical significance

Excessive angiogenesis contributes to cancer, diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis, while insufficient angiogenesis impairs wound healing and recovery from ischemia. Judah Folkman's hypothesis that tumours depend on angiogenesis led to anti-angiogenic cancer therapies, such as the anti-VEGF antibody bevacizumab, and to anti-VEGF eye treatments including ranibizumab and aflibercept. Conversely, therapeutic angiogenesis has been explored for ischemic heart and limb disease.
