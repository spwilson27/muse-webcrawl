Monarchianism

Monarchianism is a doctrine in early Christian theology that emphasised the unity (monarchia) of God the Father, in reaction against views that seemed to divide the Godhead or to set up a second God alongside the Father. Monarchian teachings flourished in the second and third centuries and were eventually condemned as heretical by the wider Church, but they played a significant role in the development of the doctrine of the Trinity.

Forms

Historians generally distinguish two types of Monarchianism:

  • Dynamic (or Adoptionist) Monarchianism held that Jesus was a mere man who was adopted by God and filled with the divine power (dynamis) at his baptism. Its representatives included Theodotus of Byzantium and, in the third century, Paul of Samosata.
  • Modalistic Monarchianism (also called Modalism or, in its later form, Sabellianism) held that the Father, Son and Holy Spirit are not distinct persons but modes or manifestations of the one God. Noetus of Smyrna, Praxeas and Sabellius were its chief exponents. Because it implied that the Father himself suffered on the cross, its opponents called it Patripassianism.

History

Monarchian ideas arose as an attempt to safeguard Jewish-Christian monotheism against the Logos theology of the apologists, which spoke of the Son as a distinct divine being. Tertullian wrote extensively against Praxeas in his treatise Adversus Praxean, and the controversy helped to fix the vocabulary of "one substance, three persons" in Western theology. Modalism was condemned by Pope Callixtus I's opponents and by later councils, yet modalistic language repeatedly resurfaced in later centuries.

Legacy

Although rejected by the developing orthodox tradition, Monarchianism forced theologians such as Tertullian and Hippolytus to clarify how unity and distinction could be affirmed together, and thus contributed substantially to the eventual Trinitarian settlement of the fourth century. Modern Oneness Pentecostalism revives a form of modalistic teaching.
