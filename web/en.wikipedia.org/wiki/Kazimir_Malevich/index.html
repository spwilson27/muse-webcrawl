Kazimir Severinovich Malevich (23 February 1879 – 15 May 1935) was a Russian avant-garde artist and art theorist, born in Kiev to a Polish family. He founded Suprematism, one of the earliest movements of pure abstract art, and his painting Black Square (1915) is among the most famous works of modern art.

Early career

Malevich moved to Moscow in 1904 and passed through phases of Impressionism, Symbolism, Fauvism and Cubo-Futurism, exhibiting with the Jack of Diamonds group. In 1913 he designed the sets and costumes for the Futurist opera Victory over the Sun, in which an early version of the black square motif appeared as a stage backdrop.

Suprematism

At the "Last Futurist Exhibition of Paintings 0.10" in Petrograd in December 1915, Malevich hung Black Square across the corner of the room — the position traditionally reserved for religious icons — alongside some 38 other Suprematist works built from geometric forms floating on white grounds. His manifesto From Cubism to Suprematism declared the supremacy of pure feeling over depiction. The movement peaked with White on White (1918). After the Revolution, Malevich taught in Vitebsk, where he founded the UNOVIS group and clashed with Marc Chagall, and later directed the State Institute of Artistic Culture in Petrograd.

Later years and legacy

Under Stalin, abstract art fell from favour and Malevich was marginalised; a 1927 trip to Berlin and the Bauhaus was his only journey abroad. He returned partly to figurative painting before dying of cancer in 1935, and was buried in a coffin he designed, decorated with a black square. Long suppressed in the USSR, his work was rediscovered internationally and is now recognised as foundational for abstract and minimalist art.
