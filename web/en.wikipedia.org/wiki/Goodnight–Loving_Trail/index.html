Goodnight–Loving Trail

Established1866
FoundersCharles Goodnight and Oliver Loving
RouteYoung County, Texas → Fort Sumner, New Mexico → Colorado
UseCattle drives, 1866–1880s

The Goodnight–Loving Trail was a cattle trail in the southwestern United States blazed in 1866 by ranchers Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving. It ran from Young County in north-central Texas westward across the Llano Estacado and along the Pecos River to Fort Sumner in New Mexico, where the partners sold cattle to provision the Navajo and Apache people held at the Bosque Redondo reservation and the army garrisons nearby. In later years the trail was extended northward into Colorado and eventually toward Wyoming.

History

The first drive, in the summer of 1866, pushed some 2,000 head across a waterless stretch of about ninety miles; the cattle, mad with thirst, stampeded toward the Pecos, and part of the herd was lost, but the surviving animals sold at a handsome profit. In 1867, on a drive up the Pecos, Loving was wounded by Comanche raiders while scouting ahead with "One Arm" Bill Wilson; he reached Fort Sumner but died of gangrene, having asked that his body be returned to Texas for burial — a journey Goodnight duly made with the coffin the following winter, an episode immortalised in Larry McMurtry's novel Lonesome Dove, whose characters Gus McCrae and Woodrow Call are modelled on Loving and Goodnight.

Legacy

The trail opened the Pecos valley and eastern New Mexico to the cattle industry, and its northern extension helped stock the ranges of Colorado and Wyoming. Goodnight went on to found the JA Ranch in the Texas Panhandle and to pioneer the chuckwagon, while the trail itself remains one of the best-known routes of the great post–Civil War cattle drives, used until barbed wire and railroads closed the open range in the 1880s.
