Standard form contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A standard form contract (sometimes referred to as a contract of adhesion or a boilerplate contract) is a contract between two parties, where the terms and conditions of the contract are set by one of the parties, and the other party has little or no ability to negotiate more favourable terms and is thus placed in a "take it or leave it" position.

While these types of contracts are not inherently illegal, there does exist a potential for unconscionability. In addition, in several jurisdictions, contract law places limits on enforceable terms, particularly where a contract is between a business and a consumer and where the contract is for a basic service.

Historical development and usage

Standard form contracts became prevalent in the late 19th and early 20th centuries with the growth of mass production, insurance, banking and transport, where businesses transacted with large numbers of customers on identical terms. They reduce transaction costs by eliminating individual negotiation, but they shift drafting power to the party that prepares the form.

Adhesion contracts

The term "contract of adhesion" was introduced by Edwin Patterson in 1919 and popularised in Anglo-American law by Friedrich Kessler in 1943. An adhesion contract is a standard form contract prepared by one party and offered to the weaker party, who has no realistic choice but to accept it. Courts may scrutinise such contracts for procedural and substantive unconscionability.

Legal treatment

  • United States: Section 211 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts governs standardised agreements; terms that the drafter has reason to believe the other party would not have assented to may be excluded. The Uniform Commercial Code also empowers courts to refuse enforcement of unconscionable terms.
  • European Union: The Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Directive (93/13/EEC) renders unfair terms in consumer contracts non-binding.
  • United Kingdom: The Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 restrict exclusion clauses and unfair terms.
  • Australia: The Australian Consumer Law prohibits unfair terms in standard form consumer and small business contracts.

The rule of contra proferentem

Under the interpretive rule of contra proferentem, ambiguities in a standard form contract are construed against the party that drafted it. The rule is applied widely in insurance law, where policy documents are drafted by insurers.

Criticism

Critics note that standard form contracts in consumer contexts—such as software end-user licence agreements, mobile phone plans and gym memberships—are rarely read, and that the fiction of assent strains classical contract theory. Defenders respond that standardisation lowers prices and that regulatory oversight addresses abuse.
