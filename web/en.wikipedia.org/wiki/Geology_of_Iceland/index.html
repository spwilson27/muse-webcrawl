Geology of Iceland

Iceland is a geologically young island formed by volcanism where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — the divergent boundary between the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates — rises above sea level, coinciding with a mantle hotspot. The island began forming about 16–18 million years ago and is still growing as the plates separate by roughly 2 centimeters per year.

Formation

Basaltic lava eruptions along the rift zones built the island's foundation; the oldest rocks, in the Westfjords and East Iceland, are about 16 million years old, while the active volcanic zones crossing the country from southwest to northeast contain rocks younger than about 0.7 million years. The hotspot, often called the Iceland plume, supplies extra magma that sustains unusually productive volcanism for a mid-ocean ridge.

Volcanism

Iceland has around 30 active volcanic systems. Famous eruptions include Laki (1783–84), whose gases caused a global climatic event and famine; Eldfell on Heimaey (1973); Eyjafjallajökull (2010), whose ash cloud disrupted European air travel; and the Fagradalsfjall and Sundhnúkur eruptions on the Reykjanes Peninsula in the 2020s. Eruptions beneath glaciers (jökulhlaups) can trigger catastrophic glacial outburst floods.

Glaciers and landscape

About a tenth of Iceland is covered by glaciers, including Vatnajökull, Europe's largest by volume. Glacial erosion, rivers, and volcanic deposition continually reshape the landscape of lava fields, black sand plains, fjords, and geothermal areas, whose abundant hot water and steam Iceland exploits for heating and electricity.
