The economy of Georgia is an emerging free-market economy. After the collapse of the Soviet Union, Georgia suffered a severe economic contraction, but reforms after the 2003 Rose Revolution — deregulation, anti-corruption measures, and simplified taxes — produced rapid growth, averaging around 5–6% per year in many years, and a markedly improved business climate.

History

Under the Soviet Union, Georgia had a relatively high standard of living, supplying wine, citrus, tea, and tourism to the rest of the USSR. Independence in 1991 brought civil war, loss of markets, and hyperinflation; GDP collapsed by more than half. Stabilisation came in the mid-1990s with the introduction of the lari, followed by construction of the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan oil pipeline and the South Caucasus gas pipeline, which made Georgia a key energy transit corridor.

Sectors

Services dominate the economy, led by trade, transport, real estate, and a growing tourism industry. Agriculture still employs a large share of the workforce — at times around two-fifths — while producing only a modest share of GDP; wine production, with an 8,000-year tradition, is a signature export. Hydropower supplies most electricity. Money transfers from Georgians working abroad, especially in Russia and the EU, remain an important source of foreign exchange, and growth slowed sharply in 2009 (global financial crisis and the 2008 war with Russia) and 2020 (COVID-19) before rebounding.
