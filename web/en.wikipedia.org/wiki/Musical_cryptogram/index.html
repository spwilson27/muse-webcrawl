Musical cryptogram

A musical cryptogram is a cryptogrammatic sequence of musical symbols—usually pitches—which can be taken to refer to an extra-musical text by some logical relationship, usually the names of persons or words encoded as notes. Composers have used cryptograms to sign works, pay homage, or embed messages.

Famous examples

  • B-A-C-H motif: In German notation, B♭–A–C–B♮ spells "Bach". J. S. Bach used it himself, and many composers (Schumann, Liszt, Rimsky-Korsakov, Busoni) built works on it.
  • DSCH motif: Dmitri Shostakovich's musical signature D–E♭–C–B, from his initials in German transliteration, appears in his Eighth String Quartet and Tenth Symphony.
  • A–E♭–C–B motif (Schumann): Schumann used "SCHA" and the town name Asch in Carnaval.
  • F-A-E Sonata: A collective work by Dietrich, Schumann, and Brahms for Joseph Joachim, on his motto Frei aber einsam ("free but lonely").
  • Edward Elgar: proposed pitch ciphers underlie the "Enigma" Variations.
  • Alban Berg and Arnold Schoenberg: letter-derived note rows appear in works such as Berg's Lyric Suite and Schoenberg's name motif E♭–C–B–B♭–E–G ("S-C-H-B-E-G").

Systems

The most common scheme derives from the German note names, in which H is B natural and B is B flat, and S denotes E flat. French and other systems extend the alphabet by cycling letters over the seven note names, a method used by Maurice Ravel (for a Haydn homage) and by Olivier Messiaen, who devised his own "alphabet" in later works.
