A computer reservation system (CRS) is a computerised system used to store and retrieve information and conduct transactions related to travel. Originally built by airlines to manage their own seat inventory and bookings, reservation systems grew into global distribution systems (GDS) that sell the inventory of many airlines, hotels, railways and car-rental companies to travel agents and consumers worldwide.

History

Before computers, airlines tracked seats with manual boards and card indexes. American Airlines, working with IBM, developed the Sabre system, which became fully operational in 1964 and is often cited as a pioneering large-scale real-time transaction-processing system. United Airlines followed with Apollo, and European carriers combined to create Amadeus (1987) and Galileo. In the regulated era the systems were accused of display bias — favouring their owner airlines' flights on agents' screens — which led to government rules in the United States and Europe.

Functions

A CRS maintains schedules, seat availability, fare rules and passenger records. Each booking is stored as a Passenger Name Record (PNR) identified by a record locator. Beyond simple booking, modern systems support yield management (adjusting prices by predicted demand), e-ticketing, check-in and departure control, and connections to hotel, rail and ancillary content. The principal GDS companies — Amadeus, Sabre and Travelport — charge suppliers booking fees and provide the backbone for most travel-agency and many online sales.

Changes since the 2000s

Low-cost carriers often bypass the GDS in favour of direct web sales, and airlines have pushed the IATA New Distribution Capability (NDC) standard to sell richer offers through their own channels. Nevertheless, reservation systems remain the central infrastructure of the travel industry, processing billions of transactions annually.
