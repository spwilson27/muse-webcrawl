Broiler

A broiler is any chicken raised specifically for meat production. Modern broilers are fast-growing hybrid breeds, selected over decades so that they reach slaughter weight of around 2–3 kg in as little as five to seven weeks. Broiler meat accounts for the great majority of chicken consumed worldwide.

Broilers are typically reared indoors in large flocks.

Breeding

Commercial broilers descend mainly from crosses developed from the Cornish and White Plymouth Rock breeds. Two breeding companies — Aviagen (Ross and Cobb brands) and Hubbard — supply most of the world's broiler genetics. Selective breeding has dramatically increased growth rate and breast-meat yield: a broiler of the 2000s reaches in about 45 days the weight a bird of the 1950s reached in over 110 days.

Production

Broilers are usually kept in climate-controlled sheds at high stocking densities, fed energy-rich cereal and soybean diets, and processed automatically at slaughter. Over 70 billion broilers are produced globally each year; Brazil, the United States and China are the largest producers.

Welfare concerns

Rapid growth has been linked to leg disorders, heart failure (ascites) and sudden death syndrome, and stocking density and lameness have made broiler welfare a major animal-welfare issue. In response, slower-growing breeds, lower densities and standards such as the Better Chicken Commitment have gained ground in parts of Europe and North America.
