Gibberellin

Class: Diterpenoid plant hormones
First isolated: 1930s, from Gibberella fujikuroi
Known forms: Over 130 (GA1–GAn)
Bioactive forms: Chiefly GA1, GA3, GA4, GA7

Gibberellins (GAs) are a large family of plant hormones — tetracyclic diterpenoid acids — that regulate many aspects of plant growth and development, including seed germination, stem elongation, leaf expansion, trichome development, pollen maturation, and the induction of flowering. More than 130 gibberellins have been identified in plants, fungi, and bacteria, though only a few are biologically active in their own right; the rest are precursors or deactivated forms.

Discovery

Gibberellins were first recognised by Japanese scientists studying bakanae ("foolish seedling") disease of rice, in which infected plants grew spindly and pale and often died. In 1926 Eiichi Kurosawa showed the effect was caused by a substance secreted by the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi; the active compounds were isolated in the 1930s and named gibberellins, and British and American teams characterised gibberellic acid in the 1950s.

Signalling

Gibberellin is perceived by the soluble receptor GID1. The GA–GID1 complex binds DELLA proteins — nuclear repressors of growth — and targets them for destruction by the ubiquitin–proteasome system, releasing the plant's growth programmes from repression. The Green Revolution semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties that transformed 20th-century agriculture work precisely through this pathway: wheat's Rht genes encode altered DELLA proteins, and rice's sd1 gene encodes a defective gibberellin biosynthesis enzyme.

Applications

Commercially, gibberellic acid is sprayed on seedless table grapes to enlarge berries and loosen clusters, used to promote uniform seed germination and malting in brewing, and applied to delay rind ageing in citrus. Conversely, gibberellin-biosynthesis inhibitors are used as growth retardants in cereals and ornamental horticulture.
