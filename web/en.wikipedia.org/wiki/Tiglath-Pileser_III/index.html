Tiglath-Pileser III (Akkadian: Tukultī-apil-Ešarra, "my trust is in the heir of Esharra"), also called Tilgath-Pilneser in the Hebrew Bible, was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 745 BC until his death in 727 BC. He is widely regarded as the founder of the Neo-Assyrian Empire in its mature, expansionist form, having carried out sweeping military, administrative, and provincial reforms that transformed Assyria into the dominant power of the Near East.

Accession and reforms

Tiglath-Pileser came to the throne in 745 BC, probably through a revolt against his predecessor Ashur-nirari V. He reorganized the empire by reducing the size of provinces and placing them under royal appointees, diminishing the power of the great magnates. He made the army a standing professional force, incorporated conquered peoples' contingents, and systematized the policy of mass deportation — relocating rebellious populations across the empire to break local resistance and supply labor. He also formalized a dual system of rule in which conquered territories were either annexed as provinces under Assyrian governors or left as tribute-paying vassal states.

Campaigns

Tiglath-Pileser's campaigns extended Assyrian control in every direction. In the north he defeated Sarduri II of Urartu; in the west he subdued the Aramean and Neo-Hittite states of Syria, defeated a coalition including Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel, annexed Damascus in 732 BC, and reduced Israel, deporting much of the population of Galilee and Gilead. Judah under Ahaz became an Assyrian tributary. In the east he campaigned against the Medes, and in 729 BC he took Babylon, ruling it under the name Pulu, thereby uniting the crowns of Assyria and Babylonia.

Legacy

The administrative and military system Tiglath-Pileser created underpinned Assyrian expansion for the following century under Sargon II, Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon. His reign is documented by royal annals, summary inscriptions, and reliefs from his palace at Kalhu (Nimrud), many of which are now in the British Museum. He is mentioned in the biblical Books of Kings and Chronicles in connection with the Syro-Ephraimite War and the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel.
