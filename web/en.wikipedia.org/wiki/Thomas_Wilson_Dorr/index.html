Thomas Wilson Dorr (5 November 1805 – 27 December 1854) was an American lawyer and politician who led the Dorr Rebellion (1841–1842) in the U.S. state of Rhode Island, an attempt to extend the voting franchise beyond landowners and rewrite the state's governing charter. For a brief period in 1842, Rhode Island had two rival governments — one led by Dorr, elected under a "People's Constitution", and the other by the incumbent Governor Samuel Ward King under the existing colonial charter.

Background

Born into a wealthy Providence family, Dorr graduated from Harvard in 1823 and was admitted to the bar in 1827. In the 1830s he became active in politics and reform causes, including abolitionism and school reform. Rhode Island still governed itself under the royal charter of 1663, which limited suffrage to men who owned land worth at least $134, along with their eldest sons — a rule that excluded a large and growing majority of adult white men, especially mill workers in the growing industrial towns.

The Dorr Rebellion

In 1841 the Rhode Island Suffrage Association, with Dorr as a leading figure, held an extralegal "People's Convention" and drafted a new constitution extending the vote to all adult white men. The constitution was ratified in a popular referendum, and in April 1842 Dorr's supporters held elections under it; Dorr was inaugurated as governor on 3 May 1842. The charter government refused to yield, declared the Dorrite proceedings treasonable, and called out the militia. In May 1842 Dorr's attempt to seize the state arsenal in Providence failed when his cannon misfired and his followers dispersed, and a second rising at Chepachet in June collapsed without a fight. Dorr fled the state.

Trial and later life

Dorr returned to Rhode Island in October 1843 and was arrested. In 1844 he was tried for treason against the state, convicted, and sentenced to life imprisonment at hard labour. His harsh sentence provoked widespread sympathy; he was released in 1845 after a general amnesty, his civil rights were restored in 1851, and in 1854 the verdict against him was annulled. He died later that year, his health broken by imprisonment.

Legacy

Although the rebellion was defeated, it forced Rhode Island to adopt a new constitution in 1843 that substantially widened the franchise (while imposing new restrictions on naturalised citizens). Dorr is now generally regarded as a principled democrat, and the episode remains a landmark in the history of American suffrage reform.
