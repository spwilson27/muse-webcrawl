Kavanah

Kavanah or kavvanah (Hebrew: כַּוָּנָה, "intention" or "direction [of the heart]") is a concept in Judaism referring to the sincere, focused intention and devotion with which a person performs a religious act, particularly prayer and the commandments (mitzvot). Derived from the root k-v-n, "to direct", it denotes directing one's heart toward God while acting or speaking.

In rabbinic tradition

The rabbis of the Mishnah and Talmud debated whether commandments require intention to be valid — mitzvot tzerikhot kavanah — a question discussed especially in connection with hearing the shofar and reading the Shema. For prayer, the sages were firm: "One who makes his prayer a fixed task finds his prayer is not supplication" (Mishnah Berakhot 4:4), and the Talmud insists a person should direct the heart in the first blessing of the Amidah at minimum. Maimonides codified that prayer without kavanah is not prayer, and described freeing the mind of distractions and regarding oneself as standing before the Shekhinah.

Development

Medieval philosophers treated kavanah as intellectual contemplation of God, while the kabbalists developed elaborate meditative intentions (kavvanot) on the letters and combinations of the divine names, systematized by Isaac Luria; prayer with the correct kavvanot was believed to effect the repair (tikkun) of the cosmos. Hasidism democratized intense devotion, valuing heartfelt, joyful prayer and devekut (cleaving to God) over esoteric technique, though the tension between spontaneous fervour and halakhic requirement remained a live theme.

Modern discussion

In contemporary Judaism, kavanah remains a central problem of religious life in an age of distraction. Thinkers such as Abraham Joshua Heschel framed it as the difference between reciting words and praying: ritual behaviour without inner direction risks becoming rote, the very thing the rabbinic strictures against "fixed" prayer warned against. Teachers across denominations use the term broadly for mindful intentionality in any sacred act, from candle-lighting to acts of charity.
