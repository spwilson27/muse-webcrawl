Monorail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A monorail train straddling a concrete beam above a city street]
A straddle-beam monorail in a city.

A monorail is a railway in which the track consists of a single rail or beam, typically elevated, with vehicles either straddling the beam or suspended beneath it. Monorails serve airports, amusement parks and some urban transit roles — notably in Japan, where the Tokyo Monorail (1964) and the extensive Osaka Monorail carry everyday passengers. Despite futuristic promise, cost and switching complexity have kept them a niche solution.

Types

Most modern systems are straddle-beam types (ALWEG design), riding on rubber tyres over a concrete beam, as in Seattle (1962) and Kuala Lumpur. Suspended monorails, such as Germany\'s Wuppertal Schwebebahn (1901), hang below the rail and remain rare curiosities that nonetheless carry heavy daily use.

History

Early monorails date to the 1820s; the concept boomed after the Second World War, promoted by Walt Disney\'s Disneyland monorail (1959) as the transport of the future. Urban adoption was strongest in Japan, where the Hitachi ALWEG design became a standard for medium-capacity transit.

Advantages and limits

Elevated beams need little land, are quiet, and rubber tyres climb steep grades; but proprietary designs, complex beam switches and capacity limits versus conventional metro have restricted wider use. Mumbai and São Paulo opened large straddle monorails in the 2010s with mixed results.
