VHF omnidirectional range

VHF omnidirectional range, commonly abbreviated VOR, is a type of short-range radio navigation system for aircraft, enabling a receiver in the aircraft to determine its bearing (azimuth) from a ground station. VOR stations broadcast a VHF radio composite signal in the 108.00 to 117.95 MHz band that encodes the bearing information; the airborne receiver interprets it to give the pilot the magnetic bearing from the station, known as a radial. Developed in the United States in the 1940s, VOR became the standard air navigation system worldwide for decades.

Principle of operation

A VOR station transmits two 30 Hz signals: a reference phase signal, frequency-modulated onto a subcarrier and radiated omnidirectionally, and a variable phase signal, amplitude-modulated and radiated from a rotating or electronically phased antenna pattern. The phase difference between the two signals at the receiver equals the aircraft's azimuth from the station relative to magnetic north. The station also transmits its identifier in Morse code, and many VORs are co-located with distance measuring equipment (DME) or military TACAN, forming VORTAC stations.

Use in navigation

By selecting a course on the cockpit course deviation indicator, a pilot can fly toward or away from a station along a chosen radial and track airways defined by intersecting radials from successive stations. VOR accuracy is typically within a few degrees. Although satellite navigation (GNSS/GPS) has displaced VOR as the primary means of navigation in many regions, VOR networks are retained as a backup, and aviation authorities maintain minimum operational networks of stations.

Types

  • Conventional VOR (CVOR) — the original design using a rotating antenna pattern.
  • Doppler VOR (DVOR) — uses a large circular antenna array and Doppler principles to reduce errors caused by reflections from terrain and buildings.
  • VOR/DME and VORTAC — stations combined with distance and military bearing/distance equipment.
