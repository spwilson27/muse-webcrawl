Striga, commonly known as witchweed, is a genus of parasitic plants that occur naturally in parts of Africa, Asia, and Australia. The brightly coloured flowers grow in the family Orobanchaceae. While most species are hemiparasites (photosynthetic parasites) of grass roots, several are devastating pests of cereal crops.

Description

Striga species are annual herbs with bright green stems and leaves and small, showy flowers in shades of red, pink, white, or yellow. Their tiny seeds — a single plant can produce tens of thousands — remain viable in soil for many years. Germination is triggered by strigolactones, chemical signals exuded by host roots; the seedling then forms a haustorium that penetrates the host root and draws water and nutrients.

Agricultural impact

The most damaging species, Striga hermonthica and Striga asiatica, attack maize, sorghum, pearl millet, rice, and sugarcane, while Striga gesnerioides parasitises legumes such as cowpea. Infestations affect tens of millions of hectares in sub-Saharan Africa and can cause yield losses of 30–100%, threatening the livelihoods of over 100 million people. Damage is largely done underground before the parasite emerges, making control difficult.

Control

Management strategies include resistant crop varieties, "push–pull" intercropping with Desmodium (which suppresses Striga germination), herbicide-coated seed, soil fertility improvement, crop rotation, and hand weeding before seed set. Striga asiatica was introduced into the United States (the Carolinas) in the 1950s and was the target of a long federal eradication programme.
