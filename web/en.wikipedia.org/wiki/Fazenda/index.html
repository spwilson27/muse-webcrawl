Fazenda

A fazenda (Portuguese pronunciation: [faˈzẽdɐ]) is a large plantation or estate in Brazil, historically devoted to export crops such as sugarcane and coffee and worked first by enslaved Africans and later by immigrant and wage labour. The word derives from the Portuguese fazer, "to do" or "to make", and in modern usage denotes a large farm or ranch of any kind.

History

Fazendas originated in the colonial sugar economy of the Brazilian northeast, where the engenho (sugar mill) formed the centre of a self-sufficient estate dominated by the planter family. In the nineteenth century the coffee frontier in São Paulo and Minas Gerais made coffee fazendas the engine of the Brazilian economy; Brazil became the world's largest coffee producer, and the great fazendeiros formed a political oligarchy during the Old Republic's "coffee with milk" politics. Enslaved labour underpinned the system until abolition in 1888, after which European and Japanese immigrants were recruited as colonos.

Modern fazendas

Today the term covers large-scale farms and cattle ranches across Brazil, including the vast cattle and soy estates of the Cerrado and Mato Grosso. Historic fazendas with their colonial casa-grande (big house), chapels and slave quarters survive as heritage sites, particularly in the Paraíba Valley coffee region.
