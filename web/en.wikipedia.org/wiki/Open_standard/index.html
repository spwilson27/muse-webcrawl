Open standard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An open standard is a standard that is, or is intended to be, publicly available, has various rights to use associated with it, and may also have various properties of how it was designed (e.g. an open process). There is no single universally accepted definition; standards bodies, governments, and advocacy groups define "openness" somewhat differently, but common elements include public availability of the specification, a consensus-based development process open to interested parties, and implementation rights free of charge or on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms.

Definitions

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) defines open standards as standards made available to the general public that are developed (or approved) and maintained via a collaborative and consensus-driven process. The European Union's European Interoperability Framework requires that the standard be adopted and maintained by a not-for-profit organisation, published with its full specification available for free or at nominal charge, and with any patents made available royalty-free or irrevocably. Definitions by figures such as Bruce Perens and organisations such as the W3C (whose standards are royalty-free under its patent policy) emphasise that implementers must not be locked out by licence fees or restrictions.

Examples

Widely recognised open standards include the Internet protocol suite (TCP/IP), HTML and HTTP from the W3C and IETF, the USB specification, the PDF format (ISO 32000), and office document formats such as ODF (ISO 26300). Standardisation occurs through formal standards development organisations (ISO, IEC, ITU), industry consortia (W3C, OASIS, Khronos Group), and community processes (IETF RFCs).

Significance

Open standards are promoted as a means of ensuring interoperability, preventing vendor lock-in, lowering barriers to market entry, and preserving long-term access to digital information — reasons why many government policies mandate open standards for public-sector IT procurement.
