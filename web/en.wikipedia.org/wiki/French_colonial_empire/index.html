French colonial empire

At its peak in the 1920s–30s the empire covered some 11.5 million km².

The French colonial empire (French: Empire colonial français) comprised the overseas colonies, protectorates and mandate territories ruled by France from the 16th century onward. Beginning with settlements in the Americas, it developed in two broad phases: a first empire centred on Canada, Louisiana, the Caribbean sugar islands and trading posts in India, largely lost to Britain by 1763 and further reduced by 1803; and a second empire built from 1830 onward in Africa and Southeast Asia, which by the interwar period covered about 11.5 million square kilometres — roughly 8.6% of the world's land area — making it the second-largest empire in history after the British Empire.

First colonial empire

France founded Quebec in 1608 and built New France in North America, claimed Louisiana in 1682, and developed profitable plantation colonies in the Caribbean, above all Saint-Domingue (Haiti), whose sugar and coffee were produced by enslaved Africans. Defeat in the Seven Years' War forced France to cede Canada and its Indian territories to Britain in 1763. Louisiana was sold to the United States in 1803, and Saint-Domingue won independence as Haiti in 1804 after a slave revolution.

Second colonial empire

The conquest of Algiers in 1830 began a new expansion. Under the Third Republic, France seized or consolidated control of Algeria, Senegal, French West and Equatorial Africa, Madagascar, Indochina (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos), and Pacific islands, driven by rivalry with Britain and Germany, economic interests and the ideology of a mission civilisatrice. Administration combined direct rule, assimilationist rhetoric and, in practice, the discriminatory indigénat legal regime for colonial subjects.

Decolonisation

After the Second World War the empire was reorganised as the French Union (1946) and then the French Community (1958), but national liberation movements prevailed: France lost Indochina after defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, granted independence to most of sub-Saharan Africa in 1960, and ended the bitter Algerian War with the Évian Accords of 1962. France retains a number of overseas departments and territories, including French Guiana, Guadeloupe, Martinique, Réunion and Mayotte.
