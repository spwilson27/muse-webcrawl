The komusō (Japanese: 虚無僧, "priest of nothingness" or "emptiness monk") were mendicant monks of the Fuke school of Zen Buddhism in feudal Japan, prominent during the Edo period. They were instantly recognisable by the tengai, a deep woven basket hat that covered the entire head, symbolising the absence of ego, and by their playing of the shakuhachi bamboo flute.

Practice

Komusō practised suizen ("blowing Zen"), regarding the playing of solo flute pieces called honkyoku as a form of meditation and a path to enlightenment rather than as music. They travelled the country begging for alms, playing at the gates of houses. Their temples, such as Myōan-ji in Kyoto, preserved the honkyoku repertoire, which continues to be performed today.

History

The Fuke school traced its lineage to the Chinese Chan master Puhua (Fuke in Japanese). During the Edo period the komusō received official privileges from the Tokugawa shogunate, including the exclusive right to play the shakuhachi for religious purposes and freedom to travel. Because their faces were hidden, the order attracted masterless samurai (rōnin), and the shogunate was rumoured to use some komusō as spies. After the Meiji Restoration the Fuke school was abolished in 1871 and the monks' privileges were revoked, though the shakuhachi tradition survived as music.
