Social action

In sociology, social action is an act which takes account of the actions and reactions of other individuals. The concept was made central to the discipline by Max Weber, who defined sociology itself as the interpretive understanding of social action in order to arrive at a causal explanation of its course and effects.

Weber's typology

Weber distinguished four ideal types of social action: instrumentally rational action (Zweckrational), calculated to achieve a goal by the most effective means; value-rational action (Wertrational), pursued for its own sake out of ethical, religious or aesthetic conviction; affectual action, driven by emotion; and traditional action, guided by habit and custom. Real behaviour usually mixes these types.

Other theorists

Talcott Parsons built his "action theory" on Weber, classifying action within his AGIL schema, while Alfred Schütz examined the subjective meaning actors attach to conduct phenomenologically. Jürgen Habermas later contrasted instrumental and strategic action with communicative action oriented to mutual understanding. Rational choice theory in economics and political science models social action as utility-maximising choice under constraints.
