Industrial district

An industrial district is a place where workers and firms, specialised in a main industry and auxiliary industries, live and work. The concept was first developed by the economist Alfred Marshall, who described the concentration of specialised industries in particular localities in his Principles of Economics (1890).

Industrial districts cluster specialised small firms in one locality.

Marshallian districts

Marshall explained localisation by three advantages, later called Marshallian externalities: a pooled local market for workers with specialised skills; ready access to specialised suppliers and services; and knowledge spillovers, since ideas circulate easily when firms in the same trade are neighbours — in his phrase, the mysteries of the trade "are in the air".

Italian districts

The term gained new prominence in the 1970s and 1980s through studies of the "Third Italy" — regions such as Emilia-Romagna and Tuscany — where networks of small and medium-sized firms in textiles, footwear, ceramics and machinery prospered through flexible specialisation, combining competition with cooperation, shared institutions and deep roots in local society. The Italian economist Giacomo Becattini revived and developed the Marshallian concept for these cases.

Related concepts

Industrial districts are related to, but distinct from, business clusters (popularised by Michael Porter), industrial parks and science parks: the district emphasises the socio-economic fabric of a community of firms and people rather than a planned development or a purely agglomerative logic.
