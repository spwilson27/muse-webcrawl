I Walked with a Zombie is a 1943 American horror film directed by Jacques Tourneur and produced by Val Lewton for RKO Radio Pictures. Loosely inspired by Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre and by reports of voodoo practice in the Caribbean, it stars Frances Dee as a Canadian nurse who travels to the fictional West Indian island of Saint Sebastian to care for the catatonic wife of a sugar planter, and becomes entangled in the family's secrets and the island's voodoo religion. Despite its exploitative title — imposed by the studio from a magazine article — the film is regarded as one of the finest of Lewton's celebrated series of low-budget horror films.

Plot

Nurse Betsy Connell is hired to care for Jessica Holland, the nearly comatose wife of planter Paul Holland. On the island she falls in love with Paul, learns that Jessica's condition may be medical — or that she may be a zombie, emptied of will by voodoo — and witnesses the rhythms of the island's Afro-Caribbean culture, dominated by the legacy of slavery on the Holland family's plantation. Hoping to cure Jessica, Betsy takes her to a voodoo ceremony at the houmfort. The film never resolves whether Jessica's state is supernatural or psychiatric: its horror lies in sustained ambiguity, shadow, and sound rather than spectacle. The ending, in which Jessica and Paul's brother Wesley die and are carried into the sea by the zombie guardian Carrefour, is narrated by a calypso singer whose songs comment on the story throughout.

Production and style

Like Lewton's Cat People (1942), the film was made for under $150,000, reusing standing sets and relying on suggestion: drums heard off-screen, long takes through sugar cane, and stark black-and-white photography by J. Roy Hunt. The script drew on material by Curt Siodmak and Ardel Wray, who researched Haitian Vodou; the film treats the religion and the island's Black inhabitants with unusual seriousness for a 1940s studio picture, including an explicit acknowledgement of the Middle Passage and slavery.

Reception and legacy

Contemporary reviews were mixed, but the film's reputation grew steadily; it is now routinely listed among the greatest horror films ever made and among the best of Tourneur and Lewton's collaborations. Its atmosphere and oneiric procession scenes influenced generations of filmmakers, and the ambiguity between the medical and supernatural explanations of Jessica's condition remains a model for psychological horror.
