Metal-halide lamp

A metal-halide lamp

A metal-halide lamp is an electric lamp that produces light by an electric arc through a gaseous mixture of vaporized mercury and metal halides (compounds of metals with bromine or iodine). It is a type of high-intensity discharge (HID) gas discharge lamp. Developed in the 1960s, they are similar to mercury vapor lamps, but contain additional metal halide compounds in the quartz arc tube, which improve the efficiency and color rendition of the light.

Construction

Like other gas discharge lamps, metal-halide lamps consist of an arc tube with electrodes at each end, enclosed in an outer bulb. The arc tube is usually made of quartz (or ceramic in ceramic metal-halide lamps) and contains a starting gas, typically argon, mercury, and a mixture of metal halide salts such as sodium iodide and scandium iodide, or rare-earth halides in newer types. When the lamp is cold, the metal halides are condensed on the tube walls; when the arc strikes and heats the tube, the halides vaporize and the metals enter the arc, emitting their characteristic spectral lines.

Characteristics

Metal-halide lamps have high luminous efficacy of around 75–100 lumens per watt, roughly three to five times that of incandescent lamps, and produce an intense white light with good color rendering (CRI typically 65–90). Available wattages range from about 20 watts to over 10,000 watts. They require a ballast to regulate current and provide the starting voltage, need several minutes to warm up to full brightness, and cannot restart instantly after being extinguished while hot.

Applications

Metal-halide lamps are widely used for lighting large indoor and outdoor areas, including sports stadiums, industrial facilities, parking lots, retail stores, and street lighting. Specialized versions are used in aquariums (especially reef tanks), film and television production, stage lighting, and plant growth. Ceramic metal-halide lamps offer improved color stability and are common in retail lighting.
