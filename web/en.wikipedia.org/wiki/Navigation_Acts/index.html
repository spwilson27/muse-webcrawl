The Navigation Acts, or more broadly the Acts of Trade and Navigation, were a long series of English laws that developed, promoted, and regulated English ships, shipping, trade, and commerce with other countries and with its own colonies. The laws also regulated England's fisheries and restricted foreigners' direct trade with its colonies.

The Acts

The first Navigation Act, passed by the Commonwealth Parliament in October 1651 under Oliver Cromwell, required that goods imported into England and its colonies be carried in English ships with English crews — a measure aimed at the Dutch carrying trade and a contributing cause of the First Anglo-Dutch War. The Restoration Act of 1660 listed "enumerated" colonial goods (such as sugar, tobacco, and cotton) that could be shipped only to England or other English colonies. The Staple Act of 1663 required European goods bound for the colonies to pass through England, and the Plantation Duty Act of 1673 imposed duties on inter-colonial trade in enumerated goods.

Effects and repeal

The Acts were a cornerstone of mercantilism, fostering the growth of English shipping but burdening colonial merchants and consumers. Lax enforcement ("salutary neglect") for much of the 18th century softened their impact; stricter enforcement after 1763, and the customs measures that followed, contributed to the grievances behind the American Revolution. The Acts were progressively dismantled in the 19th century with the rise of free trade and were repealed in 1849 and 1854.
