Territory (animal)

In ethology, territory is the sociographical area that an animal of a particular species consistently defends against conspecifics (and, occasionally, animals of other species). Animals that actively defend territories are known as territorial animals.

A European robin, a famously territorial bird

Function

Territories secure resources for their holders: food, nesting sites, shelter and access to mates. Whether territoriality evolves depends on whether the benefits of exclusive use exceed the costs of defence — the principle of economic defendability. A territory differs from a home range, which is simply the area an animal habitually uses but does not necessarily defend.

Defence and advertisement

Territory owners advertise their presence and boundaries to minimise costly fights: birds sing from prominent perches, mammals scent-mark with urine, faeces or gland secretions, and reef fish display bright colours. Intruders are typically challenged by displays and chases, escalating to physical combat only when signals fail. Experiments show that residents usually win contests — the "dear enemy" effect describes how neighbours, once boundaries are settled, are tolerated more than strangers.

Examples

Territoriality occurs across the animal kingdom: wolves and lions defend group territories; songbirds such as robins hold individual feeding and breeding territories; male dragonflies patrol stretches of pond edge; limpets defend grazing patches of rock; and in many species only males, only females, pairs or whole colonies are territorial, sometimes only during the breeding season.
