Term limits in the United States

Term limits in the United States apply to many offices at both the federal and state level, and date back to the American Revolution. The idea that officeholders should rotate in office was reflected in several colonial charters and in the Articles of Confederation, which limited delegates to the Continental Congress to no more than three years in any six-year period.

At the federal level, the Twenty-second Amendment to the United States Constitution limits the president of the United States to two four-year terms. Before its ratification in 1951, no president had served more than two terms except Franklin D. Roosevelt, who was elected four times. By contrast, members of the United States Congress — senators and representatives — face no constitutional term limits, though efforts to impose them by state law were struck down in U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton (1995), in which the Supreme Court held that states could not add qualifications for federal office beyond those in the Constitution.

State-level limits

Fifteen states limit the terms of their state legislators. The modern wave of state legislative term limits began in 1990, when California, Colorado, and Oklahoma adopted them by ballot initiative. Thirty-six governors are subject to some form of term limit, most commonly two consecutive four-year terms. Virginia's governor is unique in being barred from serving consecutive terms but permitted to serve again after leaving office.

Debate

Supporters argue term limits reduce the power of incumbency, encourage new ideas, and limit careerism in politics. Critics counter that they remove experienced legislators, shift power to lobbyists and executive-branch staff, and restrict voters' choices. Empirical studies of term-limited state legislatures have found mixed effects on responsiveness and policy outcomes.
