Dachau trials

The Dachau trials were a series of war crimes trials conducted by the United States Army in the American occupation zone of Germany after World War II. Held between 1945 and 1948 within the grounds of the former Dachau concentration camp, the proceedings tried personnel of Nazi concentration camps as well as other accused war criminals, including those responsible for atrocities against downed American airmen.

Proceedings

The first and most publicised case, the Dachau Concentration Camp trial (November–December 1945), tried 40 members of the Dachau camp staff, including commandant Martin Weiss. All 40 were convicted; 36 were sentenced to death, and most of the death sentences were carried out at Landsberg Prison in 1946. Subsequent proceedings covered the Mauthausen, Buchenwald, Flossenbürg, Mühldorf, and Dora-Mittelbau camps, as well as the Malmedy massacre trial of Waffen-SS personnel involved in the murder of American prisoners during the Battle of the Bulge.

Legal character

Unlike the international tribunal at Nuremberg, the Dachau trials were conducted before U.S. military courts. A distinctive feature was the use of a "common design" theory of liability, under which participation in the operation of a concentration camp system was itself treated as a war crime. In total, the Dachau proceedings comprised nearly 500 cases involving more than 1,600 defendants; many sentences were later reviewed and reduced during the early 1950s amid Cold War pressures, and some convictions — particularly from the Malmedy trial — remained controversial.
