A makhtesh (Hebrew: מַכְתֵּשׁ, plural makhteshim) is a geological landform considered typical of Israel's Negev desert and the Sinai Peninsula. Although commonly called an "erosion crater", it is more precisely a deep valley with steep walls surrounded by a plateau, formed by erosion rather than by impact or volcanic activity.

Formation

A makhtesh forms where a hard layer of rock (usually limestone or dolomite) overlies softer sandstone. Rivers erode the soft underlying layer once the anticline crest is breached; the cavity is enlarged from below while the hard cap resists, creating a single-drainage valley enclosed by steep walls. Unlike an impact crater, a makhtesh has an outlet — a wadi through which the eroded material escapes.

Examples

The world's largest makhtesh is Makhtesh Ramon, about 40 km long, 2–10 km wide and up to 500 m deep, famous for its coloured sands and diverse geology. Other Negev examples are Makhtesh Gadol (the "Large Makhtesh", also called Makhtesh Hatira) and Makhtesh Katan (the "Small Makhtesh", Makhtesh Hazera); two further examples lie in Egypt's Sinai. The valleys are protected areas and popular hiking destinations.

Terminology

The Hebrew word makhtesh means a mortar — a bowl-shaped vessel. The term was adopted internationally by geologists because the landform was first described scientifically in the Negev.
