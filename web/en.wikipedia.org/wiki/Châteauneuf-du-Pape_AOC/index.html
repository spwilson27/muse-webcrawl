Châteauneuf-du-Pape is a French wine appellation (Appellation d'origine contrôlée) in the southern Rhône Valley, centred on the village of Châteauneuf-du-Pape between Avignon and Orange. It is the most famous appellation of the southern Rhône, known for powerful, full-bodied red wines, and it played a founding role in the French appellation system.

History

The name means "the Pope's new castle" and recalls the 14th century, when the papacy resided at Avignon and Pope John XXII built a summer residence at Châteauneuf and promoted its vineyards. In 1923 the local growers' syndicate under Baron Pierre Le Roy de Boiseaumarié drafted strict rules on vineyard boundaries and permitted grapes — a model for the national AOC system formally created in 1935–36, in which Châteauneuf-du-Pape was among the first appellations recognized.

A famous local curiosity is the 1954 municipal decree by mayor Lucien Jeune banning the landing of flying saucers (soucoupes volantes) in the vineyards, issued after a wave of UFO reports in France.

Terroir

The appellation covers about 3,200 hectares. Its best-known soil is the layer of large rounded quartzite stones — galets roulés — left by the ancient Rhône, which store the day's heat and release it at night, aiding ripening; other sectors have sandy or limestone soils. The climate is hot and dry, swept by the mistral wind, and summers are among the driest in France.

Grapes and wine

Thirteen grape varieties were traditionally authorized, and the regulations now permit 18, including colour variants. Red Châteauneuf-du-Pape is dominated by Grenache, usually blended with Syrah, Mourvèdre and Cinsault; the wines are high in alcohol, rich and spicy, and the best can age for decades. White Châteauneuf-du-Pape, a small minority of production, is made from Grenache blanc, Roussanne, Clairette and others, and is typically drunk young.
