Relaxation (NMR)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), relaxation is the process by which nuclear magnetisation returns to its equilibrium state after being disturbed, for example by a radiofrequency pulse. It is characterised by two time constants, described phenomenologically by the Bloch equations.

T1 and T2

Spin–lattice relaxation, with time constant T1, governs the recovery of the component of magnetisation parallel to the static field as excited nuclei transfer energy to their surroundings (the "lattice"). Spin–spin relaxation, with time constant T2, governs the decay of magnetisation in the transverse plane as individual spins fall out of phase with one another. In general T2 ≤ T1; in mobile liquids the two are often nearly equal, while in solids T2 is very short. The effective transverse decay in an inhomogeneous field, T2*, is faster still.

Mechanisms and applications

The dominant relaxation mechanism in diamagnetic liquids is usually fluctuating dipole–dipole coupling caused by molecular tumbling; paramagnetic species with unpaired electrons relax nuclei far more efficiently, which is why gadolinium-based contrast agents shorten T1 in magnetic resonance imaging. Measurements of T1 and T2 probe molecular dynamics, chemical exchange and molecular structure, and differences in relaxation times between tissues are the primary source of contrast in MRI.
