Heterozygote advantage

Heterozygote advantage is a case of balancing selection in which a heterozygous genotype has a higher relative fitness than either of the corresponding homozygous genotypes. It is also known as overdominance when the heterozygote's phenotype lies outside the range of both homozygotes. Heterozygote advantage can maintain genetic polymorphism in a population indefinitely, because selection favours whichever allele is rare.

Sickle cell and malaria

The best-known example is the sickle cell trait. Individuals homozygous for the sickle haemoglobin allele (HbS/HbS) suffer sickle cell disease, and those homozygous for normal haemoglobin (HbA/HbA) are more susceptible to severe malaria. Heterozygotes (HbA/HbS) have red blood cells that are inhospitable to the malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum yet do not sickle dangerously, giving them the highest fitness in malaria-endemic regions. This explains the high frequency of the HbS allele in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean and South Asia despite its lethal effects in homozygotes.

Other proposed examples

Heterozygote advantage has been proposed for carriers of cystic fibrosis (possible resistance to cholera or typhoid), Tay–Sachs disease, and certain HLA types that present a broader range of pathogen antigens; for several of these cases the evidence remains debated. The classic experimental case in Drosophila and in laboratory populations demonstrates the principle clearly even where specific natural examples are contested.

Theory

If the fitnesses of the three genotypes AA, Aa and aa are 1−s, 1 and 1−t respectively (s, t > 0), the allele frequencies converge to a stable equilibrium at which the frequency of allele A is t/(s+t). Because the disadvantageous alleles are continuously reintroduced by the heterozygote's advantage, neither allele is eliminated — a stable polymorphism results.
