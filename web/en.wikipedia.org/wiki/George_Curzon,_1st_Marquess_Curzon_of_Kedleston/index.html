George Curzon, 1st Marquess Curzon of Kedleston

George Nathaniel Curzon, 1st Marquess Curzon of Kedleston (11 January 1859 – 20 March 1925) was a British statesman, Viceroy of India and Foreign Secretary.

Born: Kedleston Hall, Derbyshire
Died: London
Offices: Viceroy of India (1899–1905); Foreign Secretary (1919–1924)

George Nathaniel Curzon, 1st Marquess Curzon of Kedleston, was a British Conservative statesman who served as Viceroy of India from 1899 to 1905 and as Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs from 1919 to 1924. A traveller and writer on Asia before entering high office, he was one of the most capable — and most imperious — imperial administrators of his age.

Early life

Curzon was born at Kedleston Hall, Derbyshire, the eldest son of the 4th Baron Scarsdale. Educated at Eton and Balliol College, Oxford, where he was president of the Oxford Union, he entered Parliament in 1886. He travelled extensively in Central Asia, Persia, and the Far East, publishing works such as Persia and the Persian Question (1892) and coining the phrase "the Great Game" in its popular sense.

Viceroy of India

As Viceroy, Curzon pursued administrative efficiency, famine relief, educational and police reform, the creation of the North-West Frontier Province, and the preservation of Indian monuments, restoring the Taj Mahal and passing the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act of 1904. His partition of Bengal in 1905 provoked fierce opposition and was reversed in 1911. He quarrelled with the commander-in-chief, Lord Kitchener, and resigned in 1905.

Foreign Secretary

Curzon served in wartime coalition governments and became Foreign Secretary under Lloyd George and Bonar Law. He gave his name to the "Curzon Line", proposed as Poland's eastern frontier in 1919–20, and negotiated the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) with Turkey. Widely expected to become Prime Minister in 1923, he was passed over in favour of Stanley Baldwin, partly because he sat in the House of Lords.

Death and legacy

Curzon died in London in 1925. His career embodied the confidence and the contradictions of the late British Empire; a famous Oxford rhyme mocked his superiority: "My name is George Nathaniel Curzon, I am a most superior person."
