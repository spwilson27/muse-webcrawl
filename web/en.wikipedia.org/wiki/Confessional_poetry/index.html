Confessional poetry

Confessional poetry or "confessionalism" is a style of poetry that emerged in the United States during the 1950s and 1960s, centred on extreme, previously taboo moments of the individual poet's life — mental illness, divorce, sexuality, trauma, and suicidal feelings — written in a direct, autobiographical "I" voice.

Origins

The term was coined by critic M. L. Rosenthal in his 1959 review of Robert Lowell's Life Studies, a landmark collection dealing with Lowell's family and mental breakdowns. W. D. Snodgrass's Heart's Needle (1959) was another early influence.

Major figures

  • Robert Lowell — Life Studies, For the Union Dead
  • Sylvia Plath — Ariel (posthumous, 1965), "Daddy", "Lady Lazarus"
  • Anne Sexton — To Bedlam and Part Way Back, Live or Die (Pulitzer Prize)
  • John Berryman — The Dream Songs
  • Theodore Roethke and Randall Jarrell as precursors/associates

Characteristics and criticism

Confessional poems use intimate detail, psychoanalytic contexts, and a blurred line between poet and speaker. Critics have both praised their honesty and charged narcissism and sensationalism; later scholars note the label can diminish the craft and broader social concerns of Plath and Sexton in particular.

Legacy

Confessionalism influenced later autobiographical writing, memoir poetry, and spoken-word traditions, and remains a contested but central category in postwar American poetry. See contemporary literature.
