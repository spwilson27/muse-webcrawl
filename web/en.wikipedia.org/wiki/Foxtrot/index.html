Foxtrot

The foxtrot is a smooth, progressive ballroom dance characterised by long, continuous flowing movements across the dance floor. It is danced to big band (usually vocal) music, in 4/4 time, and is one of the standard dances of International Style ballroom competition. The dance is similar in its look to waltz, although the rhythm is in four rather than three.

History

The foxtrot emerged in the United States in the 1910s, amid the ragtime dance craze. It is commonly associated with the vaudeville actor Harry Fox, who in 1914 performed a fast trotting dance to ragtime in New York — the "Fox's trot" of contemporary accounts. Popularised by the dance team of Vernon and Irene Castle, the dance quickly spread to Europe. Over time the fast original dance slowed and split into the smooth ballroom foxtrot and the faster quickstep; the slower social rhythm also survived as the "slow foxtrot" and influenced dances such as the peabody.

Technique

The foxtrot is danced in closed hold with a "slow, quick, quick" rhythm, using feather steps, three steps, natural turns and weaves, with continuous rise and fall gentler than in waltz. In International Style it is one of the five standard dances, alongside waltz, tango, Viennese waltz and quickstep; American Style smooth foxtrot permits open and separated figures.

Other uses of the name

"Foxtrot" is also the word for the letter F in the NATO phonetic alphabet, which has given the name to numerous unrelated works and products.
