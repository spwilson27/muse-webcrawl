Correspondence theory of truth

[Image placeholder: statement mirroring reality]

The correspondence theory of truth is a theory of truth in philosophy which holds that the truth or falsity of a statement is determined by how it relates to the world and whether it accurately describes, or corresponds to, that world. A belief is true when it corresponds to a fact; the proposition "snow is white" is true if and only if snow is in fact white.

The view has roots in Aristotle, who wrote in the Metaphysics that "to say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true". It was taken up in medieval philosophy, notably by Thomas Aquinas, who defined truth as the adequacy of the intellect to the thing (adaequatio intellectus et rei). In the twentieth century the theory was defended in various forms by Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore, and developed by J. L. Austin, while Alfred Tarski's semantic theory of truth gave a formally precise account closely related to the correspondence intuition.

Criticisms

Critics have pressed two main difficulties: specifying the correspondence relation between a thought or sentence and a mind-independent fact, and saying what a "fact" is without circularity. Rival accounts — coherence theories, pragmatist theories, and deflationary theories of truth — are often motivated by these problems. Nevertheless, correspondence remains the dominant " common sense" account of truth and a reference point against which alternatives define themselves.
