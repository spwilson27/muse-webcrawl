Agrarian reform

Agrarian reform is the redistribution and reorganization of agricultural land and farming systems by government action, most commonly involving the transfer of land from large landowners to tenant farmers, laborers, or smallholders. It encompasses changes in land ownership, tenancy law, credit, and rural institutions, and has been pursued for goals ranging from social justice and poverty reduction to increased agricultural productivity and political stability.

History

Land redistribution has ancient precedents, including the reforms of the Gracchi brothers in Rome. In the 20th century it became a central policy across the developing world: post-revolutionary Mexico distributed communal ejidos; Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan carried out sweeping reforms under or after Allied occupation, which are credited with supporting their later industrialization; China's collectivization and later household responsibility system transformed its countryside. In Latin America, reforms in Bolivia (1953), Cuba (1959), and Chile (1960s–70s) redistributed large estates, while many other countries' reforms were partial or reversed.

Approaches and debate

Programs differ in whether landowners are compensated, whether land is transferred to individuals or collectives, and whether reform is market-assisted (as promoted by the World Bank in the 1990s) or expropriative. Supporters argue redistribution reduces inequality and can raise productivity on small farms; critics note risks of fragmentation, output disruption, and political misuse, as in Zimbabwe's fast-track program of the 2000s.

Contemporary efforts

Land reform remains a live issue in countries such as South Africa, the Philippines, Brazil, and Colombia, where movements of landless peasants and constitutional mandates continue to press for redistribution.
