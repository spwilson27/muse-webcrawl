The French Social Party (French: Parti social français, PSF) was a French nationalist political party founded in 1936 by François de La Rocque, following the dissolution of his Croix-de-Feu league by the Popular Front government. The PSF grew rapidly to become the largest right-wing party in France before the Second World War, claiming between 600,000 and over a million members, and it anticipated aspects of post-war Gaullism.

Programme

The PSF advocated an authoritarian reform of the Republic: a strong executive, proportional representation, votes for women, corporatist organisation of the economy, and social welfare measures, under the slogan "Work, Family, Fatherland" — later adopted by Vichy. It recruited heavily among veterans, the middle classes and women, and built an extensive social and youth organisation. Historians have long debated whether the Croix-de-Feu and PSF should be classified as fascist; many regard La Rocque's movement as a conservative, nationalist mass party that rejected both fascist revolution and parliamentary liberalism.

War and aftermath

Excluded from power in the late 1930s, the party's influence declined after 1940. La Rocque initially cooperated with Vichy's National Revolution but also fed intelligence to the Resistance; he was arrested by the Gestapo in 1943 and deported. The PSF was dissolved in 1945, though many of its cadres and ideas resurfaced in post-war movements of the national right.
