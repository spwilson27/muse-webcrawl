Sunningdale Agreement

The Sunningdale Agreement was an attempt to end the Troubles in Northern Ireland by establishing a power-sharing executive for Northern Ireland and a cross-border Council of Ireland. It was agreed at a conference held at the Civil Service College in Sunningdale, Berkshire, on 6–9 December 1973, among the British government of Edward Heath, the Irish government of Liam Cosgrave, and the Northern Ireland parties that had formed the new power-sharing Executive.

Terms

The Northern Ireland Executive, headed by former Ulster Unionist prime minister Brian Faulkner as chief executive and the SDLP's Gerry Fitt as deputy, took office on 1 January 1974. The agreement provided for a Council of Ireland with ministerial and consultative functions linking Belfast and Dublin, which unionists feared was a step toward a united Ireland. Faulkner's support for the deal split the Ulster Unionist Party; hardliners under Harry West won most unionist seats in the February 1974 UK general election.

Collapse

In May 1974 the loyalist Ulster Workers' Council called a general strike, bringing Northern Ireland's power supply and economy to a halt and demanding new Assembly elections. The British government declined to break the strike, and Faulkner resigned on 28 May 1974; the Executive collapsed and direct rule from London resumed. The strike is widely seen as the most successful act of the loyalist campaign during the Troubles.

Legacy

Sunningdale's basic architecture — power-sharing within Northern Ireland plus an institutional Irish dimension — was revived a quarter-century later in the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, leading nationalist politician Seamus Mallon to call the later settlement "Sunningdale for slow learners."
