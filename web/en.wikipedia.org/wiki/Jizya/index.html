Jizya (Arabic: جِزْيَة) is a per-capita annual tax historically levied in territories governed by Islamic law on non-Muslim subjects — the dhimmis. In return, non-Muslim subjects were permitted to practise their faith, keep their property and communal autonomy, and were exempt from the zakat tax paid by Muslims and from military service, while receiving the protection of the state. The jizya is described in the Qur'an (verse 9:29) and was applied from the early caliphates through the Ottoman Empire, with rates and enforcement varying widely across centuries and regions.

Scriptural basis and rationale

Qur'an 9:29 instructs fighting "those who do not believe in Allah or in the Last Day... until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled." Classical jurists across the major schools of law treated the tax as the fiscal counterpart of the dhimma pact: the state protected non-Muslim life, property and worship, and the jizya substituted for military service and for zakat, which non-Muslims did not pay. Jurists typically assessed it only on free adult males of sound mind and means; women, children, the elderly, the poor, monks and the seriously ill were generally exempt.

Historical application

Under the Rashidun and Umayyad caliphates the jizya (often intertwined with the kharaj land tax) supplied a large share of state revenue, and tax arrangements in conquered Persia and Byzantium adapted earlier Sasanian and Byzantine poll taxes. Rates were commonly graduated into three wealth classes — in some periods 48, 24 and 12 dirhams annually. In Mughal India, Akbar abolished the jizya in 1564 as part of his policy of religious tolerance; Aurangzeb reimposed it in 1679, a move historians link to both fiscal pressure and his programme of Islamic orthodoxy, and it lapsed again in the early 18th century. In the Ottoman Empire the tax (cizye) was collected for centuries until the Hatt-ı Hümayun of 1856 formally abolished it as part of the Tanzimat reforms, replacing it with a military-exemption tax on non-Muslims that itself was later abandoned.

Dhimma system

The jizya was one element of the broader dhimma framework governing Jews, Christians and, by extension in various places, Zoroastrians, Hindus and others. In addition to taxation, dhimmis were in many times and places subject to legal and social restrictions codified in texts such as the "Pact of Umar" — on building places of worship, bearing arms, dress, and testimony — though enforcement varied greatly, and non-Muslim communities often prospered as merchants, physicians, translators and administrators.

Modern usage and legacy

As modern nation-states adopted equal citizenship, the jizya disappeared from law across the Muslim world during the 19th and 20th centuries. In the 2010s the Islamic State (ISIS) attempted to revive the tax on Christians in territory it controlled in Syria and Iraq, a move condemned across the Muslim world as extortion rather than legitimate jurisprudence. The concept remains debated in discussions of Islamic legal history, minority rights and the fiscal organisation of pre-modern states.
