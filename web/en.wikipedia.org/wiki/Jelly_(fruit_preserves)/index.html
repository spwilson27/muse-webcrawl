Jelly (fruit preserves)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A jar of jewel-red crabapple jelly, translucent enough to glow against the light]
A jar of jewel-red crabapple jelly, translucent enough to glow against the light

Jelly, in the context of Fruit preserves, is a clear, translucent spread made from fruit juice, sugar and Pectin, cooked to a gel that holds its shape yet spreads smoothly. Unlike jam, it contains no pieces of fruit — the juice is extracted by simmering fruit and straining it through muslin (a 'jelly bag') without pressing, to keep it sparkling clear. Classic jellies include redcurrant (the traditional accompaniment to roast lamb and the base of Cumberland sauce), crabapple, quince and grape — the last being the standard partner of peanut butter in the American PB&J.

Making

Jelly-making is a test of pectin management: under-ripe fruit for pectin, added lemon for acid, sugar at roughly 55–60% for the gel, and a brief boil to the setting point (104.5 °C) — overcooking clouds the jelly and dulls the flavour. Commercial production (Welch's grape jelly dates to 1923, from Concord grapes) standardised what home preservers did by eye and cold-plate wrinkle tests.

Terminology

In North America 'jelly' means exclusively the clear preserve; in Britain it more often means a gelatine dessert (American 'Jell-O'), with the preserve distinguished as 'jam' even when clear (bramble jelly is the exception proving usage). The distinction has caused much transatlantic recipe confusion.
