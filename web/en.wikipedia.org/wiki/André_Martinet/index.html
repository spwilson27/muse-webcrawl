André Martinet (6 April 1908 – 22 July 1999) was a French linguist, one of the leading figures of structural and functional linguistics in the twentieth century. He is best known for the concept of double articulation — the organisation of language into meaningful units (monemes) built from meaningless sound units (phonemes) — which he regarded as the defining property of human language.

Career

Martinet studied English and German and wrote his doctoral thesis on consonant gemination in Germanic. Influenced by the Prague school of Trubetzkoy and Jakobson, he developed a functional approach to phonology. After the Second World War he taught at Columbia University in New York (1947–1955), where he directed the journal Word and influenced American linguistics, before returning to France as professor at the Sorbonne.

Main works and ideas

His Économie des changements phonétiques (1955) explained sound change through principles of economy — least effort balanced against communicative need. Éléments de linguistique générale (1960), translated as Elements of General Linguistics, became a standard introduction to structural linguistics. Martinet emphasised that every feature of a language should be explained by its function in communication.

Legacy

Martinet founded the International Auxiliary Language Association's research work in the 1940s (contributing to Interlingua) and trained generations of French linguists. His functionalist school remains a major strand of European linguistics.
