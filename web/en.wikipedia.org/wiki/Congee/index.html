Congee

Congee (from Tamil kañci) is a type of rice porridge eaten in many Asian countries. It is made by boiling rice in a large amount of water for an extended period until the grains break down and the mixture thickens into a soft, creamy porridge. In China it is called zhou (粥) in Mandarin and juk in Cantonese (also the Korean name); related dishes include Japanese okayu, Vietnamese cháo, Thai jok, Filipino lugaw and arroz caldo, Indonesian bubur, and South Asian preparations such as kanji and khichdi-adjacent rice gruels.

Preparation and serving

Congee ranges from thick to soupy depending on the rice-to-water ratio (often between 1:5 and 1:12) and regional taste. It may be cooked plain and served with savoury accompaniments, or simmered with flavouring ingredients such as chicken, pork, fish, century eggs, offal, mushrooms, or seafood. Common toppings include chopped scallions, julienned ginger, white pepper, soy sauce, sesame oil, fried garlic or shallots, and fried dough sticks (youtiao). In Cantonese cuisine, congee is a breakfast staple and dim sum item; pork-and-century-egg congee and sampan (seafood) congee are classic varieties.

Cultural role

Because it is soft and easily digested, congee is traditionally served to the ill, the elderly, infants, and convalescents, and in Chinese tradition it is associated with frugality and relief of famine — a small amount of rice stretched to feed many people. It also appears in festival contexts, for example laba zhou, a multi-grain congee eaten on the Laba festival. The English word entered the language through Portuguese traders and the Tamil-speaking Coromandel Coast, and in Anglo-Indian usage "congee" also named the starchy water drained from boiled rice.
