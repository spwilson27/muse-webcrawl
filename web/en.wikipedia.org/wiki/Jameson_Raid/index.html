Jameson Raid

Date29 December 1895 – 2 January 1896
LocationSouth African Republic (Transvaal)
ResultBoer victory; raiders surrendered
CommandersLeander Starr Jameson (raiders); Piet Cronjé (Boers)

The Jameson Raid (29 December 1895 – 2 January 1896) was a botched raid against the South African Republic (the Transvaal) carried out by British colonial administrator Leander Starr Jameson and his Company troops. It was intended to trigger an uprising by the predominantly British foreign workers (Uitlanders) in the Transvaal, but it failed disastrously, worsened Anglo-Boer relations, and was a contributory cause of the Second Boer War (1899–1902).

Background

The discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 transformed the Transvaal and drew in tens of thousands of Uitlanders, who soon outnumbered the Boers but were denied full political rights by President Paul Kruger's government. Cecil Rhodes, prime minister of the Cape Colony and head of the British South Africa Company, plotted with Uitlander leaders in Johannesburg to combine an uprising there with an armed incursion that would "restore order". Jameson assembled a column of about 500 men — Rhodesian police and volunteers — on the border at Pitsani.

The raid

When the Johannesburg rising faltered and its leaders asked him to wait, Jameson, impatient and believing delay fatal, invaded anyway on 29 December 1895. His men cut telegraph wires — but missed the wire to Pretoria — and rode for Johannesburg, shadowed by Boer commandos under Piet Cronjé. Exhausted and outnumbered, the raiders were surrounded and defeated at Doornkop on 2 January 1896, suffering about 30 killed; Jameson surrendered. The Johannesburg conspirators were arrested; their leaders were tried, sentenced to death, and the sentences commuted to heavy fines.

Consequences

The raid disgraced Rhodes, who resigned as Cape prime minister amid suspicions — never fully resolved at the time — about how much the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, and the British government had known. Kaiser Wilhelm II's congratulatory "Kruger telegram" to the Transvaal president soured Anglo-German relations. Jameson and his officers were tried in London and given short prison sentences; Jameson later returned to politics and became prime minister of the Cape Colony (1904–1908). In the Transvaal the raid deepened Boer distrust, drove Kruger to arm heavily, and set the stage for war three years later.
