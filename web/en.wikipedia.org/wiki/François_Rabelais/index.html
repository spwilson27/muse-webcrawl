François Rabelais (French: [fʁɑ̃swa ʁablɛ]; born between 1483 and 1494; died 9 April 1553) was a French writer, physician, Renaissance humanist, monk and Greek scholar. He is regarded as one of the great writers of world literature and among the creators of modern European writing. His best-known work is Gargantua and Pantagruel, a series of comic novels about giants, filled with satire, wordplay and exuberant lists.

Life

Rabelais was born in Touraine, the son of a lawyer. He became a Franciscan friar, then a Benedictine monk, chafing under restrictions on his Greek studies, and eventually left monastic life to study medicine at Montpellier. He practised medicine in Lyon, where his first books appeared, and travelled to Rome in the entourage of Cardinal Jean du Bellay, whose protection — together with that of the du Bellay family and Margaret of Navarre — shielded him from charges of heresy.

Gargantua and Pantagruel

Under the anagrammatic pen name Alcofribas Nasier, Rabelais published Pantagruel (1532), followed by Gargantua (1534), the Third Book (1546) and the Fourth Book (1552); a fifth appeared posthumously. The books follow the giants Gargantua and his son Pantagruel through education, war, quests for the "Dive Bouteille" (Holy Bottle), and encounters with the foolish lawyer-sophist Panurge. Episodes such as the Abbey of Thélème, whose only rule is "Do what thou wilt," satirise scholasticism, monasticism, war and legal pedantry while celebrating learning, bodily life and laughter.

The Sorbonne repeatedly condemned his books, and Rabelais wrote under threat throughout his later career. He died in Paris in 1553.

Legacy

The adjective "Rabelaisian" denotes his bawdy, exuberant humour. Mikhail Bakhtin's study Rabelais and His World made the novels a key text for theories of carnival and grotesque realism. Rabelais' inventive French — he is credited with coining or popularising hundreds of words — remains a touchstone of French literature.
