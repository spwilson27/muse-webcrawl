Persistence of vision (ray)

In computer graphics and display technology, persistence of vision — here specified as the (ray) sense — refers to the behaviour of a beam or ray of light on a phosphor-coated screen: after the electron beam of a cathode-ray tube (CRT) passes, the phosphor continues to glow briefly, so the traced ray remains visible as it fades. This phosphor persistence determines how smoothly vector and raster displays render motion and how much flicker the viewer perceives.

Phosphor persistence

Phosphors are classified by persistence time, from very short (microseconds) to long (seconds). Short-persistence phosphors suit fast-refresh monitors, while long-persistence phosphors were used in radar and oscilloscope displays so that a slowly swept trace remained visible. Vector displays such as the original Asteroids arcade machine drew scenes as connected rays of light whose visibility depended directly on this persistence.

Relation to the optical phenomenon

The term is shared with the better-known perceptual phenomenon in which an afterimage appears to persist for roughly a tenth of a second after the stimulating image disappears — the effect historically invoked to explain motion pictures. The display-engineering sense concerns the physical decay of light emission rather than retinal processing, though the two combine in practice to determine perceived smoothness.

Applications

Understanding ray persistence matters in CRT design, storage tubes, radar plan-position indicators, and modern "persistence of vision" (POV) displays that spin arrays of LEDs so a single moving column of light paints a full image.
