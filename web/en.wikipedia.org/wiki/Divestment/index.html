Divestment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance and economics, divestment or divestiture is the reduction of some kind of asset for financial, ethical, or political objectives, or the sale of an existing business by a firm. A divestment is the opposite of an investment. In corporate finance, divestiture is a common way for companies to dispose of subsidiaries, business units, product lines, or real assets that no longer fit their strategy.

Corporate divestiture

Companies divest for many reasons: to raise cash, to focus on core businesses, to remove underperforming or unrelated units, to satisfy regulators or antitrust authorities, or because management believes the parts are worth more than the whole (breaking a "conglomerate discount"). Common methods include:

  • Sell-off – sale of a unit to another company for cash or securities.
  • Spin-off – distribution of shares in the unit to the parent's existing shareholders, creating an independent company.
  • Equity carve-out – sale of a minority stake in a subsidiary through an initial public offering.
  • Liquidation – selling the unit's assets piecemeal and closing it.

Campaigns and political divestment

"Divestment" also describes campaigns urging institutions to withdraw investments for moral or political reasons. The most prominent historical example was the campaign in the 1970s and 1980s for universities, pension funds, and governments to divest from companies operating in apartheid South Africa. Later campaigns have targeted tobacco, Sudan during the Darfur crisis, Israeli policies (the BDS movement), and, most prominently in the 2010s and 2020s, fossil fuels, in which foundations, universities, and sovereign funds pledged to sell coal, oil, and gas holdings to combat climate change. The effectiveness of such campaigns – whether they change corporate behaviour or merely transfer ownership – is debated.
