Potentiometer

A typical rotary potentiometer, used as a volume control

TypePassive electronic component
Terminals3 (two ends and a wiper)
FunctionAdjustable voltage divider
SymbolResistor with an arrow through it

A potentiometer is a three-terminal resistor with a sliding or rotating contact that forms an adjustable voltage divider. When only two terminals are used — one end and the wiper — it behaves as a variable resistor, or rheostat. Potentiometers are widely used as user controls: volume and tone knobs on audio equipment, brightness and speed controls, and position sensors in joysticks, servomechanisms, and throttle controls.

Construction

The resistive element may be a track of carbon composition, cermet, conductive plastic, or a coil of resistance wire, over which a movable wiper slides. Rotary potentiometers turn through about 270–300 degrees; multiturn versions use a worm gear for fine adjustment over many rotations; linear or slider potentiometers move the wiper along a straight track. The relationship between wiper position and resistance may be linear ("lin") or approximately logarithmic ("log" or audio taper), the latter matching the human ear's response in volume controls.

History and applications

The device traces back to nineteenth-century instruments for measuring electromotive force by comparison against a known voltage — the original laboratory meaning of "potentiometer" as a measuring instrument. As a circuit component it became ubiquitous in twentieth-century electronics. Digital potentiometers, integrated circuits that emulate the function under microprocessor control, are now common in enclosed products, while mechanical potentiometers remain standard where a direct manual control is wanted.
