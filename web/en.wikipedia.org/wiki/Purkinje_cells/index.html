Purkinje cell

Purkinje cells, or Purkinje neurons, are a class of GABAergic inhibitory neurons located in the cerebellar cortex of the brain. They are among the largest and most elaborate neurons in the mammalian brain, with huge, fan-like dendritic trees, and they provide the sole output of the cerebellar cortex to the deep cerebellar nuclei. They are named after their discoverer, the Czech anatomist Jan Evangelista Purkyně, who described them in 1839.

Morphology

Purkinje cell bodies form a single sheet — the Purkinje layer — between the molecular and granular layers of the cerebellar cortex. Their dendrites branch extensively within a single plane (like a fan or espaliered tree) in the molecular layer, and each tree is crossed by the axons of granule cells, the parallel fibers, forming up to ~200,000 synapses per Purkinje cell in rats. Spine-covered distal dendrites (spiny branchlets) receive parallel-fiber input, while smooth proximal dendrites receive a single powerful climbing fiber from the inferior olive.

Electrophysiology

Purkinje cells fire two kinds of spikes: regular simple spikes (driven largely by parallel-fiber input) and complex spikes triggered by climbing fibers. The pairing of parallel-fiber activity with climbing-fiber signals induces long-term depression (LTD) at parallel-fiber synapses — a plasticity mechanism central to theories of cerebellar motor learning originating with David Marr and James Albus, and demonstrated experimentally by Masao Ito.

Function and clinical aspects

By inhibiting the deep cerebellar nuclei, Purkinje cells shape the cerebellum's contribution to motor coordination, timing and error correction. Their degeneration causes cerebellar ataxia, as in several spinocerebellar ataxias; anti-Purkinje-cell antibodies (anti-Yo) occur in paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration. Purkinje cells are also exquisitely vulnerable to hypoxia and to alcohol-related damage.
