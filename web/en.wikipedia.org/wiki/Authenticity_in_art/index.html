Authenticity in art

Authenticity in art is the question of whether a work of art is genuinely what it is represented to be—created by the artist to whom it is attributed, at the time and in the manner claimed. Authenticity underpins the art market, museum scholarship, and art law, and its absence may indicate a forgery, a copy, a misattribution, or a workshop production. The concept also extends beyond authorship: philosophers distinguish nominal authenticity (correct attribution of origin) from expressive authenticity (a work's being a true expression of its maker's values and beliefs).

Determining authenticity

Establishing authenticity typically rests on three kinds of evidence:

  • Provenance – the documented history of ownership of the work, tracing it back toward the artist's studio.
  • Connoisseurship – expert judgement based on close visual analysis of style and technique, such as the Morellian method of attending to minor details like ears and hands.
  • Technical examination – scientific methods including X-radiography, infrared reflectography, dendrochronology for panel paintings, pigment and binder analysis, radiocarbon dating, and examination of canvas weaves and watermarks.

For major artists, catalogues raisonnés—scholarly compilations of an artist's complete oeuvre—serve as standard references, and committees or foundations (such as artist-endowed authentication boards) have historically issued opinions on attribution, though several have disbanded because of litigation risks.

Degrees of attribution

Attributions are often qualified: a work may be described as "by" an artist, "attributed to", "studio/workshop of", "circle of", "follower of", "manner of", or "after" a prototype by the artist. These categories carry significant implications for value: authenticated works by major artists command prices orders of magnitude higher than workshop pieces or copies.

Famous cases

Celebrated authenticity disputes include the paintings of Han van Meegeren, whose forged "Vermeers" fooled leading experts in the 1930s and 1940s; the Getty kouros, whose authenticity remains debated; the La Bella Principessa drawing attributed by some scholars to Leonardo da Vinci; and the Knoedler Gallery scandal of the 1990s–2000s, in which fake Abstract Expressionist paintings were sold for tens of millions of dollars. In 2017 the sale of Salvator Mundi for $450.3 million—the highest price ever paid for a painting—was accompanied by continuing scholarly debate over the extent of Leonardo's hand in the work.

Philosophical issues

Philosophers such as Nelson Goodman have argued that authenticity matters aesthetically because knowledge of a work's history of production affects how it is perceived, even where a copy is perceptually indistinguishable. Others debate whether multiples, prints, photographs, and conceptual works produced by assistants or fabricators can be "authentic" in the same sense as autograph paintings.
