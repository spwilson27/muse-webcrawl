Isra and Mi'raj

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Al-Aqsa Mosque, Jerusalem, destination of the Night Journey

The Israʾ and Miʿraj (Arabic: الإسراء والمعراج) are the two parts of the Night Journey that, according to Islamic tradition, the prophet Muhammad took during a single night around the year 621 CE. The Isra is the journey from Mecca to "the farthest mosque" — identified with Al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem — and the Mi'raj is his ascension from there through the heavens.

Account

According to the hadith and sira literature, the archangel Gabriel brought the winged creature Buraq, which carried Muhammad from the Kaaba in Mecca to Jerusalem, where he led earlier prophets in prayer. He then ascended through the seven heavens, meeting Adam, Jesus and John the Baptist, Joseph, Idris (Enoch), Aaron, Moses and Abraham in successive heavens, and reached the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary (Sidrat al-Muntaha). There he received from God the command of the five daily prayers (salah), reduced, on Moses' advice, from an original fifty.

The journey is alluded to in the Quran at 17:1 — "Glory be to Him who carried His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque" — which gives Surah Al-Isra its name, and is further referenced in Surah An-Najm. Muslim interpreters historically debated whether the journey was physical or a vision; the prevailing Sunni view holds it was bodily.

Observance

The event is commemorated on Laylat al-Mi'raj, traditionally dated to the night of 26–27 Rajab in the Islamic calendar, marked by prayers, storytelling and, in some places, festive illumination. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem is associated in tradition with the spot of the ascension.
