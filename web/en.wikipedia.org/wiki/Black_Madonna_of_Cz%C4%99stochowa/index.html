The Black Madonna of Częstochowa (Polish: Czarna Madonna z Częstochowy) is a venerated icon of the Virgin Mary housed at the Jasna Góra Monastery in Częstochowa, Poland. The painting, a Byzantine-style Hodegetria depicting Mary gesturing toward the Christ Child, is one of Poland's most important religious and national symbols and a major pilgrimage destination, drawing millions of pilgrims annually.

History

Tradition attributes the icon to Saint Luke the Evangelist, painted, it was said, on a tabletop from the Holy Family's house. Documented history is later: the icon is generally dated by art historians to the Byzantine or early medieval period and was repainted after suffering damage. According to tradition, Prince Władysław Opolczyk brought the icon to Jasna Góra in 1382 and entrusted it to the Pauline monks. In 1430 Hussite raiders looted the monastery and slashed the painting; the scars on the Virgin's cheek, from a later act of desecration, remain a distinctive feature.

The siege of Jasna Góra

In 1655, during the Swedish invasion known as the Deluge, a small force of monks and soldiers defended Jasna Góra against a much larger Swedish army for 40 days. The successful defence became a turning point in Polish morale, and in 1656 King John II Casimir proclaimed the Virgin of Częstochowa "Queen of Poland" in his Lwów Oath. The icon received canonical coronation in 1717.

Devotion

The darkened complexion of the figures — the result of centuries of candle smoke and ageing varnish as much as original pigment — gives the icon its "Black Madonna" name. Copies of the image are found in Polish churches and diaspora parishes worldwide. Pope John Paul II prayed before the icon on several pilgrimages to his homeland, and the feast of Our Lady of Częstochowa is celebrated on 26 August.
