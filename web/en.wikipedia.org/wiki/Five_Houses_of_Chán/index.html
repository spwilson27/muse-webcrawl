The Five Houses of Chán (Chinese: 禪五家, Chán wǔjiā) were the five principal schools or "houses" into which Chinese Chán (Zen) Buddhism differentiated during the late Tang and Five Dynasties periods. Each house traced its lineage to a particular master, preserved distinctive teaching styles, and is remembered in classic Chán literature such as the recorded sayings and koan collections. The phrase "five houses and seven schools" adds the later Yangqi and Huanglong branches of the Linji house.

The five houses

  • Guiyang house (潙仰宗) — founded by Guishan Lingyou (771–853) and his disciple Yangshan Huiji; the first to form, noted for circle diagrams (ensō-like symbols) and the "99 circles" of Yangshan; it died out by the end of the Song dynasty.
  • Linji house (臨濟宗) — founded by Linji Yixuan (d. 866), famed for shouts (he), blows, and demanding encounter dialogue; transmitted to Japan as Rinzai Zen.
  • Caodong house (曹洞宗) — named from Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) and Caoshan Benji; emphasised "silent illumination" and the Five Ranks of Dongshan; transmitted to Japan by Dōgen as Sōtō Zen. (This house gave rise to the AED batch's Dōgen connection.)
  • Yunmen house (雲門宗) — founded by Yunmen Wenyan (864–949), known for terse, enigmatic one-character answers ("every day is a good day"); extinct as an independent line after the Song.
  • Fayan house (法眼宗) — founded by Fayan Wenyi (885–958), synthesised Chán with Huayan philosophy and Yongming Yanshou's inclusive approach; also faded by the Northern Song.

Historical development

The houses crystallised in the regional warlord courts of the 9th and 10th centuries, where masters gathered disciples and patrons. Song-dynasty texts, such as the Transmission of the Lamp (1004), retrospectively codified their genealogies back to Huineng and ultimately to Bodhidharma and the Buddha. In the Song, the Linji and Caodong houses dominated, producing the great koan collections — the Blue Cliff Record and the Book of Equanimity respectively.

Legacy

Only Linji and Caodong survive as living traditions, chiefly through their Japanese descendants Rinzai and Sōtō, Korean Seon (which draws on Linji/Kanhwa practice), and the modern Chinese Chán revival, where most lineages claim Linji transmission. The distinctive verses describing each house's style — "Caodong meticulous, Yunmen lofty and solitary…" — remain staples of Zen pedagogy.
