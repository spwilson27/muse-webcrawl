Triumph

A triumph (Latin: triumphus) was a civil ceremony and religious rite of ancient Rome, held to publicly celebrate and sanctify the success of a military commander who had won a great victory. It was among the highest honours the Roman state could bestow.

The procession

On the day of the triumph, the general — the triumphator — rode in a four-horse chariot along the Via Sacra to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, wearing the purple and gold regalia of the kingly and divine past, his face painted red. Before him marched magistrates, senators, musicians, and wagons loaded with spoils, paintings of conquered lands, and captured prisoners in chains; behind him marched his soldiers, singing ribald songs. Tradition held that a slave stood behind the triumphator holding the golden Corona Etrusca above his head and whispering memento mori — "remember you are mortal." The ceremony ended with sacrifices to Jupiter and, typically, the execution of the enemy leader.

Rules and later use

Under the Republic, triumphs were granted by the Senate subject to conditions such as a victory over a foreign enemy with at least 5,000 enemy killed. Under the Empire, only the emperor and his family could triumph. The word passed into general usage for any great victory or achievement, and triumphal arches — such as the Arch of Titus — commemorate individual triumphs.
