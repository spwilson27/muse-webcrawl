Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (6 December 1778 – 9 May 1850) was a French chemist and physicist. He is known mostly for his discovery that water is made of two parts hydrogen and one part oxygen by volume (with Alexander von Humboldt), for two laws related to gases, and for his work on alcohol–water mixtures, the degrees of which are named after him in many countries.

Gas laws

In 1802 Gay-Lussac published the law that bears his name: for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature. In 1808 he announced his law of combining volumes—that gases react in simple whole-number ratios by volume—which provided crucial support for the atomic theory and was later reconciled with it by Avogadro's hypothesis.

Scientific career

Trained at the École Polytechnique, Gay-Lussac made daring balloon ascents in 1804 to collect air samples at high altitude, discovering that the composition of the atmosphere does not vary with height. With Louis Jacques Thénard he isolated boron in 1808 by reducing boric acid with potassium. He also investigated iodine, cyanogen and fermentation, and served as professor at the École Polytechnique and the Sorbonne and as chief assayer of the French mint. Later in life he entered politics, becoming a peer of France. His name is one of the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
