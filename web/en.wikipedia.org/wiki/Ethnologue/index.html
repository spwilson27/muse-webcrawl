Ethnologue

Ethnologue: Languages of the World is an annual reference publication, in print and online, that provides statistics and other information on the living languages of the world. It is published by SIL International (formerly the Summer Institute of Linguistics), a Christian non-profit organisation. First issued in 1951 by Richard S. Pittman, it has grown into one of the most comprehensive catalogues of the world's languages.

Contents

Each entry in Ethnologue typically gives a language's name and alternate names, its ISO 639-3 code, the countries and regions where it is spoken, an estimate of the number of speakers, its classification within a language family, dialects, language development and literacy information, and the status of Bible translation. Languages are also assigned a vitality status on the EGIDS scale (Expanded Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale), running from 0 (international) to 10 (extinct).

The 25th edition (2022) listed 7,159 living languages. The number changes between editions as languages are newly documented, reclassified, or cease to be spoken.

History

The first edition, published in 1951, was a modest ten-page mimeographed list covering 46 languages, compiled to serve missionary linguists. Under editors including Barbara F. Grimes, who oversaw many editions, the work expanded steadily in scope and became a standard reference for linguists, governments, and development agencies. Since 2015 the full database has been published online, with detailed data behind a subscription; summary data remains freely available.

Reception and criticism

Ethnologue is widely used as a starting point for information on little-documented languages, and its ISO 639-3 codes are an international standard. Critics have noted inaccuracies in some speaker estimates, questioned the splitting or lumping of varieties into separate languages, and observed that its publisher's missionary aims can shape its classification choices — for example in counting sign languages and distinguishing languages from dialects. Nevertheless it remains, alongside Glottolog, one of the two principal global language catalogues.
