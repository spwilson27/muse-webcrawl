North End, Boston

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North End is a neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts, United States. It is the city's oldest residential community, where people have lived continuously since it was settled in the 1630s. Though small at about 0.9 km², the neighborhood has nearly 100 restaurants — above all Italian ones — and a wealth of historic sites, making it a popular destination for visitors.

History

The North End was home in the colonial era to the Old North Church (Christ Church, built 1723), from whose steeple Robert Newman hung the signal lanterns on 18 April 1775 that warned of the British march on Lexington and Concord; Paul Revere's house, the oldest surviving house in downtown Boston, stands on North Square. In the 19th and early 20th centuries the district successively housed Irish, Jewish, and then Italian immigrants, becoming Boston's "Little Italy". In January 1919 it was the scene of the Great Molasses Disaster, when a burst storage tank flooded the neighborhood with molasses, killing 21 people.

The neighborhood was cut off from the rest of Boston by the elevated Central Artery highway for half a century; the Big Dig depressed the highway by 2003 and replaced it with the Rose Kennedy Greenway, reconnecting the North End to downtown.

Culture

The North End is known for its Italian American community, its bakeries and cafes on Hanover Street, and its summer "feasts" honoring Catholic saints, the oldest and largest being Saint Anthony's Feast, held since 1919.
