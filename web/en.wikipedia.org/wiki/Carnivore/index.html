Carnivore

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A carnivore is an animal (or plant) that eats primarily the flesh of other animals. As secondary or higher consumers in food webs, carnivores regulate herbivore populations and shape ecosystems. The term also names the mammalian order Carnivora (dogs, cats, bears, weasels and relatives), many — but not all — of which are meat-eaters; pandas, in that order, eat bamboo.

Adaptations

Meat-eating animals typically have sharp teeth or beaks for tearing, strong jaws, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, and speed, stealth or venom for capture; scavengers add keen smell and strong stomach acid. Because energy transfer between trophic levels wastes roughly 90%, carnivores are always rarer than their prey — a constraint on the size and recovery of large predators.

Ecological role

Apex predators such as wolves, lions and sharks exert top-down control: removing them can release herbivores and degrade vegetation (trophic cascades), as famously argued for wolf reintroduction in Yellowstone (1995). Carnivorous plants — Venus flytraps, pitcher plants — invert the pattern, trapping animals for nitrogen in poor soils.
