Australian Aboriginal sign languages

Australian Aboriginal sign languages are the highly developed manual communication systems traditionally used by Aboriginal Australian peoples. Unlike sign languages of the Deaf, they were used primarily by hearing people as an alternative to speech — during periods when speech was forbidden by mourning customs, in ritual contexts, for communication at a distance or while hunting, and with people who did not share a spoken language. Several distinct systems exist, corresponding to different language groups, particularly in Central Australia.

Contexts of use

The most widespread context was the speech taboo imposed on women in mourning (and in some regions on other mourners): a widow might be forbidden to speak for months or years and conduct all communication in sign. Signs were also used in men's and women's ceremonial life, between groups lacking a common language, and silently during hunts. Because sign use was so pervasive, these systems developed large vocabularies — thousands of signs in the most developed systems — and grammar of their own, loosely related to the structure of the local spoken language.

Documented systems

The linguist Adam Kendon documented Central Australian sign languages extensively from the 1970s, recording systems among the Warlpiri, Warumungu, Warlmanpa, Mudburra and Djingili peoples. Yolngu sign language of Arnhem Land and the systems of Cape York are also described. In communities with high rates of deafness, signing was shared by deaf and hearing people alike. Use has declined with the shift to English and the weakening of mourning taboos, but sign remains in use in some communities and is being documented and revitalised.

Relation to other sign languages

Aboriginal sign languages are unrelated to Auslan, the sign language of the Australian Deaf community (which derives from British Sign Language), though deaf Aboriginal people in some regions historically used the local Aboriginal system.
