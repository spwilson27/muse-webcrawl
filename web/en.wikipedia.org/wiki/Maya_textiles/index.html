Maya textiles

Traditional Maya huipiles and woven cloth from the Guatemalan highlands

Maya textiles are the clothing and other textile arts of the Maya peoples, the indigenous peoples of Mexico and Central America. Women have traditionally created textiles in Maya society, and the textiles are worn by both men and women. Textiles are created usually from locally grown cotton, although wool has also been used in some areas since it was introduced by the Spanish during the colonial era.

The Maya have a long history of producing textiles. Maya textiles have been used to create clothing for use in everyday life, as well as for use in ceremonies and rituals. Textiles have also been used as a form of currency, and have been used in trade. The designs and patterns used in Maya textiles often have symbolic meaning, and can represent aspects of the Maya cosmos, such as the sun, the moon, and the stars, as well as animals, plants, and other elements of the natural world.

History

Textile production among the Maya dates back to the pre-Columbian era. Because textiles are perishable, very few ancient Maya textiles have survived in the archaeological record, although depictions of woven garments appear in Maya murals, pottery, and carved monuments. Elite women were shown weaving on backstrap looms, and finely woven cotton cloth was a valuable tribute and trade good in the Maya world.

During the Spanish colonial period, Maya weaving traditions persisted despite pressure to adopt European dress and production methods. Community-specific garment styles became entrenched during this period, so that the design of a woman's huipil could identify her home village. In the modern era, handwoven textiles remain central to Maya cultural identity, particularly in highland Guatemala and in Chiapas, Mexico.

Techniques

The traditional Maya loom is the backstrap loom (also called a hip loom), in which one end of the loom is attached to a post or tree and the other end is held by a strap around the weaver's lower back. Tension is maintained by the weaver leaning back. Ground looms and, after Spanish contact, treadle looms have also been used, particularly for producing larger pieces of cloth.

Weavers use a variety of techniques, including plain weave, brocade (in which supplementary weft threads create raised patterns), embroidery, and tapestry techniques. Natural dyes made from plants, insects such as cochineal, and minerals were traditionally used, although synthetic dyes are now common.

Garments

The most characteristic women's garment is the huipil (also spelled güipil), a loose-fitting tunic made from one or more woven panels, often richly brocaded or embroidered with symbolic designs. Women also wear a wraparound skirt called a corte, held with a woven sash or faja. Men's traditional dress includes woven trousers or shorts, shirts, sashes, and in some communities wool jackets or ponchos.

Each community traditionally had its own distinctive patterns, colours, and designs, so that a person's dress announced their town of origin. In recent decades these community distinctions have begun to blur, although many weavers' cooperatives work to preserve local traditions.

Symbolism

Brocaded motifs on Maya textiles carry deep symbolic associations drawn from Maya cosmology and the natural world: birds, deer, serpents, toads, stars, and the world tree recur frequently. Some designs are interpreted as representations of the cosmos, with the woven cloth itself mirroring the structure of the universe. The act of weaving has itself been linked in Maya thought to creation and to the moon goddess Ixchel, patroness of weaving and childbirth.
