Ethnic violence

Ethnic violence is violence directed against persons or property on the basis of the victims' ethnic identity. It encompasses forms ranging from hate crimes and communal riots to pogroms, ethnic cleansing and genocide. Ethnic violence differs from other organised violence in that the perceived ethnic difference between perpetrators and victims is central to the targeting.

Causes and theories

Scholars debate whether ethnic violence flows from ancient hatreds between groups ("primordialism"), from elite manipulation of ethnic identities for political gain ("instrumentalism"), or from social and institutional identities constructed over time ("constructivism"). Donald Horowitz's influential study of ethnic riots emphasised group status anxiety, rumours and the perceived permissiveness of authorities. James Fearon and David Laitin argued that interethnic peace is actually the norm, and that violence is a breakdown of the mechanisms — information flow and in-group policing — that normally sustain it. Security-dilemma models, borrowed from international relations, explain violence when the collapse of state authority leaves groups fearing attack by their neighbours.

Forms

Common forms include spontaneous or organised riots between communities, pogroms tolerated or encouraged by states, forced displacement ("ethnic cleansing"), and mass killing intended to destroy a group in whole or in part, defined since 1948 as the crime of genocide. Historical examples widely studied include the Armenian genocide, the Holocaust, the Partition violence in India in 1947, the Rwandan genocide of 1994, and the ethnic cleansing campaigns of the Yugoslav wars.

Prevention and resolution

Responses include power-sharing constitutions, federalism, prosecution of incitement and atrocity crimes through tribunals such as the ICTY and ICTR and the International Criminal Court, and local peacebuilding. Early-warning systems monitor hate speech, arms flows and displacement as indicators of escalation.
