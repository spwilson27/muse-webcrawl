Athens in the classical period

Ancient Athens (Greek: Ἀθῆναι, Athênai) was a powerful city-state (polis) of ancient Greece, centred on the rocky Acropolis in Attica. It is famous as the birthplace of democracy, for its role in defeating the Persian invasions, for the empire it led in the 5th century BCE, and for an extraordinary flowering of philosophy, drama, art, and architecture — including Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes — that profoundly shaped Western civilisation.

The Parthenon atop the Acropolis, symbol of classical Athens.

History

Athens was inhabited from Neolithic times and was a Mycenaean centre. After the Bronze Age collapse it gradually unified Attica (the legendary synoecism of Theseus). In 594 BCE Solon's reforms addressed debt and political exclusion; after the tyranny of the Peisistratids, Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/507 BCE laid the foundations of democracy. Athens led the Greek resistance at Marathon (490 BCE) and Salamis (480 BCE), then converted the Delian League into an empire, provoking the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE) against Sparta, which ended in Athenian defeat. Democracy was restored in 403 BCE, and Athens remained a cultural centre under Macedonian and later Roman rule.

Government

Classical Athenian democracy was direct: the Assembly (ekklesia) of all adult male citizens voted on laws and policy; the Council of 500 (boule) prepared business; most offices were filled by lot; and large popular juries staffed the courts. Citizenship excluded women, slaves, and resident aliens (metics). Leaders such as Pericles, who dominated the mid-5th century, wielded influence through election as strategos (general) and force of oratory.

Society and culture

At its height the city and Attica held perhaps 250,000–300,000 people, including tens of thousands of slaves who worked, among other places, the silver mines at Laurion that financed the fleet. The great festivals — the Panathenaea and the Dionysia, with its dramatic competitions — structured the civic year. Public building under Pericles produced the Parthenon, Propylaea, and Erechtheion.
