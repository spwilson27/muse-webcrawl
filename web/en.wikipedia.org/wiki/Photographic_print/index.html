Photographic print

A photographic print is an image produced on light-sensitive paper from a photographic negative or positive, or — in modern usage — a hard-copy output of a digital image on paper. Traditional prints are made by exposing photographic paper through a negative in an enlarger or by contact printing, then processing the paper in a sequence of chemical baths.

A finished photographic print drying in the darkroom.

Processes

Historical print processes include the salted paper print, the albumen print, the gelatin silver print, the platinum print and the carbon print. The gelatin silver process became the standard black-and-white method of the twentieth century. Colour prints from negatives are made by the chromogenic (C-print) process, in which dye couplers in three emulsion layers form cyan, magenta and yellow dye images during development. The RA-4 process is its modern standardised form.

Making a darkroom print

In a typical darkroom workflow the printer selects the paper grade or filtration for contrast, exposes the paper under an enlarger, then develops, stop-baths, fixes and washes the print. Techniques such as dodging and burning — selectively holding back or adding exposure — allow local control of density. Test strips and trial prints are used to judge exposure before a final print is made.

Digital prints

Since the 1990s, prints have increasingly been produced from digital files by inkjet printers, dye-sublimation printers, and laser exposure of conventional colour paper (for example in minilab systems). The term "giclée" is often applied to high-quality inkjet art prints.

Preservation

Print longevity depends on the process and storage conditions. Fibre-based gelatin silver prints that are properly fixed, washed and toned can remain stable for more than a century, while resin-coated and chromogenic prints are generally less stable in the dark. Storage in cool, dry, pollutant-free conditions slows deterioration.
