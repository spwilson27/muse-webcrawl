Quakers are members of the Religious Society of Friends, a Christian movement founded in England in the 1650s by George Fox and fellow seekers who had become dissatisfied with the established church. Early Friends were nicknamed "Quakers" — tradition says because Fox told a magistrate to tremble (quake) at the word of the Lord. Persecuted under the Restoration, they were central to the founding of Pennsylvania under William Penn (1681), granted as a "holy experiment" in religious toleration.

Beliefs and practice

Quakerism centres on the "Inner Light" — that of God in everyone — and, in its unprogrammed tradition, on expectant worship: silent waiting in which any participant may speak as led. Most Friends historically dispensed with outward sacraments, clergy and creeds. The movement's corporate "testimonies" — peace, equality, simplicity, integrity, community and stewardship (often summarised as SPICES) — produced an outsized record of social action: early abolitionism, prison reform (Elizabeth Fry), conscientious objection (recognised partly through Quaker advocacy), relief work through the American Friends Service Committee (founded 1917; Nobel Peace Prize 1947, shared with its British counterpart), and plain speech and dress in earlier centuries.

Diversity today

Modern Friends range from liberal, universalist-leaning unprogrammed meetings (strongest in Britain and the eastern US) to programmed, pastor-led and evangelical Friends churches — which now form the majority of the world's Quakers, concentrated in Kenya and the Americas. Membership worldwide is a few hundred thousand, far outweighed by the movement's historical and cultural influence.
