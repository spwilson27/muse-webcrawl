Möbius inversion formula

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the classical Möbius inversion formula is a relation between pairs of arithmetic functions, each defined from the other by sums over divisors. It was introduced into number theory in 1832 by August Ferdinand Möbius.

A large generalization of this formula applies to summation over an arbitrary locally finite partially ordered set, with Möbius' classical formula applying to the set of natural numbers ordered by divisibility: see incidence algebra.

Statement of the formula

The classical version states that if g(n) and f(n) are arithmetic functions satisfying

g(n) = Σd | n f(d)   for every integer n ≥ 1

then

f(n) = Σd | n μ(d) g(n/d)   for every integer n ≥ 1

where μ is the Möbius function and the sums extend over all positive divisors d of n (with the conventional notation that n/d is the quotient of n by d). Written in the notation of the Dirichlet convolution, the first equation is often written as

g = f ∗ 1

where ∗ denotes the Dirichlet convolution, and 1 is the constant function 1(n) = 1. The second equation is then written as

f = g ∗ μ.

The equation shows that μ is the convolution inverse of 1, and in the ring of arithmetic functions under Dirichlet convolution these are the two most basic relations of this kind.

Series relations

Let

an = Σd | n bd   then   bn = Σd | n μ(d) an/d.

The transform is an involution: applying it twice returns the original sequence. The corresponding relation for Dirichlet series follows by multiplication by 1/ζ(s), where ζ is the Riemann zeta function.

Multiplicative functions

In the context of multiplicative functions, every completely multiplicative function f defines an arithmetic function by summation, and the inversion is still given by convolution with μ. An example is the relation between Euler's totient function φ and the identity function: n = Σd | n φ(d), from which φ = id ∗ μ.

Proofs

The proof of the classical formula uses the fact that Σd | n μ(d) equals 1 if n = 1 and 0 otherwise, together with rearrangement of the double sum. A slicker proof observes that the formula is equivalent to μ ∗ 1 = ε in the Dirichlet convolution ring, where ε is the multiplicative identity concentrated at n = 1.

Applications

  • Deriving the formula φ(n) = n Πp | n (1 − 1/p) for Euler's totient function.
  • Counting the number of necklaces and aperiodic words in combinatorics.
  • The inclusion–exclusion principle is a special case of Möbius inversion on the Boolean lattice of subsets.
  • In physics and combinatorics, inversion on locally finite posets underlies Rota's theory of incidence algebras.

Generalizations

Other Möbius inversion formulas occur throughout mathematics: the Weisner theorem, inversion in incidence algebras of partially ordered sets, and the Fourier transform viewed as a kind of inversion. The theory of umbral calculus also contains inversion formulas of this type.
