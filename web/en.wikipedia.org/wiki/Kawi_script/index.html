Kawi script

The Kawi script (from Sanskrit kavi, "poet") is a Brahmic abugida script that was used across much of Maritime Southeast Asia — Java, Bali, Lombok, Sumatra and the Philippines — from roughly the 8th to the 16th century. It was the vehicle of the Old Javanese (Kawi) language and is the ancestor of traditional Indonesian scripts including Javanese (Hanacaraka), Balinese, Sundanese, and Philippine scripts such as Baybayin.

History

Kawi descends from the Pallava script of South India, transmitted through trade and the spread of Hinduism and Buddhism, itself deriving from Brahmi. The earliest Kawi inscriptions in Java date to the 8th century, in the era of the Mataram kingdom. Over centuries of use on stone, copper plates and palm leaf, the script developed local forms, and scribes added diacritics to represent sounds of Old Javanese and Sanskrit loanwords precisely, including aspirated and retroflex consonants.

Classic literary Kawi flourished in the courts of East Java (Kediri, Singhasari and Majapahit), producing the great kakawin poems such as the Arjunawiwaha, the Sutasoma and the Nagarakretagama. With Islamization and later European colonization the script gave way regionally — to Jawi and Latin in many areas — while Bali and Java preserved daughter scripts in continuous use.

Characteristics

Like other Brahmic scripts, Kawi is an abugida: each consonant letter carries an inherent vowel /a/, modified by dependent vowel signs, and a virama (pangkon) cancels the vowel; consonant clusters are written with subjoined forms. Punctuation marks frame texts on copper-plate charters, which record land grants (sīma) with elaborate legal curses.

Descendants and encoding

Regional descendants include the Javanese, Balinese, Buginese (Lontara), Batak and Philippine scripts; the Laguna Copperplate Inscription of 900 CE, the earliest dated Philippine document, is written in a Kawi-related script. The script was added to the Unicode Standard in version 15.0 (2023), enabling digital encoding of Kawi texts.
