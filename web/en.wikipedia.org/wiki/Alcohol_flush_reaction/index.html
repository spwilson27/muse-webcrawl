Alcohol flush reaction

Alcohol flush reaction is a condition in which a person develops flushes or blotches associated with erythema on the face, neck, shoulders, ears, and in some cases, the entire body after consuming alcoholic beverages. The reaction is the result of an accumulation of acetaldehyde, a metabolic byproduct of the catabolic metabolism of alcohol (ethanol).

Cause

The flush reaction is caused by a deficiency in the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), which normally converts acetaldehyde into acetate. The ALDH2*2 allele encoding the deficient enzyme is common in people of East Asian descent, which is why the condition is sometimes informally called "Asian flush" or "Asian glow". Estimates suggest roughly 30–50% of East Asians carry at least one copy of the allele.

Symptoms

Symptoms include facial redness and warmth, a rapid heartbeat, nausea, headache and general discomfort. The reaction typically begins within minutes of drinking.

Health implications

Because acetaldehyde is a carcinogen, people with ALDH2 deficiency who drink regularly face a significantly elevated risk of oesophageal cancer. The flushing response is unpleasant enough that it tends to discourage heavy drinking, and populations with high rates of the deficiency have historically had lower rates of alcoholism; however, social pressure to drink can override this protective effect.
