Tax wedge

The tax wedge is the difference between what labour costs an employer and what the employee takes home, expressed as a share of total labour cost. It includes income tax plus employee and employer social security contributions, minus cash benefits such as family allowances. The concept is used by the OECD in its annual Taxing Wages publication to compare the burden on employment across countries.

Measurement

The wedge is usually calculated for a single worker on the average wage and for families at various income levels. In recent OECD data the average wedge for a single average worker was roughly 35%, ranging from under 25% in countries such as Chile and Colombia and about 30% in the United States, to around 50% or more in Belgium, Germany, France and Austria. Family wedges are generally lower because of child benefits and joint provisions.

Economics

A tax wedge, like any tax placed between buyer and seller, drives a gap between the price paid and the price received; in labour markets it may reduce employment or hours, depress net wages, or encourage informal work, depending on the elasticities of labour supply and demand and on the value workers place on the social insurance the contributions buy. Because employer and employee shares are economically equivalent in the long run, economists treat the combined wedge — not its statutory split — as what matters. Policy debates over "making work pay", in-work credits and minimum wages are framed in terms of reducing wedges at the bottom of the earnings distribution, where they can interact with benefit withdrawal to create very high effective marginal tax rates.
