Portuguese cuisine

Portuguese cuisine is the food tradition of Portugal, shaped by the country's Atlantic coastline, its Mediterranean climate, and its history as a maritime and colonial power. It is characterized by hearty use of seafood, olive oil, bread, soups, grilled meats, and an exceptional repertoire of egg-based convent sweets. Portugal's Age of Discovery spread ingredients — chili peppers, tomatoes, potatoes, tea, and spices — around the world, while Portuguese techniques such as tempura (via Japan) travelled in the other direction.

Salt cod and seafood

Bacalhau (salted, dried cod) is the emblematic ingredient, with a traditional claim of 365 recipes — one for each day of the year. Famous preparations include Bacalhau à Brás (shredded cod with matchstick potatoes and egg), Bacalhau com natas, and grilled bacalhau with peppers. Grilled sardines are the centerpiece of Lisbon's Festas de Santo António in June. Other staples are octopus (polvo à lagareiro), cataplana seafood stews from the Algarve, and açorda bread soups.

Meats and hearty dishes

Pork dominates: roast suckling pig (leitão) from Bairrada, Alentejo black pork, bifana pork sandwiches, and the Porto francesinha — a stacked meat sandwich drowned in beer-and-tomato sauce and melted cheese. Cozido à portuguesa is the national boiled dinner of meats, sausages, and vegetables.

Sweets

Portuguese conventual sweets (doces conventuais) grew from egg yolks left over after whites were used to clarify wine and starch habits. The most famous is the pastel de nata, the custard tart created by monks at Lisbon's Jerónimos Monastery and sold since 1837 by the Pastéis de Belém bakery.

Wine

Portugal produces Port and Madeira fortified wines, Vinho Verde, and Douro and Alentejo table wines; the Douro Valley is among the world's oldest demarcated wine regions (1756).
