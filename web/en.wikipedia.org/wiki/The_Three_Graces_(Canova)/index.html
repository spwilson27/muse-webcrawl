The Three Graces (Canova)

ArtistAntonio Canova
Years1813–1816 (first version)
MediumMarble
LocationsHermitage Museum; V&A / National Gallery of Scotland

The Three Graces is a Neoclassical marble sculpture by the Italian artist Antonio Canova depicting the three Charites of Greek mythology — Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia, daughters of Zeus, said to represent beauty, charm and joy. Canova carved two versions.

Versions

The first version was commissioned by Empress Joséphine, Napoleon's former wife, in 1813 and completed after her death; it passed to her son Eugène de Beauharnais and is now in the Hermitage Museum, Saint Petersburg. A second version was commissioned by John Russell, 6th Duke of Bedford, after he saw the first in Canova's studio; completed in 1817, it differs slightly in details such as the pillar's form and the garland. After a celebrated public campaign in the 1990s to keep it in Britain, it was jointly acquired by the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and the National Galleries of Scotland, between which it alternates.

Artistry

The Graces stand close together, arms entwined, heads inclined in a single flowing group — a deliberate contrast to the stiff, side-by-side arrangement of earlier versions of the subject. Canova's famously soft, flesh-like treatment of marble, achieved through fine abrasion and a light wax finish, made the work the definitive Neoclassical treatment of the theme.
