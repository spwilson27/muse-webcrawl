Waterhole

TypeNatural or artificial depression holding water
Also known asWater hole, water-hole; gnamma (Australia); billabong (in some contexts)
Ecological roleDrinking water for wildlife in arid and seasonal environments

A waterhole (or water hole) is a depression in the ground, either natural or artificial, in which water collects and from which animals may drink. Waterholes are a vital resource in arid and seasonally dry landscapes such as savannas, deserts and the Australian outback, where they may be the only source of surface water for long periods. They form gathering points for wildlife, including both prey animals and the predators that hunt them.

Formation

Natural waterholes occur where impermeable rock or clay lies close to the surface — in weathered granite hollows (in Australia called gnammas), in deflation basins scoured by wind, along the beds of ephemeral rivers, and in solution hollows in limestone. Many are filled by rain and dry out progressively during the dry season, shrinking to muddy remnants before disappearing entirely. Artificial waterholes, such as bore-fed troughs and pumped pans in African game reserves and Australian pastoral districts, supplement natural supplies for livestock and wildlife.

Ecology

Because water is scarce, waterholes concentrate animal activity. Herbivores must visit regularly to drink, making waterholes focal points for predator–prey interactions and for the transfer of nutrients and seeds between the aquatic margin and the surrounding landscape. Heavy trampling creates a distinctive grazed and compacted "piosphere" around heavily used holes. In dry periods competition at shrinking waterholes can be intense, and carcasses and dung around them enrich the sediment with nutrients, supporting algae, invertebrates and amphibians when rains return.

In culture

Waterholes feature prominently in Aboriginal Australian traditions, where many are sacred sites associated with Dreaming stories and songlines, and in the folklore of the American West and southern Africa, where they were landmarks for travellers and stock routes. The term has also been borrowed metaphorically: the "water hole" is a band of quiet radio frequencies between the hydrogen and hydroxyl spectral lines, proposed in SETI research as a logical channel for interstellar communication, taking its name from the idea of a meeting place where species gather.
