Mitzvah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Reading from a Torah scroll]

In Judaism, a mitzvah (Hebrew: מִצְוָה, miṣvā, plural mitzvot) is a commandment — a religious duty or precept commanded by God. In the Torah there are traditionally counted 613 mitzvot, comprising 248 positive commandments ("you shall") and 365 negative commandments ("you shall not"). In colloquial modern usage, especially in English, mitzvah often refers more loosely to any good deed or act of kindness.

Meaning

The word derives from the Hebrew root ṣ-v-h, "to command". In the Hebrew Bible it denotes a command of God to an individual or to the people of Israel, and by extension the deed performed in fulfilment of the command. Rabbinic tradition treats the mitzvot as the framework of Jewish life and law (halakha), binding the community in covenant with God.

The 613 commandments

The enumeration of 613 (Hebrew: תרי״ג מצוות, taryag mitzvot) is attributed to the 3rd-century sage Rabbi Simlai in the Talmud (Makkot 23b–24a), which notes that 613 is the numerical value of the word Torah plus the two commandments heard directly from God. Medieval authorities produced influential lists, most famously Maimonides in his Sefer ha-Mitzvot, whose ordering and criteria were debated by Nachmanides and others. The 248 positive commandments were traditionally correlated with the parts of the human body and the 365 negative ones with the days of the solar year.

Many of the 613 — those concerning the Temple in Jerusalem, sacrifices, and the agricultural laws of the Land of Israel — cannot be performed since the Temple's destruction in 70 CE; a commonly cited figure is that roughly 270–300 remain applicable today, depending on where one lives.

Rabbinic mitzvot

Beyond the commandments counted as biblical, the sages instituted additional observances, traditionally numbered as seven rabbinic mitzvot, including reciting blessings, washing hands before bread, lighting Hanukkah candles, reading the Scroll of Esther on Purim, reciting Hallel, and observing eruv. Commandments also developed through custom (minhag).

Performance and significance

Denominations differ in their approach: Orthodox Judaism regards the mitzvot as binding divine law; Conservative Judaism also treats halakha as binding but historically developing; Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism emphasise ethical commandments and individual autonomy in ritual observance. The concept of kavanah (intention) is debated — whether a mitzvah performed without conscious intent is valid. Jewish tradition also ascribes reward for performing mitzvot, while many authorities stress that they should be done for their own sake.

Bar and bat mitzvah

A boy who reaches age 13 and a girl who reaches 12 or 13 (depending on denomination) become bar mitzvah or bat mitzvah — "son/daughter of the commandment" — personally responsible for observing the mitzvot, an occasion marked by a synagogue ceremony and celebration.
