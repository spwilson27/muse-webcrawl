Flashtube

A flashtube (also called a flashlamp) is an electric arc lamp designed to produce extremely intense, incoherent, full-spectrum white light for a very short duration. The tube is made of glass or quartz with electrodes at either end, and is filled with a gas, typically xenon, that becomes conducting and emits light when a large current is passed through it.

History

The flashtube was developed in the 1930s by Harold Edgerton at MIT for use in high-speed photography and stroboscopy, replacing older flash powders and flashbulbs. His "strobotac" and related devices made it possible to photograph phenomena lasting only microseconds.

Applications

Flashtubes are used in photographic flash units and strobe lights, in laser pumping (notably for ruby and Nd:YAG lasers), in optical beacons and obstruction lights, in photocopiers and document scanners, in medical and cosmetic devices for intense pulsed light (IPL) treatment, and in some disinfection systems. Krypton-filled tubes are used where a spectrum closer to sunlight is not needed and efficiency in the near-infrared is preferred, particularly for pumping neodymium lasers.

Operation

A flashtube circuit stores energy in a large capacitor, which is discharged through the tube after a high-voltage trigger pulse ionises the gas. Pulse durations range from microseconds to milliseconds, with peak powers reaching megawatts. Lifetimes are limited by electrode sputtering and solarisation of the tube wall, and tubes can explode violently if overdriven.
