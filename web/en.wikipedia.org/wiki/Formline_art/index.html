Formline art

Formline art is the distinctive two-dimensional design system of the Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast of North America, including the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Heiltsuk, and Kwakwaka'wakw. It is used to depict crest figures, animals, and supernatural beings on house fronts, totem poles, boxes, masks, textiles, and regalia.

Elements

Formline designs are built from a small vocabulary of shapes described by anthropologist Bill Holm in his influential 1965 study Northwest Coast Indian Art: An Analysis of Form: the formline itself — a flowing, tapering black (later also red) line that defines the primary forms — along with ovoids, U-forms, and split U-forms that articulate joints, eyes, feathers, and other features. The primary formlines are typically black, secondary elements red, and tertiary details blue-green in classic northern formline.

Contemporary practice

After decades of suppression under colonial policies, formline art revived strongly from the mid-twentieth century, with artists such as Bill Reid and Robert Davidson achieving international recognition. Today formline is a living tradition taught in Indigenous communities and art schools, and contemporary artists continue to adapt it to new media.
