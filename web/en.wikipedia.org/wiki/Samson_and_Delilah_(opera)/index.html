Samson and Delilah (French: Samson et Dalila), Op. 47, is a grand opera in three acts and four scenes by Camille Saint-Saëns to a French libretto by Ferdinand Lemaire. Based on the biblical tale of Samson and Delilah from the Book of Judges, it is the composer's most frequently performed opera.

Composition and premiere

Saint-Saëns began the work in 1868, initially conceiving it as an oratorio. Encouraged by Franz Liszt, he completed it as an opera, and Liszt conducted the premiere at the Grossherzogliches Theater in Weimar in 1877, in German translation. French theatres, wary of biblical subjects on stage, delayed production; the opera was first heard in France at Rouen in 1890 and reached the Paris Opéra in 1892, after which it entered the international repertory.

Synopsis

In Gaza, Samson leads the Hebrews against the Philistines. The Philistine seductress Delilah, urged by the High Priest of Dagon, lures Samson to her valley dwelling and discovers that his strength lies in his uncut hair. While he sleeps she has it shorn; Samson is captured and blinded. In the final scene, led into the temple of Dagon to be mocked, Samson prays for the return of his strength and pulls down the temple pillars, killing himself and his enemies.

Music

The opera is famous for Delilah's seductive arias, above all "Mon cœur s'ouvre à ta voix" and "Printemps qui commence", and for the Bacchanale of Act 3. Saint-Saëns' score combines French lyricism with orientalist colour, monumental choruses and elaborate ballet in the grand opera tradition.
