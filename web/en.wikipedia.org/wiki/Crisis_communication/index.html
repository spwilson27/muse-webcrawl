Crisis communication

Crisis communication is a sub-specialty of the public relations profession that is designed to protect and defend an individual, company, or organization facing a public challenge to its reputation. The field studies how organisations communicate before, during and after a crisis in order to minimise damage, maintain stakeholder trust and resume operations.

Approaches and theory

Scholarly approaches include W. Timothy Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT), which matches response strategies — denial, diminishment, rebuilding and bolstering — to the level of responsibility stakeholders attribute to the organisation, and William Benoit's image repair theory, which catalogues strategies such as denial, evasion of responsibility, reducing offensiveness, corrective action and mortification.

Practice

Effective practice generally involves crisis preparedness planning, designation of spokespeople, rapid first response, honesty and transparency, instructing information for public safety, adjusting information that expresses concern, and consistent messaging across channels. Social media has compressed response times, with the first hours of a crisis widely treated as decisive. Frequently cited case studies include the 1982 Tylenol tampering response by Johnson & Johnson, often praised for openness and consumer-safety action, and poorly handled responses blamed for prolonging reputational harm in major oil spills and product recalls.
