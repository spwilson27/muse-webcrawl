The SDP–Liberal Alliance was a centre political alliance in the United Kingdom between the newly formed Social Democratic Party (SDP) and the Liberal Party, in existence from 1981 until the two parties merged in 1988 to form the Social and Liberal Democrats, soon renamed the Liberal Democrats. At its peak in late 1981 the Alliance led national opinion polls with ratings above 50 per cent, threatening to "break the mould" of British two-party politics.

Formation

The SDP was founded in March 1981 by the "Gang of Four" — Roy Jenkins, David Owen, Bill Rodgers and Shirley Williams — Labour moderates who broke with their party over Europe and defence policy. Liberal leader David Steel negotiated an electoral pact: the two parties would not stand against each other, would agree joint candidates, and would campaign on a shared programme under joint leadership.

Elections

At the 1983 general election the Alliance won 25.4 per cent of the national vote — only two points behind Labour — but, because its vote was evenly spread under first-past-the-post, only 23 seats. The result was simultaneously a breakthrough and a demonstration of the electoral system's bias against third parties. Internal tensions grew, especially over defence: Owen's SDP favoured retaining a nuclear deterrent while many Liberals leaned toward disarmament, frictions visible during the 1987 campaign, which returned 22 seats on 22.6 per cent of the vote.

Merger

After 1987, Steel and most SDP members pushed for a full merger, achieved in March 1988 despite Owen leading a continuing rump SDP. Under Paddy Ashdown the merged party became the Liberal Democrats.
