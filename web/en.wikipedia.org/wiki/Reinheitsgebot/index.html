The Reinheitsgebot (German pronunciation: [ˈʁaɪn.haɪtsɡəˌboːt], literally "purity order") is a series of regulations limiting the ingredients in beer in Germany and the states of the former Holy Roman Empire. The best-known version, the Bavarian purity law of 1516, restricted beer ingredients to water, barley, and hops; yeast was added to the list after its role in fermentation was discovered. The decree is often cited as one of the oldest food-quality regulations still invoked today, and 23 April, the day it was issued, is celebrated in Germany as the Day of German Beer.

1516 Bavarian law

The law was issued on 23 April 1516 in Ingolstadt by the Bavarian dukes William IV and Ludwig X. It stipulated that beer could be made only from water, barley and hops, and it also fixed prices: from Michaelmas to Georgi (23 April), a litre was not to cost more than one Pfennig, and from Georgi to Michaelmas no more than two Pfennigs. Brewing was further restricted seasonally — from Georgi to Michaelmas beer could not be brewed at all, an early cold-chain rule that favoured bottom fermentation in cool months.

"...we wish that in future, in all cities, markets and in the countryside, no other ingredients than barley, hops and water be used for beer..."

Purposes

Historians debate the law's motives. It protected consumers from adulterants that brewers had used for flavouring and preservation — including soot, herbs, and in some accounts toxic plants. It also served economic goals: reserving wheat and rye for bakers helped keep bread prices stable and bread available, which is why wheat beers were long a princely monopoly in Bavaria. The price caps and ingredient rules together also appear to have been a response to poor harvests and spoiled beer that had caused public discontent.

Spread and modern law

The Bavarian rule spread through German states and was adopted across the German Empire in 1906. In the modern Federal Republic it lives on in the Vorläufiges Biergesetz (Provisional Beer Law) of 1993, which permits a somewhat wider set of malts and, for top-fermented beers, certain sugars, but still restricts bottom-fermented beer to malt, hops, yeast and water. A 1987 European Court of Justice ruling held that Germany could not bar imported beers made with other ingredients from being sold as beer, though domestic brewers advertising compliance continue to treat the Reinheitsgebot as a mark of quality, and German brewers' associations have sought UNESCO recognition for it as intangible cultural heritage.
