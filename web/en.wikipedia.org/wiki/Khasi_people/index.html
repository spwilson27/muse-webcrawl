Khasi people

The Khasi people are an ethnic group of Meghalaya in north-eastern India, with a significant population in the bordering state of Assam and in parts of Bangladesh. They speak Khasi, an Austroasiatic language distantly related to Khmer and Vietnamese — the northernmost Austroasiatic speech community — and are noted for their matrilineal social system, in which descent, inheritance and clan membership pass through women.

Distribution and language

Numbering around 1.4 million, the Khasi inhabit the Khasi Hills of central Meghalaya, centred on the state capital Shillong, together with related groups such as the Pnar (Jaintia) of the Jaintia Hills and the Bhoi and Lyngngam. Khasi is written in a Latin-based script introduced by the Welsh missionary Thomas Jones in 1841 and is one of the few Austroasiatic languages of South Asia.

Society and matriliny

Khasi society is organised into exogamous matrilineal clans (kur). Children take their mother's clan name; ancestral property is held by the women of the family and passes to the youngest daughter (ka khadduh), who acts as custodian of the family home and cares for aged parents, while maternal uncles traditionally exercise authority in family affairs. Marriage is generally monogamous, divorce is relatively easy, and post-marital residence is traditionally matrilocal. The matrilineal system has attracted worldwide scholarly attention and periodic internal debate about reform.

Religion and culture

The traditional Khasi religion, Ka Niam Khasi, venerates a creator god, U Blei, and ancestral spirits, with rituals conducted by priests (lyngdoh). From the 1840s Welsh Calvinistic Methodist missionaries converted a large proportion of the population, and today the majority of Khasi are Christians — Presbyterian, Catholic and others — while a minority maintain the indigenous faith. Khasi culture is famed for the living root bridges of the southern hills, woven from the aerial roots of rubber fig trees, for festivals such as Shad Suk Mynsiem (the "dance of peaceful hearts") performed by women, and for a rich oral literature of folktales and origin myths.

History

The Khasi hills were governed by a confederation of small chiefdoms ruled by syiems. The British annexed the region in the 19th century after the Anglo-Khasi War (1829–1833) led by the resistance hero U Tirot Sing. After Indian independence, the hill districts joined Assam and then, following a sustained autonomy movement, became part of the new state of Meghalaya ("abode of the clouds") in 1972. The Khasi are a Scheduled Tribe, with autonomous district councils under the Sixth Schedule of the Indian Constitution.
