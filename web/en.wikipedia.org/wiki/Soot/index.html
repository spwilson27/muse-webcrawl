Soot

Soot
Composition: Amorphous carbon, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons
Source: Incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons
Health effects: Respiratory and cardiovascular disease; carcinogenic
Climate effect: Warming (black carbon)

Soot is a mass of impure carbon particles resulting from the incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons such as coal, wood, oil and diesel fuel. It consists largely of amorphous carbon and condensed organic compounds, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).

Formation and sources

Soot forms in fuel-rich flames where there is insufficient oxygen for complete combustion to carbon dioxide and water. Major sources include diesel engines, residential wood burning, coal power plants, forest fires, candles and cooking fires. Historically, soot accumulation in chimneys created fire hazards and the occupational disease chimney sweeps' carcinoma, identified by Percivall Pott in 1775 — one of the earliest recognised links between occupation and cancer.

Health and environment

As a component of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), soot penetrates deep into the lungs and bloodstream and is associated with respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease and lung cancer; the WHO classifies outdoor air pollution and diesel exhaust as carcinogenic. Deposited on snow and ice, soot (black carbon) reduces albedo and accelerates melting, making it a significant short-lived climate pollutant.

Uses

Deliberately manufactured soot — carbon black — is produced at industrial scale as a reinforcing filler in tyres and rubber, and as a pigment in inks and paints.
