Stent

In medicine, a stent is a metal or plastic tube inserted into the lumen of an anatomic vessel or duct to keep the passageway open, and stenting is the placement of a stent. The word originates with Charles Stent, a 19th-century English dentist whose name was given to a dental impression compound and later generalized.

Coronary stents

The most common use is in coronary arteries narrowed by atherosclerosis, where a stent is deployed during percutaneous coronary intervention (angioplasty) to scaffold the vessel open after balloon dilation. Bare-metal stents were introduced in the late 1980s — the first, the Wallstent, and the Palmaz–Schatz stent, were landmark devices. Drug-eluting stents, coated with polymers releasing antiproliferative drugs such as sirolimus or paclitaxel, greatly reduced restenosis (re-narrowing from neointimal growth) compared with bare-metal stents, at the cost of requiring prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy to prevent stent thrombosis.

Other uses

Stents are also used in peripheral arteries, the carotid and renal arteries, the biliary tract, the oesophagus, the colon, the trachea and bronchi, and the ureters (ureteric stents). Vascular stents may be balloon-expandable or self-expanding (often nitinol). Bioresorbable scaffolds, designed to dissolve after the vessel heals, have been investigated as a further evolution.

Risks

Complications include restenosis, stent thrombosis, and the bleeding risks of accompanying antiplatelet medication.
