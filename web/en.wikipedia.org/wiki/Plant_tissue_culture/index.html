Plant tissue culture

Plant tissue culture is the growth of plant cells, tissues, or organs in sterile conditions on a defined nutrient medium, exploiting the totipotency of plant cells — the capacity of a single cell to regenerate a whole plant, an idea developed from Gottlieb Haberlandt's experiments in 1902. It is a foundational technique of plant biotechnology and commercial horticulture.

Techniques

Explants (pieces of leaf, stem, root, or meristem) are surface-sterilised and placed on media such as Murashige and Skoog medium (1962), containing minerals, sugars, vitamins, and plant hormones. The balance of auxin and cytokinin determines development — famously demonstrated by Skoog and Miller: high auxin favours root formation, high cytokinin favours shoots, and intermediate ratios produce undifferentiated callus. Cultures are kept under controlled light and temperature. Major techniques include meristem culture (to eliminate viruses and produce clean stock), embryo rescue, anther and pollen culture (to produce haploid and doubled-haploid breeding lines), protoplast culture and fusion, and somatic embryogenesis. In micropropagation, shoots are multiplied through repeated subculture, rooted, and hardened off, yielding thousands of uniform plants from one parent in months.

Applications

Tissue culture underlies the commercial production of orchids, bananas, potatoes, and many ornamentals; the conservation of rare and endangered species; the production of secondary metabolites and pharmaceuticals in cell cultures; and plant genetic engineering, in which transformed cells are regenerated into transgenic plants. Its costs and the risk of somaclonal variation — genetic changes arising in culture — are its main limitations.
