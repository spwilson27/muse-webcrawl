DNA profiling (also called DNA fingerprinting) is a forensic and identification technique that compares specific regions of DNA that vary greatly between individuals — in modern practice, the numbers of short tandem repeats (STRs) at a standard set of loci. Because unrelated people almost never share a full profile, the method can identify individuals from tiny samples of blood, hair, skin or semen with very high confidence.

History

The technique was developed in 1984 by geneticist Sir Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, who noticed that minisatellite repeat patterns could produce individual-specific patterns. It was first used forensically in 1986 in the investigation of the murders of two Leicestershire schoolgirls, which both exonerated an innocent suspect and identified Colin Pitchfork as the killer — the first person convicted with DNA evidence.

Method

Modern profiling amplifies a standard panel of STR loci by PCR, separates the fragments by capillary electrophoresis and records allele lengths. Profiles are stored in national databases such as CODIS in the United States and the UK National DNA Database, allowing crime-scene samples to be matched against offenders. Statistical analysis expresses the strength of a match as a likelihood ratio based on population allele frequencies.

Applications and issues

Beyond criminal justice, DNA profiling is used in paternity and immigration disputes, identifying disaster and war victims, wildlife forensics and historical investigations. It has raised concerns about database scope and retention, familial searching, contamination and the reliability of complex low-template mixtures, while also exonerating many wrongly convicted people through projects such as the Innocence Project.
