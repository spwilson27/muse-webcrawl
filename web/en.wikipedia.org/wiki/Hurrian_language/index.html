Hurrian language

Hurrian is an extinct language of the Hurrians (Ḫurri), a people of the ancient Near East who established the kingdom of Mitanni in northern Mesopotamia and Syria. It is attested from roughly 2300 BCE until the collapse of the Mitanni state around 1000 BCE. Hurrian is an agglutinative language and, together with Urartian, forms the small Hurro-Urartian language family, unrelated to the neighbouring Semitic, Indo-European, and Sumerian languages.

Corpus

The most important Hurrian text is a long letter from King Tushratta of Mitanni to Pharaoh Amenhotep III, preserved among the Amarna letters in Egypt. Other sources include texts from Hattusa (the Hittite capital, where Hurrian religious literature was extensively copied), Ugarit, Mari, and Nuzi. The famous "Hurrian songs" from Ugarit (c. 1400 BCE), written in cuneiform with musical notation, include the Hymn to Nikkal, among the oldest substantially complete pieces of notated music known.

Grammar

Hurrian is characterised by ergative alignment: the subject of an intransitive verb and the object of a transitive verb share the absolutive case, while the transitive subject takes the ergative. Word order is typically subject–object–verb. Nouns take long chains of suffixes expressing case, number, and possession; verbs are formed with sequences of derivational and inflectional suffixes. Hurrian also shows extensive use of suffixaufnahme (case agreement spreading through a noun phrase).
