Cinema of Mexico

The cinema of Mexico is one of the oldest and most important film industries in the Spanish-speaking world. Mexican cinema dates to the arrival of the cinematograph in 1896, and the country became a dominant producer of Spanish-language film during its Golden Age in the 1930s–1950s, exporting movies across Latin America and Spain.

A Mexico City cinema during the Golden Age of Mexican film.

Golden Age

The Época de Oro (Golden Age) is generally dated from 1936, with the success of Allá en el Rancho Grande, to the late 1950s. Stars such as Pedro Infante, Jorge Negrete, María Félix, Dolores del Río, and Cantinflas (Mario Moreno) became icons across the Hispanic world. Directors included Fernando de Fuentes, Emilio "El Indio" Fernández — whose María Candelaria (1944) won the Grand Prix at Cannes — and the Spanish exile Luis Buñuel, who made Los olvidados (1950) in Mexico. Cinematographer Gabriel Figueroa defined the era's visual style.

Decline and New Mexican Cinema

By the 1960s and 1970s the industry declined amid competition from television, low-budget genre production, and state interference. A revival — the "Nuevo Cine Mexicano" — began in the 1990s with films such as Como agua para chocolate (1992), and gained international momentum with Alejandro González Iñárritu's Amores perros (2000), Alfonso Cuarón's Y tu mamá también (2001), and Guillermo del Toro's Pan's Labyrinth (2006). Cuarón, Iñárritu, and del Toro — the "Three Amigos" — went on to win multiple Academy Awards, and Cuarón's Roma (2018) won three Oscars including Best Director.

Industry today

Mexico produces well over a hundred feature films annually in recent years, supported by institutions such as IMCINE and the Cineteca Nacional in Mexico City. Major festivals include the Morelia International Film Festival and the Guadalajara International Film Festival.
