Fritz Lang

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Fritz Lang in the 1920s, monocle in place, directing Metropolis: the monocled master of Weimar cinema at the camera]

Friedrich Christian Anton 'Fritz' Lang (5 December 1890 – 2 August 1976) was an Austrian-born German-American film director, screenwriter and producer, a master of both Weimar cinema and Hollywood film noir. His German masterpieces — Dr. Mabuse the Gambler (1922), Die Nibelungen (1924), Metropolis (1927) and M (1931) — defined expressionist and dystopian cinema; fleeing the Nazis, he made in America Fury (1936), You Only Live Once (1937), The Big Heat (1953) and two dozen more films of fate and paranoia.

Lang's Vienna childhood, wounds in the First World War, and work at UFA under Erich Pommer formed him; his wife Thea von Harbou scripted the German classics. M, his first sound film, cast Peter Lorre as a child murderer hunted by Berlin's underworld and remains among the most influential thrillers ever made.

Flight from Germany

Lang's famous account — offered the leadership of Nazi film by Goebbels in 1933, he fled to Paris that night — is doubted in its details by historians, but the flight was real: his films were banned, von Harbou (who joined the Nazi Party) divorced him, and by 1936 he was in Hollywood. There his outsider's eye made film noir's most fatalistic entries, though studio battles dogged him to the end of his American career in 1956.

Late work and standing

Returns to Germany (the late Mabuse films, The Thousand Eyes of Dr. Mabuse, 1960) and a self-portrait as the director in Godard's Contempt (1963) closed his career. The Cahiers generation canonised him: Lang's geometry of doom — grids, mirrors, clocks, the Mabuse network as prototype of the modern conspiracy film — is foundational to cinema's language.
