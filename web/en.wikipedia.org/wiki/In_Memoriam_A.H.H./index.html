In Memoriam A.H.H.

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alfred Tennyson wrote the poem over seventeen years after the death of Arthur Henry Hallam.

In Memoriam A.H.H. is a long poem by the English poet Alfred Tennyson, completed in 1849 and published in 1850. It is a requiem for the poet's beloved friend Arthur Henry Hallam (1811–1833), who died suddenly of a cerebral haemorrhage in Vienna at the age of twenty-two. The poem was an immediate success and helped secure Tennyson's appointment as Poet Laureate in 1850, succeeding Wordsworth.

Structure and form

The poem consists of a prologue, 131 cantos of widely varying length, and an epilogue celebrating the wedding of Tennyson's sister. Each stanza is a quatrain of iambic tetrameter rhyming ABBA — the enclosing "In Memoriam stanza" — in which the outer lines frame and, critics have said, "hug" the inner pair, enacting the poem's movement of grief returning upon itself.

Themes

Written over seventeen years, the poem traces a slow progress from despair toward a qualified faith. It is famous for its confrontation with Victorian science: geology's evidence of extinction and deep time ("Nature, red in tooth and claw") tests the poet's belief in a loving God and in personal immortality. The poem's resolution — "There lives more faith in honest doubt, / Believe me, than in half the creeds" — made it a defining text of the Victorian crisis of faith.

'Tis better to have loved and lost
Than never to have loved at all.

Reception and influence

Queen Victoria kept the poem by her bedside after Prince Albert's death, saying it was her greatest comfort next to the Bible. Lines from it entered the language, and its stanzas are read at memorial services to this day. T. S. Eliot called it "the most sustained example of mastery of language" in Tennyson while judging its faith "a poor thing" — a tension that has shaped its critical reputation ever since.
