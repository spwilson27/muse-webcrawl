Ventricular septal defect

A ventricular septal defect (VSD) is a defect in the ventricular septum, the wall dividing the left and right ventricles of the heart. It is the most common congenital heart defect, occurring in about 2–6 per 1,000 live births. The size of the opening can vary from pin-hole to complete absence of the septum, creating a common ventricle.

Signs and symptoms

Small defects are usually asymptomatic and are detected by a loud, harsh holosystolic murmur at the left sternal border. Large defects allow blood to shunt from the left to the right ventricle, causing pulmonary overcirculation, breathlessness, poor feeding and failure to thrive in infancy. Over time, unrepaired large defects can cause irreversible pulmonary hypertension (Eisenmenger syndrome), at which point the shunt reverses and cyanosis appears.

Types

VSDs are classified by location: perimembranous (the most common, in the membranous part of the septum), muscular (in the muscular septum, sometimes multiple, the "Swiss-cheese" type), inlet (posterior, associated with atrioventricular septal defects), and outlet or supracristal (below the semilunar valves, commoner in Asian populations and liable to aortic valve prolapse).

Treatment

Many small muscular and perimembranous defects close spontaneously in childhood. Large or symptomatic defects are closed surgically with a patch, usually in infancy; some defects can be closed with catheter-delivered devices. Infective endocarditis prophylaxis and follow-up are recommended in certain cases.
