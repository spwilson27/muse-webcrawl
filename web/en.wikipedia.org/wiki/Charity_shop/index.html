Charity shop

A charity shop is a retail establishment run by a charitable organisation to raise money. Charity shops are a type of social enterprise. They sell mainly second-hand goods donated by members of the public, and are staffed largely by volunteers. Because the items for sale have been donated, and because business premises in some jurisdictions attract tax relief, charity shops can sell goods at competitive prices. After defraying costs, the remainder of income is used for the registered purposes of the charity.

Charity shops are most widespread in the United Kingdom, where several thousand operate, but they are also found in Ireland, Australia (where they are often called opportunity shops or op shops), New Zealand, Canada, and the United States, where they overlap with the thrift store tradition represented by organisations such as Goodwill Industries and the Salvation Army.

History

Charitable resale has nineteenth-century roots in organisations such as the Salvation Army and settlement houses, but the modern high-street charity shop is largely a British development of the twentieth century. Oxfam opened what is often described as the first modern charity shop in Broad Street, Oxford, in 1947, initially to sell donated goods to raise funds for famine relief in Greece. The model spread rapidly after the Second World War as charities recognised the reliable income stream shops could provide.

By the 1990s charity shops had become a fixture of British high streets, and trade bodies such as the Charity Retail Association (founded 1999) emerged to represent the sector. Shops increasingly also sell new goods, run gift aid schemes on donated items, and operate online through marketplaces.

Operation

Most charity shops accept donations of clothing, books, household goods, furniture, and media directly from the public at the shop, through donation banks, or by house-to-house collection. Volunteers and a smaller number of paid staff sort donations, pricing saleable items and sending unsaleable textiles to recycling merchants (the "rag trade"). Electrical goods must be safety-tested before resale in the UK.

Tax and regulation

In the United Kingdom, charity shops benefit from mandatory relief of 80 per cent on business rates (often topped up to 100 per cent by local authorities), and profits applied to charitable purposes are exempt from tax. Under the Gift Aid scheme, when a donor who is a UK taxpayer signs a declaration, the charity can reclaim basic-rate income tax on the proceeds of sale of their donated goods, treating the shop as the donor's agent.

Criticism

Commentators sometimes criticise the density of charity shops on declining high streets, the competition they pose to commercial second-hand dealers, and the proportion of donations that end up recycled rather than resold. Defenders reply that shops provide affordable goods, volunteering opportunities, local employment, and divert large quantities of material from landfill.
