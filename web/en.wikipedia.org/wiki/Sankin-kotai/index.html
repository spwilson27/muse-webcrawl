Sankin-kotai

Sankin-kōtai (Japanese: 参勤交代, "alternate attendance") was a policy of the Tokugawa shogunate during the Edo period of Japanese history. It required each daimyō (feudal lord) to spend alternating years in attendance at the shogun's court in Edo (modern Tokyo) and in his own domain (han), while his wife and heir remained in Edo permanently as hostages. The system was formalised in the Laws for the Military Houses (Buke shohatto) of 1634–35 and endured until 1862.

Purpose and effects

The policy served the shogunate as an instrument of control: the enormous expense of maintaining Edo residences and of the great processions (daimyō gyōretsu) to and from the capital drained domain finances and left lords little means for rebellion, while the hostage families guaranteed loyalty. At the same time, sankin-kōtai had far-reaching unintended consequences: it sustained a superb road network (the Five Routes, including the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō) with post towns, inns, and porters; it made Edo a vast consumer market; and it spread metropolitan culture, fashions, and dialects throughout Japan, knitting the country together culturally.

Practice

Most daimyō spent alternate years in Edo, travelling with retinues that could number hundreds or thousands of attendants, carefully graded by law according to rank. Lords from distant domains might travel for weeks. The burden was so great that the requirement was relaxed under the eighth shogun, Tokugawa Yoshimune, temporarily, and in 1862 — amid the shogunate's decline — attendance was reduced to a hundred days every three years, effectively ending the system before the Meiji Restoration abolished the domains themselves.
