Nazca (sometimes spelled Nasca) is a city and a system of valleys on the southern coast of Peru. It is the capital of the Nazca Province in the Ica Region, located about 370 km (230 mi) south of Lima. The city and its surrounding desert plateau are world-famous for the Nazca Lines, the enormous geoglyphs created by the ancient Nazca culture.

Nazca culture

The Nazca culture flourished from about 100 BCE to 800 CE, developing from the earlier Paracas tradition. It is renowned for its polychrome ceramics, fine textiles, and the underground aqueducts called puquios, many of which still function today. The ceremonial centre of Cahuachi, with its adobe pyramids, was a major pilgrimage site. The culture declined after about 750 CE amid environmental stress, including El Niño flooding and deforestation of the huarango trees that stabilised the desert soils.

Nazca Lines

Between roughly 500 BCE and 500 CE, people of the region created hundreds of geoglyphs by removing dark desert pavement stones to reveal the lighter soil beneath. The figures — including a hummingbird, monkey, spider, condor and long straight lines — are best seen from the air and were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1994. Their purpose is debated, with theories linking them to water rituals, astronomy and sacred pathways.

Modern city

Modern Nazca is a service centre for tourism and agriculture in the Nazca valley. The town was heavily damaged by an earthquake in 1996 and rebuilt. Visitors use the small María Reiche airport for sightseeing flights over the lines.
