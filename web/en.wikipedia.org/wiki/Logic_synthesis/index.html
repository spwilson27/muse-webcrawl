Logic synthesis

Logic synthesis is a process in electronic design automation by which an abstract specification of desired circuit behaviour — usually written in a hardware description language such as Verilog or VHDL at the register-transfer level (RTL) — is automatically converted into a design implementation in terms of logic gates, producing a gate-level netlist.

Process

Synthesis tools first elaborate the RTL into an intermediate representation, then optimise the logic (for example by minimising Boolean functions and sharing common subexpressions), and finally map the result onto the cells of a target technology library for an application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) or onto the lookup tables of an FPGA, subject to constraints on timing, area, and power supplied by the designer. Logic synthesis sits within the larger design flow between architectural design and physical design (placement and routing).

History

Commercial logic synthesis became practical in the late 1980s, notably with tools from Synopsys, transforming digital design from manual gate-level work to the RTL-based methodology used today. Earlier research systems and minimisation algorithms, such as the Espresso heuristic logic minimiser, laid the groundwork. Modern flows add steps such as design-for-test insertion, clock gating for power reduction, and formal equivalence checking between the RTL and the synthesised netlist.

Related concepts

A distinction is drawn between high-level synthesis, which starts from an even more abstract algorithmic description, and logic synthesis proper, which starts from RTL. Binary decision diagrams and SAT solvers are among the computational techniques used inside synthesis and verification tools.
