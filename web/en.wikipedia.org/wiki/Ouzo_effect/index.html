Ouzo effect

The ouzo effect (also louche effect and spontaneous emulsification) is the phenomenon in which a clear, transparent solution of an anise-flavoured liqueur, such as ouzo, pastis, rakı, arak or sambuca, becomes milky white when water is added. The effect occurs without the addition of any surfactant or emulsifying agent, and the resulting emulsion is remarkably stable — its droplets can remain dispersed for months.

Mechanism

The flavouring oil in anise-based liquors is chiefly anethole, which dissolves readily in ethanol but is almost insoluble in water. In the undiluted liquor, the high ethanol content keeps the anethole in solution. When water is added, the ethanol concentration falls below the level needed to hold the oil in solution, and the anethole is forced out. Rather than separating into a bulk oil layer, it nucleates into a vast number of sub-micron droplets that scatter light, producing the characteristic milky appearance.

Because the droplets form by nucleation rather than by mechanical mixing, the process is a form of spontaneous emulsification related to the solvent-shifting or "nanoprecipitation" methods used to make nanoparticles. The droplets grow slowly by Ostwald ripening, but in the ouzo system this growth is unusually slow, which accounts for the emulsion's long-term stability.

History and naming

The clouding of absinthe and other anise drinks on dilution — the louche — was well known to drinkers in the nineteenth century, and the louche was prized as a sign of quality in absinthe preparation. The physical mechanism was analysed in detail by Stephen A. Vitale and Joseph L. Katz in a 2003 paper in Langmuir, which coined the term "ouzo effect" and measured the ternary phase diagram of the water–ethanol–anethole system.

Applications

The ouzo effect has become a model system in colloid science and is exploited in the laboratory preparation of emulsions and polymer nanoparticles, where it allows monodisperse droplets to be produced without high-shear mixing or surfactants.
