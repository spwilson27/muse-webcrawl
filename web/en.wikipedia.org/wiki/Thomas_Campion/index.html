Thomas Campion

Thomas Campion (12 February 1567 – 1 March 1620) was an English composer, poet and physician of the late Elizabethan and Jacobean eras. He is best known for his lute songs, or ayres, for which he typically wrote both words and music, and for his masques performed at the court of James I.

Life

Born in London, Campion studied at Peterhouse, Cambridge, without taking a degree, and then read law at Gray's Inn. He later studied medicine at the University of Caen, taking his degree in 1605, and practised as a physician in London. In 1619 he was implicated, though ultimately cleared, in the scandal surrounding the murder of Sir Thomas Overbury.

Works

Campion published five books of ayres between 1601 and 1617, the first jointly with Philip Rosseter. Songs such as "My sweetest Lesbia", "When to her lute Corinna sings" and "Shall I come, sweet Love, to thee?" remain staples of the lute-song repertoire. His treatise Observations in the Art of English Poesie (1602) controversially attacked rhyme and advocated quantitative metres, provoking Samuel Daniel's Defence of Rhyme. His New Way of Making Fowre Parts in Counter-point (c. 1614) was an influential harmony manual. He also wrote masques including the Lord Hay's Masque (1607) and the Somerset Masque (1613).

Legacy

Campion's lyric poetry is admired for its musicality and economy; he is regarded, with John Dowland, as one of the finest composers of the English ayre.
