National Archaeological Museum, Athens

National Archaeological Museum
Greek: Εθνικό Αρχαιολογικό Μουσείο
Established: 1829; current building opened 1889
Location: Exarcheia, Athens, Greece
Collection size: More than 11,000 exhibits
Type: Archaeological museum

The National Archaeological Museum (Greek: Εθνικό Αρχαιολογικό Μουσείο) in Athens is the largest archaeological museum in Greece and one of the most important in the world devoted to ancient Greek art. Its collections span Greek civilization from prehistory to late antiquity.

History

Greece's first national archaeological museum was founded on Aegina in 1829 under Governor Ioannis Kapodistrias and moved with the capital to Athens. The present neoclassical building in the Exarcheia district, designed in part by Ludwig Lange and Ernst Ziller, was constructed between 1866 and 1889. During the Second World War the collection was buried and dispersed for protection; the museum was renovated extensively for the 2004 Olympic Games.

Collections

The museum's five major permanent collections include the Prehistoric Collection, with treasures from Mycenae excavated by Heinrich Schliemann—among them the gold funerary "Mask of Agamemnon"—and the frescoes of Thera (Santorini); the Sculpture Collection, including the bronze Poseidon (or Zeus) of Artemision and the Marathon Boy; the Vase and Minor Objects Collection; the Metallurgy Collection; and the Egyptian and Near Eastern Collection, the only one of its kind in Greece.

Famous individual pieces include the Antikythera mechanism, the world's oldest known analog computer, recovered from a shipwreck in 1901; the Antikythera Youth bronze; the gold cups of Vaphio; the Dipylon amphora; and the Jockey of Artemision. The museum occupies about 8,000 square meters of exhibition space and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors annually.
