Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), also called chronic myelogenous leukemia, is a cancer of the white blood cells. It is a form of leukemia characterized by the uncontrolled growth of predominantly myeloid cells in the bone marrow and their accumulation in the blood. CML is a clonal stem-cell disorder driven in nearly all cases by the Philadelphia chromosome.

Cause

The disease is caused by a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, t(9;22), which creates the shortened chromosome 22 known as the Philadelphia chromosome (identified in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford) and the BCR–ABL1 fusion gene. Its product is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives cell proliferation and genomic instability.

Signs and course

CML classically progresses through three phases: a chronic phase, often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on a blood count; an accelerated phase; and a blast crisis resembling acute leukemia. Symptoms can include fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, and fullness from an enlarged spleen (splenomegaly). Untreated, the disease historically progressed to blast crisis within a few years.

Treatment

The introduction of imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting BCR–ABL1, in 2001 transformed CML from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition for most patients, with life expectancy approaching that of the general population. Later-generation inhibitors include dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, and asciminib, used for resistance or intolerance. Response is monitored by quantitative PCR measurement of BCR–ABL1 transcripts, and some patients with deep molecular responses can discontinue therapy under supervision. Allogeneic stem-cell transplantation remains an option for selected patients.
