Accent (sociolinguistics)

In sociolinguistics, an accent is a distinctive way of pronouncing a language, especially one associated with a particular country, region, or social class. Accent concerns pronunciation only — the sounds of speech, including vowels, consonants, stress, rhythm, and intonation — and is distinguished from a dialect, which also encompasses vocabulary and grammar. Every speaker has an accent; there is no "accentless" speech.

Development and identity

Children generally acquire the accent of their peers rather than their parents, and accent is typically fixed by early adolescence. Accent is a powerful marker of identity: listeners rapidly infer a speaker's regional origin, class, and sometimes ethnicity from pronunciation. Speakers may code-switch or modify their accent ("accent reduction" training) in professional contexts, and actors learn accents for roles with dialect coaches.

Prestige and discrimination

Some accents carry overt prestige — Received Pronunciation in Britain historically signaled education and status — while others are stigmatized, a phenomenon studied as linguistic discrimination or accentism. Research using matched-guise techniques shows that listeners attribute qualities such as intelligence, competence, and trustworthiness to speakers based on accent alone. William Labov's classic department-store study in New York City (1966) demonstrated systematic social stratification of the pronunciation of /r/.

Second-language accents

A "foreign accent" arises when the sound patterns of a speaker's first language influence their pronunciation of a second language. The strength of a foreign accent depends strongly on the age at which the second language was learned (see critical period hypothesis), as well as exposure and motivation.
