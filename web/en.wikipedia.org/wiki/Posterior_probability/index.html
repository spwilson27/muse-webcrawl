Posterior probability

The posterior probability is a type of conditional probability that results from updating the prior probability with information summarized by the likelihood via Bayes' rule. From an epistemological perspective, the posterior probability contains everything there is to know about an uncertain proposition (such as a scientific hypothesis, or parameter values), given prior knowledge and a mathematical model describing the observations available at a particular time.

Definition

Given a hypothesis H and evidence E, the posterior probability P(H | E) is computed by Bayes' theorem:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)

where P(H) is the prior probability, P(E | H) is the likelihood, and P(E) is the marginal likelihood (or evidence), acting as a normalizing constant.

Posterior distribution

In Bayesian statistics, the posterior distribution is the probability distribution of an unknown quantity, treated as a random variable, conditional on the evidence obtained from an experiment or survey. Credible intervals — the Bayesian analogue of confidence intervals — are derived directly from the posterior distribution. After further evidence is obtained, the resulting posterior may become the prior for a future analysis; in this way the posterior from one study serves as the prior for the next.

Computation

When the prior and likelihood belong to conjugate families, the posterior has a closed form. In general cases, posterior distributions are computed approximately, for example by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling methods such as Gibbs sampling or the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, or by variational approximations.

Example

Suppose a medical test for a disease with prevalence 1% has sensitivity 99% and a false-positive rate of 5%. For a person who tests positive, the posterior probability of actually having the disease is 0.99 × 0.01 / (0.99 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99) ≈ 16.7% — much lower than intuition often suggests, illustrating the base rate fallacy.
