History of electroconvulsive therapy

The history of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) in psychiatry began in 1938 in Rome, when Ugo Cerletti and Lucio Bini first induced seizures with electric current to treat severe mental illness, building on earlier convulsive therapies using drugs. ECT spread rapidly through asylums worldwide, was transformed mid-century by anaesthesia and muscle relaxants, fell into disrepute in the 1960s and 1970s, and survives today in modified form as a treatment mainly for severe and drug-resistant depression and catatonia.

Origins: convulsive therapy

In 1934 the Hungarian physician Ladislas von Meduna began inducing seizures with camphor and later metrazol (pentylenetetrazol), based on the mistaken theory that schizophrenia and epilepsy were antagonistic. Manfred Sakel's insulin coma therapy was another somatic treatment of the era. Cerletti, who had experimented with electric shock in animals, and his assistant Bini developed a device to deliver brief controlled currents through the head; in April 1938 they treated a 39-year-old man diagnosed with schizophrenia, who improved after a course of treatments.

Spread and modification

ECT was taken up enthusiastically in Europe and America because it was quicker and more reliable than chemical convulsion, and appeared effective for depressive and manic states. Early "unmodified" ECT caused convulsions strong enough to fracture bones; the introduction of curare and then succinylcholine as muscle relaxants, together with general anaesthesia (from the 1950s), greatly reduced injuries. Techniques changed from sine-wave to brief-pulse stimulation, and from bilateral to right unilateral electrode placement, to reduce memory side effects.

Controversy and decline

The rise of antipsychotic and antidepressant drugs from the 1950s, abuses such as the punitive or indiscriminate use of ECT, its depiction in works such as Ken Kesey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (1962) and the 1975 film, and genuine concerns about memory loss led to a sharp decline and legal restrictions in some jurisdictions.

Modern practice

Research and clinical experience from the 1980s onward re-established ECT as highly effective for severe major depression that has not responded to medication, for catatonia and for some cases of mania, with response rates often exceeding those of pharmacotherapy. Modern guidelines (such as those of the APA and NICE) regulate consent, stimulus dosing and monitoring; debate continues about the extent of persistent autobiographical memory loss in a minority of patients.
