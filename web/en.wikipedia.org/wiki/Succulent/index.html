Succulent plant

In botany, succulent plants are plants with parts that are thickened, fleshy and engorged to store water, an adaptation to arid climates or soil conditions in which water is scarce. The word derives from the Latin sucus, meaning juice or sap. Water may be stored in leaves, stems or roots, giving the plants their distinctive plump appearance.

Adaptations

Succulence is usually accompanied by other drought adaptations: a thick waxy cuticle that limits transpiration, reduced leaf surface area or leaves modified into spines, shallow but extensive root systems, and crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), in which stomata open at night to take in carbon dioxide, greatly reducing water loss. Succulence has evolved independently many times — a case of convergent evolution — in more than 25 plant families, including the Cactaceae, Crassulaceae, Aizoaceae, Agavoideae and Euphorbiaceae.

Distribution and examples

Succulents are characteristic of deserts and semi-deserts worldwide: cacti in the Americas, aloes and mesembs in southern Africa, and echeverias and agaves in Mexico. Familiar examples include Aloe vera, jade plant (Crassula ovata), prickly pear (Opuntia), and the living stones (Lithops), which mimic pebbles to avoid herbivory. Not all cacti-like plants are cacti; many succulent euphorbias closely resemble them.

Cultivation

Because they tolerate neglect and dry indoor air, succulents are popular houseplants. They require bright light, sharp drainage and infrequent watering; overwatering and the resulting root rot is the commonest cause of failure in cultivation.
