Pesticide resistance

Pesticide resistance is the genetically inherited ability of a pest population — insects, weeds, fungi, rodents, or other organisms — to survive exposure to a pesticide that previously controlled it. It is a textbook example of rapid natural selection: repeated applications kill susceptible individuals and leave resistant ones to reproduce, so resistance spreads through the population, often within a few years of a product's introduction.

Scale of the problem

The first documented case was San Jose scale insects surviving lime-sulphur washes in 1914. Since then, resistance has been recorded in hundreds of species: more than 600 arthropod species, over 270 weed species resistant to herbicides (notably glyphosate), and many plant-pathogenic fungi. Resistance to DDT in houseflies and malaria mosquitoes appeared within years of the insecticide's widespread use in the 1940s–50s.

Mechanisms

Mechanisms include target-site resistance (mutations altering the protein the pesticide attacks, e.g. the kdr mutation in sodium channels conferring pyrethroid resistance), metabolic resistance (enhanced detoxification by enzymes such as cytochrome P450s, esterases, and glutathione transferases), reduced penetration through the cuticle, and behavioural avoidance. Cross-resistance arises when one mechanism defeats several chemicals; multiple resistance stacks several mechanisms in one population.

Management

Integrated pest management (IPM) aims to slow resistance by rotating chemicals with different modes of action, using mixtures, applying pesticides only above economic thresholds, preserving refuges of susceptible pests (a legal requirement for Bt crops in several countries), and combining chemical with biological and cultural controls. Resistance is widely cited as a driver of the "pesticide treadmill", in which farmers must apply more or newer chemicals to achieve the same control.
