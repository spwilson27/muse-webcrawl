Pediplain

A pediplain (from Latin pes, genitive pedis, "foot") is an extensive plain formed by the coalescence of adjacent pediments — broad, gently sloping rock-cut surfaces at the foot of mountain fronts or scarps. Pediplains are characteristic landforms of arid and semi-arid regions and of the theory of pediplanation developed by geomorphologist Lester Charles King.

Formation

Pediments form primarily by scarp retreat: mountain fronts and escarpments erode back parallel to themselves, largely through weathering and sheetwash, leaving an expanding, low-gradient bedrock plain thinly veneered with sediment. As neighbouring pediments grow, they merge into a continuous pediplain, dotted with residual hills called inselbergs that are remnants of the former uplands.

King's model

In the mid-twentieth century, Lester King generalised his observations in South Africa into a global model in which pediplanation, rather than W. M. Davis's humid peneplanation, was the dominant mode of landscape evolution. King's scheme envisioned cyclic development of pediplains across continents, and uplifted, dissected pediplains were used to correlate ancient erosion surfaces, particularly in Africa, Australia, and Brazil.

Distribution and debate

Classic pediplains occur in the African savannas, the Australian outback, and cratonic interiors worldwide. Later research questioned the universality of King's cycles and the mechanisms of pediment formation (including the roles of subsurface weathering and lateral planation by streams), but pediments and pediplains remain standard concepts in arid-land and long-term landscape evolution studies.
