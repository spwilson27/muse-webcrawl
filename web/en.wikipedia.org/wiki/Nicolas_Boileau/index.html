Nicolas Boileau

Born 1 November 1636, Paris, France

Died 13 March 1711 (aged 74), Paris, France

Occupation Poet, critic

Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux (1 November 1636 – 13 March 1711), commonly called Boileau, was a French poet and critic who codified the classical rules of French literature. As the leading literary legislator of his age, he shaped the taste of the court of Louis XIV and exerted an influence across Europe comparable to that of Horace, whose Ars Poetica he imitated and translated in spirit.

Works

Boileau first became known for his Satires (1666– ), verse attacks on bad writers and social follies in the manner of Juvenal and Horace. His masterpiece is L'Art poétique (1674), a didactic poem in four cantos prescribing the rules of the genres — pastoral, epic, tragedy, comedy, ode — and insisting on reason, clarity, truth to nature, and the sublime tempered by good sense. In the same year he published the mock-heroic poem Le Lutrin and his translation of the treatise On the Sublime attributed to Longinus. His Épîtres (epistles) extended his criticism and moral reflection.

Position and influence

Appointed historiographer royal with Racine in 1677 and elected to the Académie française in 1684, Boileau defended the "Ancients" in the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns, notably in his Réflexions critiques sur Longin, while championing writers such as Molière, Racine, and La Fontaine against mediocre rivals. Alexander Pope's Essay on Criticism is heavily indebted to Boileau, and his maxims — such as "What is well conceived is clearly said" — passed into common literary currency.
