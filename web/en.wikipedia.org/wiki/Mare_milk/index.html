Mare milk

Milking a mare in Kyrgyzstan

Mare milk is milk lactated by female horses, known as mares, to feed their foals. It is rich in whey protein, polyunsaturated fatty acids and vitamin C, and relatively low in fat and casein compared with cow's milk. Mare milk is notably high in lactose, which makes it unsuitable for some people with lactose intolerance, but its composition is among the closest of farm-animal milks to human milk.

Traditional use

Nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppe — Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Mongols and others — have milked mares for millennia. Because of its high sugar content, mare milk ferments readily into kumis (also spelled koumiss), a mildly alcoholic, effervescent drink central to steppe hospitality and festivals; the Scythians were already described by Herodotus as mare-milkers. Mares must be milked frequently in small quantities through the day, traditionally with the foal present to stimulate let-down.

Modern production and uses

Today mare milk is produced commercially in parts of Europe, notably Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Hungary, where it is sold fresh, frozen or powdered, and marketed for skin care and dietary use. It has been used as a substitute food for infants allergic to cow's-milk protein under medical supervision, and kumis has historically been promoted in sanatoria for digestive and respiratory complaints.
