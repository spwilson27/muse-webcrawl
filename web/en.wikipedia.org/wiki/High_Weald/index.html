The High Weald is an area of high, wooded, rolling terrain in south-eastern England, lying mainly in Kent and East Sussex with parts in West Sussex and Surrey. It forms the central, elevated part of the Weald, between the chalk ridges of the North Downs and South Downs. Much of it is designated the High Weald National Landscape (formerly the High Weald Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty), covering about 1,461 square kilometres and recognised as one of England's finest medieval landscapes.

Geology and landscape

The High Weald sits on the Hastings Beds of sandstone and clay at the core of the Weald anticline, eroded into a series of ridges and steep-sided valleys (gills), many still wooded. The landscape is characterised by small, irregular fields enclosed in Saxon and medieval times, abundant ancient woodland, sunken lanes, scattered farmsteads and small villages, and historic features such as hammer ponds and pits from the Wealden iron industry.

History

In Saxon times the Weald was a vast forest (the Andredesweald) used for swine pasture by communities on its fringes. Colonisation of the High Weald intensified in the medieval period, creating the dispersed settlement pattern that survives today. From Roman times through the 17th century the area was a major centre of iron production, using local ore, charcoal and water power; later industries included glassmaking, hop growing and quarrying.
