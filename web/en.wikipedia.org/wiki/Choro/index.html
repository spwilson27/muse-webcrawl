Choro


Placeholder image: a traditional choro ensemble

Choro (Portuguese pronunciation: [ˈʃoɾu], "cry" or "lament"), also popularly called chorinho ("little cry" or "little lament"), is an instrumental Brazilian popular music genre that originated in 19th-century Rio de Janeiro. Despite its name, the music often has a fast and happy rhythm, characterised by virtuosity, improvisation and counterpoint.

Choro is considered the first characteristically Brazilian genre of urban popular music, and it profoundly influenced the later development of samba and bossa nova.

History

Choro emerged in Rio de Janeiro in the 1870s, as Brazilian musicians fused European dance forms — the polka, waltz, mazurka and schottische — with Afro-Brazilian rhythms such as the lundu. The flautist Joaquim Callado is often credited with organising the first choro ensembles, known as regionais. The pianist Chiquinha Gonzaga was an important early composer in the idiom.

In the early 20th century the genre was consolidated by Pixinguinha (Alfredo da Rocha Viana Jr.), whose group Os Oito Batutas brought choro to wide audiences and to Paris in 1922. Other central figures include Jacob do Bandolim, Waldir Azevedo, and the guitarist Garoto. Composers of art music such as Heitor Villa-Lobos, whose cycle of Chôros (1920–1929) bears the genre's name, also drew on its language.

Instrumentation and form

A traditional regional typically includes flute (or another melody instrument such as mandolin or clarinet), six-string guitar, seven-string guitar (violão de sete cordas) providing bass lines and counter-melodies, cavaquinho, and pandeiro. A classic choro is cast in a rondo-like three-part form (A–A–B–A–C–A) in 2/4 time, with modulations to neighbouring keys in the contrasting sections.
