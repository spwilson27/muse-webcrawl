Richard Sorge (4 October 1895 – 7 November 1944) was a German journalist and Soviet military intelligence (GRU) officer active before and during World War II. Operating in Tokyo under the cover of a loyal Nazi correspondent, he ran one of the most successful espionage networks of the war.

Early life

Sorge was born in Baku, in today's Azerbaijan, to a German mining engineer father and a Russian mother; the family returned to Germany when he was a child. Wounded while serving in the German army in World War I, he became disillusioned with the war, studied economics and politics, joined the Communist Party of Germany, and moved to the Soviet Union, where he was recruited by Soviet military intelligence.

Tokyo ring

After postings in Shanghai, Sorge arrived in Tokyo in 1933 posing as a correspondent for German newspapers and a committed Nazi Party member. He gained the confidence of the German embassy, including ambassador Eugen Ott, while assembling a ring that included the Japanese journalist Hotsumi Ozaki, an adviser close to Prime Minister Fumimaro Konoe, and the radio operator Max Clausen.

The ring supplied Moscow with high-grade intelligence, most famously advance warning of the German invasion of the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941 — a warning Stalin distrusted — and, crucially, the report in the autumn of 1941 that Japan intended to strike south against the United States and British and Dutch colonies rather than north against Siberia. This assurance is widely credited with enabling Stalin to transfer Far Eastern divisions west for the defence of Moscow.

Arrest and legacy

Japanese counter-intelligence unravelled the ring in October 1941. Sorge was arrested, tried for espionage, and hanged at Sugamo Prison on 7 November 1944; Ozaki was executed the same day. The Soviet Union publicly denied he was its agent for two decades, then posthumously named him a Hero of the Soviet Union in 1964. He is routinely ranked among the most effective spies of the 20th century.
