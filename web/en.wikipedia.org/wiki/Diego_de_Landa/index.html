Diego de Landa Calderón (1524–1579) was a Spanish Franciscan friar and bishop of Yucatán who occupies a paradoxical place in history: he ordered the destruction of most of the sacred books of the Maya peoples, yet his own Relación de las cosas de Yucatán is the single most important early source on Maya civilisation.

Career in Yucatán

Landa arrived in Yucatán in 1549 and devoted himself to evangelisation, learning the Yucatec Maya language and founding convents. As provincial of the Franciscans he gained broad authority over the Maya population. Alarmed by reports of continuing "idolatry" — including human sacrifice in caves — he launched an inquisitorial campaign centred on the town of Maní.

The auto-da-fé of Maní

In July 1562, Landa staged a great auto-da-fé at Maní in which thousands of Maya ritual objects and at least dozens of hieroglyphic books were burned in the plaza. In his own account: "We found a large number of books in these characters and, as they contained nothing in which there were not to be seen superstitions and lies of the devil, we burned them all, which they regretted to an amazing degree, and which caused them much affliction." Thousands of Maya were tortured during the campaign and many died. Recalled to Spain to answer charges of exceeding his authority, Landa was eventually absolved and returned as bishop in 1573.

The Relación

Written in Spain around 1566 as part of his defence, the Relación de las cosas de Yucatán describes Maya calendar, writing, religion, agriculture, and daily life in extraordinary detail. Its most famous passage — Landa's so-called "alphabet", in which a Maya scribe drew glyphs in response to Spanish letter names — was misunderstood for centuries but provided Yuri Knorozov a key to the phonetic decipherment of the Maya script. The survival of only four authentic Maya codices is the direct legacy of the bonfires Landa inspired.
