Bridgman–Stockbarger method

The Bridgman–Stockbarger method is a technique for growing large single crystals (boules) of semiconductor and optical materials by directional solidification of a melt. It is named for Harvard physicist Percy Williams Bridgman and MIT physicist Donald C. Stockbarger, who developed related versions of the method in the 1920s and 1930s.

TypeCrystal growth process
InventorsPercy Bridgman; Donald Stockbarger
PrincipleDirectional solidification through a controlled temperature gradient
ApplicationsSemiconductors, scintillators, optical crystals (e.g. CdTe, CaF₂, NaI)

Process

A crucible containing the molten material, often with a seed crystal at its pointed tip, is slowly translated through a furnace with a steep, controlled temperature gradient — or, equivalently, the furnace temperature profile is moved along the crucible. Solidification begins at the seed and progresses through the melt, so the whole charge crystallizes as a single crystal replicating the seed's orientation. In Stockbarger's refinement, a baffle separates hot and cold furnace zones to sharpen the gradient at the melting point.

Variants and applications

Variants include the horizontal Bridgman method, the gradient freeze technique (in which the crucible is stationary and the temperature field is shifted electronically), and vertical gradient freeze. The method is widely used for materials that are difficult to grow by the Czochralski process, including cadmium telluride for solar cells and X-ray detectors, alkali halide scintillation crystals, and fluorides for optics.
