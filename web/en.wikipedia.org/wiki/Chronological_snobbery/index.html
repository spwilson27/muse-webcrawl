Chronological snobbery

Chronological snobbery is a term coined by writers C. S. Lewis and Owen Barfield for the uncritical assumption that whatever is newer — in thought, art, or custom — is superior to what is older, and that ideas from earlier ages can be dismissed simply because of their date. Lewis defined it as "the uncritical acceptance of the intellectual climate common to our own age and the assumption that whatever has gone out of date is on that account discredited."

[Image: A timeline contrasting old and new books]

Origin

Lewis credited Barfield with the term and popularised it in his autobiography Surprised by Joy (1955) and in his 1943 essay "On the Reading of Old Books" (published as the introduction to The Incarnation of the Word of God by Athanasius). Barfield's arguments helped convert Lewis from a position of reflexive modernism to an appreciation of pre-modern literature and thought.

Argument

Lewis did not contend that older ideas are necessarily right, but that they are not necessarily wrong, and that every age — including one's own — has characteristic blind spots that are invisible to those inside them. His remedy was the reading of old books: "It is a good rule, after reading a new book, never to allow yourself another new one till you have read an old one in between." Old books expose the reader to assumptions other than the present age's, allowing "the clean sea breeze of the centuries" to blow through the mind.

Usage

The phrase is now used broadly in cultural and intellectual criticism for any automatic privileging of the present — for example, dismissing earlier science, theology or literature as worthless merely because it has been superseded or has fallen from fashion.
