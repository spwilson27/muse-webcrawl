Quintessence

Placeholder: the fifth element filled the heavens above the Moon in Aristotelian cosmology.

Quintessence (from Latin quinta essentia, "fifth essence") is the Latin name for the fifth element proposed by Aristotle and medieval philosophers, after earth, water, air, and fire. Aristotle called it aither (αἰθήρ) and held that it filled the region of the universe above the terrestrial sphere: unlike the corruptible elements below the Moon, the heavenly quintessence was eternal, unchanging, and moved naturally in perfect circles.

In medieval and early modern thought

Medieval scholastics elaborated the doctrine: the quintessence composed the celestial spheres that carried the planets, and alchemists adopted the term for a concentrated extract or elixir obtained by distillation — the pure essence of a substance, a sense that survives in the everyday English meaning of "quintessence" as the perfect embodiment of a quality. With the Scientific Revolution and the discovery that celestial and terrestrial matter obey the same laws, the physical doctrine was abandoned; Newton's universal gravitation left no role for a special heavenly substance, though the word "aether" was revived in the nineteenth century for the luminiferous medium of light, itself discarded after the Michelson–Morley experiment and Einstein's relativity.

In modern cosmology

The term has been revived in a new sense: quintessence is a hypothetical form of dark energy described by a dynamic scalar field whose equation of state evolves over time, in contrast to the constant vacuum energy of the cosmological constant. Proposed in the late 1980s and named by analogy with the classical fifth element, quintessence models attempt to explain the accelerating expansion of the universe discovered in 1998 and to address the coincidence problem — why dark energy's density is comparable to matter's today. Variants include "tracker" fields that follow the dominant component, and k-essence, in which the field's kinetic term drives the acceleration.
