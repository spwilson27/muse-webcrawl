Pulsus paradoxus

Pulsus paradoxus, also known as paradoxical pulse, is an abnormally large decrease in stroke volume, systolic blood pressure, and pulse wave amplitude during inspiration. The normal fall in systolic pressure during inspiration is less than 10 mmHg; a fall greater than 10 mmHg is pulsus paradoxus. The "paradox" is that the heart continues to beat — heart sounds remain audible — while the peripheral pulse disappears at the height of inspiration.

Mechanism

During inspiration, intrathoracic pressure falls, drawing more venous blood into the right side of the heart and expanding the pulmonary vascular bed. Normally the resulting decrease in left ventricular filling is small. When the heart is constrained — for example by fluid in the pericardial sac — expansion of the right ventricle bows the interventricular septum into the left ventricle, sharply reducing left ventricular stroke volume and systolic pressure.

Causes

  • Cardiac tamponade — the classic and most important cause.
  • Severe asthma and COPD exacerbations — large swings in intrathoracic pressure.
  • Constrictive pericarditis — in a minority of cases.
  • Massive pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, and hypovolemic shock.

Measurement

Pulsus paradoxus is measured with a sphygmomanometer: the cuff is deflated slowly, and the difference between the pressure at which Korotkoff sounds are first heard only during expiration and the pressure at which they are heard throughout the respiratory cycle is recorded. A difference exceeding 10 mmHg is abnormal. It can also be seen on arterial line tracings and in the waveform of a pulse oximeter.

Related signs

The reverse phenomenon, in which blood pressure rises during inspiration, is called reverse pulsus paradoxus and is seen in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and during positive-pressure ventilation. Kussmaul's sign — a rise in jugular venous pressure with inspiration — is a distinct finding associated with constrictive pericarditis.
