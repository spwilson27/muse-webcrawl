Sustainable architecture

Sustainable architecture is architecture that seeks to minimize the negative environmental impact of buildings through efficient and moderate use of materials, energy, development space, and the ecosystem at large. Sustainable architecture uses a conscious approach to energy and ecological conservation in the design of the built environment, encompassing the whole life cycle of a building — siting, design, construction, operation, maintenance, renovation, and demolition. It is closely related to the rating systems LEED, BREEAM, and DGNB, which certify environmental performance.

Sustainable buildings integrate passive design, renewable energy, and low-impact materials.

Principles

Core strategies include optimizing a site's solar orientation and natural ventilation; high-performance insulation and glazing; on-site renewable energy from solar panels, heat pumps, and geothermal systems; rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling; and selecting durable, local, recycled, or rapidly renewable materials with low embodied carbon. The Passive House standard demonstrates that heating demand can be cut by up to 90 percent through design alone.

History

Vernacular architecture long embodied sustainable principles, from adobe dwellings to Persian windcatchers. The modern movement grew from the 1970s energy crisis, the work of pioneers such as Hassan Fathy and Glenn Murcutt, and the 1990s launch of BREEAM (1990) and LEED (1998). Contemporary landmarks include the Bullitt Center in Seattle, often called the "greenest commercial building in the world," and Milan's Bosco Verticale residential towers.

Criticism

Critics note risks of "greenwashing," the high cost of certification, and the tension between efficient new construction and the often greener option of reusing existing buildings, since demolition wastes embodied energy.
