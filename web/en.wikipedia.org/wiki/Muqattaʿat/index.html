The Muqattaʿat (Arabic: حروف مقطعة, ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿa, "disconnected" or "separated letters") are combinations of one to five Arabic letters that open 29 of the 114 chapters (surahs) of the Qur'an, immediately after the basmala. In recitation the letters are pronounced by their names rather than as a word. Their meaning has been debated since the earliest centuries of Islam.

The letters

Fourteen distinct letters — exactly half the Arabic alphabet — appear in various combinations, including Alif Lām Mīm (الم, opening Surahs 2, 3, 29, 30, 31, 32), Alif Lām Rā (الر), Alif Lām Mīm Ṣād (المص), Kāf Hā Yā ʿAyn Ṣād (كهيعص, opening Surah Maryam), Ṭā Hā (طه), Ṭā Sīn Mīm (طسم), Yā Sīn (يس), Ṣād (ص), Ḥā Mīm (حم, opening seven consecutive surahs), ʿAyn Sīn Qāf (عسق), Qāf (ق) and Nūn (ن). Six surahs are named after their opening letters (Ṭā Hā, Yā Sīn, Ṣād, Qāf among them).

Interpretations

Classical commentators proposed that the letters are abbreviations of divine names or attributes (e.g. Alif Lām Mīm for "I am God, the All-Knowing"), oaths, names of the surah or of the Prophet, or a mystery whose knowledge belongs to God alone — a view held by many early authorities, who classed them among the mutashabih (ambiguous verses). Some traditions hold that they form part of the Qur'an's inimitability (i'jaz): a challenge showing that the revelation is composed of the very letters of the Arabs' own alphabet yet cannot be imitated. Modern scholarship has added literary and structural analyses, and numerical theories — notably claims of patterns based on the number 19 advanced by Rashad Khalifa — which remain controversial and are rejected by mainstream scholarship.

In manuscript and print practice

The letters count as the first verse of their surahs in the Kufan verse-numbering tradition, and elaborate calligraphic treatment of them is a feature of illuminated Qur'ans. In most printed editions they are written fully connected in form but read as separate letter-names.
