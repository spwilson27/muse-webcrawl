Flat feet

Flat feet, also called pes planus or fallen arches, is a postural deformity in which the arches of the foot collapse, with the entire sole of the foot coming into complete or near-complete contact with the ground. There is a functional relationship between the structure of the arch and the biomechanics of the lower leg. In some individuals the condition is painless and causes no disability; in others it is associated with foot pain, ankle, knee, or back problems.

Causes and prevalence

Most infants and young children have flat feet because the arch has not yet developed and the sole is padded with fat; the arch normally forms by about age six. Persistent flexible flat feet in adults are usually hereditary. Acquired flat foot in adults commonly results from dysfunction or rupture of the posterior tibial tendon, which supports the arch, and is more common in women over 40. Other causes include rheumatoid arthritis, injury, obesity, and tarsal coalition — a congenital fusion of foot bones that produces a rigid flat foot.

Flexible and rigid flat foot

In flexible flat foot, the arch reappears when the person stands on tiptoe or sits; this form is usually benign. Rigid flat foot, in which the arch is absent in all positions, more often has an underlying structural cause and is more likely to be painful.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by physical examination, footprint analysis, and, when needed, X-rays. Treatment is required only when symptoms are present. Measures include activity modification, orthotic arch supports, stretching and strengthening exercises (particularly of the posterior tibial tendon), and weight management. Studies have found little evidence that orthotics change the development of children's arches. Surgery — tendon transfer, osteotomy, or fusion — is reserved for severe, disabling cases that fail conservative care.

Military service

Flat feet were historically grounds for rejection from military service, on the assumption that sufferers could not withstand prolonged marching. Modern evidence has largely overturned this view, and most armed forces no longer treat asymptomatic flat feet as disqualifying.
