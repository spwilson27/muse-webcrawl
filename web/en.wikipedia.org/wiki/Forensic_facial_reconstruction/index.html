Forensic facial reconstruction

Forensic facial reconstruction (or forensic facial approximation) is the process of recreating the face of an individual from their skeletal remains through an amalgamation of artistry, anthropology, osteology and anatomy. It is most often used to identify unknown human remains when other methods fail, and is also applied to historical figures.

Forensic facial reconstruction
Field: Forensic anthropology
Methods: 2D drawing, 3D clay sculpture, computer modelling
Inputs: Skull, tissue-depth data, ancestry/age/sex estimates

Methods

In the traditional 3D method, tissue-depth markers based on population averages are placed on a cast of the skull, and muscles and skin are modelled in clay following anatomical rules for features such as nose projection and mouth width. Two-dimensional reconstructions draw over skull photographs or radiographs; computer methods build faces in software and can produce variants quickly.

Accuracy and limits

The skull constrains the face but does not determine soft features such as ears, lips and hairstyles, so reconstructions are approximations intended to trigger recognition, not portraits. Success rates vary, and the technique is debated, but it has contributed to many identifications and to public images of figures such as Egyptian pharaohs.
