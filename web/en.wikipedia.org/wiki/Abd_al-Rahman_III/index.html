Abd al-Rahman III (Arabic: عبد الرحمن الثالث; 7 January 891 – 15 October 961), known by the honorific an-Nasir ("the Defender"), was Emir of Córdoba from 912 and, from 929, the first Caliph of Córdoba. Reigning for 49 years — the longest of any Córdoban Umayyad — he reunified a fragmenting al-Andalus, proclaimed a caliphate to rival Baghdad and the Fatimids, and raised Córdoba into one of the largest, wealthiest, and most cultured cities of the medieval world.

Accession and reunification

Abd al-Rahman succeeded his grandfather Abdullah at 21, inheriting an emirate shrunk by decades of rebellion: the muladí warlord Umar ibn Hafsun held the south from Bobastro, Toledo and Zaragoza were effectively independent, and much of the territory paid only nominal obedience. In a methodical series of campaigns lasting two decades, Abd al-Rahman combined force with pardons and honours to recover the rebel cities one by one; Ibn Hafsun died in 917 and his sons were defeated by 928, when Bobastro fell. Toledo submitted in 932. For the first time in generations, a single authority ruled Muslim Spain.

Proclamation of the Caliphate

In January 929 Abd al-Rahman assumed the title of caliph and commander of the faithful, taking the regnal name an-Nāṣir li-Dīn Allāh. The move answered two rivals: the Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad and, more urgently, the Fatimid caliphate proclaimed in North Africa in 909, which threatened Umayyad interests across the straits. Córdoba henceforth contested control of the western Maghreb, occupying Melilla and Ceuta and sustaining client rulers against Fatimid expansion.

Foreign relations

To the north, Abd al-Rahman campaigned repeatedly against the Christian kingdoms of León and Navarre; his armies were checked at the Battle of Simancas (939), after which he largely directed frontier warfare through subordinates. His court received embassies from Constantinople — the Byzantine emperor Constantine VII sent a celebrated illuminated manuscript of Dioscorides' De materia medica — and from Otto I of Germany, whose ambassador John of Gorze spent three years in Córdoba (953–956). Under Abd al-Rahman and his son, the Jewish courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut rose to a leading role in diplomacy and finance.

Court, building, and later years

From 936 the caliph built the vast palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra outside Córdoba, the seat of a court famed for its library, workshops, and ceremonial; its reception of ambassadors in the great Salon Rico became a byword for splendour. Córdoba itself grew to perhaps half a million inhabitants, with paved, lit streets and hundreds of mosques and baths. In his last years Abd al-Rahman was said to have counted, in a diary found after his death, only fourteen days of pure happiness in his long reign. He died on 15 October 961 and was succeeded by his son al-Hakam II, under whom the caliphate reached its cultural zenith.
