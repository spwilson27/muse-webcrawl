Fermat's spiral

Fermat's spiral (also known as a parabolic spiral) is a plane curve named after Pierre de Fermat, who described it in a 1636 letter. In polar coordinates it is given by r2 = a2θ, so the distance between successive turns decreases as the curve winds outward — the opposite behaviour to the Archimedean spiral.

Properties

The spiral consists of two branches, for positive and negative values of r, symmetrically arranged about the origin. The area between successive turns decreases with distance from the centre, and the curve is the pedal curve of a class of related spirals; it is closely connected with the lituus and the hyperbolic spiral through inversion.

Applications

Fermat's spiral models the arrangement of florets in sunflowers and other phyllotactic patterns: placing points at angles of 137.5° (the golden angle) along the spiral produces the densely packed, scale-free distributions seen in nature and used in computer graphics for sampling and distributing points on a disc. The pattern also appears in the design of spiral antennas and in models of galaxy arms.
