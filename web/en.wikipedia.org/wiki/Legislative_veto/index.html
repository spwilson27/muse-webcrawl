Legislative veto

A legislative veto is a provision in a statute by which a legislature reserves the power to override or block an action of the executive branch — such as an agency rule, a reorganisation plan or an arms sale — by resolution of one house, both houses, or even a committee, without presenting the matter to the head of state or government for approval. The device was widely used by the United States Congress in the twentieth century until the Supreme Court held it unconstitutional in INS v. Chadha (1983).

Use in the United States

Congress began attaching legislative vetoes to delegations of authority in the 1930s, starting with the 1932 reorganisation statute requested by President Hoover. By the early 1980s, veto provisions appeared in nearly 200 statutes covering rulemaking, war powers reporting, arms sales, spending rescissions and agency reorganisations. Supporters saw the device as an essential check on the growing administrative state; critics saw it as an unconstitutional shortcut around the requirements of bicameral passage and presentment to the President.

INS v. Chadha

In Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919 (1983), the Supreme Court, 7–2, struck down the one-house veto. Jagdish Rai Chadha, a student from Kenya, had been ordered deported; the Attorney General suspended the deportation, but the House of Representatives vetoed the suspension by resolution. Chief Justice Warren Burger's majority opinion held that action by one house that is legislative in purpose and effect must satisfy both bicameralism and the Presentment Clause of Article I. The decision technically invalidated legislative vetoes in scores of statutes, though many agencies continued to observe informal report-and-wait arrangements and congressional review practices as a matter of comity.

Subsequent practice

Congress adapted by substituting joint resolutions of disapproval (which are presented to the President and may be vetoed), embodied most prominently in the Congressional Review Act of 1996, and by relying on appropriations riders, oversight and statutory deadlines. State legislatures in the United States have used analogous committee vetoes over agency rules, with varying treatment under state constitutions.

Other countries

Similar devices exist elsewhere in attenuated forms: many parliaments exercise annulment or negative-resolution procedures over delegated legislation (as in the United Kingdom, where statutory instruments may be annulled by either House within a fixed period), though these operate under constitutional arrangements that do not require executive presentment.
