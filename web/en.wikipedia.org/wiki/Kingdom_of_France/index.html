The Kingdom of France was the monarchy that governed France from the accession of Hugh Capet in 987 until the abolition of the monarchy in 1792 during the French Revolution, and again under the Bourbon Restoration and July Monarchy in the 19th century. From a modest royal domain around Paris, it grew into one of Europe's most powerful and populous states.

Medieval kingdom

The early Capetian kings slowly extended royal authority over the great feudal principalities — Normandy, Anjou, Champagne — aided by the growth of royal administration and law. Philip II Augustus (r. 1180–1223) quadrupled the royal domain and defeated the Angevin empire; Louis IX (Saint Louis) made France a centre of Gothic culture and crusading. The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) against England devastated the kingdom but ended in French victory under Charles VII, aided by Joan of Arc.

Early modern and absolute monarchy

The Valois and then Bourbon kings weathered the Italian Wars and the Wars of Religion (1562–1598). Under Louis XIV (r. 1643–1715), the "Sun King", France became the model of absolute monarchy, centred on the palace of Versailles, with Europe's largest population and army and a global colonial empire. Fiscal strain, war debts, and social inequality under his successors culminated in the calling of the Estates-General in 1789 and the Revolution.

End and restoration

The constitutional monarchy of 1791 gave way to the Republic in September 1792; Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. The monarchy was restored in 1814–15 and finally ended with the revolution of 1848.
