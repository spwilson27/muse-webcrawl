User innovation

User innovation refers to innovation by consumers and end users, rather than by manufacturers or suppliers. Instead of passively accepting products as given, users — both firms and individual consumers — often develop new products, modify existing ones, or find new applications for them in order to meet their own needs.

Lead users

The concept was developed by economist Eric von Hippel of MIT, beginning with studies of scientific instruments in the 1970s. Von Hippel identified lead users as users who experience needs months or years before most of the market and who benefit strongly from a solution. Because needs are "sticky" — costly to transfer from the user who experiences them to a manufacturer — innovation frequently takes place at the user's site.

Examples

Well-documented cases include the mountain bike, developed by cycling enthusiasts in California before manufacturers adopted it; major innovations in semiconductor process equipment and scientific instruments; the open-source software movement, in which users write and freely reveal code; and sports equipment such as the planing windsurf board and snowboard.

Free revealing and toolkits

User innovators often reveal their designs freely, which can seed whole industries. Manufacturers can support the process by providing toolkits for user innovation and design — design environments that let customers develop and test custom solutions themselves, a strategy used for products from custom integrated circuits to video games.
