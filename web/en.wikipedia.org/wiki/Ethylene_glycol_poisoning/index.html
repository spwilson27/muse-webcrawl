Ethylene glycol poisoning

Ethylene glycol poisoning
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, toxicology
SymptomsEarly: intoxication, vomiting, abdominal pain. Later: decreased consciousness, headache, seizures, kidney failure
CausesDrinking ethylene glycol (e.g. antifreeze)
TreatmentAntidotes (fomepizole or ethanol), hemodialysis

Ethylene glycol poisoning is poisoning caused by drinking ethylene glycol, a sweet-tasting liquid most commonly encountered in automobile antifreeze. Early symptoms include intoxication resembling alcohol intoxication, vomiting and abdominal pain. Later symptoms may include a decreased level of consciousness, headache and seizures. Long-term outcomes may include kidney failure and brain damage. Toxicity and death may occur after drinking even a small amount.

Ethylene glycol itself is relatively non-toxic; poisoning results from its metabolites. The body converts it, via the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase, first to glycolaldehyde, then to glycolic acid, glyoxylic acid and finally oxalic acid. Oxalic acid combines with calcium to form calcium oxalate crystals, which deposit in the kidneys and other tissues, causing damage. The accumulation of glycolic acid also produces a severe metabolic acidosis.

Signs and symptoms

Poisoning is classically described in three stages. In the first stage (30 minutes to 12 hours after ingestion), the person appears intoxicated, with slurred speech, ataxia, nausea and vomiting. In the second stage (12 to 24 hours), heart and lung problems predominate, including rapid heart rate, high or low blood pressure and rapid breathing as the body compensates for acidosis. In the third stage (24 to 72 hours), acute kidney injury develops, with flank pain and reduced urine output. Calcium oxalate crystals may be visible in the urine under a microscope.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis may be suspected from the history of antifreeze ingestion, the presence of an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis and an osmolar gap, and calcium oxalate crystals in the urine. Definitive diagnosis requires measuring the ethylene glycol level in the blood, which may not be rapidly available. Because many antifreeze products contain fluorescein dye, the urine may fluoresce under a Wood's lamp, though this sign is unreliable.

Treatment

Treatment begins with stabilisation of the airway, breathing and circulation. The antidotes of choice block alcohol dehydrogenase so that ethylene glycol is excreted unchanged rather than converted to toxic metabolites. Fomepizole is the preferred antidote; ethanol, which competes for the same enzyme, is an alternative. Hemodialysis is used in severe cases to remove ethylene glycol and its metabolites and to correct acidosis and kidney failure. Sodium bicarbonate may be given for severe acidosis. Gastric decontamination is of limited benefit because ethylene glycol is absorbed rapidly.

Epidemiology

Thousands of cases of ethylene glycol exposure are reported to poison control centres in the United States each year, though deaths are comparatively uncommon when treatment is prompt. Poisoning may be accidental, particularly in children attracted by the sweet taste, or intentional. Animals, especially dogs and cats, are highly susceptible, and small amounts of spilled antifreeze can be fatal to pets.
