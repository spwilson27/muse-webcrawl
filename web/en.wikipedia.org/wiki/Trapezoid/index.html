Trapezoid

In geometry, a trapezoid (in American and Canadian English) or trapezium (in British and other English) is a quadrilateral that has at least one pair of parallel sides. The parallel sides are called the bases of the trapezoid, and the other two sides are called the legs (or lateral sides).

Terminology dispute

The meanings of the words trapezoid and trapezium are swapped between American and British usage, a confusion dating to an error by the lexicographer Charles Hutton in 1795. In American usage, a trapezoid has at least one pair of parallel sides and a trapezium has none; in traditional British usage it is the reverse. Additionally, definitions divide between the inclusive definition (at least one pair of parallel sides, so parallelograms count as trapezoids) and the exclusive definition (exactly one pair).

Special cases

  • Isosceles trapezoid — the legs are equal in length and the base angles are equal; it is cyclic and symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of the bases.
  • Right trapezoid — has two adjacent right angles.
  • Scalene trapezoid — no sides of equal length.
  • Tangential trapezoid — has an incircle tangent to all four sides.

Properties

The midsegment (median) of a trapezoid joins the midpoints of the legs and is parallel to the bases, with length equal to the average of the lengths of the bases. The two diagonals divide each other in proportion to the lengths of the bases. A trapezoid is a cyclic quadrilateral if and only if it is isosceles.

Area

The area of a trapezoid is half the sum of the lengths of the two parallel sides (bases) multiplied by the height (the perpendicular distance between them): A = (a + b)/2 × h. This formula underlies the trapezoidal rule for approximating integrals.
