Cognitive triad

Beck's cognitive triad: negative views of the self, the world and the future reinforce one another in depression.

The cognitive triad, also known as the negative triad, is a cognitive-therapeutic view of the three key elements of a person's belief system present in depression. It was proposed by psychiatrist Aaron T. Beck in 1967 as part of his cognitive theory of depression, and is a central concept in cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).

The three elements

The triad consists of unrealistically negative views about:

  • The self — "I am worthless and inadequate";
  • The world (or personal domain) — "The world is unfair and makes impossible demands";
  • The future — "Things will never improve; my suffering will continue."

These three patterns of thinking are held to be automatic, involuntary and mutually reinforcing, driving the low mood, withdrawal and inactivity characteristic of depressive episodes.

Treatment

CBT directed at depression seeks to identify the automatic thoughts reflecting each leg of the triad, test them against evidence through Socratic questioning and behavioural experiments, and restructure the underlying schemas. Related measures, such as the Cognitive Triad Inventory, assess the three domains for research and clinical monitoring.
