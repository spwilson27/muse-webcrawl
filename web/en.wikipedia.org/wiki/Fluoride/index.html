Fluoride

Fluoride is an inorganic, monatomic anion of fluorine with the chemical formula F−, whose salts are typically white or colourless. Fluoride contributes a distinctively bitter taste. It is a component of minerals such as fluorite (CaF₂), the principal source of the ion. In the body and in nature, fluoride is present in trace amounts.

Fluorite, a common fluoride mineral

Chemistry

Fluoride is the conjugate base of hydrogen fluoride, a weak acid. It is the smallest halide ion and is strongly solvated in water. Its salts span a wide range of solubilities: sodium fluoride is moderately soluble, while calcium fluoride (fluorite) is nearly insoluble — a property important in geochemistry and biology. Industrially, fluorides are produced from fluorite and from fluorosilicic acid, a by-product of phosphate fertiliser manufacture.

Dental health

Fluoride promotes the remineralisation of tooth enamel and the formation of fluorapatite, which is more resistant to acid attack by oral bacteria. For this reason it is added to toothpaste and, in many communities, to drinking water (water fluoridation) to prevent dental caries. Excess intake during tooth development can cause dental fluorosis, mottling of the enamel; very high doses over long periods can cause skeletal fluorosis.

Other uses

Fluoride compounds are used in the production of aluminium, in steel-making fluxes, in glass and enamel manufacture, and (as uranium hexafluoride chemistry) in the nuclear fuel cycle. Stannous fluoride and sodium fluoride are common active ingredients in oral-care products.

Occurrence

Fluoride occurs naturally in water, soil, and foods such as tea. In some regions, notably parts of India, China, and the East African Rift, natural levels in groundwater are high enough to pose a fluorosis risk, and defluoridation is necessary.
