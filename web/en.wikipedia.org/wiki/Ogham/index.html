Ogham (Modern Irish: [ˈoː(ə)mˠ]; Old Irish: ogam [ˈɔɣamˠ]) is an Early Medieval alphabet used primarily to write the early Irish language (in the "orthodox" inscriptions, 4th to 6th centuries AD) and later the Old Irish language (orthodox ogham, 6th to 9th centuries). There are roughly 400 surviving orthodox inscriptions on stone monuments throughout Ireland and western Britain.

The alphabet

The ogham alphabet consists of twenty characters (feda) arranged in four groups (aicmí) of five, formed from notches and strokes cut along or across a central stemline — in stone inscriptions, the edge of the stone itself. Five additional letters, the forfeda, were devised later in the manuscript tradition. Medieval tradition associated each letter with a tree name, giving ogham the popular name of the "Celtic tree alphabet".

Inscriptions

Most inscriptions are short memorial formulae giving a name and ancestry, typically "X son of Y". The great majority are found in the south of Ireland (Kerry, Cork and Waterford), with others in Wales, the Isle of Man, Scotland and England, some of the Scottish examples being in Pictish. The earliest inscriptions date to about the 4th century AD and represent the earliest written record of the Irish language.
