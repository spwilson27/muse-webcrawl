Receiver (legal)

A receiver is a person appointed by a court, a government regulator, or a secured creditor to take custody and control of the property or business of another party, typically when that party is in financial distress, is the subject of litigation, or is unable to manage its own affairs. The process is known as receivership.

Appointment and powers

Receivers act as officers of the court and fiduciaries for all interested parties rather than agents of any single creditor. Depending on the appointing instrument, a receiver may operate a business as a going concern, preserve assets pending litigation, collect rents and debts, or liquidate property and distribute the proceeds. Court-appointed receivers are common in shareholder disputes, fraud cases, matrimonial litigation and the enforcement of judgments.

Types

In the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth jurisdictions, an administrative receiver was historically appointed by the holder of a floating charge over substantially all of a company's assets; reforms under the Enterprise Act 2002 largely replaced this with administration for later charges. In the United States, receiverships are used by federal agencies such as the Securities and Exchange Commission to take over fraudulent enterprises, and by courts in mortgage foreclosure and partnership dissolution cases. Government receivers also manage failed institutions in some regulatory regimes, complementing bankruptcy proceedings.

Comparison with bankruptcy

Unlike a bankruptcy trustee, a receiver is not necessarily bound by a statutory scheme of distribution and may be appointed over particular assets rather than an entire estate. Receivership can be faster and more flexible than bankruptcy, but it lacks some of bankruptcy's protections, such as the automatic stay.
