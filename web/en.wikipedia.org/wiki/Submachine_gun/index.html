Submachine gun

A submachine gun (SMG) is a magazine-fed automatic carbine designed to fire handgun cartridges. The term was coined by John T. Thompson, inventor of the Thompson submachine gun, to describe an automatic firearm firing pistol ammunition, in contrast to a "machine gun" firing rifle cartridges. Submachine guns combine automatic fire with compact size, making them effective in close-quarters combat.

History

The first practical submachine gun was the German MP 18, introduced in 1918 during the First World War. The interwar Thompson became famous in the United States, and during the Second World War all major armies fielded inexpensive stamped-metal designs such as the British Sten, Soviet PPSh-41, German MP 40, and American M3 "Grease Gun," produced in the millions.

Modern use

After the Second World War, assault rifles took over the general infantry role, but submachine guns such as the Israeli Uzi and the German Heckler & Koch MP5 remained standard for special forces, vehicle crews, and police units. Since the late 20th century, many SMG roles have been taken over by short-barrelled carbines and personal defence weapons.
