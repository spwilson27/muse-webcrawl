Riddle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A riddle is a statement, question or phrase having a double or veiled meaning, put forth as a puzzle to be solved. Riddles typically take two main forms: enigmas, which express a problem metaphorically and require ingenuity to answer, and conundrums, whose effects rest on punning in the question or its answer. They are found in the literature and oral traditions of nearly every culture.

History

Among the oldest written riddles are those preserved in Babylonian school texts and in the ancient Egyptian Rhind Mathematical Papyrus's playful problems. In Greek tradition, the Sphinx posed her riddle to travellers to Thebes — "What goes on four feet in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?" — devouring those who failed, until Oedipus answered "Man." The biblical Samson posed a riddle to the Philistines at his wedding feast, and the Queen of Sheba tested Solomon with "hard questions."

In the Old English Exeter Book of the 10th century, some ninety riddles survive, many of them describing everyday objects in the first person through layered metaphor. Riddle contests also feature in Norse myth — Odin wagers his head in a contest of knowledge with the giant Vafthrudnir, and in the Hervarar saga King Heidrek is posed riddles — and in modern fantasy, notably the riddle game between Bilbo Baggins and Gollum in The Hobbit.

Structure and function

Folklorists analyse riddles as a descriptive element (often a deliberately misleading description made of several traits) and a block element that prevents the obvious answer. Riddles serve as entertainment, as tests of wisdom and status, as teaching devices, and in some traditions as ritual contests. They survive today in children's collections, jokes ("Why did the chicken…?" being a riddle-joke), puzzle books and escape rooms.
