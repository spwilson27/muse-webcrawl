Hurrians

The Hurrians (cuneiform: 𒄷𒌨𒊑, Ḫu-ur-ri) were a people who inhabited the Ancient Near East during the Bronze Age. They spoke Hurrian, a language isolate related only to Urartian, and occupied a broad arc from northern Mesopotamia and Anatolia to the Levant. Hurrian-speaking communities are attested from the late 3rd millennium BCE, and in the mid-2nd millennium BCE they formed the powerful kingdom of Mitanni.

History

Early Hurrian centres included Urkesh (Tell Mozan) in the Khabur headwaters, one of the oldest known Hurrian cities, flourishing around 2300 BCE. By the 17th–16th centuries BCE Hurrian principalities across northern Mesopotamia were united into the kingdom of Mitanni, which contested supremacy with Egypt, the Hittites, and Assyria. Its capital, Washukanni, has never been securely located. Mitanni's kings corresponded with pharaohs as equals in the Amarna letters, and dynastic marriages linked the two courts. After about 1345 BCE Mitanni declined under Hittite and Assyrian pressure; its eastern rump, Hanigalbat, was finally annexed by Assyria in the 13th century BCE.

Culture and religion

Hurrians transmitted much Mesopotamian literature and religion across the Near East and served as a conduit of Babylonian learning to the Hittites. Their chief gods were the storm god Teshub and his consort Hebat, along with Shaushka and the twin bulls Seri and Hurri. Hurrian myths, including the Kumarbi cycle, strongly influenced Hittite and, indirectly, Greek mythology. The oldest known notated musical composition, the Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal (c. 1400 BCE) from Ugarit, preserves a melody in cuneiform notation.

Language

Hurrian is an ergative, agglutinative language unrelated to the Semitic and Indo-European languages around it; with Urartian it forms the small Hurro-Urartian family. It was written chiefly in cuneiform, and a long Hurrian letter from King Tushratta of Mitanni to Amenhotep III is a key source for its grammar.
