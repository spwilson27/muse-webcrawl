Pistachio

The pistachio (Pistacia vera) is a member of the cashew family, Anacardiaceae. It is a small to medium-sized tree originating in Persia (Iran). The tree produces seeds that are widely consumed as food — roasted and salted in the shell as a snack, or used in desserts such as baklava and ice cream. The kernel is distinctive for its green colour, caused by chlorophyll retained in the seed.

Cultivation

Pistachio trees are dioecious — male and female flowers grow on separate trees — and require long, hot summers and cold winters. Iran, the United States (mainly California), and Turkey are the leading producers. Trees take about five to seven years to begin bearing and reach full production after 15–20 years; they bear heavily in alternate years, a trait known as alternate bearing.

History

Archaeological evidence shows pistachios were eaten in central Asia from at least 6,750 BC. Legend associates them with the Queen of Sheba. The Romans introduced pistachios to Europe in the 1st century AD, and the nut became established in Mediterranean cultivation before being brought to California in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Nutrition and uses

Pistachios are rich in protein, fibre, vitamin B6, thiamine, and antioxidants. The kernels are eaten roasted, used in confectionery and Middle Eastern and Mediterranean cooking, and pressed for oil. The vivid green pistachios of Bronte, Sicily, and of Iran are especially prized.
