Icosahedron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regular icosahedron

Faces: 20 equilateral triangles

Edges: 30

Vertices: 12

Dual: Regular dodecahedron

In geometry, an icosahedron is a polyhedron with 20 faces. The name comes from Ancient Greek eíkosi (εἴκοσι) "twenty" and hédra (ἕδρα) "seat". The most familiar form is the regular icosahedron, one of the five Platonic solids, whose faces are 20 congruent equilateral triangles meeting five at each of its 12 vertices.

Properties

The regular icosahedron has Schläfli symbol {3,5} and symmetry group of order 120, the icosahedral group, shared with the dodecahedron, its dual polyhedron. Its vertices can be given elegantly using the golden ratio φ: the 12 vertices are the cyclic permutations of (0, ±1, ±φ). For edge length a, the surface area is 5√3 a² and the volume is (5/12)(3 + √5) a³. The dihedral angle is approximately 138.19°.

Occurrences

Icosahedral symmetry appears widely in nature: many viruses, including herpesviruses and adenoviruses, have icosahedral capsids; the mineral pyrite forms pseudo-icosahedral crystals; and radiolarian skeletons described by Ernst Haeckel display the form. Twenty-sided dice (d20) are icosahedra, and geodesic domes approximate the shape by subdividing its faces.

Related forms

Truncating, stellating or subdividing the icosahedron yields many Archimedean and Johnson solids, including the truncated icosahedron familiar as the pattern of a football (soccer ball) and the structure of buckminsterfullerene (C60), which is based on the same geometry.
