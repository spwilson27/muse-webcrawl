Magyars (Hungarian: magyarok), also known as Hungarians, are a nation and ethnic group native to Hungary (Magyarország) and historical Hungarian lands who share a common culture, history, ancestry, and language. The Hungarian language belongs to the Ugric branch of the Uralic language family, making it unrelated to the Indo-European languages of Hungary's neighbours. There are an estimated 14–15 million ethnic Hungarians worldwide, of whom about 9.5 million live in Hungary itself; significant minorities live in Romania (especially Transylvania and the Székely Land), Slovakia, Serbia, and Ukraine, with large diaspora communities in the United States, Canada, Germany, and elsewhere.

Origins and migration

The Magyars emerged from the Uralic-speaking peoples of the forest-steppe east of the Ural Mountains and migrated westward across the Eurasian steppe over many centuries, interacting with Turkic and Iranian peoples — from whom they adopted elements of steppe culture, animal husbandry, and vocabulary. By the ninth century the Magyar tribal federation, led by the Grand Prince Álmos and later his son Árpád, occupied the steppe north of the Black Sea (Etelköz). Pressured by the Pechenegs and Bulgars, the Magyars crossed the Carpathians in about 895–896 and conquered the Carpathian Basin, the event known in Hungarian as the Honfoglalás ("conquest of the homeland"), traditionally marking the founding of Hungary.

The raiding era and state formation

For half a century Magyar horsemen raided widely across Europe, from France and Italy to Iberia and the Byzantine Empire, until defeats — decisively at the Battle of Lechfeld in 955 against Otto I — ended the incursions in the west. Under Grand Prince Géza and his son Stephen, the Magyars settled, adopted Christianity, and organised a kingdom: Stephen I was crowned king in 1000 or 1001 and later canonised as Saint Stephen, founder of the Hungarian state. The Árpád dynasty he established ruled until 1301.

Identity and culture

Magyar identity centres on the Hungarian language, with its rich literary tradition from János Arany and Sándor Petőfi to modern authors, and on distinctive folk culture, music, and cuisine. Historical traumas — the Mongol invasion of 1241–42, Ottoman occupation of central Hungary in the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries, the crushed revolutions of 1848–49 and 1956, and the Treaty of Trianon (1920), which left a third of ethnic Hungarians outside Hungary's new borders — remain powerful elements of national memory.
