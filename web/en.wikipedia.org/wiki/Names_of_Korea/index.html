Names of Korea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The names of Korea derive from a series of historic states that ruled the Korean Peninsula. The English name "Korea" comes from Goryeo (고려), the dynasty that ruled from 918 to 1392, itself named for the ancient kingdom of Goguryeo. In Korean, the two modern states use different names: South Korea is Hanguk (한국, "Han country"), short for Daehan Minguk (대한민국, "Great Han People's Nation"), while North Korea is Joseon (조선), formally Joseon Minjujuui Inmin Gonghwaguk (조선민주주의인민공화국, "Joseon Democratic People's Republic").

Historical names

Early names include those of the Three Kingdoms — Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla. After unification, Goryeo gave the peninsula the name known to medieval Western and Persian merchants as "Corea". The succeeding dynasty, Joseon (1392–1897), revived an ancient name associated with Gojoseon, the legendary first Korean kingdom. In 1897 King Gojong proclaimed the Daehan Jeguk (Korean Empire), introducing "Han" (한) — from the Samhan confederacies of the south — into the state's official name.

Division and modern usage

After the division of 1945, the South adopted "Hanguk" while the North retained "Joseon", so the two states' Korean names for the peninsula itself differ: Hanbando in the South and Joseonbando in the North. Each side traditionally used its own name for the other (Bukhan, "North Han"; Namjoseon, "South Joseon"), though usage has softened in periods of reconciliation. In Chinese and Japanese the names reflect the same split (Hánguó vs. Cháoxiǎn; Kankoku vs. Chōsen).
