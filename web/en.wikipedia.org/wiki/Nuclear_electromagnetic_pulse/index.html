Nuclear electromagnetic pulse

A nuclear electromagnetic pulse (nEMP) is a burst of electromagnetic radiation created by a nuclear explosion. The resulting rapidly varying electric and magnetic fields can couple to electrical and electronic systems, inducing damaging currents and voltage spikes. A high-altitude detonation produces a high-altitude electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) capable of affecting infrastructure over a continental scale.

Mechanism and waveform

Gamma rays from the explosion ionise the upper atmosphere (Compton scattering), and the resulting electrons, deflected by the Earth's magnetic field, radiate a coherent electromagnetic pulse. International standards describe the HEMP waveform in three components: E1, a very fast, intense pulse (nanoseconds) that can break down insulation and damage electronics; E2, resembling lightning, lasting up to about a second; and E3, a slow, magnetohydrodynamic phase (seconds to minutes) that drives quasi-DC currents into long conductors such as power grids, akin to a severe geomagnetic storm.

Historical observations and concern

The 1962 Starfish Prime high-altitude test damaged streetlights and communications in Hawaii some 1,400 km away, and Soviet tests over Kazakhstan induced fires in power systems. Because modern economies depend on microelectronics and long transmission networks, EMP has been the subject of governmental commissions and hardening programmes for military systems and critical infrastructure. Assessments of the likely severity and feasibility of deliberate EMP attack remain debated among experts.
