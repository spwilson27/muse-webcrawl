Alexander Mitchell Palmer (4 May 1872 – 11 May 1936) was an American attorney and politician who served as the 50th United States Attorney General from 1919 to 1921 under President Woodrow Wilson. He is remembered chiefly for the "Palmer Raids" of 1919–1920, a series of mass arrests and deportations of suspected radicals carried out during the First Red Scare.

Early life and Congress

Palmer was born in White Haven, Pennsylvania, and educated at Swarthmore College, where he was a member of the debate team and graduated in 1891. He studied law and built a successful legal practice in Stroudsburg, becoming active in the Democratic Party. Palmer served three terms in the U.S. House of Representatives (1909–1915), where he supported progressive causes including women's suffrage and restrictions on child labour. Declining appointment as Secretary of War on pacifist and Quaker grounds, he served during the First World War as Alien Property Custodian, administering German-owned assets seized in the United States.

Attorney General and the Palmer Raids

Appointed Attorney General in March 1919, Palmer responded to a wave of anarchist mail bombs — one of which exploded on his own doorstep in June 1919 — by creating the General Intelligence Division of the Bureau of Investigation under the young J. Edgar Hoover. In November 1919 and again on 2 January 1920, federal agents and local police arrested thousands of suspected communists, anarchists and union members, many held without warrants or counsel. Hundreds of non-citizens were deported, including Emma Goldman, who sailed to Russia aboard the USAT Buford, the so-called "Soviet Ark".

Public support collapsed after Palmer's predicted May Day 1920 revolutionary uprising failed to materialise, and a report by twelve prominent lawyers condemned the raids as unconstitutional. Assistant Secretary of Labor Louis Freeland Post cancelled most of the deportation warrants. Palmer's bid for the 1920 Democratic presidential nomination failed, and he left office in 1921.

Later life

Palmer returned to private law practice in Washington, D.C., remaining active in Democratic politics. He died in 1936.
