The Stockholm Bloodbath (Swedish: Stockholms blodbad) was a trial that led to a series of executions in Stockholm between 7 and 9 November 1520. The event is also known as the Stockholm massacre. The events took place after the coronation of Christian II of Denmark as king of Sweden, when guests at the coronation festivities were invited to a meeting at Tre Kronor Castle and, on the orders of the king acting through Archbishop Gustav Trolle, around 82 people — including nobles, bishops, burgomasters and citizens who had supported the Sture party — were executed for heresy.

Background

Sweden was formally part of the Kalmar Union but had been governed in practice by regents of the Sture family resisting Danish domination. Christian II invaded Sweden to enforce his claim; after the death of the regent Sten Sture the Younger at the Battle of Bogesund in 1520, Stockholm surrendered on terms promising an amnesty. Christian was crowned hereditary king of Sweden on 4 November 1520.

The trial and executions

Archbishop Gustav Trolle, who had been deposed and imprisoned by the Sture regime and whose castle had been demolished, demanded compensation and prosecuted his enemies for heresy — a charge that conveniently placed them outside the promised amnesty. A tribunal convicted the accused after a closed proceeding. On 8 and 9 November, dozens were beheaded in the main square, Stortorget; others were hanged. The bodies were later burned, and the corpse of Sten Sture was exhumed and burned as a heretic.

Aftermath

The bloodbath destroyed Christian's legitimacy in Sweden and directly provoked the Swedish War of Liberation led by Gustav Vasa, whose father Erik Johansson Vasa was among those executed. Sweden permanently left the Kalmar Union in 1523, and Christian earned the epithet "Christian the Tyrant" in Swedish memory, while Danish historiography has sometimes called him "Christian the Good". The famous woodcut known as Blodbadsplanschen (1524) is one of the earliest images of Stockholm.
