Chick lit

Chick lit is a genre of popular fiction marketed primarily to women, typically featuring young urban professional heroines navigating careers, friendships and romantic relationships with humour and a light, confessional tone. The genre boomed in the mid-1990s and 2000s, characterised by bright pastel covers and bestsellers such as Helen Fielding's Bridget Jones's Diary (1996) and Sophie Kinsella's Confessions of a Shopaholic (2000).

Origins

The term "chick lit" was used ironically as the title of the 1995 anthology Chick Lit: Postfeminist Fiction, edited by Cris Mazza and Jeffrey DeShell, before being adopted by publishers as a marketing label. The genre's commercial breakthrough came with Bridget Jones's Diary, which sold millions of copies and spawned a film series, and Candace Bushnell's Sex and the City, adapted into the hit HBO series.

Characteristics and criticism

Typical chick lit centres on a heroine in her twenties or thirties, written in first person with a witty, self-deprecating voice, addressing work, dating, weight, shopping and female friendship. Critics have alternately praised the genre for candidly representing young women's lives and dismissed it as formulaic and consumerist; some authors have rejected the label as trivialising, arguing that comparable books by men are marketed as literary or comic fiction. By the 2010s publishers largely retired the term in favour of "women's fiction" or romantic comedy, though its conventions persist widely.
