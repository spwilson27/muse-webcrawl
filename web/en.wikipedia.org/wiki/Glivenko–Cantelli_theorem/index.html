Glivenko–Cantelli theorem

In the theory of probability, the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem (sometimes referred to as the fundamental theorem of statistics), named after Valery Ivanovich Glivenko and Francesco Paolo Cantelli, describes the asymptotic behaviour of the empirical distribution function as the number of independent and identically distributed observations grows. Specifically, the empirical distribution function converges uniformly, with probability one, to the true cumulative distribution function.

Formally, if X1, X2, … are independent and identically distributed random variables with cumulative distribution function F(x), and the empirical distribution function is Fn(x), then the theorem states that the supremum over x of |Fn(x) − F(x)| converges to zero almost surely as n tends to infinity.

Significance

The theorem justifies the use of the empirical distribution function as an estimator of the underlying distribution and underpins much of nonparametric statistics. The rate of convergence is studied in related results such as the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality, which bounds deviations of the empirical distribution function from the true distribution.
