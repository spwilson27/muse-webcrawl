Urdu poetry


A mushaira (poetry symposium)

Urdu poetry (Urdu: اُردُو شاعری) is a rich tradition of verse in the Urdu language, developed from the seventeenth century onward in the courts and salons of the Deccan, Delhi and Lucknow. It is performed and enjoyed at gatherings called mushairas, where poets recite to an appreciative audience that responds with cries of vāh vāh (bravo).

Forms

The dominant form is the ghazal, a sequence of autonomous rhyming couplets (sher) on themes of love, loss and wine, unified by a refrain (radif) and rhyme (qafia), closing with the poet's pen-name (takhallus). Other classical forms include the masnavi (long narrative poem), the marsiya (elegy for the martyrs of Karbala, perfected at Lucknow by Anis and Dabir), the qasida (ode), the rubai (quatrain) and the nazm (thematic poem, the vehicle of much modern verse).

Major figures

Early masters include Wali Deccani, Mir Taqi Mir — called Khuda-e-Sukhan ("God of poetry") — and Mirza Muhammad Rafi Sauda. The nineteenth century saw the rivalry of Mirza Ghalib and Ibrahim Zauq at the last Mughal court. In the modern period Muhammad Iqbal recast poetry as philosophical and political exhortation, while Faiz Ahmad Faiz fused classical imagery with progressive politics. Women poets such as Parveen Shakir and prose-poets and satirists extended the tradition, and the ghazal was popularised in music by singers like Mehdi Hassan and Jagjit Singh.
