Hippolytus of Rome

Saint Hippolytus
Bornc. 170
Diedc. 235, Sardinia
Feast13 August (Roman Catholic); 30 January (Orthodox)

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170 – c. 235) was one of the most prolific early Christian theologians and a leading figure in the church of Rome in the early 3rd century. He is often described as an antipope — a rival bishop of Rome — in opposition to Pope Callixtus I and his successors, though he was later reconciled and died a martyr; he is venerated as a saint in both the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches.

Life

A presbyter at Rome, Hippolytus attacked Pope Callixtus for leniency in readmitting sinners and for alleged doctrinal laxity, and led a schismatic congregation. In 235, during the persecution under Maximinus Thrax, he and Pope Pontian were exiled to the mines of Sardinia, where both died; tradition holds that they were reconciled before death. Their relics were brought back to Rome under Pope Fabian.

Works

Hippolytus wrote in Greek. His major writings include the Refutation of All Heresies (Philosophoumena), the Apostolic Tradition — a crucial source for early Christian liturgy and church order, whose attribution to him is debated — a Chronicle of world history, commentaries including the earliest surviving Christian commentary on Daniel, a treatise on the Antichrist, and a cycle of Easter tables inscribed on his statue, discovered in 1551.
