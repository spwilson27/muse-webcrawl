Alcubierre drive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Visualisation of an Alcubierre warp bubble: space compressed ahead of a spacecraft and expanded behind it]
The Alcubierre metric: a bubble of warped spacetime.

The Alcubierre drive is a speculative warp-drive idea in general relativity, proposed by Mexican physicist Miguel Alcubierre in 1994, by which a spacecraft could achieve apparent faster-than-light travel if a configurable energy-density field lower than that of vacuum (negative mass–energy) could be created. Rather than moving through space, the craft would ride inside a 'warp bubble': spacetime contracts in front of it and expands behind, carrying the bubble — and the ship, locally at rest and experiencing no time dilation or relativistic mass increase — faster than light relative to the outside.

The catch is the energy condition: early estimates required negative energy exceeding the mass-energy of the observable universe; refinements (van den Broeck and others) reduced the requirement enormously but still demand exotic matter that may not exist, in configurations quantum inequalities probably forbid at the needed scale, and the bubble's interior cannot be controlled from inside (the 'horizon problem') nor created from subluminal beginnings. NASA's Eagleworks laboratory explored interferometric tests without confirmed results. The Alcubierre metric remains a legitimate solution-generating exercise in relativity — valuable chiefly as a probe of energy conditions and causality, not an engineering programme.

Causality concerns

Like traversable wormholes, a superluminal warp bubble can be combined with relativity of simultaneity to build closed timelike curves — time machines — subject to Hawking's chronology protection conjecture. The drive is thus less a propulsion concept than a stress test of general relativity's permissiveness, repeatedly teaching the same lesson: the theory allows the geometry, and quantum physics seems to forbid the ingredients.
