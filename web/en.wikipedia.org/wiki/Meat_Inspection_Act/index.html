Federal Meat Inspection Act

Meat Inspection Act of 1906
Enacted: 30 June 1906 (signed by Theodore Roosevelt)
Statute: 34 Stat. 669; codified at 21 U.S.C. § 601 et seq.
Administered by: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service

The Federal Meat Inspection Act of 1906 (FMIA) is a United States Congress Act that works to prevent adulterated or misbranded meat and meat products from being sold as food and to ensure that meat and meat products are slaughtered and processed under strictly regulated sanitary conditions. These requirements also apply to imported meat products, which must be inspected under equivalent foreign standards.

Background

Public outrage over conditions in Chicago's stockyards and packing plants — crystallised by Upton Sinclair's 1906 novel The Jungle, which described diseased meat, filthy plants and exploited immigrant labour — spurred Congress to act. President Theodore Roosevelt commissioned the Neill–Reynolds report, which confirmed the revolting conditions, and signed the Act on 30 June 1906, the same day as the Pure Food and Drug Act. The large packers, fearing loss of export markets, ultimately supported federal inspection as a badge of quality.

Provisions

The Act requires US Department of Agriculture inspection of all cattle, sheep, swine, goats and horses slaughtered for human food in interstate or foreign commerce, both before slaughter (antemortem) and after (postmortem, of each carcass). It mandates sanitary standards for packing houses, condemns adulterated or misbranded products, and authorises inspectors' continuous presence in slaughter plants. Amendments, notably the Wholesome Meat Act of 1967, extended equivalent standards to intrastate plants and state inspection programs.

Modern administration

Inspection is carried out today by the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS), whose inspectors examine every carcass in federally inspected establishments; since the 1990s the system has been supplemented by HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) requirements placing primary food-safety planning duties on the plants themselves.
