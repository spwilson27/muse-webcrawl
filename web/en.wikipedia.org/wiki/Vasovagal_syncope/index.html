Vasovagal syncope

Vasovagal syncope
Also calledNeurocardiogenic syncope, common faint
SpecialtyCardiology, neurology
SymptomsLight-headedness, nausea, sweating, pallor, brief loss of consciousness
MechanismVagus nerve–mediated bradycardia and vasodilation

Vasovagal syncope is the most common cause of fainting. It occurs when the body overreacts to certain triggers — such as the sight of blood, severe emotional distress, or prolonged standing — causing a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure through overactivity of the vagus nerve and withdrawal of sympathetic tone. The resulting fall in blood flow to the brain produces a brief loss of consciousness, usually lasting less than a minute.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes are typically preceded by a prodrome: light-headedness, nausea, warmth, sweating, pallor, tunnel vision, and muffled hearing. If the person lies down, the faint may be averted. After consciousness returns — usually within seconds once the person is horizontal — there may be lingering fatigue, nausea and weakness. Brief myoclonic jerks can occur and are sometimes mistaken for seizure activity.

Triggers and mechanism

Common triggers include standing for long periods (especially in heat), pain, venipuncture or the sight of blood, emotional stress, coughing, straining, and urination (micturition syncope). The reflex is thought to begin with vigorous contraction of an under-filled ventricle, which activates cardiac mechanoreceptors; the brainstem misreads this as hypertension and responds with a paradoxical surge of vagal activity (bradycardia) and peripheral vasodilation — the Bezold–Jarisch reflex — collapsing blood pressure.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the characteristic history and the exclusion of cardiac causes; a tilt table test can reproduce the reflex. Most patients need only reassurance, trigger avoidance, adequate hydration and salt intake, and physical counter-pressure manoeuvres such as leg-crossing and muscle tensing at the onset of symptoms. Recurrent cases may be treated with midodrine or fludrocortisone; pacemakers are reserved for selected patients with severe cardioinhibitory responses, particularly older adults.

Prognosis

Vasovagal syncope is benign in itself and does not increase mortality, though falls during episodes can cause injury, and recurrent faints can impair quality of life and restrict activities such as driving.
