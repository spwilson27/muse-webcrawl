Explicit memory

Explicit memory (or declarative memory) is one of the two main types of long-term human memory, the other being implicit (procedural) memory. Explicit memory is the conscious, intentional recollection of factual information, previous experiences, and concepts. It can be divided into semantic memory — general facts and knowledge about the world — and episodic memory — memory of personally experienced events situated in time and place.

History and evidence

The distinction emerged from studies of amnesia, notably the case of patient H.M., who after bilateral removal of medial temporal lobe structures in 1953 could no longer form new explicit memories, yet could still learn new motor skills he did not remember practising. Brenda Milner, William Scoville, and later Larry Squire and Endel Tulving established that explicit memory depends critically on the hippocampus and related medial temporal structures, whereas implicit memory relies on other systems such as the basal ganglia and cerebellum.

Characteristics

Explicit memories are accessible to conscious recall and can usually be verbalised ("declared"). They are encoded with the involvement of the hippocampal system, consolidated over time, and are typically assessed with recall and recognition tests. Explicit memory declines in normal ageing and is severely impaired in Alzheimer's disease and other conditions affecting the medial temporal lobe.
