Tow-in surfing

Tow-in surfing: a personal watercraft tows the surfer up to speed so he can catch a wave too large and fast to paddle into.

Tow-in surfing is a surfing technique developed in the 1990s in which a surfer is towed into a large, fast-moving wave by a partner driving a personal watercraft (jet ski) or a helicopter. The technique allows surfers to ride waves far bigger than those that can be caught by paddling alone, and it revolutionized big wave surfing, making waves of 50 feet and more rideable.

History

The technique was pioneered in Hawaii in the early 1990s by surfers including Laird Hamilton, Dave Kalama, Darrick Doerner and Buzzy Kerbox, who sought a way to ride the enormous outer-reef waves at Peʻahi ("Jaws") on Maui. Because such waves travel too fast for a paddling surfer to match their speed, the tow rope and jet ski provided the necessary momentum. The method was controversial at first, with some purists regarding it as an illegitimate use of machinery, but it quickly became the standard approach to the world's largest surf.

Technique

The tow-in team consists of a surfer and a jet ski driver. The surfer holds a tow rope while standing on a small, heavily weighted surfboard equipped with foot straps. The driver accelerates alongside the forming wave and releases the surfer at the right moment; the surfer then lets go of the rope and rides the wave using the board's straps for control during steep drops and aerial maneuvers. After the ride, the driver returns to pick up the surfer before the next set arrives — a rescue role that is essential in heavy surf.

Equipment

Tow-in boards are shorter, narrower and heavier than ordinary big-wave "guns," with foot straps similar to those on windsurf boards. Personal watercraft used for towing are fitted with rescue sleds — flat platforms towed behind the ski on which a surfer can lie during pickups in the impact zone.

Safety

Tow-in surfing is extremely dangerous. Surfers train with breath-holding and underwater rock running, and modern safety practice includes inflatable buoyancy vests that can bring an unconscious surfer to the surface. Several surfers have died riding giant waves, underscoring the risks of wipeouts in waves with faces of 60 feet or more.

Notable locations

  • Peʻahi (Jaws), Maui, Hawaii
  • Nazaré, Portugal, site of the largest waves ever ridden
  • Mavericks, California
  • Teahupoʻo, Tahiti
  • Cortes Bank, off California
