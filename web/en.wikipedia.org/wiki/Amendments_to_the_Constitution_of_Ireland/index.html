Amendments to the Constitution of Ireland

The Constitution of Ireland, adopted in 1937, may be amended only by referendum, in which a proposal approved by the Oireachtas (parliament) is put to a vote of the people. Since 1937, there have been dozens of amendments, covering matters from European integration to social questions such as divorce, abortion, and marriage equality.

Procedure

Under Article 46, an amendment is initiated as a bill in Dáil Éireann, must be passed by both houses of the Oireachtas, and is then submitted to the people. It is enacted if a majority of valid votes cast favour it. The President of Ireland must sign the bill into law and has no power to refuse once the referendum has carried.

Notable amendments

The First Amendment (1939) extended the definition of "time of war". The Third Amendment (1972) permitted Ireland to join the European Communities. The Fifth Amendment (1972) removed the "special position" of the Catholic Church. Later referendums approved divorce (Fifteenth Amendment, 1995), abolished the death penalty (Twenty-first, 2001), approved the Nice and Lisbon treaties at second attempts, introduced marriage equality (Thirty-fourth, 2015) — the first country to do so by popular vote — and repealed the constitutional ban on abortion (Thirty-sixth, 2018). In 2019 the Thirty-eighth Amendment eased the constitutional regulation of divorce.

Failed proposals

Not all proposals pass: voters rejected two attempts to change the electoral system (1959, 1968), the first Nice (2001) and Lisbon (2008) treaty referendums, the proposed abolition of the Seanad and a lowering of the presidential age (2013 and 2015), and, in March 2024, proposals on the family and on care.
