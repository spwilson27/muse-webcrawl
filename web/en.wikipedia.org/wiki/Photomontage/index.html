Photomontage

Photomontage is the process and the result of making a composite photograph by cutting, gluing, rearranging and overlapping two or more photographs into a new image. Sometimes the resulting composite image is photographed so that the final image may appear as a seamless physical print. Today, similar results are achieved with image-editing software, and the technique is more commonly termed compositing.

History

Combination printing began in the mid-19th century, when photographers such as Oscar Gustave Rejlander ("The Two Ways of Life," 1857) and Henry Peach Robinson combined multiple negatives to overcome technical limitations and to create allegorical scenes. Spirit photographers and Victorian hobbyists also produced playful composite portraits.

Dada and political montage

During and after the First World War, Berlin Dadaists—including Hannah Höch, Raoul Hausmann, and John Heartfield—claimed to have invented photomontage as an art of cut-and-pasted fragments drawn from the mass-media press. Heartfield's anti-Nazi montages for the magazine AIZ in the 1930s made the technique a weapon of political satire. In the Soviet Union, Alexander Rodchenko, Gustav Klutsis, and El Lissitzky used photomontage for revolutionary posters and book design, while Surrealists exploited its uncanny juxtapositions.

Techniques

Traditional photomontage involves cutting prints with scissors or scalpel, pasting them onto a support, retouching seams, and often re-photographing the composite. Darkroom methods include sandwiching negatives, multiple exposure, and combination printing. Digital tools allow layers, masks, and blending, making seamless composites routine in advertising, film, and art.

Contemporary use

Photomontage remains common in political posters, album covers, editorial illustration, and contemporary art. Its capacity to fabricate convincing imagery underlies modern debates about photographic truth and manipulated media.
