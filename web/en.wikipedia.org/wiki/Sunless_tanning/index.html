Sunless tanning

Sunless tanning, also known as UV-free tanning, self tanning, or fake tanning, refers to the effect of a suntan without exposure of the skin to ultraviolet radiation. Methods include topical products (lotions, foams, and sprays), professional spray-tanning booths and airbrush application, bronzers, and tanning pills. Sunless tanning grew in popularity as the links between UV exposure, skin cancer, and photoaging became widely known.

DHA-based products

Most self-tanners are based on dihydroxyacetone (DHA), a three-carbon sugar that reacts with amino acids in the stratum corneum — the outermost layer of dead skin cells — in a Maillard-type browning reaction. The colour develops over two to four hours, deepens for up to 24–72 hours, and fades over five to seven days as the skin exfoliates. DHA was discovered as a tanning agent in the 1950s, when it was used in studies on children with glycogen storage disease at Cincinnati Children's Hospital and noticed to stain skin brown. Coppertone's Quick Tan, launched in 1960, was the first commercial product.

Other methods

Bronzers are temporary cosmetic dyes and powders washed off with soap and water. Spray booths apply a fine DHA mist for an even coat; professional airbrush application allows contouring. Tanning pills typically contain canthaxanthin, a carotenoid food colouring, which deposits in body fat to give an orange-brown hue; they are not approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for tanning, and high doses have been associated with retinal crystal deposits. Melanotan peptides, which stimulate melanogenesis, are unapproved and considered unsafe by regulators.

Safety

DHA is approved for external cosmetic use in the United States and European Union; it acts only on dead surface cells and is not considered to provide meaningful sun protection, so sunscreen remains necessary. Regulatory advice cautions against inhaling or ingesting DHA sprays and recommends protecting the eyes, lips, and mucous membranes during spray tanning.
