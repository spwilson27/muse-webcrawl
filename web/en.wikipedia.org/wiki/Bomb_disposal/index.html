Bomb disposal

Bomb disposal is an explosives engineering profession concerned with the safe handling, render-safe procedures, and destruction of explosive devices. It encompasses the disposal of conventional munitions, improvised explosive devices (IEDs), and in some jurisdictions chemical, biological, and nuclear hazards. Practitioners are known as bomb disposal operators, explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) technicians, or, in British usage, Ammunition Technicians and "bomb doctors" in earlier eras.

History

Organised bomb disposal emerged during the Second World War, when unexploded German bombs fell across British cities during the Blitz. Royal Engineers bomb disposal sections, and later dedicated units, developed techniques for identifying fuzes and rendering bombs safe, at extremely high casualty rates: in the first years of the war many officers were killed defuzing new and unfamiliar German fuzes, some deliberately booby-trapped. The experience shaped modern doctrine, which prioritises remote operations and the preservation of life over property.

Techniques

The central principle of modern bomb disposal is to minimise human exposure. Suspect devices are first assessed from a distance, often with the aid of a remotely operated robot equipped with cameras. Render-safe procedures may include the use of a water-jet disruptor (such as the pigstick), which fires a high-velocity slug of water to tear a device apart faster than its firing circuit can react; X-ray imaging to identify internal components; and controlled detonation in place or after removal to a safe area. Where manual approach is unavoidable, the operator wears a bomb suit of aramid armour with a helmet and visor, though such suits protect mainly against fragmentation and overpressure at moderate distances.

Improvised explosive devices

IECs and IEDs used by terrorists and insurgents present particular challenges because their construction is unconstrained by military standards. Conflicts in Northern Ireland, Iraq, and Afghanistan drove major advances in electronic countermeasures against radio-controlled IEDs, in armoured vehicles, and in forensic exploitation of recovered devices to identify bomb-makers.
