Environmental chemistry

Environmental chemistry is the scientific study of the chemical and biochemical phenomena that occur in natural places. It examines the sources, reactions, transport, effects and fates of chemical species in the air, soil and water environments, and the effect of human activity and biological processes on these. It draws on analytical chemistry, atmospheric chemistry, aquatic chemistry and soil chemistry, and overlaps with environmental science and toxicology.

Scope

Key topics include the chemistry of pollutants such as heavy metals, persistent organic pollutants (including pesticides and PCBs), nutrients causing eutrophication, acid deposition, photochemical smog, ozone depletion and greenhouse gases. Environmental chemists develop sampling and analytical methods capable of measuring contaminants at trace concentrations, and model how substances partition between air, water, sediment and organisms, including bioaccumulation up food chains.

History

The field grew rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s following public concern over pesticide residues, highlighted by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), and the recognition of problems such as mercury poisoning at Minamata and acid rain. Regulatory frameworks such as clean air and clean water legislation created demand for standardised environmental monitoring.

Applications

Practitioners work in regulation, water and waste treatment, contaminated-land remediation, environmental impact assessment and the design of greener chemical processes (green chemistry), which aims to prevent pollution at source.
