Foundling hospital

A foundling hospital is an institution, historically often charitable or religious, that cares for foundlings — children abandoned by their parents and discovered by others. Such institutions spread across Europe from the Middle Ages onward as a response to infanticide and child abandonment.

History

The Ospedale degli Innocenti in Florence, founded in 1419 and designed in part by Brunelleschi, is considered the first dedicated foundling hospital and an early Renaissance landmark. Many hospitals used a ruota (foundling wheel), a rotating hatch in the wall into which a baby could be placed anonymously from the street. Paris's Hôpital des Enfants-Trouvés and St Petersburg's and Moscow's great foundling homes each received thousands of infants a year in the 18th and 19th centuries, though mortality was extremely high, largely because children were farmed out to rural wet nurses.

In London, Thomas Coram established the Foundling Hospital by royal charter in 1739; it opened in Bloomsbury in 1745, supported by patrons including Handel, whose Messiah performances benefited the charity, and the artist Hogarth. Mothers left tokens — coins, buttons, scraps of fabric — by which a child might later be reclaimed. The London hospital's work continues through the children's charity Coram, and its collection is preserved at the Foundling Museum.

Modern parallels

Contemporary analogues include baby hatches in several countries and safe-haven laws in the United States permitting anonymous surrender of newborns at hospitals and fire stations.
