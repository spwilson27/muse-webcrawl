Constructivism (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the philosophy of mathematics, constructivism asserts that it is necessary to find (or "construct") a mathematical object to prove that it exists. In classical mathematics, one can prove the existence of an object by deriving a contradiction from the assumption that it does not exist — a non-constructive proof — but constructivists regard such existence claims as incomplete until an explicit example or algorithm is given.

Constructive logic

Constructive reasoning is naturally formalized in intuitionistic logic, where the law of excluded middle and full proof by contradiction are unavailable for existence claims. Under the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, a proof of ∃x P(x) must supply a specific witness together with a proof that it satisfies P, and a proof of a disjunction must establish one of the disjuncts. Constructive proofs therefore carry computational content: from them one can in principle extract algorithms, an idea made precise by the Curry–Howard correspondence.

Schools and history

The earliest systematic constructivism was L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism, developed in the early twentieth century and formalized by his student Arend Heyting. Other varieties include the finitism associated with Kronecker and Hilbert's finitary methods, the Russian school of constructive mathematics founded by Andrey Markov, Errett Bishop's constructive analysis (presented in his 1967 Foundations of Constructive Analysis), and Martin-Löf's constructive type theory. Kronecker's famous dictum — "God made the integers; all else is the work of man" — anticipates the constructive demand that objects be built from the natural numbers by explicit procedures.

Constructive mathematics in practice

Much of ordinary algebra and number theory is already constructive, but analysis requires reworking: the classical intermediate value theorem, for example, is replaced by approximate versions, and the claim that every real number is either rational or irrational is not constructively acceptable. Constructive results are valued in computer science because they can be directly implemented; proof assistants such as Coq and Agda are built on constructive type theories.
