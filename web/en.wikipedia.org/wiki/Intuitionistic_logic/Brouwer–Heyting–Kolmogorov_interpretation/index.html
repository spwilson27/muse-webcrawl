Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In proof theory, the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, or BHK interpretation, is the standard explanation of intuitionistic logic in terms of proofs and constructions. It is named after L. E. J. Brouwer, who originated the ideas, Arend Heyting, who described them in the 1930s, and Andrey Kolmogorov, who gave a similar account in terms of problems and their solutions.

The interpretation

The BHK interpretation explains the meaning of each logical connective by specifying what counts as a proof (construction) of a compound statement:

StatementA proof of it is…
P ∧ Qa pair consisting of a proof of P and a proof of Q
P ∨ Qa proof of P or a proof of Q, together with an indication of which one it is
P → Qa construction converting any proof of P into a proof of Q
¬Pa construction converting any proof of P into a proof of a contradiction (since ¬P is defined as P → ⊥)
∃x P(x)a witness a together with a proof of P(a)
∀x P(x)a construction which, given any a, produces a proof of P(a)

Consequences

Under this interpretation, the law of excluded middle P ∨ ¬P is not generally valid: to assert it one would need, for an arbitrary P, either a proof of P or a construction refuting P, which amounts to a general decision procedure for all propositions. For the same reason, double-negation elimination fails: a proof that P cannot be refuted does not itself construct a proof of P. The interpretation thus explains why intuitionistic logic is weaker than classical logic while remaining meaningful: every intuitionistic proof is a genuine construction.

Relation to other ideas

The BHK interpretation is the informal ancestor of the Curry–Howard correspondence, which identifies intuitionistic proofs with computer programs (terms of the lambda calculus), and of Martin-Löf type theory, where propositions are types and proofs are their inhabitants. It also underlies realizability interpretations, in which proofs are coded by computable functions.
