Type theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, logic, and computer science, a type theory is a formal system in which every term has a type, and operations are restricted to terms of certain types. Type theories were originally devised to avoid the paradoxes of naive set theory: Bertrand Russell's theory of types, presented in Principia Mathematica (1910–1913, with Alfred North Whitehead), stratified objects into a hierarchy to block Russell's paradox.

History and major systems

Alonzo Church's simply typed lambda calculus (1940) recast type theory in a form suited to logic and computation. Per Martin-Löf's intuitionistic (dependent) type theory, developed from the 1970s, treats propositions as types and proofs as terms, providing a foundation for constructive mathematics and for proof assistants such as Agda. Thierry Coquand and Gérard Huet's calculus of constructions underlies Coq. Homotopy type theory, developed in the 2000s and presented in a 2013 book from the Institute for Advanced Study, adds the univalence axiom and higher inductive types, connecting type theory with homotopy theory.

Key concepts

  • Types and terms: a judgment "t : T" states that term t has type T.
  • Dependent types: types that depend on values, e.g. "the type of vectors of length n", enabling precise specifications.
  • Propositions as types (the Curry–Howard correspondence): proving a proposition is constructing a term of the corresponding type.
  • Typing rules: inductive rules for forming and checking terms, making proof checking decidable in well-designed systems.

Applications

Type theory is the foundation of typed programming languages (from ML and Haskell to Rust's ownership types) and of modern proof assistants (Coq/Rocq, Lean, Agda). It is also studied as an alternative foundation of mathematics alongside set theory and category theory.
