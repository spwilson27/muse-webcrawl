Constructive proof

Constructive proof
FieldMathematical logic, constructivism
RequirementExhibit the object, or an algorithm to find it
Contrasts withNon-constructive (pure existence) proof

In mathematics, a constructive proof is a method of proof that demonstrates the existence of a mathematical object by creating or providing a method for creating the object. This is in contrast to a non-constructive proof (also known as an existence proof or pure existence theorem), which proves the existence of a mathematical object without giving an example of it or a method for constructing it.

Examples

A classic non-constructive proof shows that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational: consider √2√2 — if it is rational, we are done; if not, then (√2√2)√2 = √22 = 2 is rational, so either way such a pair exists, but the proof does not say which pair works. (A constructive repair takes a = √2, b = 2 log2 9/… — more simply, √2 and log2 9 can be shown to work.) Constructivists such as L. E. J. Brouwer rejected such arguments because they rely on the law of excluded middle; in intuitionistic logic, a proof of "there exists x with property P" must in principle supply a witness together with a proof that it has P.

Constructivism and computation

Under the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, proofs are constructions, and via the Curry–Howard correspondence a constructive proof of an existence statement corresponds to a computer program producing the object. Constructive mathematics, developed by Errett Bishop and others, shows that much of analysis can be rebuilt with constructive proofs, and proof assistants such as Coq and Agda enforce constructivity so that proofs can be executed as algorithms. Constructive proofs are also prized outside foundational debates because they supply algorithms and explicit bounds, whereas a classical compactness or contradiction argument may give no usable information.
