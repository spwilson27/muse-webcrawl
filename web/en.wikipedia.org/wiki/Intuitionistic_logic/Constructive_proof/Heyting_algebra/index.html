Heyting algebra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a Heyting algebra (also known as a pseudo-Boolean algebra) is a bounded lattice equipped with a binary operation of implication that satisfies certain conditions. Heyting algebras were introduced by Arend Heyting in 1930 to formalize intuitionistic logic: just as Boolean algebras are the algebraic models of classical propositional logic, Heyting algebras are the algebraic models of intuitionistic propositional logic.

Definition

A Heyting algebra is a bounded lattice H with a binary operation → such that, for all a, b, c in H, c ≤ (a → b) if and only if c ∧ a ≤ b. The element a → b is called the relative pseudo-complement of a with respect to b: it is the largest element whose meet with a is below b. Negation is defined as ¬a = (a → ⊥), where ⊥ is the bottom element. Every Boolean algebra is a Heyting algebra in which a → b = ¬a ∨ b; the converse fails, because in a general Heyting algebra the identity a ∨ ¬a = ⊤ need not hold.

Examples

The canonical examples are topological: for any topological space, the open sets form a complete Heyting algebra, where implication is the interior of (X ∖ U) ∪ V. This connection is exploited in the topological semantics of intuitionistic logic and in topos theory. Other examples include the lattice of open sets of a locale, and any finite distributive lattice. A three-element chain {0, ½, 1} is a Heyting algebra that is not Boolean, since ¬½ = 0 and ½ ∨ ¬½ ≠ 1.

Properties

Heyting algebras validate exactly the theorems of intuitionistic propositional logic (the logic is complete with respect to this class of algebras). Every Heyting algebra is distributive. Regular elements — those satisfying ¬¬a = a — form a Boolean algebra, and the double-negation translation embeds classical logic into intuitionistic logic, showing their equiconsistency.
