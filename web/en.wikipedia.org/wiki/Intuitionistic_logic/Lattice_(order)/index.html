Lattice (order)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lattice is an abstract structure studied in order theory and abstract algebra. It consists of a partially ordered set in which every pair of elements has a unique supremum (join, ∨) and a unique infimum (meet, ∧). Equivalently, a lattice can be defined algebraically as a set with two commutative, associative, idempotent operations satisfying the absorption laws.

Definitions

As a partial order, a lattice is a poset (L, ≤) such that for all x, y in L, the least upper bound x ∨ y and the greatest lower bound x ∧ y exist. Algebraically, the two viewpoints coincide: from the poset one defines meet and join, and from the operations one recovers the order by x ≤ y iff x ∧ y = x. A lattice is bounded if it has a greatest element ⊤ and a least element ⊥; it is complete if every subset has a join and a meet.

Examples

The power set of a set, ordered by inclusion, is a complete Boolean lattice with meet = intersection and join = union. The natural numbers ordered by divisibility form a lattice with meet = gcd and join = lcm. The open sets of a topological space form a complete lattice that is generally only a Heyting algebra. Subgroups of a group, subspaces of a vector space, and ideals of a ring all form lattices.

Special classes

  • Distributive lattices: meet distributes over join and vice versa.
  • Boolean algebras: complemented distributive lattices — the algebraic semantics of classical logic.
  • Heyting algebras: lattices with relative pseudo-complementation — the semantics of intuitionistic logic.
  • Modular lattices, important in the theory of groups and projective geometry.

Applications

Lattice theory, systematized by Garrett Birkhoff in the 1930s, underlies topics from formal concept analysis and domain theory in computer science to the logic of quantum mechanics, where the lattice of closed subspaces of a Hilbert space is non-distributive.
