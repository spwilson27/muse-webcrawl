Proof by contradiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, a proof by contradiction is a form of proof that establishes the truth of a proposition by showing that the assumption that it is false leads to a contradiction. It is also known by its Latin name reductio ad absurdum. The method is valid in classical logic because of the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination: if ¬P implies a contradiction, then ¬¬P holds, and classically P follows.

Form of the argument

The general schema is: to prove P, assume ¬P and derive a contradiction (both Q and ¬Q, or another absurdity ⊥). Conclude P. This differs from the constructively valid proof of negation: to prove ¬P, one assumes P and derives a contradiction — that step is legitimate even in intuitionistic logic, since ¬P is defined as P → ⊥. The intuitionistically contentious step is only the final one, using a contradiction derived from ¬P to conclude P outright.

Classical examples

  • Irrationality of √2 (Euclid): assuming √2 = a/b in lowest terms leads to both a and b being even, contradicting minimality.
  • Infinitude of the primes (Euclid): assuming finitely many primes p1…pn, the number p1···pn + 1 has a prime divisor not on the list, a contradiction.
  • Cantor's diagonal argument shows the real numbers are uncountable by deriving a contradiction from any supposed enumeration.

Constructive status

Constructive mathematicians accept proofs by contradiction for negative statements and for decidable properties, but not to establish positive existence claims, where they demand a constructive proof exhibiting a witness. Many classical contradiction proofs can be reworked constructively, sometimes yielding stronger, computational information.
