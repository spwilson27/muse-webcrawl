Existence theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an existence theorem is a theorem which asserts the existence of some object (or class of objects) satisfying stated conditions. Existence theorems may be pure, guaranteeing existence without indicating how the object can be found, or constructive, supplying a method or example that exhibits the object.

Pure existence proofs

A classical strategy proves existence by contradiction: assume no such object exists and derive an absurdity. Such non-constructive proofs are common in analysis and topology — for example, proofs that a continuous function on a compact set attains its maximum, or existence results for solutions of differential equations obtained from fixed-point theorems such as the Banach and Schauder fixed-point theorems. Another pure existence argument uses counting: a probabilistic method argument shows that if a randomly chosen object fails to have a property with probability less than one, then some object must have it.

Constructive existence

Constructivists, following Brouwer's intuitionism, hold that an existence claim is fully justified only by a construction of the object — the standpoint expressed in the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation of the existential quantifier. In constructive mathematics, an existence theorem is therefore always backed by a construction or algorithm. Even in classical practice, constructive proofs are prized because they yield methods of computation, numerical approximations, and explicit examples.

Examples

  • The fundamental theorem of algebra: every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root — classically proved by topological or analytic arguments rather than by exhibiting the root.
  • Picard's existence theorem for ordinary differential equations, guaranteeing a local solution to an initial value problem.
  • The Nash equilibrium existence theorem in game theory, proved via the Kakutani fixed-point theorem.
  • The classification of existence results for solutions of partial differential equations, such as the Lax–Milgram theorem.

Existence and uniqueness

Existence theorems are often paired with uniqueness theorems, which show that at most one object satisfies the conditions; together they establish that exactly one object exists, a pattern sometimes called "existence and uniqueness" (∃!).
