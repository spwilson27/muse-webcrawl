Proof assistant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A proof assistant or interactive theorem prover is a software tool that helps in the development of formal proofs by human–machine collaboration. The user guides the proof, while the machine checks every step against the rules of a formal logic, guaranteeing that the resulting proof is valid. Proof assistants are used to verify major mathematical theorems and to check the correctness of critical software and hardware.

Foundations

Most proof assistants are based on typed lambda calculi and the Curry–Howard correspondence: propositions are types, proofs are programs (terms) of those types, and checking a proof is type checking. Coq (now Rocq) is based on the calculus of inductive constructions; Lean on a dependent type theory with a small trusted kernel; Agda on Martin-Löf type theory. A common design principle, the de Bruijn criterion, requires proofs to be checkable by a small, independent program.

Major systems

  • Coq — used to fully formalize the four color theorem (2005) and the Feit–Thompson odd order theorem (2012), and for the CompCert verified C compiler.
  • Lean — developed at Microsoft Research and the community; its mathematical library mathlib contains a large corpus of formalized mathematics, including a verified proof of a central result in condensed mathematics (the Liquid Tensor Experiment).
  • Isabelle/HOL — a generic prover with strong automation, used for verifying the seL4 microkernel.
  • Agda — a dependently typed language used as a proof assistant, popular in programming-language research.
  • HOL Light — used in the Flyspeck project formalizing the Kepler conjecture.

Applications

Beyond pure mathematics, proof assistants verify compilers, operating-system kernels, cryptographic protocols, and floating-point hardware designs. Their growing libraries and improved automation have made formal verification increasingly practical in both research and industry.
