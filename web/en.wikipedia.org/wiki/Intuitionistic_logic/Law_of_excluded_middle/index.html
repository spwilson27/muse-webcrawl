Law of excluded middle

Law of excluded middle
LatinPrincipium tertii exclusi / tertium non datur
StatementFor every proposition P, P ∨ ¬P ("P or not P")
StatusAxiom of classical logic; rejected in intuitionistic logic

In logic, the law of excluded middle (or the principle of excluded middle) states that for every proposition, either the proposition or its negation is true: P ∨ ¬P. It is one of the traditional "laws of thought", alongside the laws of identity and non-contradiction, and its name reflects the claim that there is no middle ground between truth and falsity — tertium non datur, "no third [possibility] is given". Aristotle discussed the principle (in connection with future contingents, such as tomorrow's sea battle) in De Interpretatione.

Classical vs intuitionistic logic

The law is valid in classical logic and classical mathematics, where it licenses proof by contradiction for existence claims and case analysis on undecidable statements. It is not valid in intuitionistic logic: because an intuitionist reads "P ∨ ¬P" as claiming an effective method to decide which disjunct holds, the law fails for statements about infinite domains that cannot be effectively decided. Adding excluded middle (or the equivalent double-negation elimination) to intuitionistic logic recovers classical logic. Related principles that intuitionists also reject or restrict include Peirce's law and unrestricted proof by contradiction; the law is also invalid in some many-valued and paraconsistent logics, while Gödel–Gentzen negative translation shows classical arithmetic can be embedded in intuitionistic arithmetic without loss of provable Π⁰₂ statements.

Consequences of rejecting it

Without excluded middle, some classical theorems split into inequivalent forms (the axiom of choice, for instance, implies excluded middle over intuitionistic set theory, by Diaconescu's theorem), and proofs must be constructive. Most working mathematicians accept the law; the intuitionistic critique, pressed by Brouwer against Hilbert's formalism in the Grundlagenstreit of the 1920s, reshaped proof theory nonetheless, and constructive logic has become practically important through computer science and type theory.
