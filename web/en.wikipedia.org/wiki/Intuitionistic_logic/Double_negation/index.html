Double negation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, double negation is the negation of the negation of a proposition. In classical logic, the rule of double-negation elimination (also called double negative elimination) licenses the inference from ¬¬P to P, and the two are logically equivalent. In intuitionistic logic, however, the elimination rule is not valid in general: from a proof that P leads to no contradiction one cannot construct a proof of P itself.

Double negation in intuitionistic logic

Intuitionistically, the introduction direction is fine — P implies ¬¬P, because a proof of P can be converted into a refutation of any refutation of P. The failure is only in elimination. Under the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, ¬¬P says that there is a construction turning any refutation of P into an absurdity; this does not exhibit a proof of P, so for undecidable propositions — such as unproved conjectures about infinitely many cases — ¬¬P may be assertible where P is not.

The double-negation translation

Although elimination fails, double negation can be used to embed classical logic into intuitionistic logic. The Gödel–Gentzen negative translation rewrites each classical formula by double-negating it (and its subformulas), in such a way that the result is intuitionistically provable exactly when the original is classically provable. An important consequence is that classical and intuitionistic Peano arithmetic are equiconsistent: if one is consistent, so is the other. Formulas that are equivalent to their double negations are called stable; for stable formulas, such as decidable equalities between natural numbers, classical reasoning is constructively acceptable.

In linguistics

In natural language, "double negation" usually refers to constructions such as "I don't know nothing", where two negative forms occur in one clause. Depending on the language and dialect, such negative concord either reinforces the negation or — in standard English usage, as in "not uncommon" — yields a weakened positive (litotes). This grammatical phenomenon is unrelated to the logical rule.
