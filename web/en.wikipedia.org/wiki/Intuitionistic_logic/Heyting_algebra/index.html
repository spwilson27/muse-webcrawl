In mathematics, a Heyting algebra (also known as a pseudo-Boolean algebra) is a bounded lattice equipped with a binary operation of implication, satisfying conditions that make it the algebraic semantics of intuitionistic propositional logic. Heyting algebras were introduced by Arend Heyting in 1930 to formalise the logic of Brouwer's intuitionism. Every Boolean algebra is a Heyting algebra, but the converse is false: in a general Heyting algebra the law of excluded middle, a ∨ ¬a = ⊤, need not hold, and negation is weaker (double negation is not the identity).

Definition

A Heyting algebra is a bounded lattice H with least element ⊥ and greatest element ⊤ in which for every pair a, b there exists a greatest element a → b (the relative pseudo-complement of a with respect to b) such that x ∧ a ≤ b if and only if x ≤ a → b. Negation is defined as ¬a = a → ⊥. Familiar examples include the lattice of open sets of any topological space — where implication and negation are interior operations, explaining why intuitionistic logic is the logic of open-set (and sheaf) semantics — and the three-element chain {0, ½, 1}, where ¬½ = 0 but ¬¬½ = 1 ≠ ½.

Properties and uses

Heyting algebras are distributive and form a variety; a Heyting algebra in which ¬¬a = a for all a is exactly a Boolean algebra. Intuitionistic propositional logic is sound and complete with respect to Heyting algebra semantics: a formula is a theorem iff it evaluates to ⊤ under every Heyting valuation. The open-set algebras of topological spaces give rise to topological (Tarski) semantics, and Heyting algebras generalise further to the localic and topos-theoretic settings used in categorical logic. Complete Heyting algebras ("frames"/locales) are the objects of point-free topology.
