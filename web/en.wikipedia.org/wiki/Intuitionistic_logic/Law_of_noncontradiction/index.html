Law of noncontradiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, the law of noncontradiction (LNC; also law of contradiction) states that contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time: formally, ¬(P ∧ ¬P). It is one of the traditional "three laws of thought", alongside the law of excluded middle and the law of identity. Unlike the law of excluded middle, the law of noncontradiction is accepted in intuitionistic logic, where it is a theorem: from P and ¬P (that is, P → ⊥) one can directly derive ⊥.

History

Aristotle gave the classical formulation in Book Gamma of the Metaphysics: "the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject and in the same respect". He considered it the most certain of all principles, one that could not be demonstrated directly but could be defended by showing that a denier must rely on it in making any meaningful assertion. The principle was discussed by Plato in the Republic and later by Avicenna, Aquinas, and Leibniz, who treated it as a first principle of reasoning.

Interpretations

The law has ontological, logical, and psychological readings: a claim about being (nothing can both be and not be), about truth (a proposition and its negation are not both true), and about belief (no one can believe a contradiction, on some accounts). Ambiguities in phrasing — the qualification "in the same respect" — matter for apparent counterexamples involving change over time or different senses of a predicate.

Challenges

Paraconsistent logics are designed to tolerate contradictions without triviality (without every statement becoming provable), and dialetheists such as Graham Priest argue that a few contradictions — notably semantic paradoxes like the liar — are actually true. These views reject either the law's unrestricted validity or its connection to triviality, though the law itself remains a theorem of most paraconsistent systems in restricted form. Heraclitus and Hegel are sometimes read as earlier critics, emphasizing the unity of opposites in becoming and dialectic.
