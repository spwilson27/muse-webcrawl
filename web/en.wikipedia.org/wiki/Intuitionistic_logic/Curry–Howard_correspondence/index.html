In programming language theory and proof theory, the Curry–Howard correspondence (also known as the Curry–Howard isomorphism or equivalence, or the proofs-as-programs and propositions-as-types interpretations) is the direct relationship between computer programs and mathematical proofs: a formal proof in a logical system corresponds, structurally, to a program in a typed lambda calculus, and the type of the program is the proposition that the proof proves. Simplifying or normalising the proof corresponds to evaluating (running) the program.

History

Haskell Curry observed in 1934 (and in his 1958 book with Robert Feys) that the types of combinators in combinatory logic matched the axiom schemes of intuitionistic implication. William Alvin Howard made the full correspondence explicit in a 1969 manuscript, published in 1980, showing that intuitionistic natural deduction corresponds to the simply typed lambda calculus. The idea was subsequently extended: Girard's System F corresponds to second-order logic; the calculus of constructions of Coquand and Huet underlies the Coq proof assistant; Martin-Löf type theory and homotopy type theory continue the programme. Classical logic corresponds, via work by Griffin and Parigot, to control operators such as call/cc and the λμ-calculus.

Significance

The correspondence is the theoretical foundation of proof assistants such as Coq, Agda and Lean: to prove a theorem is to write a program of the corresponding type, and type checking is proof checking (the de Bruijn criterion). It also informs programming language design — features of Haskell, ML and Rust trace to logical systems — and supports program extraction, whereby verified algorithms are generated from constructive proofs.
