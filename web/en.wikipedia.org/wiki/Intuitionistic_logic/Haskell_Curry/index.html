Haskell Brooks Curry (September 12, 1900 – September 1, 1982) was an American mathematician and logician. He is best known for his work in combinatory logic, a foundation for functional programming languages, three of which bear his name or concepts from his work: the programming languages Haskell and Curry, and the technique known as currying — transforming a function of several arguments into a chain of single-argument functions.

Life

Curry was born in Millis, Massachusetts, studied at Harvard University, and earned his doctorate in 1930 at the University of Göttingen under David Hilbert, with a dissertation on combinatory logic. He taught at Harvard and Princeton before joining Pennsylvania State College (later Penn State) in 1929, where he spent most of his career; he also worked with Robert Feys in Louvain, Belgium, on the two-volume Combinatory Logic (1958, 1972).

Work

Curry developed combinatory logic — building on earlier work by Moses Schönfinkel — into a formal system in which variables and abstraction are eliminated in favor of primitive combinators such as S and K. His name is attached to the Curry–Howard correspondence, the observation that the types of combinators and programs correspond to propositions of intuitionistic logic and that programs are proofs; to Curry's paradox, a self-referential paradox derivable in naive systems combining implication with unrestricted comprehension; and to the standard combinatory-logic analysis of the lambda calculus.
