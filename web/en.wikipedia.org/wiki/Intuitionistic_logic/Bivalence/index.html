Principle of bivalence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, the principle of bivalence states that every declarative sentence expressing a proposition has exactly one truth value, either truth or falsity. A logic satisfying this principle is called a two-valued logic, or bivalent logic. Classical logic is bivalent, and bivalence is closely related to — but distinct from — the law of excluded middle, which states that for every proposition P, either P or not-P is true.

Bivalence and excluded middle

The two principles are often confused. The law of excluded middle is a syntactic claim about the provability or truth of P ∨ ¬P within a system, while bivalence is a semantic claim about the range of truth values a proposition can take. In classical semantics the two coincide, but in some non-classical systems one can hold without the other: supervaluationist accounts of vagueness, for instance, preserve excluded middle while allowing truth-value gaps in borderline cases.

Rejection of bivalence

Several traditions reject bivalence. Intuitionistic logic, following Brouwer and formalized by Arend Heyting, ties truth to provability: a statement about an infinite domain, such as a conjecture not yet proved or refuted, cannot simply be assumed to be determinately true or false. Many-valued logics, beginning with the three-valued systems of Jan Łukasiewicz, introduce additional values such as "possible" or "indeterminate". Aristotle's discussion of future contingents in De Interpretatione — whether "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false — is an ancient antecedent of the debate.

Vagueness and future contingents

Vague predicates such as "heap" or "bald" generate borderline cases in which a statement seems neither clearly true nor clearly false, motivating degree theories and fuzzy logic, in which truth comes in degrees between 0 and 1. Defenders of bivalence, such as Timothy Williamson, argue that borderline statements still possess one of the two classical values, though we may be unable to know which.
