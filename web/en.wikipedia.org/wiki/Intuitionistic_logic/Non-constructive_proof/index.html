Non-constructive proof

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A non-constructive proof is a proof that asserts the existence of a mathematical object without providing an explicit example or a method for finding it. Such proofs typically proceed indirectly, by proof by contradiction using the law of excluded middle: either an object with the property exists or it does not, and the second alternative is shown to be impossible — without determining which object witnesses the claim.

Classic example

A famous illustration proves that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational. Consider √2√2: if it is rational, take a = b = √2; if it is irrational, take a = √2√2 and b = √2, for then ab = 2. The proof does not decide which case holds, so it does not exhibit a definite pair. (The Gelfond–Schneider theorem later settled the matter: √2√2 is in fact transcendental.)

Criticism and constructive mathematics

Constructivists such as L. E. J. Brouwer rejected non-constructive existence proofs as failing to give genuine mathematical content; in constructive mathematics and intuitionistic logic, a proof of existence must provide a witness. Classical mathematicians generally accept non-constructive methods, noting that many central results — fixed-point theorems, compactness arguments, probabilistic existence proofs — were first obtained non-constructively, and that constructive versions, when wanted, can often be extracted afterwards.

Other sources of non-constructivity

The axiom of choice is another source of non-constructive existence: it guarantees choice functions and objects such as non-measurable sets and bases of arbitrary vector spaces without any procedure for identifying them. Counting arguments and the probabilistic method in combinatorics likewise prove that objects with desirable properties exist while exhibiting none.
