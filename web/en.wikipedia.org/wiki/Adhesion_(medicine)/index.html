Adhesion (medicine)

Adhesions are fibrous bands of scar tissue that form between normally separate internal tissues and organs. They most commonly develop after abdominal or pelvic surgery, but can also result from inflammation, infection, endometriosis or trauma. Most adhesions cause no symptoms, but they are a leading cause of small bowel obstruction, some chronic pelvic pain and secondary infertility.

SpecialtyGeneral surgery, gynaecology
CausesSurgery, inflammation, infection, endometriosis
ComplicationsBowel obstruction, infertility, chronic pain

Formation

Tissue injury — for example cutting, cautery, drying or contact with foreign material during an operation — triggers an inflammatory response and deposition of fibrin between injured surfaces. Ordinarily this fibrin is dissolved during healing; when it persists, fibroblasts invade it and convert it into permanent fibrous bands joining organs, the bowel or the abdominal wall.

Clinical significance

Studies find adhesions in the large majority of patients after open abdominal surgery. Adhesion-related complications include adhesive small bowel obstruction (a surgical emergency), difficult and hazardous re-entry at later operations, tubal infertility when the fallopian tubes are involved, and — more controversially — chronic abdominal or pelvic pain. Paradoxically, the treatment of obstructive adhesions, surgical division (adhesiolysis), itself often produces new adhesions.

Prevention and treatment

Preventive strategies include gentle tissue handling and minimally invasive (laparoscopic) techniques, which reduce but do not eliminate adhesion formation, and barrier agents (films, gels and solutions) placed at operation to keep injured surfaces apart while they heal. Symptomatic adhesions are treated only when they cause obstruction or other definite problems, usually surgically; there is no effective drug therapy to dissolve established adhesions.
