Principle of compositionality

The meaning of a whole expression is composed from the meanings of its parts.

In semantics, philosophy of language, and mathematical logic, the principle of compositionality is the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its constituent expressions and the rules used to combine them. The principle is often associated with Gottlob Frege, though the exact formulation does not appear verbatim in his writings; it is sometimes called Frege's principle.

FieldSemantics, philosophy of language
Associated withGottlob Frege; developed by Richard Montague, Donald Davidson
MotivationProductivity and systematicity of language

Motivation

Compositionality explains how finite beings understand infinitely many novel sentences (productivity) and how anyone who understands "John loves Mary" can also understand "Mary loves John" (systematicity). If meanings were not composed from parts, each sentence's meaning would have to be learned separately — implausible for an infinite language.

Formalisation

In formal semantics, a language is compositional if its semantic interpretation function is a homomorphism from the syntactic algebra to the semantic algebra: the interpretation of a compound expression is a function of the interpretations of its immediate constituents and the syntactic operation combining them. Richard Montague's grammar of English fragments was designed to satisfy this constraint strictly.

Challenges

Apparent counterexamples include idioms ("kick the bucket"), metaphor, quotation, belief reports and other intensional contexts, and context-dependence such as indexicals. Defenders reply by reassigning meanings to parts (e.g. treating idioms as lexical units) or enriching the notion of "meaning" — sometimes at the cost of making the principle trivially true, a criticism pressed by its opponents.
