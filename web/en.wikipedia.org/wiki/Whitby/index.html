Whitby is a seaside town and port in North Yorkshire, England, at the mouth of the River Esk on the North Sea coast. It has a maritime and mineral heritage and a strong literary association as the place where, in Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula, the Count comes ashore. Its skyline is dominated by the ruins of Whitby Abbey on the East Cliff.

History

Whitby Abbey was founded in 657 as a double monastery under St Hilda; the Synod of Whitby (664) decided that the Northumbrian church would follow Roman rather than Ionan practice in computing Easter. The settlement was destroyed by Danes in the 9th century and refounded after the Norman Conquest. The town's alum industry in the 16th–17th centuries and its whaling fleet in the 18th century drove prosperity, and its shipyards built the collier brigs on which James Cook trained — HMS Endeavour began life as the Whitby-built Earl of Pembroke. Victorian Whitby mined and carved jet, the black fossilised wood popularised for mourning jewellery.

Tourism and culture

Today Whitby is a fishing port and tourist resort, known for its 199 steps up to St Mary's Church and the abbey, its fish and chips (the Magpie Café is famous), the Captain Cook Memorial Museum, and the twice-yearly Whitby Goth Weekend, inspired by the town's Dracula connection. The North Yorkshire Moors Railway terminates in the town.
