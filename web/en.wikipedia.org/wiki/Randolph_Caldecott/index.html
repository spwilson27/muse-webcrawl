Randolph Caldecott (22 March 1846 – 12 February 1886) was an English artist and illustrator, regarded as one of the founders of the modern children's picture book. His work in the late 1870s and 1880s — full of movement, humour, and interplay between words and images — profoundly influenced illustrators such as Beatrix Potter and Maurice Sendak. The American Library Association's Caldecott Medal, awarded annually for the most distinguished American picture book for children, is named in his honour.

Life and career

Born in Chester, Caldecott worked as a bank clerk in Whitchurch and Manchester while studying art at night school. He moved to London in 1872 and soon found work with magazines including Punch and the Illustrated London News, illustrating travel books about the Harz Mountains, Brittany, and North America with Henry Blackburn. From 1878 the engraver Edmund Evans printed his celebrated series of "Toy Books" — sixteen picture books including The House That Jack Built, The Diverting History of John Gilpin, Sing a Song for Sixpence, and The Three Jovial Huntsmen — which sold in the tens of thousands and were translated widely.

Style and legacy

Caldecott's innovation was to let pictures carry narrative: images extend, comment on, and sometimes contradict the rhymed text, with characters animated by energetic line and subtle wit. Plagued by ill health from childhood, he died aged 39 while travelling in Florida and is buried in Tallahassee. The Randolph Caldecott Society preserves his memory in Britain, and his books remain in print.
