Azipod is a trademarked marine propulsion unit developed by ABB in which an electric motor is mounted inside a steerable pod (gondola) suspended beneath the ship's hull, driving a fixed-pitch propeller. The pod can rotate 360 degrees, so thrust can be directed in any direction, combining propulsion and steering and eliminating the need for a conventional rudder, stern thrusters and long internal shaft lines.

Design

In an Azipod installation, electricity generated by the ship's main diesel-electric or gas-electric plant is transmitted through slip rings to the motor inside the pod. The pod itself turns on a slewing bearing under hydraulic or electric steering gear. Most large units use a "pulling" configuration, with the propeller at the forward end of the pod facing the direction of travel, which improves hydrodynamic efficiency by placing the propeller in a relatively undisturbed flow.

History

The concept grew out of Finnish icebreaker engineering: the Finnish Maritime Administration and the shipbuilder Masa-Yards, together with ABB, sought better manoeuvrability in ice. The first Azipod unit was installed in 1990 on the Finnish fairway support vessel Seili. Azimuthing pods proved especially valuable for icebreakers, which can use the pod to wash and break ice, and for the double-acting ship concept, in which a vessel travels bow-first in open water and stern-first in heavy ice. The system was adopted widely in the cruise industry from the late 1990s onward, including on the largest cruise ships built.

Advantages and criticisms

Advantages include excellent manoeuvrability and harbour performance, reduced vibration and noise, fuel savings commonly cited at 5–15 percent from hydrodynamic and arrangement efficiencies, and flexible machinery layout since engines need not align with shafts. Drawbacks include the high cost and technical complexity of the pods, vulnerability of the underwater unit to damage, and early bearing and seal problems on some installations that led to high-profile repairs and litigation in the 2000s. Competing podded systems include Rolls-Royce's Mermaid pods and Schottel propulsion units.

Applications

Azipods are installed on cruise ships, icebreakers and ice-going cargo vessels, ferries, offshore support vessels, and naval auxiliaries. Large units exceed 20 MW per pod; some of the world's largest passenger ships use three pods for a combined propulsion power of over 60 MW.
