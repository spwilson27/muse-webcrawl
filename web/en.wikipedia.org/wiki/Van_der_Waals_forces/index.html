Van der Waals force


Van der Waals attraction between two molecules

In molecular physics and chemistry, the van der Waals forces (sometimes van der Waals interactions), named after Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals, are distance-dependent interactions between atoms or molecules. Unlike ionic or covalent bonds, these attractions do not result from a chemical bond; they are comparatively weak and therefore more susceptible to disturbance.

The van der Waals forces are usually described as a combination of the attractive or repulsive forces between molecules (or between parts of the same molecule) other than those due to covalent bonds or to the electrostatic interaction of ions. The term includes: the Keesom force between two permanent dipoles (dipole–dipole attraction); the Debye force between a permanent dipole and a corresponding induced dipole; and the London dispersion force between two instantaneously induced dipoles.

Definition

Van der Waals forces include attraction and repulsions between atoms, molecules, and surfaces, as well as other intermolecular forces. They differ from covalent and ionic bonding in that they are caused by correlations in the fluctuating polarizations of nearby particles (a consequence of quantum dynamics). Relatively weak compared with covalent bonds, van der Waals forces nevertheless play a fundamental role in fields as diverse as supramolecular chemistry, structural biology, polymer science, nanotechnology and surface science.

London dispersion force

The London dispersion force is the weakest of the van der Waals components. It arises from instantaneous dipoles formed when the electron distribution of an atom or molecule fluctuates, inducing a corresponding dipole in a neighbouring particle. Its strength increases with the polarizability and size of the interacting species, which is why larger molecules generally have higher boiling points.

Geckos and van der Waals forces

Van der Waals forces are notably exploited by geckos, which can climb smooth vertical surfaces and hang from ceilings. Each toe bears hundreds of thousands of tiny hairs (setae), each splitting into hundreds of spatulae that make intimate contact with the surface; the summed van der Waals attraction is strong enough to support many times the animal's body weight.

Van der Waals equation of state

Van der Waals introduced the first equation of state based on the assumption of a finite volume occupied by the constituent molecules and of intermolecular attractive forces, correcting the ideal gas law. The equation bears his name and earned him the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physics.
