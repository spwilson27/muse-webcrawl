The Louisiana black bear (Ursus americanus luteolus) is a subspecies of the American black bear native to the bottomland hardwood forests of the lower Mississippi River valley. It is one of sixteen recognized subspecies of the American black bear and is notable as the bear that inspired the "Teddy bear" after an incident involving President Theodore Roosevelt in 1902.

Description

Louisiana black bears are large, heavy-bodied bears with long, narrow skulls and proportionately large molars compared with other black bear subspecies. Adults typically weigh between 150 and 400 pounds, with males considerably larger than females. Their coat is usually black, though a small proportion of individuals have lighter, blond or cinnamon coloration. They possess relatively long guard hairs and short claws suited to climbing trees.

Habitat and behaviour

The subspecies inhabits bottomland hardwood and floodplain forests, where it feeds on hard mast such as acorns and pecans, soft mast including berries and persimmons, carrion, and invertebrates. Bears den in hollow trees, particularly old bald cypress and overcup oak, as well as in ground nests and brush piles. Females give birth to litters of one to four cubs in winter dens.

Conservation

By the mid-twentieth century, habitat loss and overhunting had reduced the population drastically, and the bear was listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act in 1992. Decades of habitat restoration, reintroduction, and corridor protection followed; in 2016 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service removed the Louisiana black bear from the list of threatened species, citing recovery.

The "Teddy bear" connection

In November 1902, President Theodore Roosevelt joined a bear hunt near Onward, Mississippi. After his guides cornered and tied a black bear to a tree, Roosevelt refused to shoot it, deeming it unsportsmanlike. Political cartoonist Clifford Berryman drew the scene, inspiring Brooklyn shopkeeper Morris Michtom to create stuffed "Teddy's bears" — the original Teddy bear.
