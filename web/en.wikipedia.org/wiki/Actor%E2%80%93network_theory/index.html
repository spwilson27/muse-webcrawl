Actor–network theory

Key figuresBruno Latour, Michel Callon, John Law
FieldScience and technology studies; sociology
OriginEarly 1980s, Paris

Actor–network theory (ANT) is a theoretical and methodological approach to social theory in which everything in the social and natural worlds exists in constantly shifting networks of relationships. It proposes that nothing exists outside those relationships, and it treats non-human objects as actors (or "actants") alongside people.

Key concepts

ANT emerged from the work of Bruno Latour, Michel Callon and John Law in science and technology studies. Its central notions include translation — the process by which actors recruit others into a network — inscription, and generalized symmetry between human and non-human actants. Callon's study of scallop fishing in St Brieuc Bay and Latour's work on laboratory life and Pasteur are classic examples.

Criticism

Critics have charged ANT with neglecting established social structures such as class and gender, and with being descriptive rather than explanatory. Supporters reply that ANT is a method for tracing associations rather than a theory of society.
