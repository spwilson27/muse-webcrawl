The Qadiriyya (Arabic: القادرية) is one of the oldest and most widespread Sufi orders (tariqas). It derives its name from its founder, the Hanbali scholar and preacher Abdul Qadir Gilani (1077–1166), known reverentially as Ghawth al-Azam ("the Supreme Helper"), who taught in Baghdad. The order was formally organised after his death by his sons and disciples.

Teachings and practice

The Qadiriyya emphasises strict adherence to the Sharia as the foundation of the spiritual path, combined with practices of remembrance (dhikr), which in many branches is performed aloud (dhikr jali), accompanied by rhythmic movement and breathing. Followers recite litanies (wird) attributed to Gilani, observe retreats, and venerate the founder's tomb in Baghdad as a major pilgrimage site. Sub-orders and branches developed distinct local customs across the Muslim world.

Spread

From Iraq the order spread to Syria, Anatolia, Egypt, and Yemen, and along trade routes to West and East Africa (where it became dominant in regions such as Somalia and Senegal), to Central Asia, India, and Southeast Asia. In West Africa, Qadiri scholars such as Usman dan Fodio—founder of the Sokoto Caliphate—played major political as well as religious roles. In South Asia, figures such as Mian Mir of Lahore, spiritual mentor associated with the Mughal court, promoted the order. The Qadiriyya's emphasis on orthodoxy and charitable works, including public kitchens at its lodges (zawiyas / khanqahs), sustained its popularity for eight centuries.
