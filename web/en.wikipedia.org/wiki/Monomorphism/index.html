Monomorphism

In the context of abstract algebra or universal algebra, a monomorphism is an injective homomorphism. A monomorphism from X to Y is often denoted with the notation X ↪ Y.

In the more general setting of category theory, a monomorphism (also called a monic morphism or a mono) is a left-cancellative morphism. That is, an arrow f : X → Y is a monomorphism if for all objects Z and all morphisms g1, g2 : Z → X, the equality f ∘ g1 = f ∘ g2 implies g1 = g2.

A monomorphism is the categorical generalisation of injective functions in the category of sets, although in arbitrary categories monomorphisms need not correspond to injective maps: in some categories they do, in others they are more subtle. The dual concept to a monomorphism is an epimorphism.

Relation to invertibility

Left-invertible morphisms are necessarily monomorphisms: if l is a left inverse for f (that is, l ∘ f = idX), then f is monic. A monomorphism that is also a section (i.e. has a right inverse in the appropriate sense) corresponds to a split monomorphism. In the category of sets, every monomorphism is regular, but this is not true in all categories.

Examples

In the category of groups, monomorphisms are exactly the injective homomorphisms. In the category of rings, the inclusion of the integers into the rational numbers is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism, without being an isomorphism. In the category of topological spaces, monomorphisms are the injective continuous maps, while in some categories such as divisible groups, monomorphisms need not be injective.

Terminology

The companion terms monomorphism and epimorphism were originally introduced by Nicolas Bourbaki. In universal algebra, monomorphisms are often simply the injective homomorphisms between algebraic structures.
