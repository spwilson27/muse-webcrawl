Separation of powers under the United States Constitution

The separation of powers is a foundational principle of the United States Constitution, dividing the federal government into three branches — the legislative (Congress), the executive (the President), and the judicial (the federal courts) — each with distinct powers and with checks by which it can restrain the others. The doctrine drew on the writings of Montesquieu and the experience of the British constitution, and was defended by James Madison in The Federalist No. 47 and No. 51.

Constitutional structure

Article I vests legislative power in Congress, Article II vests executive power in the President, and Article III vests judicial power in the Supreme Court and inferior courts. The opening words of each article — "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress" — have grounded doctrines such as nondelegation, limiting Congress's ability to hand its lawmaking power to other actors.

Checks and balances

Rather than a strict separation, the framers blended functions so that, in Madison's phrase, "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." The President may veto legislation, subject to a two-thirds override; the Senate must confirm major appointments and consent to treaties; Congress controls appropriations and may impeach and remove officers of the other branches; and the judiciary may invalidate acts of either branch that exceed constitutional limits, a power established in Marbury v. Madison (1803).

Modern issues

The growth of the administrative state has raised continuing questions about agencies that combine rulemaking, enforcement, and adjudication, and about presidential removal power, war powers, and executive privilege. The separation of powers is also mirrored, and reinforced by federalism, in the constitutions of the individual states.
