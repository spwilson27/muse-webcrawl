Zayd ibn Ali (Arabic: زيد بن علي; c. 695–740) was the son of Ali ibn Husayn Zayn al-Abidin and the great-grandson of Ali ibn Abi Talib through Husayn ibn Ali. He led an unsuccessful revolt against the Umayyad Caliphate in Kufa in 740 and was killed in battle. His followers became the Zaydiyya, a branch of Shia Islam whose imams later ruled in Tabaristan and, for a thousand years, in Yemen.

Life

Zayd grew up in Medina, where he was known for learning in Qur'an, law and theology; tradition associates him with the early theological circles of Wasil ibn Ata and credits works such as the Majmu' al-fiqh to him. A dispute over the administration of charitable endowments brought him into conflict with his half-brother Muhammad al-Baqir's line and, in some accounts, took him to Damascus to plead before the caliph Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik, who treated him dismissively.

Revolt and death

Invited to Kufa by supporters of the Alid cause, Zayd rose in rebellion in 122 AH / 740 CE. As with earlier Alid risings, much of his Kufan support melted away; a controversy over his refusal to denounce the first caliphs Abu Bakr and Umar caused some Shia to abandon him — an episode to which tradition traces the name Rafida for those who "rejected" him. Zayd was killed by an arrow in street fighting, and his body was exhumed and crucified by the Umayyad authorities; his head was sent to Damascus. His son Yahya continued the revolt in Khurasan and was killed in 743.

Doctrine and legacy

Zaydism holds that the imam must be a descendant of Hasan or Husayn who actively asserts his claim by rising (khuruj) against unjust rule, rather than inheriting an occult office — a doctrine that distinguishes Zaydis from Twelver Shia. Zaydi theology is close in several respects to the Mu'tazila, and Zaydi law is often regarded as the Shia school nearest to the Sunni schools. Zaydi imamates endured in Yemen until 1962.
