Diels–Alder reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The archetypal Diels–Alder reaction]

The Diels–Alder reaction is a chemical reaction between a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene, commonly termed the dienophile, to form a substituted cyclohexene derivative. It is the prototypical [4+2] cycloaddition: four π-electrons of the diene and two of the dienophile reorganise in a single concerted step to form two new σ-bonds and a six-membered ring.

butadiene + ethylene → cyclohexene

History

The reaction was discovered by the German chemists Otto Diels and Kurt Alder in 1928; they received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1950 "for their discovery and development of the diene synthesis". Robert Burns Woodward and Roald Hoffmann later explained its stereospecificity in terms of orbital symmetry conservation, and the reaction featured in many landmark total syntheses of natural products.

Mechanism and selectivity

The reaction is a pericyclic, concerted [π4s + π2s] cycloaddition proceeding through an aromatic-like transition state. It is stereospecific: the geometry of both diene and dienophile is preserved in the product, and substituents that are cis in the dienophile remain cis. When bicyclic products can form, the endo product — with the dienophile's substituents oriented under the diene's π system — is usually favoured kinetically (the Alder endo rule), an effect attributed to secondary orbital interactions. Electron-rich dienes react fastest with electron-poor dienophiles ("normal-demand"), a pattern rationalised by frontier molecular orbital theory as HOMO(diene)–LUMO(dienophile) matching; the reverse pairing gives inverse-electron-demand reactions.

Variants and applications

Hetero-Diels–Alder reactions substitute an atom such as oxygen or nitrogen into the ring (e.g., imine dienophiles give tetrahydropyridines), and the retro-Diels–Alder reaction cleaves cyclohexenes back to diene plus dienophile. Lewis acid catalysis accelerates the reaction and improves regio- and enantioselectivity, and the reaction runs even in water. It is one of the most powerful methods for building six-membered rings with up to four new stereocentres in one step.
