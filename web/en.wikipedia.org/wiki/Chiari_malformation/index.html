A Chiari malformation (CM) is a structural defect in the cerebellum, characterized by a downward displacement of one or both cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum (the opening at the base of the skull). It is a common but often underdiagnosed condition, and the severity can range from asymptomatic to disabling.

Classification

Four types are traditionally described. Type I is the most common: tonsillar herniation of 5 mm or more without displacement of the brainstem, often discovered incidentally on MRI and sometimes asymptomatic. Type II (Arnold–Chiari malformation) involves herniation of both cerebellar and brainstem tissue and is associated with myelomeningocele, a form of spina bifida. Type III features herniation into a meningoencephalocele and is rare and severe. Type IV is severe cerebellar hypoplasia. The malformation is named for Hans Chiari, the Austrian pathologist who described it in the 1890s, though Arnold–Chiari recognises Julius Arnold's earlier contributions.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with type I are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur they typically begin in adolescence or adulthood and include occipital headaches worsened by coughing or straining (Valsalva maneuvers), neck pain, vertigo, visual disturbances, tinnitus, balance problems, weakness or numbness of the limbs, and sleep apnoea. Associated conditions include syringomyelia (a fluid-filled cavity in the spinal cord), hydrocephalus and scoliosis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Magnetic resonance imaging is the diagnostic test of choice. Asymptomatic type I malformations are usually observed. Symptomatic cases may be treated with surgical decompression of the foramen magnum, often with duraplasty, which enlarges the passage and restores normal cerebrospinal fluid flow; most patients experience significant symptom improvement.
