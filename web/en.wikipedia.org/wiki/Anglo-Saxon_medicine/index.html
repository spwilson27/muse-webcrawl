Anglo-Saxon medicine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anglo-Saxon medicine is the body of medical knowledge and practice in early medieval England, from roughly the fifth to the eleventh century. It is unusually well documented for its period: several medical texts survive in Old English rather than Latin, including the Herbarium, the Medicina de Quadrupedibus, Bald's Leechbook, and the Lacnunga. These works combine material derived from classical Mediterranean medicine with local herbal practice, charms, and ritual.

The texts

Bald's Leechbook (tenth century), named for an owner mentioned in its colophon, is a systematic practitioner manual organized from head to toe, covering external complaints, internal diseases, surgery, and even a remedy for the "flying venom" and "elf-shot" blamed for sudden illness. The Lacnunga ("Remedies") is a less orderly compilation of treatments, prayers, and charms, including the famous Nine Herbs Charm. The illustrated Herbarium, translated from a late-antique Latin source attributed to Apuleius, catalogues medicinal plants and their uses.

Practice and theory

Practitioners — the Old English læce, "leech", a word that also named the bloodsucking worm used in treatment — relied on herbal preparations, diet, bathing, bloodletting, and cautery. Disease was explained through a shifting mixture of humoral ideas inherited from antiquity, infection imagery of "venom" and invading "worms", and supernatural agents such as elves. Monastic scriptoria preserved and copied the texts, and monasteries likely provided much organized care.

Modern reassessment

Once dismissed as mere superstition, Anglo-Saxon remedies have attracted experimental attention. In 2015 researchers reconstructed a tenth-century eye salve from Bald's Leechbook — garlic, onion or leek, wine, and ox gall, matured in a brass vessel — and found it killed up to 90 per cent of MRSA bacteria in laboratory and animal models, stimulating continued study of medieval remedies as sources of antimicrobials.
