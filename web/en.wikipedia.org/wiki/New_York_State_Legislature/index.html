The New York State Legislature consists of the two houses that act as the state legislature of the U.S. state of New York: the New York State Senate and the New York State Assembly. The Constitution of New York vests the legislative power of the state in the Senate and Assembly. Both houses sit in the State Capitol in Albany and are elected every two years, with no term limits.

Composition

The State Senate has 63 members, each elected from a single-member district of roughly equal population. The State Assembly has 150 members, also from single-member districts. The numbers are set by the state constitution (the Senate's size has varied between 50 and 63 following court decisions allowing flexibility in the constitutional formula). Sessions begin in January; the legislature is considered one of the most professionalised and highest-paid state legislatures in the country.

Leadership

The Senate is led by the President pro tempore, who is also the Majority Leader (the lieutenant governor is the Senate's constitutional president but votes only to break ties). The Assembly is led by the Speaker, among the most powerful offices in state government. Historically, state budgets and major legislation were negotiated by the "three men in a room" — the governor, the Senate majority leader, and the Assembly speaker — a practice long criticised for opacity.

Legislative process

Bills must pass both houses in identical form and be presented to the governor, who may sign or veto; a veto can be overridden by a two-thirds vote of each house (a power rarely used — the first override in decades came in 2003). The legislature's most consequential annual task is enacting the state budget, due by 1 April, often negotiated as large omnibus bills. The governor's strong budgetary role under the 1929 "executive budget" amendment makes New York's chief executive unusually powerful in fiscal matters.

History and character

The legislature dates to the revolutionary Constitution of 1777, which succeeded the colonial assembly. Long periods of party division — notably Republican control of the Senate and Democratic control of the Assembly from 1975 to 2018 — characterised the late 20th and early 21st centuries; Democrats won unified control in 2018 and enacted a wave of legislation on elections, rent regulation, climate (the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act 2019), and criminal justice. The legislature has also been a recurring locus of ethics scandals, including the convictions of former leaders Sheldon Silver (Assembly speaker) and Dean Skelos (Senate majority leader) in federal corruption cases in the 2010s (Silver's conviction later partly overturned before a retrial conviction; both cases reshaped ethics debates).
