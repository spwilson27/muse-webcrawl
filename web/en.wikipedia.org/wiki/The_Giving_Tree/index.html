The Giving Tree is an American children's picture book written and illustrated by Shel Silverstein, first published in 1964 by Harper & Row. It has become one of Silverstein's best-known works and one of the most discussed children's books of the twentieth century.

Plot

The story follows the relationship between a boy and an apple tree. As a child, the boy plays in the tree's shade and eats her apples. As he grows older he asks for more: her apples to sell for money, her branches to build a house, and her trunk to make a boat. Each time, the tree gives gladly — "and the tree was happy." In the end, the boy, now an old man, returns needing only a quiet place to sit, and the tree, reduced to a stump, offers herself one last time.

Interpretation and controversy

The book has been read as a parable of selfless love and generosity, as an allegory of the parent–child relationship or of humanity's exploitation of nature, and — critically — as a depiction of an unhealthy, one-sided relationship. Some religious readings interpret the tree as a Christ figure. Despite early rejections from publishers who found it too sad or ambiguous for children, it became a perennial bestseller and has been translated into dozens of languages.
