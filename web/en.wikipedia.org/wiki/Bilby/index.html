Bilbies are desert-dwelling marsupials of the family Thylacomyidae, order Peramelemorphia, characterised by long ears, a pointed snout and strong forelimbs for digging. Of the two historically recognised species, the lesser bilby (Macrotis leucura) is extinct, and only the greater bilby (Macrotis lagotis) survives, classified as vulnerable.

The greater bilby, the family's only surviving species.

Description and behaviour

Greater bilbies weigh roughly 1–2.5 kg, with silky blue-grey fur, white underparts, a black-and-white tail crest and ears up to a third of their body length. They are nocturnal omnivores, digging spiral burrows up to 3 metres deep and feeding on insects, larvae, seeds, bulbs and fungi. Bilbies do not need to drink water, obtaining moisture from food. Females have a backward-facing pouch and one of the shortest gestations of any mammal, about 12–14 days.

Conservation

Once found across 70% of Australia, bilbies have retreated to scattered populations in the Tanami, Gibson and Great Sandy deserts and parts of Queensland, threatened by foxes, cats, rabbits and changed fire regimes. Captive breeding and fenced reserves support reintroductions. In Australia the bilby has been promoted as a native alternative to the Easter Bunny — the "Easter Bilby" — with chocolate bilbies raising funds for conservation.
