Effects pedal

An effects pedal, or stompbox, is an electronic device that alters the sound of a musical instrument or other audio source through audio signal processing. Most commonly used with the electric guitar, effects pedals sit on the floor and are operated with a footswitch while the musician continues to play. Pedals are typically housed in a small metal chassis and connected in a chain between the instrument and the amplifier, often mounted on a pedalboard.

A pedalboard carrying several effects pedals.

Types of effect

  • Distortion, overdrive and fuzz — clip the signal to add harmonics and sustain.
  • Modulation — chorus, flanger, phaser and tremolo vary the signal cyclically.
  • Time-based — delay and reverb repeat or diffuse the signal.
  • Filter — wah-wah, envelope filters and equalisers shape the frequency response.
  • Dynamics — compressors and noise gates control volume behaviour.
  • Pitch — octave, harmoniser and pitch-shifting effects transpose the signal.

History

Standalone effects began in the 1940s and 1950s with bulky studio and amplifier-mounted units. The first transistorised pedal, the Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone of 1962, brought the stompbox to market, and fuzz was popularised by recordings such as the Rolling Stones' "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" (1965). The 1970s saw an explosion of designs from companies such as Electro-Harmonix, MXR and Boss, whose compact pedals, introduced from 1977, set the modern format. Digital multi-effects units and, later, software amp and pedal modelling joined the analogue tradition, which survives in a large boutique pedal industry.

Use

Guitarists arrange pedals in a signal chain whose order affects the result — typically dynamics and filter effects first, then gain, modulation and finally time-based effects. Pedals are usually powered by 9-volt batteries or mains supplies, and many support "true bypass" switching so the unprocessed signal passes unchanged when the effect is off.
