Belarusian (беларуская мова, biełaruskaja mova) is an East Slavic language spoken primarily in Belarus, where it is one of the country's two official languages alongside Russian. It is closely related to Russian and Ukrainian, with which it shares descent from the Ruthenian language of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. "Belarusian" is the standard modern spelling; "Belarusan", "Belorussian" and "White Russian" also occur in older and some official sources.

History

The literary predecessor of Belarusian, Ruthenian (also called Chancery Slavonic), was a state language of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania; Francysk Skaryna printed the first books in the tradition, including a Bible, in the early 16th century. After the Union of Lublin (1569) and increasing Polonisation, written Ruthenian declined, and under the Russian Empire the printing of Belarusian in Latin type was banned in 1859 and the language was officially treated as a dialect of Russian.

A modern literary standard emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, codified by Branislaŭ Taraškievič's grammar of 1918. A Soviet orthographic reform of 1933 brought the standard closer to Russian; the pre-reform "classical" orthography (Taraškievica) remains in use among parts of the diaspora and opposition media, alongside the official norm. Belarusian suffered long periods of Russification; although it was declared the sole state language in 1990, a 1995 referendum restored Russian to equal status, and Russian now predominates in government, media and urban daily life. UNESCO classifies Belarusian as vulnerable.

Grammar and phonology

Belarusian has three genders, six or seven cases, and rich verbal aspect. Its phonology is noted for akanye (unstressed /o/ pronounced as [a]), yakanye, non-syllabic ŭ (ў) — a letter unique to the Belarusian Cyrillic alphabet — and the affricates dz and dž. The alphabet has 32 letters and is highly phonemic.

Scripts

Belarusian has historically been written in three scripts: Cyrillic (now standard), the Latin Łacinka alphabet used especially in the 19th century and in western Belarus and the diaspora, and the Arabic script used by Lipka Tatars in religious manuscripts (kitabs).

Usage today

Census data show that while a majority of ethnic Belarusians name Belarusian as their native language, only a minority — roughly a quarter — use it as their main language at home, with a mixed Russian–Belarusian vernacular called trasianka widespread in rural areas. Use of Belarusian is stronger among the cultural opposition, independent media and the post-2020 diaspora.
