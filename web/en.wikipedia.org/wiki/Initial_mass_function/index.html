Initial mass function

In astronomy, the initial mass function (IMF) is an empirical function that describes the initial distribution of masses for a population of stars at formation. It is a key input to models of stellar and galactic evolution, since a star's mass determines its lifetime, luminosity and eventual fate.

Forms

Edwin Salpeter first derived the IMF in 1955 from stars in the solar neighbourhood, finding a power law in which the number of stars per mass interval declines steeply with mass (dN/dM ∝ M−2.35): low-mass stars are far more numerous than massive ones. Later work — notably the multi-part power laws of Glenn Miller and John Scalo, Pavel Kroupa, and the log-normal-plus-power-law form of Gilles Chabrier (2003) — showed the IMF flattens below about one solar mass and turns over at still lower masses, including brown dwarfs.

Universality debate

Whether the IMF is universal or varies with environment — starburst galaxies, the early universe, dense clusters — is an active research question. Evidence from integrated galaxy light, gravitational lensing and stellar counts has been interpreted both ways; a variable IMF would substantially alter inferred stellar masses and star-formation rates of distant galaxies.
