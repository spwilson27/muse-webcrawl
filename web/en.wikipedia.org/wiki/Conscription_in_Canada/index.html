Conscription in Canada

[Image placeholder: Canadian WWI recruiting poster]

Conscription in Canada — compulsory military service — has been imposed only twice in Canadian history, during the two World Wars, and both times it provoked severe political crises because of opposition in Quebec. Canadian military forces have otherwise been all-volunteer.

First World War

Voluntary enlistment supplied the Canadian Expeditionary Force until 1917, when falling recruitment and heavy losses led Prime Minister Robert Borden's government to pass the Military Service Act. The resulting Conscription Crisis of 1917 split the country along linguistic lines; the Union Government won the December 1917 election, and about 120,000 men were conscripted, of whom roughly 47,500 went overseas.

Second World War

Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King, determined to avoid a repeat of 1917, introduced conscription for home defence only under the National Resources Mobilization Act of 1940, with the slogan "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary". A 1942 plebiscite released the government from its no-conscription pledge, though English and French Canada voted in opposite proportions. Only in late 1944, after heavy infantry losses in Europe, did King dispatch about 16,000 home-defence conscripts overseas — triggering the Conscription Crisis of 1944, the brief Terrace mutiny, and the resignation of the defence minister, though the political damage was contained.

Since 1945

Canada has maintained volunteer armed forces since 1945; the legal machinery for conscription remains only in the sense that Parliament could enact it anew. The memory of the crises has made the subject politically untouchable.
