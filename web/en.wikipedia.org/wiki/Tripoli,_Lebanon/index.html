Tripoli, Lebanon

The Citadel of Raymond de Saint-Gilles overlooking Tripoli

Tripoli (Arabic: طرابلس, Ṭarābulus) is the largest city in northern Lebanon and the second-largest city in the country after Beirut. It is the capital of the North Governorate and the Tripoli District, located about 85 kilometres north of Beirut on the Mediterranean coast.

History

Tripoli's name derives from the Greek Tripolis, "three cities", reflecting its origin as a federation of settlements founded by Phoenicians from Tyre, Sidon and Arwad. The site was later ruled by Persians, Romans, Arabs and Crusaders. The Crusaders built the Citadel of Raymond de Saint-Gilles in the early 12th century, and the city became the centre of the County of Tripoli, one of the Crusader states.

In 1289 the city fell to the Mamluk Sultan Qalawun, who destroyed the old port city and built a new inland city at the foot of the citadel; this Mamluk-era core, with its mosques, madrasas, hammams and souks, survives today. Tripoli later passed to the Ottoman Empire and, after the First World War, to the French Mandate before becoming part of independent Lebanon.

Economy and culture

Tripoli is historically famed for its soap-making, sweets (especially halawet el-jibn), olive oil and furniture workshops. Its port is Lebanon's second most important. The city's Rachid Karami International Fair grounds, designed by Brazilian architect Oscar Niemeyer, are a noted monument of modernist architecture. The Palm Islands Nature Reserve lies offshore.
