Stellarator

A stellarator is a device that confines a hot plasma with magnetic fields in order to sustain a controlled nuclear fusion reaction. Unlike the more common tokamak, which drives a large electric current through the plasma to generate part of the confining field, the stellarator produces its twisted magnetic field entirely with external coils, allowing steady-state operation without plasma current and avoiding current-driven disruptions.

History

The stellarator was invented by Lyman Spitzer of Princeton University in 1951; early devices (the figure-8 Model A and B series) were built at what became the Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory. Results in the 1950s–60s were disappointing because of poor confinement, and after the Soviet tokamak results of 1968 most research shifted to tokamaks. Interest revived with computer-optimised designs from the 1980s onward.

Modern devices

The largest operating stellarator is Wendelstein 7-X in Greifswald, Germany, which began operation in 2015 and has demonstrated long plasma discharges with optimised ("quasi-isodynamic") magnetic geometry. Other major machines include the Large Helical Device (LHD) in Japan and the HSX device in Wisconsin. The complex, non-planar superconducting coils of modern stellarators, designed by supercomputer optimisation, are their main engineering challenge.
