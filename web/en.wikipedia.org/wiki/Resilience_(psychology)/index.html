Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is the ability to cope mentally and emotionally with a crisis, to adapt successfully in the face of adversity, trauma, tragedy, threats or significant sources of stress, and to return to a pre-crisis state relatively quickly. It is sometimes described as "bouncing back" from difficult experiences. Resilience is not a fixed trait but a dynamic process influenced by individual characteristics, relationships and environment.

Historical development

Research on resilience grew out of studies in the 1970s of children who developed well despite growing up in high-risk conditions, notably Emmy Werner's Kauai longitudinal study and the work of Norman Garmezy and Michael Rutter. These studies shifted attention from risk factors alone to the protective factors that allow some individuals to thrive under adversity.

Protective factors

Frequently identified protective factors include supportive relationships with family and community, self-efficacy and a sense of control, cognitive flexibility, emotion regulation, optimism, and the ability to make meaning of difficult experiences. Contemporary researchers such as Ann Masten have described resilience as "ordinary magic" — arising from commonplace adaptive systems rather than rare personal qualities. Interventions in schools, the military and workplaces aim to build resilience skills, although the effectiveness and framing of some programmes remain debated.
