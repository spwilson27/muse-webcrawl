Osmoregulation

Osmoregulation is the active regulation of the osmotic pressure of an organism's body fluids, detected by osmoreceptors, to maintain the homeostasis of the organism's water content; that is, it maintains the fluid balance and the concentration of electrolytes (salts in solution which in this case is represented by body fluid) to keep the body fluids from becoming too diluted or concentrated. Osmotic pressure is a measure of the tendency of water to move into one solution from another by osmosis.

Osmoconformers and osmoregulators

Organisms are divided into osmoconformers, whose internal osmotic pressure tracks that of their environment (most marine invertebrates, and hagfish among vertebrates), and osmoregulators, which expend energy to keep their internal osmolarity constant in the face of environmental differences. Strict osmoregulators such as most vertebrates maintain body fluids at a concentration quite different from seawater or freshwater.

Strategies in animals

Freshwater animals face continuous water influx and ion loss: freshwater fish excrete large volumes of dilute urine and actively take up salts at the gills. Marine bony fish face the opposite problem — water loss and salt gain — so they drink seawater, excrete excess salt via chloride cells in the gills, and produce scant urine. Elasmobranchs such as sharks instead retain urea and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) to make their tissues slightly hyperosmotic to seawater. Terrestrial animals conserve water through concentrated urine (aided by the loop of Henle), waterproof integuments and behavioural adaptations; desert specialists such as kangaroo rats may never drink. Protists use contractile vacuoles to expel excess water, insects use Malpighian tubules, and plants regulate via stomata and vacuolar solute storage.

In humans

In mammals, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect changes in plasma osmolarity and trigger thirst and release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin), which increases water reabsorption in the kidney's collecting ducts. Aldosterone regulates sodium reabsorption, and atrial natriuretic peptide responds to blood volume. Failure of these systems underlies disorders such as diabetes insipidus and the syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion.
