Swindon Works was the main locomotive and carriage works of the Great Western Railway (GWR), located in Swindon, Wiltshire, England. Established in 1843, it grew into one of the largest railway engineering works in the world, and the town of Swindon itself grew up around it. The works passed to British Rail on nationalisation in 1948 and finally closed in 1986.

History

The site was chosen in 1840 because Swindon lay at the junction of the main line from London Paddington to Bristol and the line to Cheltenham, and because it was a convenient point at which locomotives could be changed for the gradients west of the town. Isambard Kingdom Brunel and Daniel Gooch selected the location, and the works opened in 1843 with a small workforce.

Under Gooch, and later chief mechanical engineers including William Dean, George Jackson Churchward, Charles Collett, and Frederick Hawksworth, Swindon built many celebrated classes of locomotive, including the Star, Castle, and King classes of express passenger locomotives. Churchward in particular made Swindon a byword for standardisation and precision engineering. At its peak the works covered some 300 acres and employed more than 14,000 people, making it one of the largest industrial complexes in Britain.

The works and the town

The works dominated Swindon's economy and society. The GWR built a model village for its workers, complete with cottages, a mechanics' institute, a medical fund society (an early forerunner of comprehensive socialised medicine that influenced the founders of the National Health Service), churches, and schools. The famous "hooter" sounded to call workers to the works and could be heard across the town.

Decline and closure

After nationalisation the works continued to build steam locomotives — the last, No. 92220 Evening Star, was completed in 1960 and was the final steam locomotive built by British Railways. Work then shifted to diesel locomotives and repairs, but employment steadily declined. The works closed in 1986, ending more than 140 years of railway engineering at the site.

Legacy

Part of the site is now occupied by the STEAM Museum of the Great Western Railway and the National Trust's headquarters at Heelis, while other former works buildings house the Swindon Designer Outlet and offices. Many locomotives built at Swindon survive in preservation.
