Schadenfreude

Schadenfreude (from German Schaden, "harm/damage", and Freude, "joy") is the experience of pleasure, joy, or self-satisfaction that comes from learning of or witnessing the troubles, failures, or humiliation of another. The word was borrowed into English in the 19th century.

Psychology

Psychological research links schadenfreude to envy, competition, and perceptions of deservingness: people are more likely to feel it toward rivals, envied high-status individuals, and those seen as responsible for their own misfortune. Neuroimaging studies have found activation in reward-related brain regions (such as the ventral striatum) when subjects witness the misfortunes of envied others. A tendency toward schadenfreude is also associated with the "dark triad" personality traits.

Related concepts

English lacks a single native word with the same meaning; "gloating" and the coinage "epicaricacy" are near-synonyms. Antonyms include sympathetic joy — comparable to the Buddhist concept of mudita — and compassion. In German philosophy, figures from Aristotle's commentators to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche discussed the emotion, Schopenhauer calling it a sign of a bad character.

In culture

The emotion is a staple of slapstick comedy, tabloid coverage of celebrity downfalls, and sports rivalry, and it gave its name to a well-known song in the musical Avenue Q.
