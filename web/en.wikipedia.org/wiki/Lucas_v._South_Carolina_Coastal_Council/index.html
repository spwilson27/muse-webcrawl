Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992), is a U.S. Supreme Court decision establishing that a government regulation which deprives a landowner of all economically beneficial use of land constitutes a categorical taking under the Fifth Amendment, requiring just compensation, unless the prohibited use was already barred by background principles of state property or nuisance law.

Background

In 1986 developer David Lucas bought two residential lots on the Isle of Palms, South Carolina, intending to build single-family homes as on neighbouring parcels. In 1988 the state enacted the Beachfront Management Act, which — applying new setback lines — barred any construction on his lots. A trial court found the lots rendered valueless and awarded compensation, but the South Carolina Supreme Court reversed, holding that the regulation prevented a public harm.

Decision

Justice Antonin Scalia's majority opinion held that where regulation denies all economically beneficial use, compensation is required without the case-specific balancing of Penn Central; the state may only avoid payment if the owner's title never included the right to the prohibited use under background nuisance and property principles. The case was remanded; South Carolina ultimately bought the lots, which were later developed.

Significance

Lucas is a landmark of modern regulatory takings law and of the property-rights movement, though its "total taking" category applies narrowly, since courts have seldom found complete deprivation of value.
