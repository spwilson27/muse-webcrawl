Dryland farming

Dryland farming (or dry farming) is the cultivation of crops without irrigation in regions of low or erratic rainfall, relying on techniques that capture and conserve soil moisture. It is practised on roughly a fifth of the world's cultivated land, including the Great Plains of North America, the steppes of Eurasia, Mediterranean-climate regions, and parts of Australia, India, and the Middle East.

Techniques

  • Summer fallow: leaving land unplanted for a season so that two seasons' precipitation serve one crop;
  • Mulching and stubble retention to reduce evaporation and wind erosion;
  • Deep furrowing and contour ploughing to catch runoff;
  • Wide plant spacing and drought-adapted varieties of wheat, sorghum, millet, chickpea, and sunflower;
  • No-till and minimum-till methods that preserve soil structure and moisture.

History

Dry farming is ancient in the Mediterranean and Near East. Its aggressive promotion on the semi-arid American plains in the early 20th century, notably by Hardy Webster Campbell's "scientific soil culture," encouraged settlement beyond the reliable rainfall line; severe erosion followed in the drought of the 1930s, contributing to the Dust Bowl. Modern dryland research emphasises soil organic matter, cover crops, and residue management to stabilise yields.
