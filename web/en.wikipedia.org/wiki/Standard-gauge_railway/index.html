Standard-gauge railway

A standard-gauge railway is a railway with a track gauge of 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in). The standard gauge is the most widely used railway gauge in the world, with approximately 55% of the world's railway lines using it; it is the gauge of most high-speed rail systems and of the principal networks in North America, most of Europe, China and many other countries.

Origins

The gauge descends from the coal-hauling wagonways of north-east England and was adopted by George Stephenson for early railways including the Stockton and Darlington (1825) and the Liverpool and Manchester (1830). The slightly odd dimension — originally 4 ft 8 in, widened by half an inch to ease running on curves — spread with British-built railways and locomotives. A Royal Commission led to the Regulating the Gauge of Railways Act 1846, which mandated 4 ft 8½ in for Great Britain, ending the gauge war against Brunel's broad gauge of 7 ft ¼ in.

A popular legend traces the gauge to the width of Roman chariots and war carts; railway historians regard the story as an urban myth, the actual lineage running through early modern coal wagonways in northern England.

Adoption

The United States standardized on the gauge by the late nineteenth century (the South converted some 11,500 miles of five-foot gauge track in a famous two-day effort in 1886). It is the norm in nearly all of continental western and central Europe (Iberia, Ireland, Finland and the former Soviet Union use broad gauges), and China has built its extensive high-speed network to standard gauge. The Shinkansen of Japan also uses standard gauge, in contrast to Japan's predominantly 1,067 mm conventional network. Break-of-gauge facilities, variable-gauge axles and dual-gauge track manage the boundaries between standard and other gauges.
