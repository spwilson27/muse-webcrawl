Cognitive load

In cognitive psychology, cognitive load refers to the amount of working memory resources used. Cognitive load theory, developed by Australian educational psychologist John Sweller from the late 1980s, holds that because working memory is severely limited in capacity and duration, instructional materials should be designed to avoid overloading it, so that learners can devote resources to building schemas in long-term memory.

Types of cognitive load

Intrinsic load is imposed by the inherent complexity of the material, measured by the number of elements that must be processed simultaneously and their interactivity. Extraneous load is generated by the way information is presented—for example, when learners must mentally integrate a diagram with a separated text explanation (the split-attention effect). Germane load refers to resources devoted to constructing and automating schemas; in later versions of the theory it is treated as the useful part of intrinsic load rather than a separate additive component.

Instructional implications

The theory supports the worked example effect (novices learn more from studying worked solutions than from solving equivalent problems), the use of integrated diagrams, reducing redundant information, and sequencing instruction from low to high element interactivity. The expertise reversal effect predicts that supports helpful for novices become extraneous as learners gain expertise.

Measurement and criticism

Cognitive load is measured with self-rating scales (such as the Paas scale), secondary-task performance, and physiological measures including pupil dilation. Critics argue that the three types of load are difficult to measure independently and that some formulations risk circularity, though the theory remains highly influential in education and training research.
