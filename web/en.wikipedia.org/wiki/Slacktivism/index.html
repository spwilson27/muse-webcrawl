Slacktivism

Slacktivism is a pejorative portmanteau of slacker and activism, describing support of a political or social cause by means that involve minimal effort or commitment — for example signing an online petition, liking or sharing a post, changing a profile picture, or wearing an awareness ribbon. Critics argue such gestures give participants a feeling of having contributed without producing meaningful change, while defenders note they can raise awareness and serve as a gateway to deeper engagement.

History of the term

The word appeared in the 1990s; an early use by activist Fred Clark described young people doing small acts such as planting trees, and it was popularized in a 2001 New York Times piece by Barrie-Anne Wakeham. The term spread widely with the growth of social media campaigning in the late 2000s and 2010s. Related expressions include clicktivism, armchair activism, and, for commercial analogues, awareness campaigns such as product (RED).

Examples and debate

Frequently cited examples include the Kony 2012 video campaign, the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge — which, unusually, raised over $100 million for ALS research — and hashtag movements that prompt widespread posting but disputed policy outcomes. Research on the question is mixed: studies suggest token public support can sometimes reduce later substantive contributions (a moral licensing effect), while other work finds online engagement correlates with offline participation and can help movements recruit and coordinate.
