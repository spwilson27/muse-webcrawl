Neo-slave narrative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The neo-slave narrative is a genre of modern fictional works by African-American and other writers that take the form of, or closely engage with, the historical slave narrative — the autobiographical accounts of enslaved people such as Frederick Douglass and Harriet Jacobs. The term was coined by critic Bernard W. Bell in The Afro-American Novel and Its Tradition (1987), which described them as "residually oral, modern narratives of escape from bondage to freedom."

Characteristics

Neo-slave narratives adopt or rework the conventions of the antebellum slave narrative — the first-person account of bondage, literacy, and escape — but with contemporary techniques such as multiple perspectives, nonlinear time, magical realism, and metafiction. They often recover interior lives and perspectives (especially women's) that the original narratives could not fully record, and interrogate how slavery's legacy persists.

Major works

Notable examples include Margaret Walker's Jubilee (1966), Ernest J. Gaines's The Autobiography of Miss Jane Pittman (1971), Ishmael Reed's Flight to Canada (1976), Octavia Butler's Kindred (1979), Charles Johnson's Oxherding Tale (1982) and Middle Passage (1990), Toni Morrison's Beloved (1987), and Colson Whitehead's The Underground Railroad (2016).
