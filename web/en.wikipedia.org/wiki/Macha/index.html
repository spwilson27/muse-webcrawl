Macha

Macha (Old Irish pronunciation roughly "MAH-kha") is a goddess of ancient Irish mythology, associated with war, horses, sovereignty and the province of Ulster. She is one of the three Morrígna, along with the Morrígan and Badb, and is closely linked with the royal site of Emain Macha (Navan Fort, County Armagh), whose name means "Macha's brooch" or "the twins of Macha", depending on the tradition.

The three Machas

Medieval Irish texts preserve several distinct figures named Macha. Macha, wife of Nemed, was said to have cleared the plain on which Emain Macha later stood and to have given her name to Ard Macha (Armagh). Another Macha was a queen of the line of the Red Branch. The best-known is Macha wife of Cruinniuc, the heroine of the tale Noínden Ulad ("The Debility of the Ulstermen").

The curse of Macha

In the tale, Macha, a mysterious and swift-running woman, married the widowed farmer Cruinniuc on the condition that he never boast of her. At a royal assembly Cruinniuc boasted that his wife could outrun the king's horses. Forced to race while heavily pregnant, Macha begged for a delay, was refused, won the race, and gave birth to twins at the finish line. In her agony she cursed the men of Ulster: in their hour of greatest need they would suffer the pangs of childbirth for five days and four nights, for nine generations. This debility explains why, in the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the hero Cú Chulainn alone defends Ulster against Queen Medb's army.

Interpretation

Scholars often interpret Macha as a sovereignty goddess whose favour legitimises kingship and whose horse associations link her to figures such as the Welsh Rhiannon and the Gaulish Epona. Her triple forms reflect the fluid, layered character of the surviving Irish mythological texts, which were recorded by Christian scribes centuries after they were composed orally.
