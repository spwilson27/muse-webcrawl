Sloths are a Neotropical group of xenarthran mammals constituting the suborder Folivora. There are six living species in two genera: the three-toed sloths (Bradypus) and the two-toed sloths (Choloepus). Noted for their slowness of movement, they spend most of their lives hanging upside down in the trees of the tropical rainforests of South America and Central America.

Biology

Sloths are built for life in the trees: they have long limbs with curved claws that hook around branches, allowing them to hang with little muscular effort. Their fur grows away from the body — from belly to back — so rain runs off while they hang inverted, and it often hosts green algae that provide camouflage and may supply nutrients. Sloths have a multi-chambered stomach in which bacteria ferment their diet of leaves, which is low in energy; correspondingly, their metabolic rate and body temperature are low, and they move slowly to conserve energy.

Three-toed sloths have nine cervical vertebrae — more than almost any other mammal — allowing them to rotate their heads up to about 270 degrees. Sloths descend to the forest floor about once a week to defecate, for reasons still debated. Despite their apparent vulnerability, sloths are strong swimmers.

Evolution

The living tree sloths are the small survivors of a once diverse group. Extinct ground sloths, such as the giant Megatherium, reached the size of elephants in the Americas and died out around the end of the Pleistocene, roughly 11,000 years ago, probably due to a combination of climate change and human hunting.

Conservation

Most sloth species are classified as Least Concern, but the pygmy three-toed sloth of Isla Escudo de Veraguas is critically endangered, and the maned three-toed sloth of Brazil's Atlantic Forest is vulnerable, mainly because of habitat loss.
