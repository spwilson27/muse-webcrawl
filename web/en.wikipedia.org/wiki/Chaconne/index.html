Chaconne

A chaconne (French: [ʃaˈkɔn]; Spanish: chacona; Italian: ciaccona) is a type of musical composition originating as a dance in the Spanish Golden Age world, which in the Baroque era developed into a major instrumental form: a set of continuous variations over a short, repeating harmonic progression or bass line (an ostinato). It is closely related to the passacaglia, and the two terms were often used interchangeably by Baroque composers.

Origins

The chacona first appeared in the late 16th century as a lively, sometimes bawdy dance-song in Spain and its American colonies, accompanied by guitar, castanets, and tambourine. Cervantes and Lope de Vega mention it in their writings. Carried to Italy and France, the dance was gradually refined at court; by the mid-17th century composers such as Frescobaldi, Monteverdi, and Lully had transformed it into a stately instrumental form in triple metre over a four- or eight-bar ground bass, frequently moving from tonic to dominant.

The Baroque chaconne

The chaconne's repeating harmonic framework — often the descending tetrachord pattern i–VII–i–V or a cadential progression — provided the scaffold for long spans of variation. Celebrated examples include:

  • Pachelbel's Chaconne in F minor for organ.
  • Buxtehude's Passacaglias and Chaconnes for organ (notably BuxWV 161).
  • Purcell's Chacony in G minor (Z. 730) for strings.
  • Lully's operatic chaconnes, grand concluding dances in his tragédies en musique.
  • The final chorus "Lasst uns den Leib begraben" precedents aside, the form culminates in J. S. Bach's Chaconne from the Partita No. 2 in D minor for solo violin, BWV 1004 — a movement of some 256 bars comprising 64 variations, widely regarded as one of the supreme achievements of the violin repertoire. Brahms wrote that "on one stave, for a small instrument, the man writes a whole world of the deepest thoughts and most powerful feelings."

Later use

Though the form receded after the Baroque era, composers continued to invoke it: the finale of Brahms's Symphony No. 4 is a passacaglia/chaconne of 30 variations over a theme adapted from Bach. In the 20th century, Britten, Webern, and others used chaconne and passacaglia structures, and Ferruccio Busoni's transcription of Bach's Chaconne for piano became a concert staple.

Distinction from the passacaglia

Musicologists have long debated the difference between the chaconne and the passacaglia. A common (though not consistently observed) distinction holds that the chaconne varies over a repeating chord progression in the bass, while the passacaglia varies over a repeating melodic bass line; in practice Baroque composers mixed the terms freely.
