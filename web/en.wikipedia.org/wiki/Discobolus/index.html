Discobolus

The Discobolus of Myron (Greek: Δισκοβόλος, "discus thrower") is a famous Greek sculpture completed around 460–450 BC, at the start of the Classical period. The original bronze, by the sculptor Myron of Eleutherae, is lost; the work is known through numerous Roman copies in marble and bronze, including the well-known example in the Museo Nazionale Romano (the Discobolus Palombara).

Description

The statue represents a young athlete coiled at the moment of maximum tension just before releasing the discus: the torso twists, one arm swings the discus back while the other balances forward, capturing potential energy in a single, harmonious and rhythmically balanced pose. Ancient authors including Quintilian and Lucian described the work's distinctive twisted posture.

Copies and influence

Several Roman copies survive in varying states of preservation; some were restored in the 18th century with the head turned incorrectly (looking forward rather than down toward the discus hand), an error corrected by comparison with better-preserved examples. The Discobolus became an emblem of classical athleticism and has been widely reproduced; its image featured in connection with the 1948 London Olympics, and a controversial episode in its modern history involved the interest of Nazi Germany in acquiring the Palombara copy, which was returned to Italy after the Second World War.
