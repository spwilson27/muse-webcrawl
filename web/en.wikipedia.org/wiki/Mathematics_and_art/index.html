Mathematics and art

Tessellation, as in the work of M. C. Escher, unites geometry and art.

Mathematics and art are related in a variety of ways. Mathematics has itself been described as an art motivated by beauty. Mathematics can be discerned in arts such as music, dance, painting, architecture, sculpture, and textiles. This article focuses, however, on mathematics in the visual arts.

Perspective and proportion

Renaissance artists transformed painting by applying geometry. Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated linear perspective around 1415, and Leon Battista Alberti and Piero della Francesca wrote treatises on it; Piero's De prospectiva pingendi is essentially a work of mathematics. Albrecht Dürer published measurements of the human body and perspective constructions. The golden ratio (φ ≈ 1.618) has often been claimed — sometimes dubiously — to govern the proportions of works from the Parthenon to Le Corbusier's Modulor system.

Symmetry, tiling, and pattern

Islamic art developed elaborate geometric girih tilings that anticipate quasicrystalline patterns, as in the Darb-e Imam shrine in Isfahan. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher, taught by crystallographers' diagrams and inspired by the Alhambra, produced famous tessellations and impossible objects; his work was admired by mathematicians such as H. S. M. Coxeter. All 17 wallpaper groups appear in historical ornamental art.

Anamorphosis and optical art

Anamorphosis — distorted images intelligible only from a particular viewpoint or in a mirror — appears in Holbein's The Ambassadors (1533). In the 20th century, Op art (Bridget Riley, Victor Vasarely) exploited precise geometry and visual perception, while anamorphic installations became a public-art genre.

Fractals and algorithmic art

Computer graphics made fractal geometry a visual medium: images of the Mandelbrot set are iconic, and algorithmic or generative art uses mathematical procedures to produce images. Fractals have been used to analyze paintings — controversially, claims that Jackson Pollock's drip paintings are fractal. Origami has developed its own mathematics (the Huzita–Hatori axioms) and applications in engineering. In architecture, parametric design and the work of Antoni Gaudí (using ruled surfaces and catenary models) and modern firms using computational geometry show the continuing bond between the disciplines.
