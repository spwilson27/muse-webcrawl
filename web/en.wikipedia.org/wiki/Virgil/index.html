Virgil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Virgil reading the Aeneid to Augustus and Octavia, in a neoclassical painting: the poet with his scroll before the imperial family]

Publius Vergilius Maro (15 October 70 BC – 21 September 19 BC), known in English as Virgil or Vergil, was the greatest poet of ancient Rome. His three works define classical Latin poetry: the Eclogues (42–39 BC), pastorals in the manner of Theocritus; the Georgics (finished 29 BC), a didactic poem on farming of unmatched finish; and the unfinished Aeneid, the epic of Aeneas and of Rome.

Born near Mantua in Cisalpine Gaul, Virgil lost family land in the triumviral confiscations, was drawn into the circle of Maecenas and Augustus, and became the voice of the Augustan settlement — singing peace after civil war, Italy's soil and Rome's destiny, with a melancholy that never quite leaves his hexameters.

Reputation

Virgil was a school author already in his lifetime; in late antiquity his works were used for divination (the Sortes Vergilianae) and he became, for the Middle Ages, a sage and magus as well as a poet — Dante's guide and master, 'the glory and light of poets'. The Renaissance recovered the poet; modern criticism stresses both his Augustan loyalties and his private voices of loss.

Style

His hexameter — flexible, musical, loaded with allusion to Homer, Apollonius and Lucretius — set the standard for Latin verse. Phrases of his are embedded in modern languages and mottos, and his Fourth Eclogue's prophecy of a child was long read as a pagan annunciation of Christ.
