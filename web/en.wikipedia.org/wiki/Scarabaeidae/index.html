The Scarabaeidae are a family of beetles, the scarab beetles, comprising more than 35,000 described species worldwide. They are stout-bodied beetles, many with striking metallic colours, and are recognised by their distinctive antennae, which end in plate-like segments (lamellae) that can fan out or be pressed together.

Biology

Scarabs occupy many ecological roles. The dung beetles (subfamilies Scarabaeinae and Aphodiinae) feed on dung, famously rolling it into balls; they provide major ecosystem services by burying dung and recycling nutrients. Flower chafers (Cetoniinae) feed on pollen, nectar, and fruit, while the larvae of many species — the white grubs of June beetles (Melolonthinae) and rhinoceros beetles (Dynastinae) among them — feed on roots and decaying plant matter. The male rhinoceros and Hercules beetles bear large horns used in contests over food and mates; the Hercules beetle is among the longest beetles in the world.

Cultural significance

The sacred scarab (Scarabaeus sacer) was revered in ancient Egypt: the god Khepri was depicted as a scarab, and the beetle rolling its ball was associated with the sun's movement and with rebirth. Scarab-shaped amulets and seals were produced for thousands of years.
