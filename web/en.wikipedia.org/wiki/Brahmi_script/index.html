Brahmi (Sanskrit: ब्राह्मी) is an ancient writing system of India that appeared fully developed by the 3rd century BCE, most famously in the edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka inscribed on pillars and rocks across the subcontinent. It is the ancestor of most scripts of South and Southeast Asia — the Brahmic family, numbering over a hundred scripts including Devanagari, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Sinhala, Thai and Tibetan.

Structure

Brahmi is an abugida: each consonant sign carries an inherent vowel /a/, modified by attached vowel signs (matras), while independent vowel letters are used initially. Conjunct consonants are written as stacked ligatures, and special marks (virama, anusvara, visarga) adapt the system to the sounds of Sanskrit and Prakrit. It is written left to right.

Origins and decipherment

The origin of Brahmi is debated: proposals include derivation from the Semitic/Aramaic alphabet via trade contacts, from the Indus script, or indigenous invention. Brahmi fell out of everyday use as its regional descendants diverged in the first millennium CE, and knowledge of it was lost until James Prinsep deciphered it in 1837 using bilingual coins and the Ashokan inscriptions — a breakthrough that recovered the history of Ashoka and early India.

Descendants

Over the centuries Brahmi developed into northern (Gupta and its successors: Nagari, Bengali-Assamese, Gujarati, Gurmukhi) and southern (Tamil, Telugu-Kannada, Malayalam, Grantha) branches, and travelled with Buddhism and Indian trade to Tibet, Central Asia and Southeast Asia, where it underlies the scripts of Burma, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and the traditional scripts of Indonesia and the Philippines.
