Ouzo

Ouzo (Greek: ούζο) is a dry anise-flavoured aperitif that is widely consumed in Greece. It is made from rectified spirits that have undergone a process of distillation and flavouring in copper stills, traditionally with aniseed, and often also with other aromatic seeds and herbs such as star anise, fennel, coriander, liquorice root, cinnamon and cloves. Ouzo is strongly associated with Greek culture and social life, and is protected within the European Union as a product with a protected designation of origin: only spirits produced in Greece (and Cyprus, for a closely related category) may be sold under the name.

History

The production of distilled spirits in the Greek world is often traced to the 14th century, when monks on Mount Athos are said to have distilled grape pomace spirits. The modern ouzo industry developed in the 19th century following Greek independence, particularly on the island of Lesbos and in the town of Tyrnavos in Thessaly, which remain famous centres of production. Distillers refined the practice of flavouring high-proof grape spirit with anise and other botanicals in copper pot stills. The word "ouzo" itself is of uncertain origin; one popular folk etymology connects it to the Italian phrase uso Marsiglia ("for use in Marseille"), allegedly stamped on crates of silk exported from Tyrnavos, though this story is generally considered apocryphal. Another theory derives it from the Turkish word üzüm, meaning "grape".

Production

Ouzo production begins with a base spirit, usually distilled from grape pomace or other agricultural products and rectified to about 96% alcohol by volume. This spirit is then redistilled in traditional copper stills together with aniseed and a producer's secret blend of aromatics. The distillation is carefully fractionated: the first runnings (the "heads") and the last (the "tails") are discarded, and only the heart of the distillate is kept. The flavoured spirit is then diluted with soft water to bottling strength, which must be at least 37.5% ABV under European Union rules. By law, at least part of the flavouring of genuine ouzo must come from distillation rather than from added essences alone ("ouzo of distillation").

The louche effect

When water or ice is added to ouzo, the clear liquid turns a milky, opalescent white. This phenomenon, known as the louche or "ouzo effect", occurs because anethole — the essential oil of anise — is soluble in alcohol but not in water. As the alcohol concentration falls, the anethole comes out of solution as a fine emulsion of microscopic droplets that scatter light. The same effect is seen in other anise spirits such as French pastis, Turkish rakı and Lebanese arak.

Serving and culture

Ouzo is traditionally served in tall, thin glasses, either neat, with cold water, or over ice, and is almost always accompanied by small plates of food — meze such as olives, feta, grilled octopus, sardines and fried cheese. In Greece it is a drink of leisurely socialising, sipped slowly at ouzeries (ouzo bars), tavernas and seaside cafés, particularly in the afternoon. It is also the quintessential accompaniment to seafood. Because of its sweetness and strength, it is regarded as a potent drink, and the Greek saying holds that ouzo should be drunk "like the old men" — slowly, with food and company.
