Corey–Chaykovsky reaction

The Corey–Chaykovsky reaction, also called the Corey–Chaykovsky epoxidation, is a chemical reaction used in organic chemistry for the synthesis of epoxides, aziridines, and cyclopropanes. It was discovered in 1961 by E. J. Corey and Michael Chaykovsky. The reaction involves the addition of a sulfur ylide to a carbonyl compound, imine, or α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound.

Mechanism and variants

The sulfur ylide — commonly dimethylsulfonium methylide, generated from trimethylsulfonium iodide and a strong base such as sodium hydride, or the more stable dimethyloxosulfonium methylide — adds nucleophilically to the carbonyl carbon. The resulting betaine then closes, expelling dimethyl sulfide (or DMSO) to form the three-membered ring. Addition to aldehydes and ketones gives epoxides; addition to imines gives aziridines; addition to enones gives cyclopropanes, because conjugate addition dominates with the more stabilised oxosulfonium ylide.

Applications

The reaction is a standard alternative to the Darzens reaction for epoxide synthesis and is widely used in total synthesis and medicinal chemistry; catalytic asymmetric variants use chiral sulfides to generate the ylide in situ. The Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky reaction refers specifically to the use of sulfoxonium ylides in epoxidation.
