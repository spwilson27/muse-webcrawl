[Image: Photograph of Lev Landau, intense and sharp-featured, in Kharkiv]
Landau in 1930s Kharkiv.

Lev Davidovich Landau (22 January 1908 – 1 April 1968) was a Soviet physicist who made fundamental contributions to many areas of theoretical physics, including the theory of Superfluidity (for which he received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physics), phase transitions, plasma physics, the Fermi liquid theory, and the density matrix (independently of von Neumann). His ten-volume Course of Theoretical Physics (with Evgeny Lifshitz) remains a monument of the discipline, and his Kharkiv–Moscow school — admission by the brutal 'theoretical minimum' examinations — defined Soviet physics.

Landau was arrested in the Great Purge (1938) and freed after a year through Kapitsa's personal intervention with Stalin's government. A 1962 car crash left him in a coma for weeks and ended his creative work; the Nobel that year was awarded for his superfluidity theory of 1941, which explained Kapitsa's experiments with the two-fluid model and predicted second sound. Landau levels, Landau diamagnetism, the Landau pole, the Ginzburg–Landau theory — his name marks physics everywhere dense matter and quantum fields meet.

Style and school

Landau classified physicists on a logarithmic scale of achievement (himself initially class 2, later promoted to 1.5; Newton class 0, Einstein 0.5) and demanded that students pass his theoretical minimum — a set of exams across all of theoretical physics — before joining his group; only 43 ever did. The Course's volumes, written with Lifshitz, trained physicists worldwide and remain in print in several languages.
