Ostomy

Ostomy

TypeSurgical procedure
CreatesStoma (artificial opening)
Main typesColostomy, ileostomy, urostomy
SpecialtyGeneral and colorectal surgery

An ostomy is a surgical procedure that creates an artificial opening, called a stoma, between the interior of the body and the exterior, usually through the abdominal wall, to allow waste to leave the body by a new route. The term also refers to the resulting opening and the system of care around it. Ostomies may be temporary or permanent.

Types

A colostomy brings part of the colon to the skin surface, producing formed or semi-formed stool. An ileostomy diverts the ileum, the end of the small intestine; its output is liquid and continuous and rich in digestive enzymes, so the pouching system must protect the skin carefully. A urostomy diverts urine, most commonly by connecting the ureters to an isolated segment of ileum (an ileal conduit) brought out as a stoma. Other ostomies include the tracheostomy in the neck and the gastrostomy and jejunostomy used for feeding.

Indications

Ostomies are created to treat colorectal and bladder cancers, inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, trauma, and congenital conditions. A temporary diverting stoma may protect a healing join (anastomosis) lower in the bowel and later be reversed.

Living with an ostomy

Output is collected in a removable pouch worn over the stoma and held by an adhesive skin barrier. Modern pouching systems are discreet, odour-proof, and allow most people to return to work, exercise, and travel. Specialist stoma care nurses teach patients appliance management and skin care. Common complications include peristomal skin irritation, retraction or prolapse of the stoma, stenosis, and parastomal hernia. Support organisations for "ostomates" exist in many countries, and estimates suggest hundreds of thousands of people live with an ostomy in the United States alone.

History

Deliberate surgical colostomy dates to the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, with the first successful operations for obstructed bowel. The modern ileostomy was transformed in the 1950s by Bryan Brooke's everted stoma design, and the development of reliable adhesives and pouches in the same decades made long-term ostomy life practical.
