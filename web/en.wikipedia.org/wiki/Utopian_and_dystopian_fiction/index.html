Utopian and dystopian fiction is a subgenre of speculative fiction that explores social and political structures through imagined societies. A utopia is a community or society possessing highly desirable or perfect qualities, while a dystopia is one with extremely bad or frightening characteristics, typically involving totalitarian control, environmental collapse, or both. The two forms are frequently used as lenses for commenting on the author's own society.

History

The word "utopia" was coined by Sir Thomas More for his 1516 book Utopia, describing a fictional island society in the Atlantic Ocean. The name derives from the Greek ou-topos ("no place") and eu-topos ("good place"), a pun More used deliberately. Earlier antecedents include Plato's Republic (c. 380 BC), which describes an ideal city ruled by philosopher-kings.

During the nineteenth century, works such as Edward Bellamy's Looking Backward (1888) presented optimistic socialist futures, while H. G. Wells explored both hopeful and warning visions. The twentieth century, marked by world wars and totalitarian regimes, produced the classic dystopias: Yevgeny Zamyatin's We (1924), Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932), and George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949).

Dystopia

A dystopia (from the Greek dys-, "bad", and topia, "place") is an imagined world or society in which people lead wretched, dehumanised or fearful lives. Dystopian fiction often explores themes of surveillance, censorship, propaganda, loss of individuality and environmental disaster. Notable examples include Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale (1985) and Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 (1953). Dystopian themes became especially prominent in young-adult fiction in the early twenty-first century.

Subgenres and related forms

  • Eutopia – a genuinely good place, as distinct from the ambiguous "utopia".
  • Anti-utopia – a critique of utopian ambitions themselves.
  • Heterotopia – a society embodying a mixture of good and bad features.
  • Cacotopia – an early synonym for dystopia, used by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.
