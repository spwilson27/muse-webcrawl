Modern Orthodox Judaism

Modern Orthodox Judaism is a movement within Orthodox Judaism that attempts to synthesise Jewish values and the observance of Jewish law (halakha) with the secular, modern world. Modern Orthodoxy holds that Jewish law is normative and binding while simultaneously involving itself in the larger society. It is closely associated with the philosophy of Torah Umadda ("Torah and secular knowledge") articulated by Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik and institutionalised at Yeshiva University in New York.

Philosophy

Modern Orthodoxy affirms the classical Orthodox tenets: the divine revelation of the Torah, the binding authority of halakha, and the thirteen principles of faith of Maimonides. Distinctively, it assigns positive religious value to secular learning, professional life, engagement with general culture, and — in its Religious Zionist form — the State of Israel. Samson Raphael Hirsch's 19th-century German Torah im Derech Eretz ("Torah with the way of the land") is an intellectual precursor, though the American movement developed its own institutions and character.

Institutions

The movement's flagship institutions include Yeshiva University and its Rabbi Isaac Elchanan Theological Seminary (RIETS), the Rabbinical Council of America (RCA), the Orthodox Union (OU), and a large network of day schools and synagogues, concentrated in the New York metropolitan area and other major US Jewish centres, with parallel communities in Israel, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia. Modern Orthodox day schools combine intensive Judaic studies with full secular curricula, and most graduates attend university.

Spectrum and debates

Modern Orthodoxy spans a spectrum from "Centrist Orthodoxy" — institutionally aligned with Yeshiva University — to more right-wing communities close to the Haredi world, and a liberal or "Open Orthodox" wing that has pushed boundaries on women's roles, including women's advanced Talmud study and, controversially, ordination. The ordination of women by Yeshivat Maharat and the use of the title "rabba" drew sharp criticism from mainstream Modern Orthodox bodies, and the movement continues to debate the pace of change on gender, attitudes to LGBTQ Jews, and relations with non-Orthodox denominations.
