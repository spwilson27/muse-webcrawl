Bergeron classification

[Image placeholder: World map of air-mass source regions labelled with Bergeron codes such as cP, mT, cA and mP]

The Bergeron classification is the most widely accepted system for classifying synoptic-scale air masses by their source regions and thermodynamic properties. It was developed by the Swedish meteorologist Tor Bergeron (1891–1977) of the Bergen School of Meteorology, and forms the basis of air-mass analysis used in weather forecasting.

The system

Air masses are designated by a combination of lowercase and uppercase letters. The lowercase letter denotes the moisture characteristics of the source region: c for continental (dry) and m for maritime (moist). The uppercase letter denotes the thermal character of the source region: A (Arctic), P (polar), T (tropical), E (equatorial), M (monsoon) and S (superior — subsiding dry air aloft).

cAContinental ArcticBitterly cold, very dry, stable
cPContinental polarCold and dry
cTContinental tropicalHot and dry

mTMaritime tropicalWarm and moist
mEMaritime equatorialVery warm, very moist

Modification

Because air masses are modified as they travel away from their source regions, a third letter may be appended: k (from German kalt) if the air mass is colder than the surface over which it moves — warming from below, becoming unstable, and producing cumulus and showers — or w (warm) if it is warmer than the surface, cooling into stable conditions with fog or low stratus. Thus mPw denotes maritime polar air being warmed over land, and cPk a cold, dry outbreak destabilised over a warmer surface. The Bergeron scheme underlies the spatial synoptic classification (SSC) used in modern climatology.
