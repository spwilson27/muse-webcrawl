Timbales

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the French cooking mould, see Timbale (food).

Timbales (pronounced tim-BAH-les) are shallow, single-headed drums with metal shells, played as a pair with sticks and typically mounted on a stand together with accessories such as a cowbell and jam block. Developed in Cuba in the early 20th century, they descend from the European timpani and side drums brought to the island for military and orchestral music, and became a defining voice of Afro-Cuban dance music — danzón, mambo, salsa, cha-cha-chá and Latin jazz.

The instrument

A standard set consists of two drums of different diameters (commonly 14 and 15 inches, called macho and hembra), only a few inches deep, with plastic or calfskin heads tuned high. The player — a timbalero — strikes the heads, the rims, and the metal shells (cáscara) with thin sticks, and often adds patterns on the cowbell (cencerro) and a woodblock. Characteristic techniques include the cáscara pattern played on the shells during verses, the mambo bell pattern in choruses, rimshot accents, and rapid fills known as abanico.

History and players

Timbales were adapted in danzón orchestras (charangas), where they replaced the older guayo and cata in the rhythm section, then moved to the forefront of the mambo era. The most famous timbalero, Tito Puente, brought the instrument to international prominence and helped make it a front-line solo instrument. Other influential players include Manny Oquendo, Orestes Vilató, Guillermo Barreto and Sheila E., who popularized the instrument in pop and funk.
