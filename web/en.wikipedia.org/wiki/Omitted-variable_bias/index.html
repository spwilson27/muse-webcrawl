Omitted-variable bias

In statistics, omitted-variable bias (OVB) is the bias that appears in estimates of parameters in a regression analysis when the assumed specification is incorrect in that it omits an independent variable that is a determinant of the dependent variable and correlated with one or more of the included independent variables.

Conditions

For omitted-variable bias to arise, two conditions must hold simultaneously:

  1. The omitted variable must be correlated with an included regressor.
  2. The omitted variable must be a determinant of the dependent variable — that is, it must have a non-zero coefficient in the true regression.

If either condition fails, omitting the variable does not bias the estimates of the other coefficients (though omitting a relevant but uncorrelated variable can still inflate standard errors).

Direction and magnitude

In the simple two-variable case, the bias equals the product of the effect of the omitted variable on the outcome and the regression coefficient from regressing the omitted variable on the included variable. Its sign can therefore be predicted from the signs of those two relationships: an omitted factor positively associated with both the treatment and the outcome biases the estimated effect upward, and so on. Because the true model is unknown, the practical remedies are to include plausible confounders, use proxy variables, or employ designs such as instrumental variables, panel-data fixed effects, or randomised assignment, which break the correlation between regressors and omitted factors.

A familiar example is estimating the return to education on earnings while omitting innate ability: ability plausibly raises both schooling and earnings, so the estimated return to schooling is biased upward.
