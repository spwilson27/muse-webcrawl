Clochán

A clochán (Irish, from cloch, "stone"; plural clocháin), also known in English as a beehive hut, is a dry-stone hut with a corbelled roof, characteristic of the south-western coast of Ireland. The walls are built of unmortared stone laid in rings that narrow progressively until they meet at the top, producing a beehive-shaped structure that sheds rain without any mortar or timber.

Distribution and history

Clocháin are most numerous on the Dingle Peninsula in County Kerry, where several hundred examples survive, singly and in clusters, on sites such as the slopes near Mount Brandon and around Fahan. They are famously associated with early Christian monastic settlements, most dramatically on Skellig Michael, where monks' cells and an oratory built in this technique stand near the summit of the island; the Gallarus Oratory, a small stone church built entirely with corbelling, is a related form. Dating is difficult because the technique remained in use for centuries: some huts are early medieval monastic cells, while others served as stores, byres, or shelters for pilgrims and farmers into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Construction

A typical clochán is circular in plan, 3–6 metres across, with a single low doorway (sometimes with a small window) and walls often over a metre thick at the base, battering inward as they rise. The corbelled dome was built by laying each stone course slightly further inward than the one below; a capstone closed the apex. Some examples are rectangular, and a few are joined in pairs or embedded in field banks and enclosure walls (cashels).
