American folk music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The term American folk music encompasses numerous genres including country, gospel, old time music, American blues, Americana, and Native American music. The music intermingled the traditions of a variety of ethnic populations — British, Irish, African, Scandinavian, German, and many others — in the territory of the United States following European colonisation. Because it developed largely outside commercial institutions, folk music long served as a record of the everyday lives, labours, migrations, and protests of ordinary Americans.

Roots and characteristics

Most songs considered traditional American folk music are of unknown authorship and were passed down orally for generations within families and communities. Ballads from the British Isles — some traceable to the Child Ballads collected in the 19th century — mixed in North America with African-American spirituals, work songs, and blues, with hymns and shape-note singing, and with the fiddle tunes of Appalachia. The banjo, derived from West African lutes, and the guitar, fiddle, mandolin, dulcimer, and harmonica became characteristic instruments. Common themes include hardship, death, faith, rambling, and the railroad.

Folk revival

Collectors such as John and Alan Lomax travelled the country for the Library of Congress's Archive of American Folk Song from the 1930s, recording performers including Lead Belly and Woody Guthrie. Guthrie's "This Land Is Your Land" (1940) became one of the most famous American folk songs. A commercial folk revival flourished from the late 1940s through the 1960s: the Weavers, Pete Seeger, Odetta, Joan Baez, and Peter, Paul and Mary brought traditional and topical songs to mass audiences, and the young Bob Dylan fused the folk idiom with rock, profoundly reshaping popular music. The Newport Folk Festival (founded 1959) was a centre of the revival; Dylan's controversial electric performance there in 1965 marked a turning point toward folk rock.

Folk music also became entwined with social movements — labour organising, the civil rights movement (which adapted "We Shall Overcome" from gospel and union song), and opposition to the Vietnam War.

Regional and related traditions

  • Appalachian music — old-time string bands, bluegrass (developed by Bill Monroe in the 1940s).
  • Cajun and zydeco — French Louisiana traditions.
  • Tejano and conjunto — Mexican-American music of the Southwest borderlands.
  • Native American music — powwow drumming, flute traditions, and ceremonial song.
  • Sea shanties, cowboy songs, and railroad songs — occupational traditions of the 19th century.

Legacy

American folk music forms the root system of much of the country's popular music: country, blues, rock and roll, and Americana all draw heavily on it. The Smithsonian Institution's Folkways Recordings preserve thousands of field and studio recordings, and festivals, archives, and a continuing singer-songwriter tradition keep the repertoire in circulation.
