Grinder (biohacker)

Grinders are a community of biohackers who augment their bodies with do-it-yourself cybernetic devices, including subdermal implants, without the assistance of medical professionals or regulatory approval. The term is used both by practitioners and by outside observers to describe people who treat the body as a platform for experimental technology, in the spirit of the "grinder" subculture depicted in cyberpunk fiction such as the work of William Gibson.

Practices

Common grinder modifications include:

  • Magnet implants – small neodymium magnets, coated in biocompatible materials such as titanium nitride or silicone, implanted in the fingertips. These allow the bearer to sense electromagnetic fields through vibration and to lift small ferrous objects.
  • RFID and NFC implants – glass-encapsulated transponder chips, roughly the size of a grain of rice, implanted in the hand and used for contactless access, identification and data storage.
  • LED implants – devices such as the Northstar implant, a star-shaped subdermal light developed by the collective Grindhouse Wetware, intended as a cosmetic and artistic modification.
  • Sensing devices – experimental implants and wearables such as the Bottlenose, which uses sonar or other sensors to feed new sensory data to the user through implanted magnets.

Community and philosophy

Grinders are part of the wider transhumanist and body-modification movements. Their philosophy emphasises open-source hardware, self-experimentation and direct, hands-on engagement with technology rather than waiting for commercial or medical institutions to provide enhancements. Much of the community's knowledge is shared through online forums, notably the forums associated with the company Dangerous Things, and through maker spaces and conferences.

Notable figures associated with the movement include Lepht Anonym, a British biohacker known for performing numerous self-surgeries and for candid discussion of their risks and failures, and Amal Graafstra, founder of Dangerous Things and an early populariser of RFID implants.

Risks and criticism

Because grinder procedures are usually performed outside medical settings, they carry risks of infection, implant rejection, coating failure and tissue damage. Medical professionals and regulators have criticised the practice, while practitioners respond that informed adults should be free to experiment on their own bodies and that community-developed procedures have steadily improved in safety.
