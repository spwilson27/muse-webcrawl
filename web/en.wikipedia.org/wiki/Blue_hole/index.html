Blue hole

The Great Blue Hole near Lighthouse Reef, Belize

A blue hole is a large marine cavern or sinkhole, which is open to the surface and has developed in a bank or island composed of carbonate bedrock (limestone or coral reef). Blue holes typically contain tidal-influenced water of fresh, marine, or mixed chemistry. They extend below sea level for most of their depth and may provide access to submerged cave passages. Well-known examples are the Dragon Hole (Sansha Yongle Blue Hole) in the South China Sea, the Great Blue Hole and Dean's Blue Hole in the Bahamas region, and the Blue Hole at Dahab in Egypt.

Formation

Most blue holes formed during past ice ages, when sea level was as much as 120 metres lower than today. Rainwater dissolved the exposed limestone, carving out cave systems; when the sea rose again, the cave roofs collapsed or the shafts filled, producing the deep blue circles visible from above. Their strikingly dark blue colour contrasts with the pale turquoise of the surrounding shallows.

Notable blue holes

The Great Blue Hole off Belize is about 300 metres across and 125 metres deep and was popularised by Jacques Cousteau, who declared it one of the world's top diving sites. Dean's Blue Hole in the Bahamas plunges to about 202 metres and hosts freediving competitions. The Dragon Hole in the Paracel Islands is the deepest known, at over 300 metres.

Ecology and exploration

Blue holes often lack oxygen in their deepest layers, preserving fossils and ancient pollen that help scientists reconstruct past climates. Stalactites recovered from flooded caves confirm their dry-land origin. They are challenging and sometimes dangerous dive sites, but support distinctive communities of marine life around their rims.
