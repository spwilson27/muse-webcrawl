The 2004 Equatorial Guinea coup attempt, commonly known as the Wonga coup, was a failed plot by mercenaries and financiers to overthrow President Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo of oil-rich Equatorial Guinea and install exiled opposition figure Severo Moto in his place. The plot collapsed in March 2004 when the mercenaries' aircraft was impounded in Zimbabwe and the advance party was arrested in Malabo.

The plot

Former British SAS officer and mercenary Simon Mann recruited a force largely drawn from ex-South African special forces soldiers, many veterans of the private military company Executive Outcomes. Backers allegedly promised funding in return for access to Equatorial Guinea's booming oil sector. On 7 March 2004, Zimbabwean authorities seized the group's Boeing 727 at Harare airport and arrested Mann and more than 60 men; simultaneously, the advance team under Nick du Toit was arrested in Malabo.

Trials and aftermath

Mann served a prison sentence in Zimbabwe before extradition to Equatorial Guinea, where he was sentenced to 34 years in 2008 but pardoned by Obiang in 2009. Mark Thatcher, son of former British prime minister Margaret Thatcher, pleaded guilty in South Africa to negligently helping to finance a helicopter for the plot and received a suspended sentence and a fine. Obiang remained in power, and the affair became the basis of novels and the 2014 film treatment in Mann's memoir "Cry Havoc."
