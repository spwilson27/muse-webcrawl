A Vindication of the Rights of Men, in a Letter to the Right Honourable Edmund Burke; Occasioned by His Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) is a political pamphlet by Mary Wollstonecraft, written in response to Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790). It was one of the first works in the pamphlet war that followed the French Revolution — the "Revolution Controversy" — and preceded Thomas Paine's Rights of Man. In it Wollstonecraft attacks hereditary privilege and the aristocratic order Burke defended, arguing for a moderate republicanism built on reason, property earned by industry, and the natural rights of man.

Background and publication

Burke's Reflections, published on 1 November 1790, denounced the Revolution and praised the British constitution's inherited traditions. Wollstonecraft wrote her reply in a matter of weeks; it was published anonymously by Joseph Johnson at the end of November 1790 — the first of dozens of responses. When the pamphlet sold well, Johnson brought out a second edition the following month with Wollstonecraft's name on the title page, an unusual step for a woman writer of the period. She was thus one of the first women to enter a major political debate under her own name.

Argument and style

Unlike many respondents, Wollstonecraft does not offer a systematic theory of government; instead she attacks Burke's premises emotionally and rhetorically, charging him with preferring theatrical sentiment and an idealized feudal past to justice for the living poor. She defends the Reverend Richard Price, whose sermon Burke had targeted, and argues that rights descend not from tradition but from God and reason: "the birthright of man... is such a degree of liberty, civil and religious, as is compatible with the liberty of every other individual". She criticizes primogeniture, the game laws, the established clergy, standing armies, and the treatment of the poor, and champions industrious commerce and the middling classes over idle aristocracy.

Reception and legacy

The pamphlet was widely reviewed and praised in reformist journals, launching Wollstonecraft's public reputation. Two years later she extended its logic in A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), arguing that the rights she had claimed for men applied equally to women — the work for which she is best known today.
