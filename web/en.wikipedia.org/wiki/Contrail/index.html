Contrail

Contrails (from "condensation trails") are line-shaped clouds produced by aircraft engine exhaust or changes in air pressure, typically at aircraft cruising altitudes several miles above the Earth's surface. Contrails are composed primarily of water, in the form of ice crystals. The combination of water vapor in aircraft engine exhaust and the low ambient temperatures at high altitudes causes the formation of the trails.

Formation

Engine exhaust contains water vapor, carbon dioxide, trace amounts of nitrogen oxides, hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide, sulfur gases, and particles of soot and metal. When hot humid air from jet exhaust mixes with the colder surrounding air, the water vapor condenses and may freeze, forming a visible trail. Contrails can also form through reductions in air pressure in wingtip vortices.

Types

Short-lived contrails disappear soon after the aircraft passes. Persistent contrails can last for hours and spread into broad cirrus-like cloud sheets called contrail cirrus. Persistent spreading contrails are of interest to climate scientists because they can affect Earth's radiative balance.

Climate impact

Studies suggest contrails contribute to climate change by trapping outgoing longwave radiation, though the net effect is uncertain. Aviation-induced cloudiness is an active area of research, and flight rerouting has been proposed to avoid regions of the atmosphere most favorable to persistent contrail formation.
