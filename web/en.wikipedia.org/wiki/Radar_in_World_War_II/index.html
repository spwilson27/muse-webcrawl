Radar in World War II

Radar in World War II greatly influenced many important aspects of the conflict. Radar (radio detection and ranging) was developed in secret by several nations in the years before and during the Second World War, and became one of the decisive technologies of the war, particularly in air defence and naval warfare.

British developments

Britain built Chain Home, a network of early-warning stations that played a vital role in the Battle of Britain (1940) by directing RAF fighters efficiently. The invention of the cavity magnetron in 1940 enabled centimetric radar small enough for aircraft and ships, and the technology was shared with the United States through the Tizard Mission.

Axis and Allied systems

Germany deployed Freya early-warning and Würzburg fire-control radars, while the United States fielded sets such as the SCR-584 gun-laying radar and airborne interception radars. Japan and the Soviet Union also developed systems, generally less advanced. H2S ground-mapping radar aided Allied bombing, and radar-equipped aircraft transformed anti-submarine warfare in the Battle of the Atlantic.

Countermeasures

The "radar war" included jamming, chaff ("Window"), radar detectors, and specialised units such as No. 100 Group RAF. Historians often rank radar alongside the atomic bomb and codebreaking as technologies that changed the war's course.
