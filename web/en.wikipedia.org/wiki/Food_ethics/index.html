Food ethics
The moral evaluation of food production, distribution, marketing and consumption.

Food ethics is the area of ethics concerned with the moral questions raised by the production, distribution, marketing, preparation, and consumption of food. It encompasses the treatment of animals raised for food, the environmental effects of agriculture, the rights and welfare of farm and food workers, the distribution of food between rich and poor, the ethics of food technologies such as genetic modification, and the responsibilities of consumers, companies, and governments in the food system.

Animal welfare and rights

Perhaps the most debated branch of food ethics concerns the use of animals. Utilitarian philosophers such as Peter Singer, in Animal Liberation (1975), argue that the suffering of animals in factory farming counts morally and cannot be justified by the trivial human pleasure of eating them; rights-based theorists such as Tom Regan argue that animals are "subjects of a life" with inherent value. Debates continue over practices including confinement systems (battery cages, gestation crates, veal crates), slaughter methods, and the ethics of eating animals at all, with positions ranging from abolitionist veganism through "conscientious omnivorism" and welfare-certified meat to defences of traditional husbandry.

Environment

Food production is a major driver of greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, water use, nutrient pollution, and biodiversity loss. Ethical questions include the fairness of diets whose footprint far exceeds others, the morality of food waste (about a third of food produced is lost or wasted), and duties to future generations around soil, climate, and the oceans. Analyses such as the EAT-Lancet Commission's "planetary health diet" attempt to define diets that are both healthy and within environmental limits.

Justice and access

Food ethics addresses food security and the right to food, hunger amid plenty, the working conditions of farm labourers and meatpacking workers, the bargaining power of farmers against retailers, and international trade rules. The fair trade movement and campaigns for living wages in supply chains arise from these concerns, as do debates about "food deserts," pricing, and the marketing of unhealthy food to children.

Technology and information

  • The ethics of genetically modified foods, gene editing, and cellular agriculture (cultured meat)
  • Labelling: what consumers have a right to know about origin, methods, additives, and health claims
  • Food safety and the precautionary principle
  • Ownership of seeds and genetic resources; biopiracy

Consumer ethics

At the individual level, food ethics asks what obligations shoppers have — to eat less meat, buy local or organic, avoid waste, or boycott products — and how effective consumer action is compared with political action aimed at changing the rules of the food system. Writers such as Michael Pollan have popularized such questions, connecting ethics with health and culture in works like The Omnivore's Dilemma (2006).
