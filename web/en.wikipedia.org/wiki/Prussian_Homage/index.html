The Prussian Homage (Polish: hołd pruski) was the formal investment, on 10 April 1525, of Albert of Hohenzollern as duke of the newly created Duchy of Prussia, a fief of the Kingdom of Poland. In a public ceremony in the market square of Kraków, Albert knelt before his uncle, King Sigismund I the Old, and swore fealty, receiving the ducal banner in return.

Background

Albert had been Grand Master of the Teutonic Order, whose monastic state in Prussia had been at war with Poland intermittently for a century. After the war of 1519–1521 ended in stalemate and a four-year truce, Albert — influenced by Martin Luther, who advised him to dissolve the Order's state, marry and rule Prussia as a secular hereditary duchy — accepted the Treaty of Kraków (8 April 1525) ending the war on Polish terms.

The ceremony

Two days after the treaty, Albert laid aside the white mantle of the Teutonic Knights, knelt on a cushion before Sigismund seated on a throne in Kraków's main square, and took the oath of fealty. The king touched him with a sword and handed him the banner bearing the black Prussian eagle with the letter "S" (for Sigismund) on its breast. The Duchy of Prussia thus became the first Protestant state in Europe, and Albert its first duke, ruling from Königsberg as a Polish vassal.

Legacy

The homage — repeated by Albert's successors — became a touchstone of Polish historical memory and a grievance in later Polish–German relations: in the 17th century the Treaty of Wehlau (1657) freed the duchy from Polish suzerainty, and in 1701 the ducal line's elector crowned himself "King in Prussia". The scene was immortalised in Jan Matejko's monumental 1882 painting Prussian Homage, now in the Sukiennice Museum in Kraków.
