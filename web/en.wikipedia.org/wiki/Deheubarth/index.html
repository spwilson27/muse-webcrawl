Deheubarth

[Image: Medieval kingdoms of Wales]
Deheubarth (in the south-west) among the medieval Welsh kingdoms

Deheubarth (Welsh pronunciation: [dɛˈhəɨbarθ], "the southern part") was a regional name for the kingdoms of south-west Wales, principally Dyfed, Ceredigion and Ystrad Tywi. It was never a single unified kingdom in its own right for long, but the term came into use in the tenth century when Hywel Dda (Hywel the Good), king of Dyfed and Seisyllwg, united the southern realms and ruled most of Wales; on his death in 950 his lands were again divided.

History

The heartland of Deheubarth was the old kingdom of Dyfed, together with Ceredigion (forming Seisyllwg under Seisyll ap Clydog) and the Tywi valley. Under Hywel Dda the region produced the Welsh lawbooks, the Laws of Hywel Dda, traditionally codified at Whitland. After periods of rule by the descendants of Hywel and by rivals from Gwynedd, Rhys ap Tewdwr re-established southern power until his death in battle in 1093, whereupon Norman lords overran much of the lowlands, leaving the native dynasty in the uplands of Cantref Mawr. His grandson Rhys ap Gruffudd, "the Lord Rhys" (d. 1197), rebuilt Deheubarth into the dominant Welsh power of the twelfth century, ruling from Dinefwr Castle, patron of the first recorded eisteddfod (1176), and recognized by Henry II as justiciar of south Wales. After his death the kingdom fragmented among his sons, and the region was absorbed piecemeal into the Principality of Wales of Llywelyn the Great and, after 1283, into the Edwardian settlement of Wales.
