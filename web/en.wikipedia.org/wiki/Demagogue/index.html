Demagogue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A demagogue is a political leader who seeks support by appealing to the desires, emotions and prejudices of ordinary people rather than by rational argument. The word comes from the Greek dēmagōgós — “leader of the people” — and was originally neutral; in classical Athens it acquired its negative sense with figures such as Cleon, whom Thucydides and Aristophanes portrayed as flattering and inflaming the assembly. Plato and Aristotle treated demagoguery as the corruption of democracy into mob rule.

Modern usage describes leaders who win power by whipping up passions against out-groups, promising simple solutions, attacking institutions (courts, press, elections) that constrain them, and presenting themselves as the people's sole authentic voice — a profile overlapping with populism and, where it hollows out democratic checks, with democratic backsliding. Because the term is pejorative, its application to particular politicians is always contested by their supporters.

Characteristics

Scholars and rhetoricians identify recurring techniques: scapegoating, conspiracy claims, performative transgression of norms, anti-elite rhetoric from wealthy or powerful speakers, and the claim “I alone can fix it”. Historical figures commonly labelled demagogues in the literature include Huey Long and Joseph McCarthy in the United States; the label's use about contemporary leaders is a staple of political controversy and is not adjudicated here.
