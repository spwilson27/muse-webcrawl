Middle Indo-Aryan languages

The Middle Indo-Aryan languages (or Middle Indic languages) are a stage of the Indo-Aryan languages of northern India, spanning roughly from 600 BC to AD 1000. They developed from Old Indo-Aryan (Vedic and Classical Sanskrit) and evolved into the modern New Indo-Aryan languages such as Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, and Punjabi.

The Prakrits

The Middle Indo-Aryan languages are collectively associated with the Prakrits — vernacular and literary languages that stood in contrast to learned Sanskrit. The most important include:

  • Pali — the language of the Theravada Buddhist canon.
  • Ardhamagadhi — associated with early Jain texts.
  • Maharashtri, Shauraseni, and Magadhi — literary Prakrits used in classical Sanskrit drama and poetry.
  • Gandhari — used in the northwest, written in the Kharoshthi script; the earliest surviving Buddhist manuscripts are in Gandhari.

The inscriptions of the emperor Ashoka (3rd century BC) were composed in regional Middle Indo-Aryan dialects and are among the earliest decipherable Indian texts.

Stages and features

Scholars distinguish early (Ashokan Prakrits, Pali), middle (the literary Prakrits), and late Middle Indo-Aryan, the latter called Apabhramsha (c. AD 600–1000), which immediately precedes the early forms of the modern languages. Characteristic changes from Sanskrit include the loss of the vocalic ṛ, simplification of consonant clusters through assimilation, loss of final consonants, and the erosion of the Sanskrit case system — trends that culminated in the largely analytic grammar of New Indo-Aryan.
