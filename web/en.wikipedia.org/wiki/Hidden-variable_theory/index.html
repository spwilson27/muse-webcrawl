Hidden-variable theory

A hidden-variable theory is a proposed explanation of quantum mechanics in which the probabilistic character of quantum predictions arises from ignorance of underlying, more fundamental ("hidden") variables that determine individual outcomes. Such theories were motivated by the hope of restoring determinism and realism to physics, most famously in the 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) argument that quantum mechanics is incomplete.

Types

Hidden-variable theories are classified as local or nonlocal, and as deterministic or stochastic, depending on whether the hidden variables fix outcomes uniquely and whether they respect relativistic locality. The best-known deterministic nonlocal example is de Broglie–Bohm theory (pilot-wave theory), in which particles have definite positions guided by the wavefunction. Stochastic and contextual models form another broad family.

Bell's theorem

In 1964 John Stewart Bell proved that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce all the statistical predictions of quantum mechanics: any such theory must satisfy inequalities (Bell inequalities) that quantum theory violates for entangled states. Experiments beginning with Alain Aspect's in the early 1980s, and loophole-free tests in 2015, have consistently confirmed the quantum predictions, ruling out local hidden variables (subject to assumptions such as measurement independence). Nonlocal hidden-variable theories such as Bohmian mechanics remain empirically viable.

Related results

The Kochen–Specker theorem constrains noncontextual hidden-variable models, and results such as the PBR theorem restrict epistemic interpretations of the quantum state. Debate continues over which assumptions of these theorems a hidden-variable theory might reasonably give up.
