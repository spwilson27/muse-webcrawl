Old Norse literature

Old Norse literature refers to the vernacular literature of Scandinavia written in the Old Norse language (more precisely in its western form, Old West Norse, close to Old Icelandic) up to about 1500. The bulk of it was produced in Iceland in the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, and includes some of the most celebrated vernacular writing of medieval Europe.

Sagas

The best-known genre is the saga: prose narratives, often mixed with verse, concerning heroes, families, kings and bishops. The Íslendingasögur (Sagas of Icelanders), such as Njáls saga, Egils saga and Laxdæla saga, deal with the Icelandic settlement age. Kings' sagas include Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla, a history of the Norwegian kings. Legendary sagas (fornaldarsögur) retell heroic tales of the distant past, such as Völsunga saga.

Poetry

Old Norse poetry comprises Eddic poetry — mythological and heroic lays preserved chiefly in the Codex Regius manuscript, known as the Poetic Edda — and skaldic poetry, the highly formal court verse of named poets (skalds), characterised by complex kennings and the dróttkvætt metre. Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda is a handbook of Norse mythology and poetic diction, indispensable for understanding both.

Other genres

Other works include laws, religious and learned treatises, translations of continental romances (riddarasögur), and historical writing such as Ari Þorgilsson's Íslendingabók and the settlement record Landnámabók.
