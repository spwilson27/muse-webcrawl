Isthmus of Panama

The Isthmus of Panama (Spanish: Istmo de Panamá) is the narrow strip of land that lies between the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean, linking North America and South America. It contains the country of Panama and the Panama Canal. Like many isthmuses, it is a location of great geopolitical and economic importance.

Formation and biogeography

The isthmus formed through the collision of tectonic plates and volcanic activity that gradually closed the Central American Seaway. Mainstream geological evidence dates the final closure to about 3 million years ago, in the late Pliocene, although land connections may have existed episodically earlier. The closure separated the Atlantic and Pacific oceans, altered global ocean circulation, and joined the previously isolated fauna and flora of the two continents, triggering the Great American Biotic Interchange.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited the isthmus for millennia. In 1513 the Spanish explorer Vasco Núñez de Balboa crossed the isthmus and became the first European to see the Pacific from the Americas. The Camino Real and later the Panama Railway (completed 1855) made it a key transit route. Work on a French canal under Ferdinand de Lesseps failed in the 1880s; the United States completed the Panama Canal, which opened in 1914, after Panama's independence from Colombia in 1903. Control of the canal passed to Panama at the end of 1999 under the Torrijos–Carter Treaties.
