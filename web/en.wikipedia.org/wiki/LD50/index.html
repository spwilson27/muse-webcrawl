Median lethal dose (LD50)

LD50
DefinitionDose lethal to 50% of a test population
Unitsmg per kg of body weight
FieldToxicology
IntroducedJ. W. Trevan, 1927

The median lethal dose, abbreviated LD50 (for "lethal dose, 50%"), is a standard measure of acute toxicity: the dose of a substance required to kill half the members of a tested population, usually laboratory animals, expressed in milligrams of substance per kilogram of body weight.

Use and interpretation

LD50 values underlie hazard classifications such as the GHS categories for acute toxicity and appear on safety data sheets. The lower the LD50, the more toxic the substance per unit mass. For example, botulinum toxin has an extraordinarily low LD50 (on the order of nanograms per kilogram), while common substances such as sucrose have values in the tens of grams per kilogram in rats. Related measures include LC50 (median lethal concentration, for gases/liquids) and TD50/ED50.

Criticism and alternatives

The classical LD50 test, introduced by J. W. Trevan in 1927, has been criticised on ethical and scientific grounds: results vary between species and laboratories, and extrapolation to humans is uncertain. Modern OECD guidelines favour methods using far fewer animals (fixed-dose, acute toxic class, and up-and-down procedures), and the original test guideline was deleted in 2001.
