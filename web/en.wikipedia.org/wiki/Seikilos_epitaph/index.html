Seikilos epitaph

The Seikilos epitaph is the oldest surviving complete musical composition, including musical notation, from anywhere in the world. It is a short song with lyrics and melody inscribed in ancient Greek musical notation on a marble tombstone (stele) from the Roman period, generally dated to the 1st or 2nd century CE.

The stele

The stele was found in 1883 near Aydın in modern Turkey, in the vicinity of ancient Tralles. During the upheavals following the Greco-Turkish War it was lost for a time, and when recovered its base had been sawn off, destroying part of the accompanying inscription. The stone is now kept in the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen. Above the song itself, an inscription reads, in translation: "I am a tombstone, an image. Seikilos placed me here as a long-lasting sign of deathless remembrance." A dedication at the bottom indicates the stele was erected by Seikilos, possibly for his wife Euterpe, though readings differ.

The song

The melody is written in the Phrygian mode using the alphabetic notation of ancient Greek music, with rhythmic marks above the letters. The lyrics, in transliteration, run:

Ὅσον ζῇς φαίνου
μηδὲν ὅλως σὺ λυποῦ
πρὸς ὀλίγον ἐστὶ τὸ ζῆν
τὸ τέλος ὁ χρόνος ἀπαιτεῖ.

Translated: "While you live, shine; do not grieve at all; life exists only for a short while, and time demands its toll." The sentiment is a gentle carpe diem addressed to the passer-by, typical of funerary epigrams.

Significance

Because the piece survives complete — words, melody, and rhythm — it can be transcribed and performed with reasonable confidence, unlike the fragmentary Delphic hymns or the excerpts preserved by Mesomedes. Recordings and performances of the Seikilos song have become a standard introduction to ancient Greek music, and its brief, poised melody is often heard as evidence of the sophistication of everyday musical culture in the Hellenistic and Roman world.
