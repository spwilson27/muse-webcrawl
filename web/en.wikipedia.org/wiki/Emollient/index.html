Emollient

Emollients are substances, often wax-like, lubricating agents used in cosmetics and medicine that soften and soothe the skin by forming an occlusive film that traps moisture. They are a common ingredient in moisturizers, lotions, creams, and ointments, and are a mainstay of the management of dry skin conditions such as eczema, psoriasis, and ichthyosis.

Mechanism

Emollients work by filling the gaps between desquamating corneocytes in the stratum corneum and by coating the skin surface, reducing transepidermal water loss and smoothing and softening the skin. Common emollient ingredients include petrolatum, mineral oil, lanolin, cetyl alcohol, silicones such as dimethicone, and plant-derived oils and butters. Petrolatum is often considered the most effective occlusive emollient.

Medical use

Regular application of emollients is a first-line therapy for atopic eczema and other forms of dermatitis, reducing dryness, itching, and the need for topical corticosteroids. Emollients are available as lotions, creams, gels, ointments, and bath and shower additives. Product choice depends on the dryness of the skin and patient preference; ointments are the most occlusive and are usually preferred for very dry, thickened skin.

Safety

Emollients are generally safe, but paraffin-based products are flammable and can soak into clothing and bedding, creating a fire risk near naked flames. Some ingredients, such as fragrances, lanolin, or the emulsifier sodium lauryl sulfate in aqueous cream, can irritate or sensitize susceptible individuals.
