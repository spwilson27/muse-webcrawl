The Swedish Empire (Swedish: Svenska stormaktstiden, "the Age of Great Power") was the period in Swedish history when Sweden emerged as a European great power and controlled most of the Baltic littoral. Conventionally dated from the accession of Gustavus Adolphus in 1611 to the Treaty of Nystad in 1721, the era saw a poor, thinly populated northern kingdom build an empire encompassing Finland, Estonia, Livonia, Ingria, parts of northern Germany and, briefly, Trøndelag and the Danish islands' approaches.

Rise

Victories over Denmark, Russia and Poland-Lithuania under Gustavus Adolphus and his chancellor Axel Oxenstierna, crowned by Swedish intervention in the Thirty Years' War, brought Sweden recognition at the Peace of Westphalia (1648), including Bremen-Verden and western Pomerania. Under Charles X Gustav, victories over Denmark in 1657–58 won Scania, Halland, Blekinge and Bohuslän — provinces that remain Swedish today — and marked the empire's greatest extent.

Administration and decline

The empire was sustained by an efficient fiscal-military state, copper and iron exports, and the reforms of Charles XI, whose "reduction" recovered crown lands and funded a standing allotment army. The system met its nemesis in the Great Northern War (1700–1721), when a coalition of Russia, Denmark-Norway and Saxony-Poland eventually broke Swedish power. Charles XII's early triumphs (Narva, 1700) gave way to catastrophe at Poltava in 1709; after his death at Fredriksten in 1718, Sweden ceded its Baltic provinces and German possessions in the treaties of 1719–21, and Russia replaced it as the dominant power of northern Europe.

Legacy

The empire left enduring marks: the modern Swedish county system, universities at Tartu and Greifswald, and a national mythology of military glory later romanticised in literature and song. Its collapse inaugurated Sweden's Age of Liberty and, ultimately, a lasting turn toward neutrality.
