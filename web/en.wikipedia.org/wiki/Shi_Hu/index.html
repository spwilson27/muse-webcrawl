Shi Hu (Chinese: 石虎; 295 – 26 May 349), courtesy name Jilong (季龍), formally Emperor Wu of (Later) Zhao, was an emperor of the Jie-led Chinese Later Zhao dynasty. He was the nephew of the dynasty's founder, Shi Le, and became one of its principal generals. After Shi Le's death in 333, Shi Hu seized power in a coup, deposing and killing Shi Le's heir, and took the throne in 334. He is remembered in traditional histories as one of the most brutal rulers in Chinese history, though also as a militarily powerful emperor who nearly unified northern China, and — paradoxically — as a great patron of Buddhism.

Shi Hu ruled Later Zhao from 334 to 349, at its height the dominant power in northern China.

Early career

Shi Hu, a Jie tribesman, grew up with his uncle Shi Le and was his most feared general, notorious even before his accession for cruelty — he is said to have executed soldiers and civilians capriciously and to have killed his own wives and concubines. Shi Le reportedly considered removing him but was dissuaded by Empress Dowager Cheng. After Shi Le's death, Shi Hu deposed the crown prince Shi Hong in 334.

Reign

Shi Hu moved the capital to Ye (Yecheng) and undertook massive construction projects, including palaces and walls, imposing heavy corvée burdens. He expanded Later Zhao's territory to cover most of northern China north of the Huai River, keeping the Eastern Jin dynasty at bay, and campaigned in Liaodong against the Murong Xianbei of Former Yan, though a great expedition in 346 was repelled. He patronised the Buddhist monk Fotudeng and, despite an initially suspicious edict, permitted widespread ordination, famously saying that as a foreigner he should worship the foreign Buddha.

Succession crisis and death

Shi Hu's choice of heirs was disastrous: his crown prince Shi Sui and then Shi Xuan were each executed with their families after plots, leaving the throne to the young Shi Shi in 349. Shi Hu died later that year, and his youngest son was promptly deposed; the succession struggle destroyed Later Zhao within two years, ending with the massacre of the Jie by Ran Min in 350.
