The Norway spruce (Picea abies) is a species of spruce native to northern, central and eastern Europe, from Norway and Poland east to the Urals and south to the mountains of the Balkans and the Alps. It is one of Europe's most important timber trees and the classic Christmas tree of northern Europe.

Description

A large, fast-growing evergreen conifer, it typically reaches 35–55 m (115–180 ft) tall with a trunk up to 1–1.5 m in diameter; exceptionally old trees are taller. The shoots are orange-brown and hairless; the needle-like leaves are 12–24 mm long, quadrangular in cross-section, dark green on all sides with inconspicuous stomatal lines. The cones, hanging downward, are 9–17 cm long — the longest of any spruce — green or reddish when young, ripening brown 5–7 months after pollination.

One Swedish Norway spruce, Old Tjikko on Fulufjället mountain, has a root system radiocarbon-dated to about 9,550 years old, making it one of the world's oldest known living clonal trees; the visible trunk is only a few centuries old.

Ecology and cultivation

Norway spruce dominates the boreal and montane forests of Scandinavia and Russia and is planted far beyond its native range, including in Britain and North America, where it sometimes naturalizes. It is the principal pulpwood and construction timber species of northern Europe ("deal" or whitewood), and its resonance wood is used for the soundboards of violins and guitars. Cultivars include weeping and dwarf forms for gardens.

Uses and culture

The species is the traditional Christmas tree of Norway, Sweden and much of Europe: each year Oslo donates a Norway spruce to London (for Trafalgar Square, in gratitude for British support in the Second World War), to New York and to Washington, D.C. Spruce beer was historically made from its shoots; the growing tips are used in syrups and seasoning in Nordic cuisine.

Threats

Plantations are vulnerable to the European spruce bark beetle (Ips typographus), whose outbreaks — worsening with drought and a warming climate — killed millions of trees in central Europe after 2018. Windthrow in shallow-rooted monocultures and acid rain damage (the "Waldsterben" of the 1980s) are further concerns.
