Christophe Plantin (c. 1520 – 1 July 1589) was a French-born printer and publisher who established in Antwerp one of the largest and most influential printing houses of the Renaissance. His firm, the Plantin Press (Officina Plantiniana), produced some 2,450 titles and was renowned for the accuracy and beauty of its scholarly editions, liturgical books, and illustrated works.

Life and career

Plantin was born near Tours, France, and trained as a bookbinder. After an injury ended his binding work, he turned to printing, settling in Antwerp around 1548–1549 and issuing his first book in 1555. His printer's mark — a hand emerging from a cloud holding a pair of compasses, with the motto Labore et Constantia ("By labour and constancy") — became famous throughout Europe.

The Polyglot Bible

Plantin's greatest undertaking was the Biblia Polyglotta (1568–1573), an eight-volume Bible printed in Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Aramaic, and Syriac, sponsored by Philip II of Spain and supervised by the scholar Benito Arias Montano. The project nearly bankrupted the firm but established Plantin's reputation as the foremost scholarly printer of his age. He became royal printer (prototypographus) to Philip II and secured a lucrative monopoly on liturgical books for Spain and its empire.

Legacy

Plantin printed works by leading scholars and scientists, including Ortelius's atlases, Dodoens's herbal, and the anatomical works associated with Vesalius's tradition, as well as music by composers of the Franco-Flemish school. After his death the business passed to his son-in-law Jan Moretus and remained in the Moretus family until 1867. The firm's premises survive as the Plantin-Moretus Museum in Antwerp, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, preserving two of the oldest printing presses in the world along with an immense archive of early modern printing.
