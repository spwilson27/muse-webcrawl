Out-group homogeneity

Out-group homogeneity
FieldSocial psychology
Also calledOutgroup homogeneity effect/bias
Key researchersQuattrone & Jones (1980); Linville; Park & Rothbart

The out-group homogeneity effect is the perception that members of an out-group are more similar to one another than members of one's own in-group: "they are alike; we are diverse." It is one of the most robust findings in the social psychology of stereotyping and intergroup relations, documented for groups defined by nationality, race, age, gender, university and even arbitrary experimental categories.

Research

George Quattrone and Edward Jones demonstrated the effect in a classic 1980 study in which Princeton and Rutgers students judged members of their own and the rival university to be more or less variable in their preferences; each group saw the out-group as more homogeneous. Later work by Patricia Linville and others measured perceived variability directly, while Bernadette Park and Myron Rothbart (1982) showed the effect in memory judgements of group traits.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. People typically have richer, more differentiated experience of in-group members, encountering them in varied roles and contexts, while out-group members are met in narrower settings. Information about the in-group is also encoded at a more individual, subordinate level, whereas out-group members are processed categorically. Motivational accounts add that perceiving the in-group as diverse protects a positive, complex group identity.

Consequences

Perceived homogeneity makes stereotypes easier to apply to whole groups and can contribute to phenomena such as the cross-race effect in face recognition—difficulty distinguishing faces of less familiar racial groups. Contact with varied out-group members and individuation training can reduce the effect.
