Order of Friars Minor Capuchin

The Order of Friars Minor Capuchin (Latin: Ordo Fratrum Minorum Capuccinorum; postnominal initials O.F.M. Cap.) is a religious order of Franciscan friars within the Catholic Church. Founded in the 1520s in Italy as a reform movement seeking a stricter observance of the Rule of St Francis, it became one of the three principal branches of the Franciscan first order.

History

The order began around 1525 when Matteo da Bascio, a Franciscan Observant friar in the Marche region of Italy, sought to return to the primitive Franciscan life of prayer, poverty and preaching. Together with Ludovico and Raffaele da Fossombrone he won papal approval; the bull Religionis zelus (1528) formally authorised the new reform. The friars' long pointed hood (cappuccio) earned them the nickname "Capuchins" — the same root later applied, by resemblance, to capuchin monkeys and to cappuccino coffee.

Life and mission

Capuchins emphasise contemplative prayer, austerity, closeness to the poor, and popular preaching. They grew rapidly in the Counter-Reformation, serving as missionaries across Europe, Africa, Asia and the Americas. Saints of the order include Padre Pio of Pietrelcina, the stigmatist whose shrine at San Giovanni Rotondo is a major pilgrimage site, and the first Native American Capuchin blessed, Solanus Casey.

Today

The order numbers roughly ten thousand friars worldwide, organised in provinces on every continent, and remains known for running soup kitchens, missions, parishes and the Sanctuary of Padre Pio.
