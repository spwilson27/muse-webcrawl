Borsippa (Sumerian: Ba-ad-DUR-si-a-AB-ba; Akkadian: Barsip) was an important ancient city of Sumer and Babylonia, located on both sides of a lake about 18 km southwest of Babylon, on the east bank of the Euphrates. Its site is the modern Birs Nimrud in Iraq's Babil Governorate. Borsippa was famous throughout antiquity as the cult centre of Nabu, the god of writing and wisdom, regarded as the son of Babylon's patron god Marduk.

History

Borsippa is mentioned in inscriptions from the reign of Ur-Nammu (third dynasty of Ur, late 3rd millennium BC) onwards and appears frequently in Babylonian texts of the 2nd and 1st millennia BC. Although politically dependent on Babylon, it retained its own religious identity; its New Year (Akitu) festival procession carried Nabu's statue to Babylon to visit his father Marduk. The city is mentioned in the Babylonian Talmud under the name Bursif, and remained inhabited into the Islamic period before being abandoned.

The Ezida and the ziggurat

The city's main temple was the Ezida, dedicated to Nabu. Beside it stood the great ziggurat E-ur-imin-an-ki ("House of the Seven Guiding [deities] of Heaven and Earth"), rebuilt by Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562 BC), who recorded restoring both Borsippa's ziggurat and Babylon's Etemenanki. The surviving baked-brick core of Borsippa's ziggurat, still some 47 m high, dominates the site and for centuries was mistakenly identified by travellers and early explorers as the Tower of Babel — hence the Arabic name Birs Nimrud, "Tower of Nimrod".

Excavations

The site was investigated in the 19th century by Henry Rawlinson and Hormuzd Rassam, who recovered stamped bricks and cylinders of Nebuchadnezzar II. Austrian excavations in the 1980s and early 2000s documented the Ezida temple area and produced cuneiform tablets, including astronomical texts consistent with Borsippa's reputation as a centre of scholarship and scribal learning.
