Hippocrates

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hippocrates (c. 460 – c. 370 BC) was a Greek physician of the Age of Pericles, traditionally regarded as the father of modern medicine. He is credited with separating medicine from religion and superstition, insisting that diseases have natural causes, and with founding systematic clinical observation and prognosis. The Hippocratic Oath, still echoed in modern medical ethics, is named after him, though like most of the Hippocratic Corpus it was probably written by others.

The Hippocratic Corpus

Some sixty medical texts survive under Hippocrates' name, written by multiple authors over centuries. They include case histories (the Epidemics), surgical and gynaecological treatises, and works on diet and environment such as Airs, Waters, Places. The corpus taught the theory of the four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile — whose balance supposedly governed health, an idea that dominated Western medicine for two thousand years.

Legacy

Little is known of Hippocrates the man; Plato and Aristotle mention him as a renowned physician from the island of Kos. His enduring legacy is methodological and ethical: careful bedside observation, honest record-keeping, confidentiality, and the principle of doing no harm.
