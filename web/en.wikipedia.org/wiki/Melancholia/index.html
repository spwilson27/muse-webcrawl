Melancholia

Melancholia (from Greek μελαγχολία, "black bile") is a concept with a long history in medicine, philosophy and art. In ancient and medieval humoral medicine it denoted a condition caused by an excess of black bile, one of the four humours, associated with a temperament marked by sadness, contemplation and despondency. Over time the term came to describe a severe form of depression characterised by profound sadness, loss of interest and psychomotor changes; modern psychiatry retains "melancholic features" as a specifier for major depressive disorder.

History

The concept was developed by Hippocratic writers and Galen, and elaborated in the famous treatise attributed to Aristotle, Problem XXX, which linked melancholy with exceptional achievement in philosophy, poetry and the arts — the origin of the association between melancholy and genius. In the Renaissance, Marsilio Ficino and Robert Burton, whose The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) remains the most famous English treatment, further developed the idea. Albrecht Dürer's engraving Melencolia I (1514) is its best-known visual representation.

Modern usage

In the DSM-5, melancholic features include anhedonia, lack of reactivity to pleasurable stimuli, early-morning awakening, diurnal variation of mood, significant weight loss and excessive guilt. In everyday language, melancholy survives as a word for a pensive, wistful sadness.
