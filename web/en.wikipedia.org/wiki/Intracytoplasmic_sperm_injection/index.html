Intracytoplasmic sperm injection

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI, pronounced "ik-see") is an in vitro fertilisation (IVF) procedure in which a single sperm cell is injected directly into the cytoplasm of an egg (oocyte). It differs from conventional IVF, in which the egg is incubated with many sperm and fertilisation occurs unaided in the laboratory dish.

History and indications

The technique was pioneered in Brussels by Gianpiero Palermo and colleagues at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel, with the first pregnancies reported in 1992. ICSI transformed the treatment of severe male-factor infertility: it can succeed with very low sperm counts, poorly motile sperm, or sperm surgically retrieved from the epididymis or testis in men with obstructive azoospermia. It is also used after previous failed fertilisation with conventional IVF, with frozen eggs, and in conjunction with preimplantation genetic testing.

Procedure

After ovarian stimulation and egg retrieval, a mature oocyte is held with a suction pipette while a single immobilised sperm is drawn into a fine glass needle and injected through the egg's zona pellucida into the cytoplasm. Fertilisation is checked the following day, and resulting embryos are cultured and transferred or frozen as in standard IVF.

Outcomes and debate

Per-cycle success rates are broadly comparable to conventional IVF. Because ICSI bypasses natural sperm selection, there has been long-running debate about slightly increased rates of certain congenital and imprinting disorders, and about its widespread use in cases without male-factor infertility, where it has not been shown to improve live-birth rates.
