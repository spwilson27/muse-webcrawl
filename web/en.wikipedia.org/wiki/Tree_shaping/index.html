Tree shaping

Tree shaping (also known by other names, including arborsculpture) uses living trees and other woody plants as the medium to create structures and art. Practitioners guide growing trees along predetermined designs using techniques such as pruning, bending, grafting and pleaching. The resulting works range from shaped trees and living fences to chairs, tables and even whole buildings grown from trees.

Techniques

The shaper typically plants young trees or saplings and progressively bends and secures their growth around frames or along desired paths. Branches that meet may be grafted together (inosculation), so that over years the pieces fuse into a single continuous structure. Common related practices include pleaching (interweaving branches of hedges), espalier (training trees against a wall), topiary, and the living root bridges of Meghalaya in India, formed over generations from the aerial roots of rubber fig trees.

History

Deliberately shaped trees appear in many cultures. Modern tree shaping is associated with figures such as Axel Erlandson, an American farmer whose "Tree Circus" in California (opened 1947) featured trees grafted into baskets, ladders and other fantastical forms — now preserved at Gilroy Gardens. In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, Australian practitioners Peter Cook and Becky Northey developed the term "pooktre" and produced grown chairs and figures, while designers have proposed grown-tree houses and furniture as sustainable alternatives to milled timber products.

Considerations

Tree shaping is a slow art: a piece of furniture may take several years to a decade to grow, and the finished structure continues to grow and change. The work requires horticultural knowledge of species' growth habits, disease management and grafting compatibility.
