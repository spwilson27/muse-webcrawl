Ten Pound Poms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ten Pound Poms (or £10 Poms) is a colloquial term used in Australia and New Zealand to describe British citizens who migrated to those countries after the Second World War under assisted passage schemes established by the Australian and New Zealand governments. The name derives from the cost of the scheme: adult migrants were charged only £10 in processing fees for their journey, while children travelled free, and the balance of the fare was paid by the two governments.

History

The Australian scheme, formally the Assisted Passage Migration Scheme, was created in 1945 by the Chifley government and its first Minister for Immigration, Arthur Calwell, as part of the "Populate or Perish" policy. Australia sought to increase its population substantially after the war, both for economic growth and for defence, fearing it could not repel a future invasion with its small population. The British government, meanwhile, faced housing shortages and unemployment pressures, and supported emigration.

The first assisted migrants arrived in 1945, and the scheme continued, with interruptions, until 1982. A similar, smaller programme operated in New Zealand from 1947, under which British migrants also paid £10.

The scheme

Applicants were required to be of British stock, in good health, and under 45 years of age. In return for the subsidised fare, migrants were expected to remain in Australia for at least two years, or repay the cost of their passage. Many travelled on former troopships and converted liners, including the Ormonde, Orontes, Oronsay, Otranto and later purpose-used vessels such as the Fairsea and Fairstar.

On arrival, many families were housed in migrant hostels — basic accommodation in converted military camps — while they found work and permanent housing. Some returned to Britain, disillusioned; these returnees were sometimes nicknamed "boomerang Poms". Those who came back to Australia again after returning to Britain were known as "yo-yo Poms".

Numbers and impact

More than one million Britons migrated to Australia under the scheme, making the British the largest single national group among post-war migrants. Assisted British migrants formed a substantial part of the wave that transformed Australia's population and culture in the decades after the war. Peak years saw arrivals of tens of thousands annually; the 1969 intake was among the largest.

Notable Ten Pound Poms

Famous migrants under the scheme include former prime minister Julia Gillard, who arrived with her Welsh parents in 1966; the parents of Tony Abbott; members of the Bee Gees (the Gibb family) and AC/DC (the Young family, from Scotland); and actor Leo McKern.
