Liège Revolution

The Liège Revolution, sometimes known as the Happy Revolution (French: Heureuse Révolution; Walloon: Binamêye revolucion), against the prince-bishopric of Liège began on 18 August 1789 and lasted until the declaration of the Republic of Liège on 18 August 1789's aftermath — effectively until the restoration of the prince-bishop by Austrian forces in January 1791. It coincided with the outbreak of the French Revolution and the Brabant Revolution in the Austrian Netherlands to the north.

Background

The Prince-Bishopric of Liège was an ecclesiastical state of the Holy Roman Empire, ruled by a prince-bishop elected by the cathedral chapter. In 1784 César-Constantin-François de Hoensbroeck became prince-bishop and pursued a programme of reactionary reform, reversing many of the enlightened policies of his predecessor and provoking widespread discontent among the bourgeoisie and the Third Estate.

Course of the revolution

Inspired by events in Paris, a crowd stormed the city hall of Liège on 18 August 1789 and forced the magistrates to accept popular leaders, notably Jean-Nicolas Bassenge. The prince-bishop fled to Trier. The revolutionaries proclaimed the rights of man and citizen, drawing on the French Declaration, and abolished the privileges of the clergy and nobility. The short-lived Republic of Liège was declared and sought union with the other Belgian states fighting Austria.

Restoration

After the defeat of the Brabant revolutionaries, Austrian troops occupied Liège in January 1791, the prince-bishop was restored, and many revolutionaries fled to France. Liège was annexed by France in 1795, bringing the prince-bishopric to a permanent end.
