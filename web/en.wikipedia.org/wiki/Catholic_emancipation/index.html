Catholic emancipation

Catholic emancipation or Catholic relief was the process in Great Britain and Ireland in the late 18th and early 19th centuries that removed most of the restrictions on Roman Catholics imposed by the penal and test laws since the Reformation. It culminated in the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, which allowed Catholics to sit in Parliament and hold most public offices.

Background

From the reign of Elizabeth I, and especially after the Revolution of 1688, Catholics in Britain and Ireland were excluded from voting, from Parliament, from the universities, the professions, and military commissions, and in Ireland from owning land on equal terms. A series of relief acts from 1778 onwards gradually dismantled parts of this code — restoring property rights and freedom of worship — but the sacramental test still barred Catholics from Westminster, an anomaly that became acute after the Act of Union 1801 brought a largely Catholic Ireland into the United Kingdom.

The campaign and the 1829 Act

In Ireland, Daniel O'Connell founded the Catholic Association (1823), financed by a mass "Catholic rent", and built a nationwide campaign for full emancipation. His election as MP for County Clare in 1828 — a seat he could not legally take — created a crisis that convinced the government of the Duke of Wellington and Robert Peel that refusal risked civil war. The Roman Catholic Relief Act of April 1829 admitted Catholics to both Houses of Parliament (on a new oath) and to nearly all offices under the Crown, though at the same time the Irish county franchise was sharply raised, disenfranchising the poorest voters who had powered O'Connell's campaign.

Consequences

A handful of disabilities survived — the monarch must still be Protestant, and a few offices remained closed — but 1829 was a constitutional landmark: it broke the exclusively Protestant constitution of the British state, split the Tory party, and demonstrated the political power of organised mass mobilisation, preparing the way for the era of reform that followed.
