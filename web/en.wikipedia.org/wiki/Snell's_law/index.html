Snell's law

Snell's law (also known as the Snell–Descartes law, the ibn-Sahl law, and the law of refraction) is a formula used to describe the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction, when referring to light or other waves passing through a boundary between two different isotropic media, such as water, glass, or air. The law states that, for a given pair of media, the ratio of the sines of angle of incidence θ1 and angle of refraction θ2 is equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in the two media, or equivalently to the inverse ratio of the refractive indices:

sin θ1 / sin θ2 = v1 / v2 = n2 / n1

with angles measured from the normal to the surface. The law follows from Fermat's principle of least time, and underlies the operation of lenses, prisms and fibre optics.

History

The law was first accurately described by the Persian scientist Ibn Sahl in Baghdad in 984 in his manuscript On Burning Mirrors and Lenses. The Dutch astronomer Willebrord Snellius re-derived it in 1621, but his work was unpublished; René Descartes gave the first published statement (with an incorrect proof from a mechanical analogy) in Dioptrique in 1637. Pierre de Fermat's least-time derivation (1662) put the law on its modern footing; Christiaan Huygens later derived it from his wave theory.

Explanation and consequences

When light passes from a medium of lower refractive index to a higher one, it bends toward the normal; in the reverse direction it bends away. Beyond the critical angle — reached when the refracted ray would lie along the surface — the light undergoes total internal reflection, the principle exploited in optical fibres and reflecting prisms. In vector form, Snell's law determines the refracted ray direction from the incident ray and the surface normal. Because refractive index depends on wavelength, Snell's law also explains chromatic dispersion in prisms.

The law generalises beyond optics: the same relation governs refraction of sound and seismic waves at interfaces, and in metamaterials with negative refractive index the refracted ray falls on the same side of the normal.
