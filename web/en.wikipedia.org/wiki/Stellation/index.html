Stellation

In geometry, stellation is the process of extending a polygon in two dimensions or a polyhedron in three dimensions to form a new figure. Starting with an original figure, the process extends specific elements — the sides of a polygon or the face planes of a polyhedron — symmetrically until they meet again to form the boundary of a new figure. The term derives from the Latin stellatus, "starred", and was coined by Johannes Kepler, by analogy with the stellated (star-shaped) figures he described in 1619.

Two dimensions

Extending the sides of a regular pentagon produces the pentagram {5/2}; extending those of a heptagon yields two distinct heptagrams. Non-regular examples include the Star of David (a stellation of the hexagon) and the stars of Lakshmi in Hindu tradition.

Three dimensions

Stellating the dodecahedron gives the three stellated forms among the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra; the icosahedron has 58 stellations, catalogued by Coxeter and co-authors in The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra (1938) under rules proposed by J. C. P. Miller. The stellated octahedron (stella octangula) is the only stellation of the octahedron, consisting of two interpenetrating tetrahedra. The cube and tetrahedron have no stellations, since their face planes do not meet again outside the original solid. Magnus Wenninger published influential books of stellation models and diagrams in the 1970s.
