Bacterial cell wall

The bacterial cell wall is a rigid structure surrounding the cell membrane of most bacteria. Its principal component is peptidoglycan (also called murein), a polymer of sugars and amino acids that forms a mesh-like layer giving the cell its shape and protecting it against osmotic pressure. The cell wall is a major target of antibiotics such as penicillin, which inhibit its synthesis.

Composition

Peptidoglycan consists of alternating residues of N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) linked by β-(1,4) glycosidic bonds. Attached to each NAM residue is a short peptide chain, typically containing L-alanine, D-glutamine, L-lysine or meso-diaminopimelic acid, and D-alanine. Cross-linking between these peptide chains, catalyzed by transpeptidase enzymes (penicillin-binding proteins), gives the wall its strength.

Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria

Bacteria are traditionally divided by the Gram stain into two major groups. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick peptidoglycan layer (roughly 20–80 nm) interwoven with teichoic acids, and they retain the crystal violet stain, appearing purple. Gram-negative bacteria have a much thinner peptidoglycan layer (only a few nanometres) sandwiched between an inner cytoplasmic membrane and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS); they do not retain the stain and appear pink or red after counterstaining.

Functions

The cell wall maintains cell shape, anchors surface structures such as flagella and pili, and protects the cell from mechanical damage and osmotic lysis. Surface molecules of the wall, including teichoic acids and LPS, interact with the host immune system during infection and are important determinants of virulence and antigenicity.

Exceptions

A few bacterial groups lack a conventional cell wall. The mycoplasmas have no peptidoglycan at all and survive only in osmotically stable environments such as host tissues. Archaea have walls built from pseudopeptidoglycan or proteinaceous S-layers rather than true peptidoglycan. Some bacteria, such as the planctomycetes, have protein-based walls.

Antibiotic targets

Because peptidoglycan is unique to bacteria, its synthesis pathway is a prime target for antibacterial drugs. Beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins) inhibit the transpeptidases that cross-link the wall, while glycopeptides such as vancomycin bind the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of the peptide precursor. The enzyme lysozyme, present in tears and saliva, attacks the glycosidic bonds of peptidoglycan.
