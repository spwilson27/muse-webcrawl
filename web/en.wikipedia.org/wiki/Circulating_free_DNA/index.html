Circulating free DNA

Circulating free DNA (cfDNA) consists of small fragments of DNA found circulating in the blood plasma and other body fluids, rather than inside cells. Most cfDNA is released from cells undergoing apoptosis or necrosis, and typically consists of fragments around 160–170 base pairs long, reflecting cleavage around nucleosomes.

Sources and biology

In healthy individuals, cfDNA is present at low concentrations and derives mainly from blood cells. Concentrations rise in many conditions, including cancer, trauma, stroke, myocardial infarction, sepsis and autoimmune disease. During pregnancy, DNA from the placenta circulates in maternal blood, making up the basis of non-invasive prenatal testing.

Clinical applications

Analysis of cfDNA underpins "liquid biopsy" tests: tumour-derived circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) can reveal cancer mutations, guide targeted therapy and monitor recurrence from a blood sample. cfDNA screening is also used in prenatal testing for fetal aneuploidies such as Down syndrome, in transplant monitoring through donor-derived cfDNA, and in detecting infections. Because cfDNA is cleared rapidly from the circulation, its levels can reflect changes in disease state in near real time.
