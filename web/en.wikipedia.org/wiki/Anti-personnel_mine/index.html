Anti-personnel mine

An anti-personnel mine is a form of land mine designed to injure or kill people, in contrast to anti-tank mines, which target vehicles. They are typically victim-activated — detonating when stepped on, disturbed, or approached — and are intended to disable enemy soldiers, deny terrain, and protect anti-tank minefields from clearance.

Types

Blast mines, such as the U.S. M14 or the Soviet PMN series, bury a small explosive charge that detonates underfoot, typically maiming the leg. Fragmentation mines propel shrapnel over a wider radius; bounding types such as the German S-mine ("Bouncing Betty") spring into the air before exploding, while directional mines such as the Claymore project fragments in a fixed arc.

Controversy and the Ottawa Treaty

Because mines remain lethal long after conflicts end, anti-personnel mines have caused extensive civilian casualties and rendered land unusable. The 1997 Ottawa Treaty (Mine Ban Treaty) prohibits the use, production, stockpiling, and transfer of anti-personnel mines; it has been joined by more than 160 states, though several major military powers, including the United States, Russia, and China, are not parties. Clearance by humanitarian demining organizations continues in dozens of affected countries.
