Great Sejm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Sejm, also known as the Four-Year Sejm (Polish: Sejm Wielki or Sejm Czteroletni), was the parliament of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth that sat in Warsaw from 1788 to 1792. Convened under King Stanisław August Poniatowski, it enacted a sweeping programme of reform intended to rescue the Commonwealth from internal paralysis and foreign domination, culminating in the Constitution of 3 May 1791 — the first codified written constitution in Europe and the second in the world after that of the United States.

Background

The Commonwealth had been weakened by the liberum veto, which allowed any deputy to dissolve the Sejm, by magnate confederations sponsored from abroad, and by the First Partition of 1772, in which Russia, Prussia, and Austria annexed about a third of its territory. The new Sejm was organised as a confederated sejm, deciding by majority vote and immune to the veto, and doubled its membership mid-session in 1790, hence its four-year duration.

Reforms

The Sejm voted to expand the army toward 100,000 men, reformed taxation by imposing income taxes on the nobility and clergy, abolished the Russian-dominated Permanent Council, and passed the Free Royal Cities Act of April 1791, granting burghers personal security, land rights, and representation. On 3 May 1791 it adopted the Government Act: a constitutional monarchy with separation of powers, majority voting, ministerial responsibility, abolition of the liberum veto and of elective monarchy, and the elevation of townspeople and (in prospect) peasants within the legal order.

Overthrow

Conservative magnates, with Russian support, formed the Targowica Confederation in 1792 and invited Catherine II to intervene. The ensuing Polish–Russian war ended in defeat; the Grodno Sejm of 1793, coerced by Russian troops, annulled the constitution, and the Second and Third Partitions (1793, 1795) erased the Commonwealth from the map. The Great Sejm's reforms and constitution nevertheless became touchstones of Polish national memory and democratic tradition.
