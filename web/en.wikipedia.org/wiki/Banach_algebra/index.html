Banach algebra

FieldFunctional analysis
Named afterStefan Banach
Key conceptsNorm, completeness, spectrum, Gelfand transform

In mathematics, especially functional analysis, a Banach algebra, named after Stefan Banach, is an associative algebra A over the real or complex numbers that also functions as a Banach space; that is, it is complete in the metric induced by its norm, and the norm satisfies ‖xy‖ ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖ for all x, y in A. This ensures that multiplication is continuous.

Examples

The canonical example is the space L1(R) of absolutely integrable functions with convolution as multiplication. The space C(X) of continuous complex-valued functions on a compact Hausdorff space X, with pointwise multiplication and the supremum norm, is a commutative Banach algebra. The algebra of bounded linear operators on a Banach space is a noncommutative example. The group algebra and C*-algebras are important classes; every C*-algebra is a Banach algebra.

Spectral theory

Because norms control invertibility, elements of a Banach algebra have a well-defined spectrum: the set of scalars λ such that x − λe is not invertible (in the unital case). The spectral radius formula relates the spectrum to the growth of ‖xn‖. The Gelfand–Mazur theorem states that a complex Banach algebra that is a division algebra must be isomorphic to the complex numbers.

Gelfand theory

For commutative Banach algebras, the Gelfand transform represents elements as continuous functions vanishing at infinity on the space of multiplicative linear functionals (characters). This underlies the commutative Gelfand–Naimark theorem, which identifies commutative C*-algebras with spaces C0(X).
