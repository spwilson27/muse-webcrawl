Eastern Shoshone

The Eastern Shoshone are a Native American people, one of the major divisions of the Shoshone, who historically ranged over what is now Wyoming, northern Utah, southern Montana and eastern Idaho. They speak the Eastern dialect of the Shoshoni language, a Central Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family. Today most Eastern Shoshone are enrolled in the federally recognised Eastern Shoshone Tribe and live on the Wind River Indian Reservation in Wyoming, which they share with the Northern Arapaho.

History

The Eastern Shoshone were equestrian buffalo hunters of the northwestern Plains and the Rocky Mountains. After acquiring horses in the early 18th century they became formidable traders and warriors, dealing with the mountain men of the fur trade era. Under Chief Washakie, the Eastern Shoshone generally pursued alliance with the United States. The Fort Bridger Treaties of 1863 and 1868 established the Wind River Reservation; subsequent cessions, including the 1896 Hot Springs cession and land sales in 1904, greatly reduced its size.

Culture

Traditional Eastern Shoshone society was organised in fluid bands led by chiefs chosen for ability. The Sun Dance, introduced from the Plains, became an important ceremony. Sacagawea, the Shoshone woman who guided the Lewis and Clark Expedition, was born among the Lemhi Shoshone, a related group, and was reunited with her people during the expedition; Eastern Shoshone tradition associates her later life with Wind River.
