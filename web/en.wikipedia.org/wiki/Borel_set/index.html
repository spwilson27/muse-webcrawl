Borel set

In mathematics, a Borel set is any set in a topological space that can be formed from open sets (or, equivalently, from closed sets) through the operations of countable union, countable intersection and relative complement. The collection of all Borel sets of a space forms its Borel σ-algebra, named after the French mathematician Émile Borel, who introduced the concept in 1898 in his work on measure theory.

Construction

Starting from the open sets, one builds the Borel hierarchy by iterating the operations transfinitely: open sets are level Σ01, their complements (closed sets) are Π01, countable unions of closed sets (the Fσ sets) form Σ02, and so on through all countable ordinals. In a metric space the hierarchy does not collapse: for every countable ordinal there are Borel sets of exactly that complexity.

Examples

Every open, closed, and countable set is Borel. Intervals of real numbers, whether open, closed or half-open, are Borel, as are most sets encountered in ordinary analysis. Non-Borel sets exist — for instance, there are Lebesgue measurable sets of the real line that are not Borel — but exhibiting one explicitly requires the axiom of choice; no concrete example can be written down.

Importance

Borel sets are the natural domain of measure theory: every Borel measure, including Lebesgue measure restricted to the Borel σ-algebra, is defined on them. In probability theory, random variables are defined as Borel-measurable functions, and in descriptive set theory the Borel sets of Polish spaces form the first level above the analytic sets. A function whose preimage of every open set is Borel is called a Borel function.
