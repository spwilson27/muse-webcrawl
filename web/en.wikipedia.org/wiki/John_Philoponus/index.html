John Philoponus (c. 490 – c. 570), also known as John the Grammarian or John of Alexandria, was a Byzantine Greek philosopher, philologist and Christian theologian in Alexandria. A student of the Neoplatonist Ammonius Hermiae, he wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle and mounted a celebrated critique of Aristotelian physics and cosmology.

Philosophy and science

In works such as Against Proclus on the Eternity of the World and his commentary on Aristotle's Physics, Philoponus argued that the world was created, rejected Aristotle's theory of projectile motion — proposing instead that a mover imparts an incorporeal kinetic power, an early form of impetus theory — and noted that objects of different weights fall at nearly the same speed. His ideas, preserved also in Arabic translation, influenced medieval Islamic and Latin thought and anticipated aspects of Galileo's mechanics.

Theology

A Miaphysite Christian, Philoponus wrote theological works defending his Christological positions; his tritheistic interpretation of the Trinity was condemned after his death. His name "Philoponus" means "lover of work", reflecting his prodigious output.
