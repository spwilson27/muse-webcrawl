The Great Sanhedrin, the supreme Jewish court and council of the Second Temple period.

The Sanhedrin (Hebrew: סַנְהֶדְרִין sanhedrīn; Greek: Συνέδριον, synedrion, "sitting together") was an assembly of judges appointed in every city in the Land of Israel in the late Second Temple period and afterwards. The Great Sanhedrin of 71 members, presided over by the nasi (president), sat in Jerusalem and functioned as the supreme court, legislature and religious authority of the Jewish people. Lesser sanhedrins of 23 members judged serious local cases.

History

According to rabbinic tradition, the institution derived ultimately from the seventy elders appointed to assist Moses. The Sanhedrin became prominent in the Hasmonean and Herodian periods and under Roman rule, when it retained jurisdiction over internal religious matters, though capital punishment required — by the time of the Roman prefects — at least Roman toleration. In the New Testament the Sanhedrin appears as the body before which Jesus was tried, and in the Acts of the Apostles it also tries Peter, John and Paul.

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the Sanhedrin was reconstituted as an academy and court centred first at Yavneh (Jamnia), under Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai and then Rabban Gamaliel II, and later moved through Galilean towns — Usha, Shefaram, Beit She'arim, Sepphoris and finally Tiberias. Under leaders such as Judah ha-Nasi (c. 200 CE), it oversaw the compilation of the Mishnah. The patriarchate and the Sanhedrin were effectively abolished by the Roman emperor Theodosius II around 425 CE.

Functions and procedure

The Sanhedrin ruled on questions of religious law (halakha), supervised the calendar and the declaration of new months, tried cases involving tribes, false prophets and the high priest, and authorised optional wars. Rabbinic sources describe elaborate procedures for capital cases: the court sat in a semicircle in the Chamber of Hewn Stone in the Temple, cases were argued with arguments for acquittal heard first, and conviction in capital cases required a majority of two while acquittal required a majority of one.

The Talmudic tractate Sanhedrin, in the order Nezikin of the Mishnah, is devoted to courts, judicial procedure and criminal law.

Modern revivals

Napoleon convened a "Grand Sanhedrin" of Jewish notables in Paris in 1807 to answer questions about Jewish loyalty to the French state — a consultative assembly rather than a continuation of the ancient court. In 2004 a group of Israeli rabbis announced the re-establishment of a Sanhedrin in Tiberias; the initiative gained limited recognition and remains controversial in the wider Orthodox world.
