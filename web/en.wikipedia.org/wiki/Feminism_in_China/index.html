Feminism in China

Feminism in China refers to the movements and ideas advocating women's emancipation in China from the late Qing dynasty to the present. Chinese feminism developed in close connection with national reform and revolution: the subjugation of women — symbolised by foot binding and exclusion from education — was widely treated by reformers as both a cause and a sign of China's weakness.

Origins

Reformers such as Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao campaigned against foot binding in the 1890s, and women activists soon spoke for themselves: the anarchist-feminist He Zhen published radical critiques of patriarchy in 1907, while Qiu Jin, executed after a failed uprising the same year, became a martyr of both feminism and the anti-Qing cause. The May Fourth era (1919 onwards) popularised Ibsen's Nora as an emblem of the liberated "new woman", and women's suffrage and education were taken up by both Nationalists and Communists.

The People's Republic

The 1950 Marriage Law of the new People's Republic banned arranged marriage, concubinage and child betrothal, and the state promoted women's labour-force participation under the slogan that "women hold up half the sky". The All-China Women's Federation channelled official women's policy. Market reforms after 1978 brought new inequalities alongside new freedoms, and the 1995 UN World Conference on Women in Beijing spurred the growth of independent NGOs.

Contemporary feminism

Since the 2010s, a younger, internet-based feminism has campaigned against sexual harassment, domestic violence and workplace discrimination, meeting periodic official repression — notably the 2015 detention of the "Feminist Five" — while #MeToo cases in China from 2018 brought the movement wider visibility. Debates continue over gender quotas, declining marriage and birth rates, and the relationship between feminism and the state.
