Dish antenna

A dish antenna, or parabolic antenna, is an antenna that uses a parabolic reflector, a curved surface with the cross-sectional shape of a parabola, to direct radio waves. The most common form is shaped like a shallow dish and is widely used for satellite television reception, radio astronomy, radar, and point-to-point microwave links.

Principle of operation

The reflector's shape causes plane waves arriving parallel to its axis to be reflected to a single focal point in front of the dish, where a feed antenna is placed; conversely, signals radiated from the focus are reflected into a narrow collimated beam. Directivity (gain) increases with the ratio of dish diameter to wavelength, so large dishes or high frequencies produce narrow beams and high gain.

Types and construction

Common variants include prime-focus dishes, in which the feed is supported in front of the dish; offset dishes, standard for consumer satellite television, in which the feed is out of the path of the incoming signal; and Cassegrain designs, which use a secondary sub-reflector and are typical in large earth stations and radio telescopes such as those of deep-space communication networks. Reflectors are made from solid sheet metal, perforated metal, or wire mesh when surface accuracy requirements permit.

Applications

The largest dish antennas are radio telescopes, such as the 500-metre FAST telescope in China and the 305-metre Arecibo telescope (1963–2020). At the other extreme, millions of households use dishes under a metre across for direct-broadcast satellite services. Dishes are also used in satellite ground stations, military and weather radar, and amateur radio moonbounce communication.
