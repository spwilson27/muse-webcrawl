Contredanse
Origin17th–18th c. France, from English country dance
FormationCouples in lines or squares
DescendantsQuadrille, square dance, contradanza

The contredanse is a French form of set dance for couples in lines or squares, fashionable in the 18th century. The name derives from the English "country dance," which French dancing masters adopted and elaborated.

History

English country dances were published by John Playford in The English Dancing Master (1651). French dancing masters — notably Raoul-Auger Feuillet — systematised the figures into the contredanse française, which spread through European courts.

Form

Dancers face each other in longways sets or in squares of four couples, exchanging places through figures directed by a caller or memorised sequences. The cotillion and later the quadrille grew directly from this tradition.

Influence

Exported to the Caribbean, the contredanse became the Cuban contradanza; in North America it survives as contra dance and square dance. Beethoven and Mozart composed sets of contredanses for court balls.
