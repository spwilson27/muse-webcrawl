Continental rise

SettingBase of the continental slope, passive margins
DepthRoughly 3,000–5,000 m
GradientGentle, typically 1:50 to 1:500
CompositionAccumulated sediment, several km thick

The continental rise is a low-relief zone of accumulated sediments that lies between the continental slope and the abyssal plain on a passive continental margin. It is formed by the settling and spreading of sediment carried down the slope by turbidity currents, debris flows, and contour currents, building a broad, gently sloping apron at the foot of the margin.

[Image: continental margin profile]

Formation

Sediment eroded from the continent crosses the shelf and is funnelled through submarine canyons in the slope by turbidity currents. Where the slope meets the deep ocean floor, the currents slow and spread out, depositing submarine fans of sand and mud. Over millions of years, overlapping fans coalesce into the continental rise, whose sediment pile may exceed several kilometres in thickness. Some of the world's greatest deep-sea fans — such as the Bengal Fan in the Indian Ocean and the Amazon Fan in the Atlantic — are integral parts of continental rises.

Characteristics

The rise typically descends from about 3,000 m to 5,000 m depth with gradients far gentler than the slope above it. It is best developed on passive (Atlantic-type) margins; on active margins such as much of the Pacific rim, an oceanic trench intercepts the sediment and a rise is absent or poorly developed. Buried beneath the rise, the boundary between continental and oceanic crust marks the true geological edge of the continent.
