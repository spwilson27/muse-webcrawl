Piano concerto

A piano concerto is a type of concerto, a solo composition in the classical music genre which is composed for piano accompanied by an orchestra or other large ensemble. Piano concertos are typically virtuoso showpieces requiring great skill, and often contrast the soloist against the orchestra in dialogue, rivalry, and cooperation. They are among the most popular works in the concert repertoire.


A solo pianist with orchestra: the piano concerto has been a central concert genre since the Classical era.

Form

The standard piano concerto has three movements: a fast opening movement, usually in sonata form with a double exposition (orchestral, then solo) and a cadenza near the end; a slow, lyrical middle movement; and a fast finale, often a rondo. Some concertos differ — for example, Liszt's concertos use continuous single spans, Brahms's Second adds a fourth movement, and Busoni's includes a male chorus in the finale.

History

Baroque and Classical

The genre descends from Baroque keyboard concertos, notably J. S. Bach's harpsichord concertos. Mozart composed 27 piano concertos, which established the form and remain core repertoire; his concertos of the 1780s in Vienna combined brilliance, operatic dialogue and depth. Beethoven's five concertos, especially the "Emperor" (No. 5), expanded the scale and drama, opening the soloist's role with the orchestra from the first bars.

Romantic era

In the 19th century the piano concerto became a vehicle for touring virtuosos. Major examples include Chopin's two concertos, Schumann's in A minor, Liszt's two concertos and Totentanz, Brahms's two concertos, Grieg's in A minor, Tchaikovsky's First, and Rachmaninoff's Second and Third. Saint-Saëns wrote five; Mendelssohn's two concertos bridged Classical and Romantic manners.

20th century and beyond

Prokofiev (five concertos), Bartók (three), Ravel (two: in G major and for the left hand), Gershwin (Rhapsody in Blue and the Concerto in F), Shostakovich (two), and Britten enriched the genre; concertos written for the left hand alone were commissioned by Paul Wittgenstein from Ravel, Prokofiev, Britten and others. Contemporary composers continue to write piano concertos as showpieces for leading soloists.
