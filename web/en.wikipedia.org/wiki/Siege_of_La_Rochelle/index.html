The siege of La Rochelle (1627–1628) was a major engagement of the Huguenot rebellions, in which the royal forces of Louis XIII, directed by Cardinal Richelieu, besieged and captured La Rochelle, the principal stronghold of French Protestantism. The city, starved into submission after fourteen months, lost the great majority of its population, and its fall effectively ended the Huguenots as an independent political and military power within France.

Richelieu's great dike closed the harbour of La Rochelle against English relief fleets.

The siege

War began in 1627 when an English expedition under the Duke of Buckingham seized the Île de Ré to support the Rochelais. Richelieu responded by encircling La Rochelle with trenches and forts, and — in a celebrated feat of engineering — built a massive sea wall (digue) across the harbour entrance, studded with artillery, to seal the city off from relief by sea. Three English attempts to relieve the city failed, the last in October 1628.

Inside the walls, the population, led heroically by Mayor Jean Guiton, endured famine that contemporary estimates say reduced some 27,000 inhabitants to about 5,000 survivors. La Rochelle capitulated on 28 October 1628. Its fortifications were demolished, its municipal privileges abolished, and Catholic worship restored, though the Peace of Alès (1629) that ended the rebellion preserved the Huguenots' religious rights while stripping their political and military ones. The siege was famously fictionalised in Alexandre Dumas's novel The Three Musketeers.
