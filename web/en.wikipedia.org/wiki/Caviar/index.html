Caviar

Caviar is a food consisting of salt-cured roe of the family Acipenseridae. Caviar is considered a delicacy and is eaten as a garnish or spread. Traditionally, the term caviar refers only to roe from wild sturgeon in the Caspian Sea and Black Sea; the word is now also used for the roe of other species, though these are often qualified as "caviar substitutes".

Varieties

The most prized traditional caviars come from three Caspian sturgeon species. Beluga, from the beluga sturgeon (Huso huso), has the largest, softest eggs and commands the highest prices. Osetra, from the Russian sturgeon, is medium-sized with a nutty flavour and golden-brown to dark colouring. Sevruga, from the stellate sturgeon, has smaller grey eggs and a more intense taste. Roe is graded by size, colour, texture and aroma, lightly salted ("malossol", Russian for "little salt"), and must be kept refrigerated.

History and production

Persians are credited with first preparing sturgeon roe from the Caspian, and caviar became a luxury of the Russian imperial court before spreading to Western Europe. Overfishing and poaching after the collapse of the Soviet Union devastated wild Caspian sturgeon stocks; CITES listed all sturgeon species, international trade in wild caviar was heavily restricted, and farmed sturgeon — now centred in countries including China, Italy and France — has come to supply most of the legal market.

Serving

Caviar is traditionally served very cold, in a non-metallic bowl nested in crushed ice, and eaten with a spoon of mother-of-pearl, bone or plastic, since metal is said to taint the flavour. It is commonly accompanied by blini, toast points, crème fraîche, or served neat with champagne or chilled vodka.

Substitutes

Roes from salmon (ikura), lumpfish, capelin, whitefish and trout are widely sold as more affordable alternatives, as are "caviars" made from seaweed or snail eggs.
