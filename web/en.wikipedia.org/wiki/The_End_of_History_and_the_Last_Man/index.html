The End of History and the Last Man

The End of History and the Last Man is a 1992 book of political philosophy by Francis Fukuyama, expanding his 1989 essay "The End of History?" in The National Interest. Fukuyama argues that the end of the Cold War signalled the endpoint of humanity's ideological evolution, with liberal democracy constituting the final form of human government.

Argument

Drawing on Hegel as read by Alexandre Kojève, Fukuyama contends that history is directional, driven by the struggle for recognition (thymos). Fascism and communism having failed, no coherent ideological alternative to liberal democracy remains. Yet the book's second half worries about the "last man," a figure drawn from Nietzsche: comfortable, self-preserving citizens of victorious liberal democracies may grow bored, crave recognition for its own sake, and destabilise the very order that satisfies them — the problem of megalothymia.

Reception

The thesis became one of the most debated in modern political science. Critics such as Samuel Huntington, with his "clash of civilizations" thesis, disputed it, and later events — the rise of populism and authoritarianism — renewed the debate. Fukuyama himself qualified aspects of the argument in later books on political order and decay, while maintaining that no superior alternative to liberal democracy has emerged.
