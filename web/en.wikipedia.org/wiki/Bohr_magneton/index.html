The Bohr magneton (symbol μB) is a physical constant and the natural unit for expressing the magnetic dipole moment of an electron caused by its orbital or spin angular momentum. It is defined by μB = eħ / (2me), where e is the elementary charge, ħ the reduced Planck constant, and me the electron mass. In SI units its value is approximately 9.274 × 10−24 joules per tesla.

History

The quantity emerged from early atomic theory: Romanian physicist Ștefan Procopiu computed the magnetic moment of the electron in 1911–1913, and Niels Bohr derived it in his 1913 atomic model, in which the electron's orbital angular momentum is quantized in units of ħ. The value is sometimes called the Bohr–Procopiu magneton in Romanian literature. The corresponding unit for protons and nuclei, the nuclear magneton μN = eħ/(2mp), is smaller by the proton-to-electron mass ratio, about 1/1836.

Usage

Atomic magnetic moments are conveniently quoted in Bohr magnetons: the spin moment of a free electron is very nearly 1 μB (more precisely about 1.00116 μB with the anomalous magnetic moment), while iron, cobalt, and nickel atoms carry moments of a few μB each, giving the Bohr magneton a central role in the physics of ferromagnetism and magnetochemistry. Effective moments of paramagnetic ions in salts and complexes are routinely expressed in these units.

Formula

In Gaussian (cgs) units the Bohr magneton is μB = eħ/(2mec) ≈ 9.274 × 10−21 erg·G−1. The constant appears throughout atomic and solid-state physics, from the Zeeman effect to electron paramagnetic resonance.
