Jackalope

The jackalope is a mythical animal of North American folklore, described as a jackrabbit with antelope horns. The word is a portmanteau of "jackrabbit" and "antelope". Numerous mounted jackalope heads — rabbits fitted with deer antlers — are displayed in taverns, restaurants and tourist attractions across the American West, and the creature has become a popular tall-tale figure and souvenir motif.

Origin of the legend

The modern jackalope is usually credited to Douglas Herrick, a taxidermist in Douglas, Wyoming, who with his brother produced the first mounted "jackalope" in the 1930s (often dated 1932) by grafting deer antlers onto a jackrabbit carcass. The mounts sold briskly to tourists, and Douglas became known as the "Jackalope Capital of the World", issuing novelty jackalope hunting licences. Folklorists note that images of horned hares are much older, appearing in European natural histories and art from the 16th century onward.

Possible scientific basis

Stories of horned rabbits may have a factual kernel: rabbits infected with the Shope papilloma virus can develop keratinous, horn-like tumours on the head and body. The virus, first described by Richard Shope in the 1930s, helped establish that viruses can cause cancer in mammals.

Folklore traits

Tall tales credit the jackalope with the ability to mimic human voices — cowboys around campfires supposedly heard them singing along in tenor — and with a fondness for whiskey, which hunters are advised to use as bait. The creatures are said to be extremely shy and dangerous when cornered, able to gore predators with their antlers.

In popular culture

The jackalope appears on postcards, T-shirts and roadside attractions throughout the western United States, serves as mascot for various businesses and festivals (including Douglas's Jackalope Days), and has been featured in animated films, television shows and video games. Similar horned-rabbit figures exist in other traditions, such as the German Wolpertinger and the Swedish Skvader.
