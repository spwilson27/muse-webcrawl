Hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis (HPG axis) is the system of endocrine signalling by which the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, and the gonads (testes and ovaries) regulate development, reproduction, and the production of sex hormones. Together with the adrenal axis it is sometimes grouped under the term hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal–adrenal (HPGA) axis.

Mechanism

Neurons in the hypothalamus secrete gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses into the portal blood supply of the anterior pituitary. GnRH stimulates pituitary gonadotroph cells to release the gonadotropins luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). In the testes, LH drives testosterone production by Leydig cells and FSH supports spermatogenesis in Sertoli cells; in the ovaries, LH and FSH coordinate follicular development, ovulation, and the production of estradiol and progesterone. Sex steroids and inhibin feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary — mostly negatively, although rising estradiol triggers the mid-cycle LH surge by positive feedback in females. Kisspeptin neurons are key regulators of GnRH release and of the onset of puberty.

Functions and disorders

The axis is active in fetal life and infancy ("mini-puberty"), becomes quiescent in childhood, and is reactivated at puberty. Disorders include hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (e.g. Kallmann syndrome, with defective GnRH neuron migration), central precocious puberty, and the axis suppression caused by undernutrition, extreme exercise, or anabolic steroid use. The axis is the target of many medicines, including combined oral contraceptives, GnRH analogues used in prostate cancer and endometriosis, and fertility treatments.
