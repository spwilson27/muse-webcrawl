Secretum secretorum

The Secretum secretorum (Latin for "The Secret of Secrets"), also known as the Sirr al-Asrar (Arabic: كتاب سر الأسرار), is a medieval treatise presented as a letter from Aristotle to his student Alexander the Great on an encyclopaedic range of topics, including statecraft, ethics, physiognomy, astrology, alchemy, magic, and medicine. It was one of the most widely read texts of the Middle Ages.

Origins

The work derives from an Arabic compilation, the Kitab Sirr al-Asrar, assembled around the ninth or tenth century, which drew on Greek, Persian, and Indian sources. A partial Latin translation was made by John of Seville around 1120, and a fuller translation by Philip of Tripoli around 1243 brought the work to a wide European audience. Roger Bacon produced an edition with glossary and commentary around 1270, which greatly increased its influence.

Contents

The treatise advises the ruler on government, the selection of ministers, conduct in war, and justice, and then ranges over occult and natural knowledge: the temperaments and physiognomy, the properties of plants and stones, alchemy, and a famous regimen of health covering diet, sleep, bathing, and the seasons. Later versions incorporated the "hygienic" material into a popular manual of practical advice.

Influence

Hundreds of manuscripts survive in Latin and in vernacular translations across Europe, and the work shaped the medieval genre of the "mirror for princes." Its pseudo-Aristotelian authority made it a vehicle for transmitting Greco-Arabic science and occult lore into Latin Christendom.
