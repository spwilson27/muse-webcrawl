Proton-exchange membrane fuel cell

Proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFC), also known as polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) fuel cells, are a type of fuel cell being developed mainly for transport applications, as well as for stationary and portable power. Their distinguishing features include a lower temperature range (50 to 100 °C) and a special proton-conducting polymer electrolyte membrane, typically Nafion.

Operation

A PEM fuel cell converts the chemical energy of hydrogen and oxygen directly into electricity, producing water as the only exhaust. At the anode, hydrogen is split by a platinum catalyst into protons and electrons. The protons pass through the membrane while the electrons travel through an external circuit, generating current. At the cathode, protons, electrons and oxygen recombine to form water. Individual cells produce about 0.7 volts and are stacked in series to reach useful voltages.

Advantages and challenges

PEMFCs offer high power density, rapid start-up and low operating temperature, making them the leading fuel-cell type for vehicles such as the Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo. Their main challenges are the cost of platinum catalysts, the need for very pure hydrogen (carbon monoxide poisons the catalyst), membrane durability, and water management — the membrane must stay hydrated to conduct protons but must not flood. Alternatives include high-temperature PEM systems operating up to 200 °C with phosphoric-acid-doped membranes, which tolerate impure fuel better.

Applications

Beyond passenger cars, PEM fuel cells power buses, forklifts, backup power units, drones, and some submarines and spacecraft systems. Stationary combined-heat-and-power units based on PEM technology are common in Japanese homes (the Ene-Farm programme). The technology is central to many "hydrogen economy" strategies, although it faces competition from lithium-ion batteries in light vehicles.
