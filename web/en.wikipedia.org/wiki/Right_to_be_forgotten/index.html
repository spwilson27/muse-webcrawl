Right to be forgotten

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to be forgotten (RTBF) is the right to have private information about a person removed from Internet searches and other directories under some circumstances. The concept has been debated and put into practice in several jurisdictions, most prominently the European Union. It balances individuals' privacy and data-protection rights against freedom of expression and the public's interest in access to information.

Google Spain v AEPD

In the landmark 2014 judgment Google Spain SL v. Agencia Española de Protección de Datos and Mario Costeja González, the Court of Justice of the European Union held that search engines are data controllers and that individuals may request the delisting of links to information that is inadequate, irrelevant or excessive in relation to the purposes of processing, including a 1998 newspaper notice about Costeja González's resolved debts. Google received hundreds of thousands of delisting requests in the years following.

GDPR

Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation (2016) codifies a right to erasure, obliging controllers to erase personal data without undue delay on grounds including withdrawal of consent, unlawful processing, or the data no longer being necessary, subject to exceptions such as freedom of expression, legal obligations and public-interest archiving.

Debate

Supporters argue the right protects people from the permanent stigmatizing effect of outdated or inaccurate information. Critics warn of censorship, the rewriting of the historical record, practical difficulties in applying territorial limits (whether delisting must be global), and burdens on publishers and intermediaries. The right has no direct equivalent in United States law, where the First Amendment strongly protects truthful publication, though sectoral rules allow removal of some categories of data.
