Busby Berkeley

Busby Berkeley (born Berkeley William Enos; November 29, 1895 – March 14, 1976) was an American film director and musical choreographer. He is famous for his elaborate musical production numbers that frequently involved complex geometric patterns created by dozens of showgirls, filmed with innovative camera techniques such as overhead "top shots."

Early career

Berkeley came from a theatrical family, served in World War I choreographing drill routines for thousands of soldiers, and became a successful Broadway dance director in the 1920s. Samuel Goldwyn brought him to Hollywood for the Eddie Cantor musical Whoopee! (1930).

Warner Bros. years

At Warner Bros. in the early 1930s, Berkeley created the dance numbers for a string of hit backstage musicals including 42nd Street and Gold Diggers of 1933 (featuring "We're in the Money" and the "Shadow Waltz") and Footlight Parade (with its famous human waterfall). Insisting on a single camera and editing in the can, he broke from stage conventions, moving the camera through formations and drilling holes in studio ceilings for his overhead shots.

Later years

Berkeley moved to MGM, directing films such as the Mickey Rooney–Judy Garland musicals Babes in Arms (1939), which earned him an Academy Award nomination, and choreographing Esther Williams's swimming spectacles in the 1950s. His career declined amid personal and financial troubles, but a 1970s revival — including the Broadway production of No, No, Nanette, which he supervised — restored his reputation as one of cinema's great visual stylists.
