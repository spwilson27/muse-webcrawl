Combinatorics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Pascal's triangle, whose entries count combinations]

Combinatorics is the branch of mathematics concerned with counting, both as a means and an end. It studies finite (or countable discrete) structures: how many ways objects can be selected, arranged, or combined subject to constraints.

Its basic tools are permutations and combinations, summarised by the binomial coefficients that appear in Pascal's triangle, together with principles such as inclusion–exclusion and the pigeonhole principle.

Areas

Enumerative combinatorics counts structures of a given kind; extremal combinatorics asks how large or small a structure can be while satisfying properties (Ramsey theory and the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem are examples); design theory constructs balanced arrangements such as block designs.

Combinatorics is closely related to graph theory, probability (discrete probability spaces), and algebra, and it is indispensable in computer science for analysing algorithms and in statistical physics.

History

Counting problems appear in ancient Indian and Chinese mathematics; systematic study grew with probability theory in the work of Pascal and Fermat in the 17th century, and the subject expanded enormously in the 20th century under figures such as Paul Erdős.
