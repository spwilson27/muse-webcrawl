Shift-and-add

Shift-and-add is a class of algorithms used in computer arithmetic to perform operations such as multiplication and division using only bit shifts and additions. Along with its close relative shift-and-subtract, it formed the basis of arithmetic in many early computers and remains widely used in hardware multipliers, dividers, and software routines on processors that lack fast multiplication instructions.

Multiplication

In binary shift-and-add multiplication, the multiplicand is examined bit by bit. For each set bit in the multiplier, a copy of the multiplicand shifted left by the bit position is added to an accumulator. The process mirrors long multiplication in decimal: each partial product is either zero or a shifted copy of the multiplicand. After processing all bits, the accumulator holds the product. The algorithm requires at most n additions for two n-bit operands.

Division

Shift-and-subtract division inverts the process. The divisor is aligned with the most significant bits of the dividend and subtracted if the result is non-negative, setting the corresponding quotient bit to 1; otherwise the quotient bit is 0 and the divisor is shifted right by one position. Repeating this for each bit position yields the quotient and remainder.

Variants and improvements

Several refinements reduce the number of steps or the latency. Booth's algorithm recodes the multiplier to skip runs of ones, reducing additions. Non-restoring division avoids a separate restore step after an over-subtraction. Higher-radix variants process two or more bits per cycle, trading additional hardware for speed.

Applications

Shift-and-add techniques appear in digital signal processors, floating-point unit implementations, CORDIC algorithms for trigonometric functions, and exponential/logarithmic approximations. They also underlie the classic "Russian peasant multiplication" method, which doubles and halves numbers instead of shifting bits.
