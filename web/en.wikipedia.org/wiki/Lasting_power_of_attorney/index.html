Lasting power of attorney

A lasting power of attorney (LPA) is a legal document under the law of England and Wales that lets a person (the "donor") appoint one or more people (the "attorneys") to help them make decisions or to make decisions on their behalf. Unlike an ordinary power of attorney, a lasting power of attorney continues to be valid — or "lasts" — even if the donor later loses the mental capacity to make their own decisions. LPAs were created by the Mental Capacity Act 2005 and came into force on 1 October 2007, replacing the earlier enduring power of attorney (EPA) system in England and Wales.

A lasting power of attorney allows decisions to be made for someone who loses mental capacity.

Types

There are two types of LPA:

  • Property and financial affairs LPA — gives the attorney authority to make decisions about money and property, such as managing bank accounts, paying bills, collecting benefits or a pension, and selling the donor's home. It can be used as soon as it is registered, with the donor's permission, or only when the donor lacks capacity, depending on the donor's choice.
  • Health and welfare LPA — gives the attorney authority to make decisions about things such as daily routine, medical care, and moving into a care home. It can only be used when the donor is unable to make their own decisions. It may also give the attorney the power to make decisions about life-sustaining treatment, if the donor expressly grants this.

Registration

An LPA must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG) before it can be used. A "certificate provider" — someone who has known the donor for at least two years, or a professional such as a solicitor or doctor — must confirm that the donor understands the document and is not under pressure to make it. The donor must be 18 or over and have mental capacity when the LPA is made. The Powers of Attorney Act 2023 introduced reforms to modernise the process, including identity checks and wider safeguards.

Safeguards and termination

Attorneys must act in the donor's best interests, follow the principles of the Mental Capacity Act 2005, and keep records of their decisions. The Court of Protection can revoke an LPA if an attorney acts improperly, and the OPG investigates allegations of abuse. An LPA ends when the donor dies, when the donor revokes it while they still have capacity, or in certain circumstances where an attorney can no longer act.

Equivalent instruments

Scotland has its own system of continuing powers of attorney and welfare powers of attorney under the Adults with Incapacity (Scotland) Act 2000, registered with the Office of the Public Guardian (Scotland). Northern Ireland retains enduring powers of attorney under a separate regime. Similar concepts exist in other jurisdictions, such as the enduring power of attorney in Ireland, Australia, and New Zealand, and durable powers of attorney in the United States.
