Amazon rubber boom

Amazon rubber boom
Period: c. 1879–1912 (first cycle)
Region: Amazon Basin, Brazil, Peru, Bolivia
Centres: Manaus, Belém, Iquitos
Cause of collapse: Cheaper Asian plantation rubber

The Amazon rubber boom (Portuguese: Ciclo da borracha) was an important part of the economic and social history of Brazil and Amazonian regions of Peru and Bolivia, concerning the extraction and commercialisation of rubber. Centred in the Amazon Basin, the boom resulted in a large expansion of European colonisation in the area, attracted immigrant workers, generated wealth, caused cultural and social transformations, and wrought havoc upon indigenous societies.

Rise

Vulcanisation (1839) and the pneumatic tyre (1888) created surging demand for rubber, which only wild Hevea brasiliensis trees of Amazonia then supplied in quantity. Manaus and Belém in Brazil and Iquitos in Peru became fabulously wealthy entrepôts; Manaus gained electric lighting, trams and the opulent Teatro Amazonas opera house (1896). Labour was drawn from drought-stricken north-eastern Brazil; tappers (seringueiros) worked under a debt-peonage system (aviamento) controlled by trading houses.

Atrocities

In the Putumayo region of Peru, the Peruvian Amazon Company of Julio César Arana enslaved indigenous peoples including the Huitoto, Bora and Andoque; tens of thousands died. Roger Casement's 1910–11 report, following his Congo investigation, exposed the abuses internationally.

Collapse

In 1876 Henry Wickham had taken Hevea seeds to Kew, from which plantation rubber was established in British Malaya and the Dutch East Indies. When Asian plantations matured after 1910, prices collapsed; by 1912 the Amazon monopoly was broken. A second, smaller boom came during World War II, when the United States funded Brazilian rubber production after Japan seized Asian plantations — the era of the "rubber soldiers" — and Henry Ford's earlier Fordlândia plantation (1928) had already failed against leaf blight.
