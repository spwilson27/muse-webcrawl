Flies are insects of the order Diptera, the name deriving from Greek di- (two) and pteron (wing). Unlike other winged insects, flies use only their forewings for flight; the hindwings are reduced to small, club-shaped balancing organs called halteres. Diptera is one of the largest insect orders, with about 160,000 described species — including house flies, mosquitoes, midges, crane flies, hoverflies, blow flies, fruit flies and horse flies — found on every continent including Antarctica, whose only native insect is a flightless midge.

Biology

Flies undergo complete metamorphosis: egg, larva (maggot), pupa and adult. Larvae are legless and often aquatic or live in decaying matter, soil, or as parasites of other animals. Adults typically have large compound eyes, short antennae, and mouthparts adapted for piercing and sucking (as in mosquitoes and horse flies) or sponging up liquids (as in house flies). Many species are important pollinators — second only to bees among insect orders — while others are vectors of serious diseases: mosquitoes transmit malaria, dengue and Zika; tsetse flies transmit sleeping sickness; black flies transmit river blindness. The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster is a cornerstone model organism of genetics.

Evolution and classification

Diptera arose in the Permian or Triassic, over 240 million years ago. The order is divided into the "lower flies" (historically Nematocera: mosquitoes, crane flies, midges, with long multi-segmented antennae) and the Brachycera (house flies, horse flies, robber flies, hoverflies), a group whose members have shorter antennae and include the huge radiation of the Cyclorrhapha.

Relation to humans

Flies pollinate crops, decompose waste and carrion, serve as food for other animals, and provide forensic timing evidence through maggot development; they also spoil food, bite livestock, and spread cholera, typhoid and dysentery. Forensic entomology, sterile-insect pest control and maggot therapy in wound care all exploit fly biology.
