Artificial island

An artificial island or man-made island is an island that has been constructed by people rather than formed by natural means. Artificial islands are usually built by land reclamation — depositing sand, rock, earth or dredged material on a shallow seabed, reef or existing islet — though they may also be formed by the incidental flooding of surrounding land, as when a canal is cut or a reservoir filled, or by permanently anchored structures.

History

Artificial islands have been created for millennia. Crannogs in the lakes of Scotland and Ireland date to prehistoric times; the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan was expanded with chinampa gardens; and Dejima, a small artificial island built in Nagasaki harbour in 1634, served as Japan's sole window on European trade for two centuries. In the seventeenth century the Dutch became masters of polder and reclamation engineering, a tradition continued in modern projects worldwide.

Modern examples

Large modern artificial islands include the Palm Islands and The World archipelago off Dubai; Kansai International Airport in Osaka Bay and Hong Kong International Airport, both built on reclaimed land in the sea; Flevopolder in the Netherlands, among the largest artificial islands by area; and the airport and port islands of Singapore, which has expanded its land area by roughly a quarter through reclamation. China has built up islands on reefs in the South China Sea since 2013, a programme that has drawn international criticism and legal challenge.

Legal status

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, artificial islands do not possess the status of natural islands: they generate no territorial sea of their own (though their coastal state may establish safety zones around them) and do not affect maritime boundary delimitation. Artificial islands built within a state's territorial sea fall under that state's jurisdiction; installations on the continental shelf are governed by the coastal state's rights over the shelf.

Environmental concerns

Reclamation can destroy marine habitats, alter currents and sedimentation, and increase flood risk; large projects are now usually subject to environmental impact assessment, and some modern schemes incorporate habitat creation or "building with nature" techniques.
