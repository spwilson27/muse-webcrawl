Neijia (Chinese: 內家; pinyin: nèijiā; lit. 'internal family') is a term in Chinese martial arts that groups together the styles said to practise neijing ("internal strength"), usually contrasted with waijia ("external family") styles that emphasise muscular force and speed. The three principal neijia arts are taijiquan (tai chi), xingyiquan, and baguazhang, which are said to share common principles despite their different appearances.

History of the term

The internal/external distinction appears as early as Huang Zongxi's 1669 epitaph for the martial artist Wang Zhengnan, which credited the Daoist sage Zhang Sanfeng with founding an "internal school" superior to the Shaolin ("external") arts. The grouping of taijiquan, xingyiquan, and baguazhang as a single family was popularised in the early 20th century, especially by Sun Lutang, who studied and published on all three arts and helped institutionalise the category through organisations such as the Central Guoshu Institute.

Principles

Neijia training emphasises cultivating qi and refining jin (trained force) through relaxation (song), whole-body coordination, rooting, and intent (yi) rather than isolated muscular tension. Common practices include standing meditation (zhan zhuang), slow solo forms, silk-reeling exercises, and two-person pushing hands (tui shou). Techniques favour yielding, neutralising, and redirecting an opponent's force; the classical writings invoke Daoist concepts such as the interplay of yin and yang and the injunction that "four ounces deflect a thousand pounds".

The three sisters

The three arts are often called the "three sisters" of neijia: taijiquan, known for slow, circular, continuous movement; xingyiquan, a compact, linear art based on the five elements (wuxing) and twelve animals; and baguazhang, characterised by continuous circle-walking and palm changes. Many practitioners cross-train in two or three of them, and teachers such as Sun Lutang held that the arts converge at advanced levels.

Criticism of the distinction

Historians and practitioners note that the internal/external dichotomy is partly a product of late imperial and Republican-era politics and marketing. Advanced "external" styles also develop internal power, and internal styles also condition the body externally; the boundary is porous, and many schools (such as Yiquan, founded by Wang Xiangzhai) deliberately set the labels aside.
