Asymptote

In analytic geometry, an asymptote of a curve is a line such that the distance between the curve and the line approaches zero as one or both of the x or y coordinates tends to infinity. In other words, the curve approaches the line ever more closely without (in the classic examples) reaching it at any finite point. The term derives from the Greek asýmptōtos, "not falling together".

Asymptote
FieldAnalytic geometry, calculus
TypesHorizontal, vertical, oblique
Classic exampleThe hyperbola and its asymptotes

Types

  • A horizontal asymptote is a horizontal line y = L that the graph approaches as x → ±∞; for a rational function, it is determined by comparing the degrees of numerator and denominator.
  • A vertical asymptote is a vertical line x = a near which the function grows without bound, typically where a denominator is zero and the function is undefined.
  • An oblique (slant) asymptote is a slanted line that the graph approaches when the degree of the numerator exceeds that of the denominator by one.

Examples

The function f(x) = 1/x has both coordinate axes as asymptotes. The hyperbola x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 is asymptotic to the lines y = ±(b/a)x. A curve may cross its horizontal or oblique asymptote finitely many times; what matters is the limiting behaviour at infinity. More general notions treat one curve as asymptotic to another, and asymptotes are fundamental in curve sketching and in the study of limits.
