Fritz Heider

Fritz Heider (19 February 1896 – 2 January 1988) was an Austrian psychologist whose work in social psychology founded several lines of research still influential today, including attribution theory, balance theory and the study of naive or commonsense psychology. He is best known for his book The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958).

Life

Heider was born in Vienna and studied at the University of Graz, where he received his doctorate in 1920. Influenced by the Gestalt psychologists and by Alexius Meinong, he moved to Berlin in the 1920s. In 1927 he emigrated to the United States, working first at Smith College — where he collaborated with Kurt Koffka — and then at the University of Kansas, where he spent the rest of his career from 1947.

Work

With Marianne Simmel, Heider published a famous 1944 experiment in which participants shown a short animation of moving geometric shapes spontaneously described them in intentional, social terms — as persons chasing, fighting or fleeing — demonstrating the human tendency to attribute agency and motives. His 1958 book developed balance theory, holding that people prefer consistency among their sentiments toward other people and objects (the P–O–X model), and laid the foundations of attribution theory: the study of how ordinary people explain the causes of behaviour, a field developed further by Harold Kelley, Edward Jones and others. Heider's earlier Ding und Medium (1926) addressed the perception of objects through media such as light and sound.
