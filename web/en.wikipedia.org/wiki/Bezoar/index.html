Bezoar

A bezoar stone; such stones were once prized as universal antidotes

A bezoar is a mass of indigestible material found trapped in the gastrointestinal system, most commonly in the stomach. In medicine the term is applied to concretions of hair (trichobezoars), plant fibre (phytobezoars), milk curd (lactobezoars) and other swallowed materials in humans and animals. The word derives from the Persian pādzahr, "protection against poison".

Historical belief

For centuries, bezoar stones taken from the stomachs of goats, antelopes and other ruminants were among the most valuable substances in the pharmacopoeia, believed to be a universal antidote to poison. They were set in gold, kept in royal treasuries, and grated into wine. The French surgeon Ambroise Paré famously tested the claim in the 16th century: a condemned cook was poisoned and given a bezoar, but died in agony anyway, demonstrating the stone's uselessness. The Portuguese bezoar goat of Goa and artificial "Goa stones" prepared by Jesuit apothecaries were especially famous.

Modern medicine

In contemporary practice, gastrointestinal bezoars are a clinical problem rather than a treasure. Phytobezoars, often formed from persimmon fibres (diospyrobezoars), are the most common type and occur particularly in patients with prior gastric surgery or poor dentition. Symptoms include early satiety, nausea, vomiting and obstruction. Treatment ranges from enzymatic or cola dissolution to endoscopic fragmentation and surgery. Trichobezoars are associated with the psychiatric conditions trichotillomania and trichophagia; a large gastric trichobezoar extending into the intestine is known as Rapunzel syndrome.
