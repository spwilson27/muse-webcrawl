Extended vocal technique

Contemporary singers employ a range of unconventional vocal sounds

Extended vocal techniques are methods of singing or vocal sound production that fall outside the conventions of classical bel canto and standard popular singing. They include overtone singing (throat singing), growling, screaming, vocal fry, multiphonics, ululation, inhaled phonation, whistle register production and the imitation of instruments or noises. The term parallels "extended technique" as applied to musical instruments.

History

Many such techniques are ancient in folk traditions — Tuvan and Mongolian throat singing, Inuit katajjaq and yodelling among them. In Western art music, twentieth-century composers began systematically demanding unconventional vocal sounds: Arnold Schoenberg's Sprechstimme, the works of Luciano Berio for Cathy Berberian (notably Sequenza III), and pieces by Karlheinz Stockhausen, John Cage and Meredith Monk expanded the recognised vocal repertoire. Singers such as Berberian, Joan La Barbara, Diamanda Galás and Demetrio Stratos became celebrated specialists, and Stratos's ability to produce multiphonics was extensively studied by phoneticians.

In popular music

Extended techniques are widespread in popular genres: growled and screamed vocals in heavy metal and hardcore punk, beatboxing in hip hop, the whistle register in pop, and overtone singing adapted by world-music and film composers. Their safe production has become a subject of vocal pedagogy and voice science, since some techniques can damage the voice if performed without training.
