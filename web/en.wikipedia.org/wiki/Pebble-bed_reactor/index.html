Pebble-bed reactor

The pebble-bed reactor (PBR) is a design for a graphite-moderated, gas-cooled nuclear reactor. It is a type of very-high-temperature reactor (VHTR), one of the six classes of Generation IV reactor designs. Its fuel consists of spherical elements ("pebbles"), roughly the size of tennis balls, each containing thousands of coated fuel particles (TRISO particles) embedded in graphite.

Design

The core is a bed of fuel pebbles through which an inert or semi-inert gas such as helium, nitrogen, or carbon dioxide flows as coolant, transferring heat to a power cycle — often a Brayton-cycle turbine or a steam generator. Pebbles are continuously circulated: fresh fuel is added at the top and spent pebbles are withdrawn from the bottom and measured for burnup, with usable pebbles recirculated. This allows refuelling without shutdown.

Safety characteristics

Advocates emphasise inherent safety features: a strong negative temperature coefficient of reactivity, and TRISO fuel coatings that retain fission products even at very high temperatures, so that under loss-of-coolant accidents the core is designed to shed heat passively by conduction and radiation without melting. Critics point to issues including graphite fire risk if air enters, production of large volumes of irradiated graphite waste, dust generation from pebble abrasion, and online-refuelling complexity.

History

The concept was pioneered in Germany: the AVR experimental reactor operated from 1967 to 1988, followed by the larger THTR-300 (1985–1989), which suffered technical and political setbacks. South Africa's PBMR project was cancelled in 2010. China pursued the design with the HTR-10 research reactor and the HTR-PM demonstration plant at Shidaowan, whose twin modules were connected to the grid in 2021 and entered commercial operation in 2023 — the first commercial pebble-bed plant. In the United States, X-energy's Xe-100 is a pebble-bed design under development.
