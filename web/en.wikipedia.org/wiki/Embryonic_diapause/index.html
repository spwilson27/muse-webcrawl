Embryonic diapause

Embryonic diapause (from late 19th-century English: dia- 'through' + pause), also called delayed implantation, is a reproductive strategy used by a number of animal species in which the blastocyst does not immediately implant in the uterus after fertilization, but is maintained in a state of dormancy. Little to no development takes place while the embryo remains unattached, extending the normal gestation period.

Distribution and function

Delayed implantation occurs in more than 100 mammal species, including bears, seals, weasels, badgers, armadillos, many bats, and marsupials such as kangaroos and wallabies. It allows animals to time births to favorable environmental conditions — for example, ensuring young are born in spring — and, in macropods, lets a mother keep a spare embryo in reserve while nursing a joey; if the joey dies, the dormant blastocyst resumes development.

Mechanisms

Diapause is controlled hormonally, often by prolactin and progesterone acting on the uterus and the blastocyst, and in many species is triggered or ended by photoperiod. During diapause the blastocyst's cells divide very slowly or not at all. Reactivation involves renewed uterine secretions and signaling between the embryo and the endometrium.

Related phenomena

Some fish, such as annual killifish, and many invertebrates undergo comparable embryonic dormancy, helping embryos survive drought or other harsh seasons.
