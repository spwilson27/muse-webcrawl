Bank failure

A bank failure occurs when a bank is unable to meet its obligations to depositors and other creditors because it has become insolvent or too illiquid to operate, and is closed by a banking regulator. In the United States, failed banks are typically placed into receivership by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), which insures deposits and arranges either a payout to depositors or the sale of the bank's assets and deposits to a healthier institution.

Causes

Failures usually follow loan losses that erode a bank's capital, mismatches between long-term assets and short-term liabilities, fraud, or a loss of confidence that triggers a bank run. Waves of failures have accompanied financial crises: some 9,000 American banks failed during the Great Depression, hundreds during the savings and loan crisis of the 1980s and 1990s, and more than 500 between 2008 and 2015 in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis. The largest American bank failure was Washington Mutual in 2008, with about $307 billion in assets. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank failed within days of each other, among the largest failures in US history, prompting regulators to invoke a systemic-risk exception to protect uninsured depositors.

Resolution

Modern regimes aim to resolve failures over a weekend so depositors keep access to insured funds, and to impose losses on shareholders and, where possible, creditors rather than taxpayers. Deposit insurance, capital requirements, stress testing and "living will" resolution plans are intended to reduce both the frequency and the systemic impact of failures.
