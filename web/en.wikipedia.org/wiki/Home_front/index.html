Home front

The home front is the civilian populace and activities of a nation whose armed forces are engaged in war abroad. The term became prominent during the First and Second World Wars, when total war required the mobilisation of entire societies: civilians were expected to sustain war production, endure rationing and bombing, and maintain morale, so that the home front came to be regarded as a front line in its own right.

First World War

Between 1914 and 1918 belligerent governments expanded state control over industry, food and labour. In Britain, the Defence of the Realm Act, the munitions crisis of 1915 and the introduction of conscription transformed civilian life; women entered factories, transport and clerical work in large numbers. Blockade and food shortages undermined the German and Austro-Hungarian home fronts, contributing to the collapse of morale and revolution in 1917–1918.

Second World War

In the Second World War the home front was directly attacked by strategic bombing, as in the Blitz on British cities and Allied bombing of Germany and Japan. Governments organised rationing, evacuation of children, civil defence, scrap and savings drives, and propaganda. In the United States the "Home Front" involved mass conversion of industry to war production, the employment of millions of women symbolised by "Rosie the Riveter", and the internment of Japanese Americans. In the Soviet Union, civilians endured siege, evacuation of factories eastward and enormous losses.

Concept and legacy

Historians use the home front to analyse morale, propaganda, gender, childhood, consumption and the expansion of state power in wartime. The blurring of civilian and military spheres it describes is now a standard feature of discussions of total war and modern conflict.
