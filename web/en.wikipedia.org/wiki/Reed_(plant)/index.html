Reeds are tall, slender grass-like plants of wetlands, forming dense reedbeds.

Reed is a common name for several tall, grass-like plants of wetlands. In its strictest sense it refers to species of the grass family (Poaceae), especially the common reed (Phragmites australis), one of the most widespread flowering plants in the world, and the giant reed (Arundo donax). In popular usage the name extends to other reed-like wetland plants, including reed mace or cattails (Typha), bur-reeds (Sparganium), and reed canary grass (Phalaris arundinacea). Stands of reeds are called reedbeds.

Description and ecology

Reeds typically grow 1–5 metres tall with hollow or pithy stems and long, flat leaves, spreading by rhizomes to form extensive colonies in shallow water and saturated soils. Reedbeds are important wildlife habitat, sheltering species such as the Eurasian bittern, marsh harrier, and reed warblers, and they filter sediment and nutrients from water, which has led to their use in constructed wetlands for wastewater treatment. In some regions, particularly North America and Australia, introduced strains of common reed are regarded as invasive, crowding out native marsh vegetation.

Uses

Reeds have been used by humans for millennia:

  • Thatching — water reed (common reed) and Norfolk reed are traditional roofing materials in northern Europe.
  • Music — the vibrating reeds of clarinets, saxophones, oboes, and bassoons are traditionally cut from giant reed cane (Arundo donax); ancient reed pipes and flutes are among the oldest instruments.
  • Craft and construction — weaving of mats, screens, and baskets; pens (qalam) cut from reed stems were the standard writing implement of the ancient and medieval worlds.
  • Other — papyrus-like writing material, light fencing, and biomass and biofuel crops.
