World lines of a stationary, a moving and an accelerating object in a spacetime diagram.

A world line is the path that an object traces in four-dimensional spacetime as it moves through space over time. The concept was introduced by Hermann Minkowski in 1908 as part of the geometric formulation of Albert Einstein's special relativity, in which space and time are united into a single continuum. Every material object has a world line: a point particle traces a curve through spacetime, while an extended body occupies a "world tube".

Definition and properties

In a spacetime diagram with time on the vertical axis and one space dimension on the horizontal axis, an object at rest has a vertical world line; an object moving at constant velocity has a straight, inclined world line; and an accelerating object has a curved world line. Because nothing material can travel faster than light, the world line of any physical object must always lie within its local light cone, inclined at less than 45 degrees from the time axis. The natural parameter along a world line is the proper time — the time measured by a clock carried with the object — and the interval between events along the curve is invariant, the same in all frames of reference.

Uses in physics

World lines are fundamental to relativity and quantum theory. In general relativity, free-falling objects follow geodesics in curved spacetime, of which their world lines are the paths; freely falling world lines maximise proper time between events, a fact that explains effects such as the twin paradox. In quantum field theory, Feynman diagrams can be read as schematic world lines of particles, and Feynman's path-integral formulation sums over all possible paths of a particle through spacetime. A world line that loops back on itself — a closed timelike curve — would allow travel into the past and appears in certain exotic solutions of Einstein's equations, such as the Gödel universe.

History

Minkowski introduced the German term Weltlinie in his famous 1908 Cologne lecture "Space and Time", in which he declared that space and time separately were "doomed to fade away into mere shadows". The four-dimensional viewpoint was initially resisted by Einstein but soon became indispensable to the development of general relativity, and world lines remain the standard way of visualising motion in relativistic physics.
