Philip Henry Sheridan (6 March 1831 – 5 August 1888) was a career United States Army officer and a Union general in the American Civil War. His career was noted for his rapid rise to major general and his close association with General-in-Chief Ulysses S. Grant, who transferred him from command of an infantry division in the Western Theater to lead the Cavalry Corps of the Army of the Potomac in the East. In 1884, he succeeded William Tecumseh Sherman as Commanding General of the U.S. Army, and in 1888 he was promoted to the rank of General of the Army.

Civil War service

Sheridan, a West Point graduate (1853), served on the frontier before the war. He rose quickly in 1862, fighting at Perryville, Stones River, and Chickamauga, and distinguished himself at Missionary Ridge. In 1864 Grant gave him command of the Army of the Potomac's cavalry, and then the Army of the Shenandoah, with orders to neutralize the Confederate forces in the Shenandoah Valley. His victories at Third Winchester, Fisher's Hill, and Cedar Creek, together with the systematic destruction of the valley's agricultural capacity ("The Burning"), devastated the Confederate war effort. His famous ride from Winchester to Cedar Creek to rally his retreating army became the subject of the popular poem "Sheridan's Ride". In 1865 his cavalry cut off Robert E. Lee's retreat at Appomattox, compelling surrender.

Postwar career

After the war, Sheridan served as military governor of Texas and Louisiana during Reconstruction, directed campaigns against Native American peoples on the Great Plains during the Indian Wars, and observed the Franco-Prussian War. He played a role in protecting Yellowstone National Park and its wildlife. As Commanding General from 1884 until his death in 1888, he professionalized the army and founded institutions that shaped its modern development.
