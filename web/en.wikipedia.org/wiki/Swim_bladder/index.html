Swim bladder

The swim bladder, or gas bladder, is an internal gas-filled organ that contributes to the ability of many bony fish (but not cartilaginous fish) to control their buoyancy, and thus to stay at their current water depth without having to expend energy in swimming. It also serves as a resonating chamber to produce or receive sound in some species, and in a few — such as lungfish — functions in respiration.

Structure and function

The bladder develops as an outpocketing of the gut. In physostomous fish (e.g. salmon, carp, catfish) it retains a pneumatic duct to the oesophagus, allowing the fish to gulp or release air at the surface. In physoclistous fish (most advanced teleosts, e.g. perch and cod) the duct is lost, and gas is secreted into the bladder from the blood by the gas gland and the counter-current exchanger called the rete mirabile, which exploits the Root effect of fish haemoglobin to generate high oxygen partial pressures; gas is resorbed through a vascularised region called the oval.

By adjusting bladder volume — typically about 5% of body volume in marine fish and 7% in freshwater fish — the animal achieves neutral buoyancy. Rapid depth changes can rupture the bladder (barotrauma), a welfare concern in catch-and-release fishing. In hearing specialists such as the otophysan fishes (carps, catfishes, characins), a chain of small bones — the Weberian apparatus — links the bladder to the inner ear, greatly enhancing hearing sensitivity.

Evolution

The swim bladder is homologous with the lungs of tetrapods; developmental and fossil evidence indicates that lungs evolved first, in early bony fishes, and the swim bladder is a modified lung repurposed from respiration to buoyancy. Some bottom-dwelling and fast-swimming fishes (tunas, many sharks among the cartilaginous fishes) have secondarily reduced or lost the bladder.

Human uses

Swim bladders of certain species are a delicacy (fish maw) in Chinese cuisine and a source of isinglass, a high-grade collagen once used to clarify beer and wine and to make glues. Demand for maw — especially from the totoaba — has driven illegal fisheries and contributed to the near-extinction of the vaquita porpoise, entangled in the nets used.
