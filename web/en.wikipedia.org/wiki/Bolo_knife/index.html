A bolo (Tagalog: iták; Hiligaynon: talibong) is a large cutting tool of Filipino origin similar to the machete. It is used particularly in the jungles of Indonesia, Malaysia and in the Pacific, and is widely used in the Philippines for agriculture and as a weapon in Filipino martial arts.

Description

The bolo is characterised by a single-edged blade that widens and curves toward the tip, shifting the centre of gravity forward and giving chopping power out of proportion to its size. Blade profiles vary regionally across the Philippines — for harvesting rice, cutting coconut, clearing brush or splitting wood — and handles are usually of hardwood or carabao (water buffalo) horn, often with decorative carving. Agricultural bolos are plain working tools, while prestige weapons may have blades of laminated or pattern-welded steel.

History

Bolos were the primary sidearm of the Katipunan revolutionaries during the Philippine Revolution of 1896 and were carried by Filipino guerrillas, including the famous bolo battalions, in the Philippine–American War and the Second World War. The United States military adopted bolo knives and bolo bayonets for jungle service, notably the Model 1910 and Model 1917 bolo bayonets, and bolo knives were issued to troops in the Pacific theatre. The names of Filipino-American units such as the 1st Filipino Infantry Regiment's "Bolo Battalion" commemorate the weapon.

Use today

The bolo remains an everyday agricultural implement in the Philippines and is a standard training weapon in eskrima/arnis and other Filipino martial arts, where short bolos are used for close-range drills. A smaller version, the gulok or utility bolo, is used in kitchens and gardens.
