Displaced persons camp

Displaced persons camps (DP camps) were temporary facilities established after the Second World War in Germany, Austria, and Italy to house the millions of people displaced by the conflict — former prisoners of war, freed concentration camp inmates, forced labourers, and refugees fleeing westward. At their peak in 1945–46, the camps and assembly centres of the Western occupation zones held roughly seven million displaced persons.

Background

By May 1945, more than eleven million people were displaced outside their home countries in Europe. The Western Allies repatriated the great majority during 1945, but about a million people — including many Jews who survived the Holocaust, Poles, Balts, Yugoslavs, and Ukrainians — refused or were unable to return, fearing persecution or unwilling to live under Soviet domination.

Administration

The camps were first run by Allied military authorities with the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA); from 1947 the International Refugee Organization (IRO) took over. Camps were often installed in former concentration camps, military barracks, schools, and requisitioned housing. Notable camps included Bergen-Belsen (the largest Jewish DP camp), Landsberg am Lech, Feldafing, and Föhrenwald, which remained open until 1957 — the last DP camp to close.

Life in the camps

Despite hardship and uncertainty, DP camps developed rich communal life: schools, newspapers, theatres, sports leagues, religious institutions, and political organisations. Jewish DPs, organised in the Central Committee of Liberated Jews, pressed for emigration to Palestine and, after 1948, to the new State of Israel.

Resettlement

Between 1947 and the early 1950s the IRO resettled over a million DPs, chiefly to the United States (under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948), Israel, Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom. By 1952 nearly all camps had closed.
