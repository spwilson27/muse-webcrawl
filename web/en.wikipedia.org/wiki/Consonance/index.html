Consonance and dissonance

In music theory, consonance and dissonance are categorizations of simultaneous or successive sounds. Traditionally, consonance has been associated with sweetness and pleasantness and intervals of simplicity: the unison, octave, perfect fifth and perfect fourth, and the major and minor thirds and sixths. Dissonance, by contrast, has been associated with tension and a desire for resolution, and applies to intervals such as the major and minor seconds, the tritone, and the major and minor sevenths.

Acoustics

From an acoustical standpoint, consonant intervals tend to have simple frequency ratios in just intonation: the octave is 2:1, the perfect fifth 3:2, the major third 5:4. Helmholtz explained consonance in terms of the absence of beats between upper partials, while later research has emphasized sensory consonance arising from the harmonicity of complex tones and the roughness produced by tones falling within the same critical band.

Cultural aspects

Perceptions of consonance are partly learned and culture-dependent: the third was treated as a dissonance in early medieval polyphony but became a standard consonance by the Renaissance, and some musical traditions cultivate sonorities that Western theory labels dissonant, such as the shimmering inharmonic timbres of the gamelan.
