Flexitarianism is a semi-vegetarian diet centred on plant foods with the occasional inclusion of meat and animal products. The word is a portmanteau of "flexible" and "vegetarian", coined in the 1990s and popularised by Dawn Jackson Blatner's 2008 book The Flexitarian Diet. Unlike vegetarianism, flexitarianism has no strict definition or governing body: the common feature is eating mostly vegetarian meals while not eliminating meat entirely.

Practice and motivations

Flexitarians typically reduce meat consumption for health reasons — diets rich in vegetables, legumes and whole grains are associated with lower risks of heart disease and type 2 diabetes — for environmental reasons, since livestock production accounts for a significant share of food-related greenhouse gas emissions, and for animal welfare or cost. Structured variants include "weekday vegetarianism" and campaigns such as Meatless Monday, which encourage one or more meat-free days each week.

Prevalence and reception

Surveys in Europe and North America consistently find self-described flexitarians outnumber strict vegetarians and vegans, and the growth of the category has driven food manufacturers and supermarkets to expand plant-based ranges. Nutritionists generally regard a well-planned flexitarian diet as healthy, while some vegetarians criticise the label as diluting ethical commitments; supporters counter that large numbers of people cutting meat consumption part-way may achieve greater aggregate reductions than a smaller number of strict vegetarians.
