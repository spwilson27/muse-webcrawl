Panel painting

A panel painting is a painting made on a flat panel made of wood, either a single board or a number of boards joined together. Until canvas became the more popular support medium in the 16th century, panel painting was the normal method of producing easel pictures and most altarpieces, when not painting directly onto walls (fresco) or on vellum (manuscript illumination).

Materials and preparation

Painters in northern Europe generally used oak, Italian painters poplar, and other regions walnut, pine, limewood or mahogany (later, in the tropics). Boards were seasoned, joined and sometimes reinforced with battens or cradling, then sized with animal glue and coated with a smooth ground — gesso (gypsum or chalk in glue) in the European tradition — which was sanded before an underdrawing was laid in. The rigid support accepts fine detail and thin glazes, and preserves paint surfaces exceptionally well, though panels are vulnerable to warping, splitting along the grain, woodworm and damage from fluctuations in humidity.

History

Panel painting has ancient roots: Egyptian funerary portraits of the Roman period (the Fayum mummy portraits, in encaustic or tempera on wood) survive in remarkable condition, and Byzantine icons continued the technique. In the medieval West, panel painting flourished from the 12th and 13th centuries — Italian masters such as Duccio and Giotto, then the Early Netherlandish painters Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden, who exploited oil paint on oak panels to achieve unprecedented luminosity. Panels remained dominant for small-scale and precision work long after canvas took over for large pictures, and panel painting survived in icon painting, folk art and modern revivals.

Conservation and study

Because panels bear tool marks, joinery and sometimes workshop brands on their reverse, and because tree-ring dating (dendrochronology) can date the boards, panel paintings provide unusually rich evidence for dating and attribution — a field sometimes aided by X-radiography and infrared reflectography of the underdrawing.
