Low-ball

The low-ball is a persuasion and selling technique in which an item or service is offered at a lower price than is actually intended to be charged, after which the price is raised to increase the likelihood that the buyer will accept the higher price. The technique exploits the psychological principle of commitment: once a person has made a decision to buy, they tend to remain committed to it even when the terms become less favourable.

Research

The technique was demonstrated experimentally by Robert Cialdini and colleagues in 1978. In their study, students who first agreed to participate in an experiment were then told it would begin at 7 a.m.; far more of them actually showed up than students who were told the early start time at the outset, even though both groups were free to withdraw. The effect is attributed to the person's initial, active commitment creating a sense of obligation and a favourable self-image tied to the decision.

Related techniques

The low-ball is related to, but distinct from, bait-and-switch, in which the originally offered item is claimed to be unavailable, and from the foot-in-the-door technique, in which compliance with a small request increases compliance with a larger one. In consumer-protection law, knowingly quoting a price one does not intend to honour may be treated as a deceptive trade practice in many jurisdictions.
