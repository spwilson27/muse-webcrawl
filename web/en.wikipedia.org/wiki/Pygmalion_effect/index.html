Pygmalion effect
FieldSocial psychology, education
Named fromPygmalion, sculptor of Greek myth, via George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion
Key studyRosenthal & Jacobson, Pygmalion in the Classroom (1968)
Related conceptsSelf-fulfilling prophecy; expectancy effects; Golem effect; observer-expectancy effect

The Pygmalion effect is the phenomenon whereby higher expectations lead to an increase in performance: a person internalises the positive labels attached to them by others, or is treated in ways that make success more likely, and so succeeds in accordance with those expectations. It is a form of self-fulfilling prophecy, named after Pygmalion, the sculptor in Greek myth whose statue came to life. Its negative counterpart — lowered expectations leading to worse performance — is the Golem effect.

Rosenthal and Jacobson's study

The effect is named for the 1968 book Pygmalion in the Classroom by psychologist Robert Rosenthal and school principal Lenore Jacobson. In a California elementary school, pupils took a test presented to teachers as a predictor of intellectual "blooming"; a random selection of children were then named to teachers as being on the verge of unusual intellectual growth. Eight months later, the named children — especially in the youngest grades — showed significantly larger IQ gains than their classmates, despite the selection having been random. Rosenthal concluded that teachers' expectations had subtly altered their behaviour — more warmth, more challenging material, more chances to respond, and richer feedback — which the pupils converted into real gains.

Criticism and later research

The original study was criticised for flawed measurement and exaggerated effects; early replications were mixed. Meta-analyses of expectancy effects in classrooms find real but modest effects, strongest in early grades and in the first weeks of acquaintance. Related research documents expectancy effects in workplaces, the military and management ("Pygmalion in management"), and links them to stereotype-based expectations by race and class. Rosenthal's broader work on experimenter expectancy — the "Clever Hans" style leakage of expectations in laboratory research — helped establish double-blind designs as standard.
