Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beam theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beam theory, or simply Timoshenko beam theory, was developed by Stephen Timoshenko and Paul Ehrenfest in the early 20th century. It is a refinement of the classical Euler–Bernoulli beam theory that accounts for the effects of shear deformation and rotational inertia, making it suitable for describing the behaviour of short beams, sandwich composite beams and beams subject to high-frequency excitation.

Assumptions

Unlike the Euler–Bernoulli theory, in which cross-sections remain perpendicular to the deformed neutral axis, the Timoshenko theory allows the cross-section to rotate independently of the slope of the deflection curve. The difference between the two angles represents the average shear strain over the section, which is corrected by a shear coefficient (often denoted κ) that depends on the cross-sectional shape — for a rectangular section the value 5/6 is commonly used.

Governing equations

For a uniform beam, the theory leads to coupled partial differential equations relating the transverse deflection w(x,t) and the rotation of the cross-section φ(x,t), involving the bending stiffness EI, the shear stiffness κGA, the mass per unit length and the rotary inertia. In the limit of a very slender beam the shear term becomes negligible and the equations reduce to the Euler–Bernoulli result.

Applications

The theory is widely used in structural and mechanical engineering, in finite element formulations for frame structures, and in the analysis of thick beams and high-frequency vibration where classical theory underestimates deflection and overestimates natural frequencies.
