Bankruptcy in the United States is governed by federal law, principally the Bankruptcy Code enacted by the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 and codified in Title 11 of the United States Code. The Constitution empowers Congress to establish "uniform Laws on the subject of Bankruptcies throughout the United States". Cases are filed in the United States bankruptcy courts, specialised units of the federal district courts, and overseen by trustees appointed under the United States Trustee Program.

Chapters of the Bankruptcy Code

Each type of bankruptcy proceeding is identified by the chapter of the Code under which it is filed:

ChapterWho may fileSummary
Chapter 7Individuals, businessesLiquidation: a trustee sells non-exempt assets and distributes proceeds to creditors; individuals receive a discharge of most debts.
Chapter 9MunicipalitiesReorganisation of debts of cities, towns and other municipalities.
Chapter 11Businesses, individualsReorganisation under a court-approved plan; the debtor usually remains "in possession" and keeps operating.
Chapter 12Family farmers and fishermenRepayment plan tailored to seasonal income.
Chapter 13Individuals with regular income"Wage earner's plan": debts repaid in part over three to five years under court supervision.
Chapter 15Foreign debtorsAncillary proceedings for cross-border insolvencies.

Key features

Filing a petition immediately triggers an automatic stay, halting most collection actions, foreclosures and lawsuits. Certain debts are non-dischargeable, including most taxes, child support, alimony, and (with limited exceptions) student loans. Debtors may claim exemptions protecting some property — a homestead, a vehicle, tools of the trade — under state or federal lists. The 2005 Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (BAPCPA) introduced a means test steering higher-income individuals from Chapter 7 toward Chapter 13, and added credit-counselling requirements.

History

Early federal bankruptcy laws were temporary responses to financial panics (1800, 1841, 1867) and were repealed once the crisis passed. The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 established the first permanent framework; it was comprehensively replaced by the 1978 Code. Historically, individual filings peaked at over two million in 2005 ahead of BAPCPA's effective date; business Chapter 11 cases include many of the largest corporate restructurings in history, such as those of Enron, Lehman Brothers and General Motors.
