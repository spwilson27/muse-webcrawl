Corregidor

Location: Entrance to Manila Bay, Philippines
Area: about 5.5 km long, up to 2 km wide
Highest point: 182 m (Topside)

Corregidor is a tadpole-shaped island at the entrance to Manila Bay in the Philippines, about 48 kilometres west of Manila. Fortified as Fort Mills during the American colonial period, it is famous for its role in the Second World War, when its garrison held out against Japanese attack in 1941–42 and was retaken in 1945.

Geography and fortifications

The island is divided into Topside, the plateau carrying the main barracks and batteries; Middleside; and Bottomside, the low eastern tail where the docks and the town of San José stood. Pre-war defences included 12-inch guns and 12-inch mortars of Batteries Way, Geary, Hearn and others, anti-aircraft artillery, and the Malinta Tunnel, a bombproof complex driven through Malinta Hill that served as headquarters, hospital and storage.

Second World War

After the fall of Bataan in April 1942, Corregidor — "the Rock" — became the last Allied stronghold in the Philippines. General Douglas MacArthur had used it as his headquarters before escaping to Australia in March. Under relentless artillery and air bombardment from Bataan, the island's beach defences were overrun on the night of 5–6 May 1942, and Lieutenant General Jonathan Wainwright surrendered the remaining U.S. and Filipino forces in the Philippines. American paratroopers of the 503rd Parachute Infantry Regiment and infantry landed in February 1945 retook the island after fierce fighting, including desperate Japanese counterattacks and mass suicides from the cliffs.

Today

Corregidor is now a national shrine and tourist destination reached by ferry from Manila. Visitors see the ruined barracks, gun batteries, the Malinta Tunnel light-and-sound show, and the Pacific War Memorial with its dome, eternal flame and museum.
