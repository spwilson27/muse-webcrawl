Samuel Goudsmit

Samuel Abraham Goudsmit (July 11, 1902 – December 4, 1978) was a Dutch-American physicist famous jointly with George Uhlenbeck for proposing the concept of electron spin in 1925. He later served as the scientific head of the Alsos Mission during the Second World War and for many years as editor-in-chief of Physical Review.

Biography

Goudsmit studied physics at the University of Leiden under Paul Ehrenfest, receiving his doctorate in 1927. In 1925, while still a graduate student, he and George Uhlenbeck proposed that the electron possesses an intrinsic angular momentum — spin — and an associated magnetic moment, an idea that explained fine structure in atomic spectra and the anomalous Zeeman effect. The hypothesis, initially met with skepticism (including from the young authors themselves, who tried to withdraw the paper), was swiftly vindicated and became a cornerstone of quantum mechanics.

In 1927 Goudsmit emigrated to the United States to join the University of Michigan, where he worked on atomic spectroscopy. During the Second World War he worked on radar at MIT's Radiation Laboratory and then became scientific chief of the Alsos Mission, the Allied effort to determine the progress of the German nuclear weapons program; his 1947 book Alsos recounted the mission's finding that the German program had never come close to producing a bomb.

After the war Goudsmit moved to Brookhaven National Laboratory and became editor-in-chief of the American Physical Society's Physical Review (1951–1974), overseeing its growth into the leading physics journal of the era. He also founded Physical Review Letters in 1958. Later in life he developed an interest in Egyptology and forensic science, co-authoring work on the statistical detection of letter forgery.
