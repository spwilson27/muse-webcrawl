Arboretum

An arboretum (plural: arboreta) is a botanical collection composed exclusively of trees of a variety of species. Originally largely created as a section in a larger garden, on a large estate, or as a park, arboreta now serve scientific, educational and conservation purposes as living collections of woody plants. The study of trees in arboreta is called dendrology.

An arboretum specialising in conifers is known as a pinetum. Arboreta are related to but distinct from botanical gardens, which maintain broader collections including herbaceous plants.

History

The term was coined by the landscape writer John Claudius Loudon in 1833. One of the earliest public arboreta in England was created by the Loddiges nursery at Hackney in the 1810s; the Derby Arboretum, designed by Loudon and opened in 1840, was one of the first publicly owned parks in Britain. In the United States, the Arnold Arboretum of Harvard University, founded in 1872 and designed with Frederick Law Olmsted, became a model for the scientific arboretum.

Functions

Arboreta support research in taxonomy, forestry and horticulture; preserve rare and endangered tree species as genetic resources; provide reference specimens labelled with their scientific names for identification; and offer public recreation and education. Many arboreta hold nationally registered collections of particular genera, such as oaks, maples or magnolias.

Notable arboreta

  • Arnold Arboretum — Boston, Massachusetts, United States
  • Westonbirt Arboretum — Gloucestershire, England
  • National Arboretum — Canberra, Australia
  • United States National Arboretum — Washington, D.C.
  • The Morton Arboretum — Lisle, Illinois, United States
