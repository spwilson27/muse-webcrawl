Debt collection is the process of pursuing payments of money or other agreed value owed by an individual or business (the debtor) on behalf of a creditor. Creditors may collect debts themselves ("first-party" collection) or hire specialized collection agencies ("third-party" collection), and delinquent debts are often sold at a steep discount to debt buyers who then attempt to collect the full amount.

Process

Collection typically begins with reminders and dunning letters when an account becomes past due. If payment is not made, the account may be reported to credit bureaus, charged off by the original creditor, and placed with an agency or sold. Collectors may negotiate settlements for less than the full balance. As a last resort, a creditor or debt buyer may sue the debtor; a judgment can permit remedies such as wage garnishment, bank levies, or liens, depending on jurisdiction.

Regulation

In the United States, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA, 1977) restricts how third-party collectors may pursue consumer debts—prohibiting harassment, false statements, and calls at unreasonable hours, and giving debtors the right to request validation of the debt and to demand that communications cease. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau supervises larger collectors, and its Regulation F (effective 2021) modernized rules for electronic communications and call frequency. Other countries regulate collection through consumer-credit and insolvency law, and some require collector licensing.

Debt types and industry

Collected debts include credit card balances, medical bills, auto loans, mortgages, student loans, and unpaid commercial invoices. The industry is economically significant but controversial; critics point to suits over time-barred or poorly documented debts, while industry groups emphasize creditors' rights and the role of collection in keeping credit available and priced lower.
