Albert Namatjira (28 July 1902 – 8 August 1959), born Elea Namatjira, was an Aboriginal Australian artist of the Western Arrernte people of the MacDonnell Ranges region of the Northern Territory. His luminous watercolour landscapes of the central Australian desert made him one of Australia's most famous artists and the founder of the Hermannsburg School of painting, and his life became a symbol of both the possibilities and the injustices faced by Aboriginal Australians in the mid-20th century.

Early life

Namatjira was born at the Lutheran mission of Hermannsburg, west of Alice Springs, and raised on the mission. He worked as a blacksmith, stockman and camel driver, and developed skills in woodwork and traditional crafts before taking up painting in his thirties.

Career

In 1934 the visiting Melbourne artist Rex Battarbee exhibited watercolours at Hermannsburg, and Namatjira, after guiding Battarbee on painting trips in 1936, learned the medium with remarkable speed. His first solo exhibition, in Melbourne in 1938, sold out, and his paintings — shimmering depictions of ghost gums, red ranges and desert light — won wide acclaim. He was awarded the Queen's Coronation Medal in 1953, and reproductions of his works hung in homes across Australia.

Citizenship and final years

As an Aboriginal person under the protection legislation of the era, Namatjira was initially denied basic rights such as voting and buying alcohol. In 1957 he became one of the first Aboriginal people in the Northern Territory to be granted full Australian citizenship rights by exemption. The following year he was convicted of supplying alcohol to a community member — an offence only because of his race-linked restrictions — and served two months in prison after appeal. His health declined and he died of heart disease in Alice Springs in 1959.

The copyright in his works, sold by his estate's administrators for a small sum in 1983, was returned to the Namatjira family trust in 2017 after a long public campaign.
