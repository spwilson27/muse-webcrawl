Islamic holidays

Islamic holidays are days of celebration and commemoration observed by Muslims. The two official holidays in Islam are Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, both of which last several days and are public holidays in Muslim-majority countries. Because the Islamic calendar is a purely lunar calendar of about 354 days, the holidays shift earlier by about 11 days each solar year and cycle through all seasons over a period of about 33 years.

Other observances

  • Ramadan — the ninth month, observed by fasting from dawn to sunset; its last ten days include Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power).
  • Islamic New Year — 1 Muharram, commemorating the Hijra.
  • Ashura — 10 Muharram; for Shia Muslims a day of mourning for Husayn ibn Ali, killed at Karbala in 680 CE; also kept as a fast day by many Sunnis.
  • Mawlid — the birthday of Muhammad, 12 Rabi' al-awwal (observed by many, though not all, communities).
  • Isra and Mi'raj — 27 Rajab, the Prophet's Night Journey and ascension.
  • Mid-Sha'ban (Laylat al-Bara'a) — the night of 14–15 Sha'ban.
  • Day of Arafah — 9 Dhu al-Hijjah, the climax of the Hajj.

Celebration

On the Eids, Muslims attend special congregational prayers, wear new or best clothes, give charity (zakat al-fitr before Eid al-Fitr), visit family and friends, exchange greetings ("Eid Mubarak"), and share festive meals and sweets. During Eid al-Adha, those who can afford it sacrifice an animal and distribute the meat to family, friends, and the poor.
