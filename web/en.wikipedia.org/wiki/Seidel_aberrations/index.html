Seidel aberrations

The Seidel aberrations are the five primary monochromatic optical aberrations identified by Philipp Ludwig von Seidel in 1856. They arise in third-order (paraxial-plus) approximations of geometrical optics and describe the ways in which an imperfect lens or mirror deviates from ideal image formation for light of a single wavelength.

The five aberrations

Spherical aberration occurs because rays passing through the periphery of a spherical surface are focused at a different point than paraxial rays. Coma causes off-axis points to appear as comet-shaped blurs, because magnification varies across the aperture. Astigmatism arises when the system has different focal lengths in the tangential and sagittal planes, so a point forms two separated line images. Field curvature (Petzval field curvature) means the image is formed on a curved surface rather than a plane. Distortion changes magnification with field position, producing barrel or pincushion deformation of straight lines, though the image itself remains sharp.

Theory

Seidel expanded the wavefront error of an optical system in powers of the ray coordinates; the third-order terms correspond to the five aberrations, each quantified by a Seidel sum computed from surface-by-surface contributions. The theory underpins classical lens design: individual aberrations can be balanced or cancelled by choice of lens shapes, spacings, stop position, and glass types. Seidel aberrations exclude chromatic aberration, which results from dispersion, and higher-order (fifth- and seventh-order) aberrations that become important in fast or wide-field systems.
