Broadcast syndication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Broadcast syndication is the practice of leasing the right to broadcasting television shows and radio programs to multiple television stations or radio stations, without going through a broadcast network. It is a system developed in the United States, where programme schedules are largely built by local affiliate stations; it is much less common elsewhere in the world, where most stations are parts of national networks or groups. Three common types of syndication are first-run syndication (programmes produced specifically for sale into syndication), off-network syndication (re-runs of programmes first shown on a network), and public broadcasting syndication.

History

Syndication flourished in American radio during the 1930s and 1940s through electrical transcription discs distributed to independent stations. In early television, companies such as Ziv Television Programs made filmed series expressly for syndication, because the networks could not use all available production. The re-run market grew once series accumulated the roughly 100 episodes (later fewer) needed to "strip" a programme five days a week. Federal Communications Commission rules of the 1970s — the Prime Time Access Rule and the financial interest and syndication rules ("fin-syn") — reserved early-evening slots for local or syndicated programming and restricted the networks' ownership of syndicated content, greatly expanding the market; the fin-syn rules were repealed in the 1990s. Studios such as King World (distributor of Wheel of Fortune, Jeopardy! and The Oprah Winfrey Show) built major businesses on first-run syndication.

Economics

Syndicated programmes are sold market by market. In a cash deal the station pays a licence fee; in barter syndication the distributor gives the programme cheaply or free in exchange for retaining part of the advertising time, which it sells to national advertisers. Off-network hits could earn their producers enormous sums in syndication, which encouraged the production of long-running series; the prospect of syndication revenue historically influenced how many seasons networks kept shows alive. Cable television, home video and streaming have since reshaped the aftermarket for television programmes.

First-run staples

Daytime talk shows, court shows, entertainment news magazines and game shows have been the characteristic genres of first-run syndication, because they are inexpensive to produce daily and can run for decades: Wheel of Fortune and Jeopardy! have aired in syndication since 1983 and 1984 respectively, and The Oprah Winfrey Show (1986–2011) became the highest-rated talk show in American television history. Off-network syndication of sitcoms such as I Love Lucy, Seinfeld and Friends has likewise been a pillar of local and cable schedules.
