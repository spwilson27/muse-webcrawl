Banksia

Banksia is a genus of about 170–180 species of flowering plants in the family Proteaceae, almost all endemic to Australia. Named in honour of the botanist Sir Joseph Banks, who collected the first specimens at Botany Bay in 1770, banksias are icons of the Australian flora, easily recognised by their characteristic cylindrical flower spikes and fruiting cones.

Description

Banksias range from prostrate shrubs to trees up to 30 metres tall (notably Banksia integrifolia and B. seminuda). The flower spike consists of hundreds or thousands of individual flowers packed in a dense cylinder or dome; colours range from cream and yellow to orange, red, and purple-brown. After flowering, the spike develops into a woody cone studded with follicles that hold the seeds, in many species opening only after fire (serotiny). Many species possess a woody lignotuber allowing resprouting after bushfire.

Ecology

Banksias are cornerstone plants in their ecosystems, producing copious nectar that feeds honeyeater birds, mammals such as honey possums, and insects. The greatest diversity is in the sandplain heaths of southwestern Western Australia, a biodiversity hotspot. Threats include land clearing and root rot caused by the introduced pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi (dieback), to which many species are highly susceptible.

Taxonomy and uses

The genus was published by Carolus Linnaeus the Younger in 1782. In 2007 the related genus Dryandra was merged into Banksia on molecular evidence. Banksias are popular garden plants and long-lasting cut flowers, and feature prominently in Australian art and popular culture, including May Gibbs' "Banksia Men" villains in the Snugglepot and Cuddlepie stories.
