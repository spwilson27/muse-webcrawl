The Eleanor crosses were a series of twelve lavishly decorated stone memorial crosses erected in the 1290s by King Edward I of England in memory of his wife, Eleanor of Castile. Each cross marked one of the nightly resting places of the queen's funeral cortège as her body was carried from Harby in Nottinghamshire, where she died on 28 November 1290, to her burial at Westminster Abbey in London. The crosses combined architecture, sculpture and heraldry and were masterpieces of English Gothic art; three originals survive.

The journey and the crosses

The cortège stopped at Lincoln, Grantham, Stamford, Geddington, Hardingstone (Northampton), Stony Stratford, Woburn, Dunstable, St Albans, Waltham, Cheapside in London, and finally Charing — a name tradition derives from chère reine, "dear queen". At each place a tall cross was built in tiers, decorated with statues of Eleanor, coats of arms of England, Castile, León and Ponthieu, and surmounted by a cross. The most famous, the Charing Cross in London, stood at what is still regarded as the city's official centre; a Victorian replica now stands in front of Charing Cross station, and the site of the original is marked by the equestrian statue of Charles I.

Survivals

Only three crosses survive substantially intact: at Geddington (the best preserved, with a unique triangular plan), Hardingstone, and Waltham Cross. The others were destroyed, mostly during the Civil War era and the Commonwealth, when Parliament ordered such monuments demolished. Several were later replaced by replicas or memorials, including those at St Albans, Dunstable and Cheapside. The crosses also inspired the preaching and market crosses of later centuries and remain a striking public expression of royal grief and power.
