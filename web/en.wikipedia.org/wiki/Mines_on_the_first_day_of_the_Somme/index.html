LocationSomme front, Picardy, France

On the morning of 1 July 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme, the British Army detonated 19 mines beneath German front-line positions along the assault front in Picardy, France. Dug by the tunnelling companies of the Royal Engineers, the mines ranged from small charges intended to destroy strongpoints and collapse trenches to two of the largest mines of the First World War — the Lochnagar and Y Sap mines at La Boisselle — each packed with tens of thousands of pounds of ammonal.

The Lochnagar Crater, fired at 07:28 on 1 July 1916, is now a memorial.

Background

Mining had been conducted on the Western Front since late 1914. For the Somme offensive, mines were prepared along the front from Serre in the north to the French boundary in the south. Most were fired at 07:28, two minutes before zero hour (07:30), to avoid showering the assaulting infantry with debris; smaller charges near Serre and at other points were fired at slightly different times. The Hawthorn Ridge mine at Beaumont-Hamel, filmed famously by Geoffrey Malins, was fired at 07:20 — ten minutes early — a decision later criticised for warning the defenders.

The largest mines

  • Lochnagar mine (La Boisselle): two chambers with 60,000 lb of ammonal, forming a crater some 91 metres across and 21 metres deep; the largest crater of the war for many years, now preserved as a memorial.
  • Y Sap mine (La Boisselle): 40,600 lb of ammonal, fired alongside Lochnagar.
  • Hawthorn Ridge mine: 40,000 lb, re-laid in the same crater for the November 1916 attack on Beaumont-Hamel.
  • Glory Hole mines and others at La Boisselle, Fricourt (the "Tambour" mines), Mametz, Carnoy and Kasino Point.

Effect

The mines destroyed strongpoints and stunned defenders in places but produced mixed results: craters' lips could impede attackers, and at La Boisselle the assault on the village failed with heavy loss despite the two huge mines. German counter-mining had also destroyed or disrupted several British galleries before the attack. The surviving craters — Lochnagar above all — remain landmarks of the Somme battlefields.
