Grapevine Cross

The Grapevine Cross (Georgian: ჯვარი ვაზისა, Jvari Vazisa), also known as the Georgian cross or the Cross of Saint Nino, is a major symbol of the Georgian Orthodox Church and dates from the Christianization of Iberia (eastern Georgia) in the 4th century AD. The cross is characterized by its slightly drooping horizontal arms.

Tradition

According to tradition, the cross was made of grapevine branches and was given to Saint Nino, a Cappadocian woman who preached Christianity in Iberia, by the Virgin Mary. Nino is said to have bound the cross together with her own hair. King Mirian III, converted through Nino's ministry, adopted Christianity as the state religion of Iberia around 337 AD.

History

The original cross was kept for centuries at Svetitskhoveli Cathedral in Mtskheta. During invasions it was repeatedly moved for safekeeping, including to the mountain church of Stepantsminda (Gergeti Trinity Church). In the 18th century the cross was taken to Russia and was returned to Georgia in the early 19th century; it is now preserved at Sioni Cathedral in Tbilisi and is displayed on feast days.

Symbolism

The drooping arms and the vine-wood material evoke Georgia's ancient winemaking tradition and the biblical image of Christ as the true vine. The grapevine cross remains a ubiquitous emblem in Georgian churches, manuscripts, and iconography.
