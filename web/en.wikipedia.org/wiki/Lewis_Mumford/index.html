Lewis Mumford (October 19, 1895 – January 26, 1990) was an American historian, sociologist, philosopher of technology, and literary critic. Particularly noted for his study of cities and urban architecture, he was influential in urban planning and a prominent public intellectual of the mid-20th century.

Life and career

Mumford was born in Flushing, Queens, and studied at the City College of New York without taking a degree. Influenced by the Scottish biologist and planner Patrick Geddes, he became a founding figure of the Regional Planning Association of America and a prolific contributor to journals including The New Yorker, for which he wrote the "Sky Line" architecture column for over thirty years.

Major works

In Technics and Civilization (1934) Mumford traced the history of technology and introduced concepts such as the "megamachine." The Culture of Cities (1938) and its successor The City in History (1961) — which won the National Book Award — surveyed urban development from the medieval town to the modern metropolis and warned against uncontrolled sprawl, congestion, and the loss of human scale. His two-volume The Myth of the Machine (1967–1970) deepened his critique of authoritarian technics.

Views and legacy

Mumford championed regional planning, garden cities, and humane, pedestrian-scaled urban design, and he sharply criticised Robert Moses's highway-centred rebuilding of New York. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and Britain's Royal Gold Medal for architecture. His thought remains a reference point in debates about technology, urbanism, and the environment.
