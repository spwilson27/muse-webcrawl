International Labour Organization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The International Labour Organization (ILO) is a United Nations agency whose mandate is to advance social and economic justice by setting international labour standards. Founded in October 1919 under the League of Nations (Part XIII of the Treaty of Versailles), it survived to become the UN's first specialised agency in 1946. Uniquely among international organisations, it is tripartite: its governing structures seat governments, employers' organisations and workers' organisations as decision-makers together — reflecting its founding theory that labour peace is built by negotiating with labour, not merely about it.

Standards and work

The ILO sets conventions and recommendations (187 member states have ratified widely varying subsets), supervised by a Committee of Experts and conference committees whose findings drive national reform. Its 1998 Declaration elevated core standards to obligations of membership itself: freedom of association and collective bargaining, elimination of forced labour, abolition of child labour, non-discrimination, and (added 2022) a safe and healthy working environment. The ILO won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1969 and publishes the field's standard research (world employment and social outlook reports, global child-labour estimates with UNICEF). Its 2019 centenary convention on violence and harassment at work illustrates its continuing norm-entrepreneurship; enforcement remains persuasive rather than coercive, the standing bargain of international labour law.
