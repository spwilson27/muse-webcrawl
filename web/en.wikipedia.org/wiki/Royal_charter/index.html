Royal charter

A royal charter is a formal grant issued by a monarch under royal prerogative as letters patent, granting a right or power to an individual or a body corporate, and creating in the process a chartered company or organisation. In the United Kingdom, royal charters are granted by the King in Council on the advice of the Privy Council. They have historically been used to incorporate cities, universities, learned societies, charities and trading companies, and to confer privileges such as monopolies and self-governing status.

History

Charters have been issued in Europe since medieval times. In England they were the principal means by which boroughs gained legal personality and trading rights; towns paid substantial sums for charters confirming their liberties. During the age of exploration and colonisation, chartered companies such as the East India Company (chartered in 1600), the Hudson's Bay Company (1670) and the British South Africa Company (1889) were granted extensive powers to trade, govern territory and maintain armed forces on behalf of the Crown.

Universities and professional bodies were likewise incorporated by charter: the Royal Society received its charters in the 1660s, and institutions such as the Royal Academy of Arts and many livery companies trace their existence to royal grants. The BBC is incorporated by a periodically renewed royal charter, which underpins its formal independence and sets out its public purposes.

Modern practice

Today a royal charter is regarded as a prestigious form of incorporation, normally reserved for bodies that work in the public interest, demonstrate excellence in their field, and are not adequately served by other forms of incorporation. Chartered status gives professional bodies the right to confer titles such as Chartered Accountant or Chartered Engineer. Amendments to a charter generally require Privy Council approval. In 2013 a royal charter on self-regulation of the press was granted following the Leveson Inquiry.
