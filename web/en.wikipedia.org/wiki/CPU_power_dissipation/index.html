CPU power dissipation

FieldComputer engineering
RelatedThermal design power, Dynamic voltage scaling

CPU power dissipation is the process in which central processing units (CPUs) consume electrical energy and convert it into heat. Managing this dissipation is a central problem in computer engineering, since the heat generated must be removed by cooling systems to keep the processor within safe operating temperatures.

The power consumed by a CMOS processor is dominated by dynamic power, which is roughly proportional to the product of capacitance, the square of the supply voltage, and the clock frequency. This relationship explains why raising clock speeds and voltages sharply increases heat output.

Dynamic and static power

Dynamic power arises from charging and discharging the capacitances of transistor gates as they switch. Static power, by contrast, comes from leakage currents that flow even when transistors are nominally off, and it has grown more significant as transistors have shrunk.

Techniques such as clock gating, power gating, and dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) are used to reduce both components, shutting down or slowing parts of the chip that are not in use.

Thermal design power

Thermal design power (TDP) is the maximum amount of heat a cooling system is expected to dissipate under a realistic workload. It guides the design of heatsinks, fans, and liquid cooling systems, though actual power draw can exceed TDP briefly during turbo boost operation.

Consequences

Excessive power dissipation limits performance through thermal throttling, increases energy costs in data centres, and shortens component lifetimes. The end of Dennard scaling in the mid-2000s made power, rather than transistor count, the primary constraint on processor performance growth.
