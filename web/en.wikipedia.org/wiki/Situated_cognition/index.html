Situated cognition

Situated cognition is a theory of learning that emphasises that knowing is inseparable from doing, and that all knowledge is situated in activity bound to social, cultural and physical contexts. It challenges the assumption — common to traditional cognitive science — that learning consists in the transfer of abstract, decontextualised knowledge from one mind to another through symbols.

Origins

The theory grew out of work in anthropology and cognitive science in the 1980s, including Jean Lave's studies of everyday arithmetic among shoppers and tailors (Cognition in Practice, 1988) and the 1989 paper "Situated Cognition and the Culture of Learning" by John Seely Brown, Allan Collins and Paul Duguid. Drawing also on Lev Vygotsky's cultural-historical psychology, situated cognition argues that concepts are like tools: they can only be fully understood through use, and using them entails adopting the worldview of the community that uses them.

Communities of practice

Lave and Étienne Wenger developed the related ideas of legitimate peripheral participation and communities of practice: newcomers learn by participating in real tasks of a community, moving gradually from the periphery toward full participation. This model motivated educational approaches such as cognitive apprenticeship, in which expert practice is made visible and learners are coached in authentic activity rather than drilled on abstract rules.

Criticism

Critics have argued that the strong version of the claim — that knowledge cannot transfer between contexts at all — is empirically overstated, since people demonstrably do apply school learning elsewhere. Situated cognition is nonetheless influential in education, organisational learning, and human–computer interaction.
