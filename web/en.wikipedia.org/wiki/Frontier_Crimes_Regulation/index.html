Frontier Crimes Regulation

Under the FCR, justice was administered through jirgas overseen by political agents.

The Frontier Crimes Regulation (FCR) was a legal code enacted by the British Raj in 1901 — building on regulations of 1872 and 1887 — to govern the tribal territories along the North-West Frontier of British India, in what became Pakistan's Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA). It remained in force, with amendments, until FATA was merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province in 2018, when the FCR was repealed.

Provisions

The FCR vested sweeping powers in the Political Agent, the colonial administrator, who combined executive and judicial authority. Criminal and civil cases were decided not by regular courts but through a jirga, a council of tribal elders whose findings the Agent could accept, modify or disregard. The code's most notorious feature was collective responsibility: an entire tribe or family could be fined, blockaded or have its property seized for the crimes of an individual member. Appeals to the regular judiciary were barred, and the accused had no right to a lawyer, to present evidence in the ordinary way, or to appeal to a higher court.

Criticism and repeal

The regulation was long condemned by human rights groups, lawyers and the people of FATA for denying fundamental rights. Amendments in 2011 softened some collective-punishment provisions, but campaigners demanded full repeal. The 25th Amendment to Pakistan's constitution, passed in May 2018, merged FATA into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, extended the jurisdiction of the regular courts and formally ended the FCR regime after more than a century.
