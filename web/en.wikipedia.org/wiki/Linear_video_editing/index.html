Linear video editing is a video editing process that involves selecting, arranging and modifying images and sound in a predetermined, ordered sequence. Regardless of changes, the finished video is built sequentially, from start to finish, by copying material from one or more source videotapes onto a record (master) tape. Because material is laid down in order, altering a shot early in a programme requires re-editing everything that follows it — the defining limitation of the linear method.

Technique

In a typical suite, an editor used an edit controller to synchronise source videotape recorders with a record machine, marking in- and out-points on the source tapes. Two modes were used: assemble editing, which lays down all tracks (video, audio and control track) continuously, and insert editing, which replaces selected tracks within an existing recording. Using two source machines (A/B-roll editing) allowed transitions such as dissolves and wipes between shots, synchronised via a video switcher. Sessions were planned on paper and later recorded in an edit decision list (EDL), a list of edits by timecode that could be saved, modified and used to re-conform a programme automatically, for example when an offline edit made on low-cost equipment was rebuilt ("conformed") in a high-end online suite.

History

Videotape was introduced by Ampex in 1956; the earliest editing was physical splicing, soon replaced by electronic editing as machines gained precise servo control and timecode. Professional formats such as 2-inch quadruplex, 1-inch Type C and Betacam SP defined successive generations of linear suites, which dominated television post-production from the 1960s to the 1990s. From the late 1980s, computer-based non-linear editing systems offered random access, instant rearrangement and undo, and by the 2000s linear tape editing had largely disappeared from mainstream production, surviving mainly in archives and some live broadcast workflows.

Comparison with non-linear editing

Linear editing is destructive (each new generation of tape copying loses quality), slow (machines must shuttle to each edit), and rigid in sequence; its advantages were simplicity, robustness, and predictable real-time performance. Non-linear systems removed these limits by treating footage as files, making linear editing obsolete for most purposes.
