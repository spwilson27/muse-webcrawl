Financial transaction tax

A financial transaction tax (FTT) is a levy on a specific type of financial transaction for a particular purpose. The concept is most commonly associated with the financial sector, where it taxes transactions such as the purchase and sale of shares, bonds, currencies, and derivatives, usually at a very low rate — hundredths or tenths of a percent — applied to a large base.

History and proposals

John Maynard Keynes proposed a transfer tax on share transactions in The General Theory (1936) to discourage speculation. In 1972, James Tobin proposed a small tax on foreign-exchange transactions — the Tobin tax — to "throw some sand in the wheels" of currency speculation. After the 2007–2008 financial crisis, interest revived: in 2011 the European Commission proposed an EU-wide FTT, and a group of member states pursued an enhanced-cooperation version, though agreement proved elusive for years. Campaigners such as the "Robin Hood tax" movement have advocated FTTs to fund development and climate action.

Existing taxes

Several countries operate forms of FTT. The United Kingdom's Stamp Duty Reserve Tax charges 0.5% on purchases of UK shares. France introduced an FTT in 2012 (later 0.3%) on purchases of large French companies' shares, and Italy and Spain have similar taxes. India levies a Securities Transaction Tax, and Hong Kong and other markets charge stamp duties on share transfers.

Arguments

Supporters argue that an FTT raises substantial revenue from a sector that is lightly taxed (financial services are exempt from VAT in the EU), discourages short-term speculation and high-frequency trading, and can reduce excessive volatility. Critics argue the cost is ultimately borne by savers and pension funds, that it reduces market liquidity and price discovery, widens spreads, may increase volatility, and can drive trading to untaxed jurisdictions. Sweden's FTT of 1984–1991 is frequently cited: trading migrated abroad, revenues were far below forecast, and the tax was abolished. Empirical assessments generally find that an FTT's effects depend heavily on design — the tax base, rate, exemptions for market makers, and the scope for relocation.
