Mexican textiles

Mexican textiles (Spanish: textiles mexicanos) have a history going back at least 5,000 years, to the beginnings of agriculture and sedentary life in Mesoamerica. Before the Spanish conquest, textiles made of cotton and ixtle (agave fiber) were central to the economy — cloth was paid as tribute to the Aztec Empire — and weaving on the backstrap loom was highly developed, with complex brocade, gauze, and embroidery techniques.

History

The Spanish introduced wool, silk, the treadle (European) loom, the spinning wheel, and new techniques such as the embroidery stitches used in colonial garments, as well as large workshop (obraje) production. Indigenous weaving survived especially in remote communities, where garments continued to mark ethnic identity, community membership, marital status, and social rank.

In the 20th and 21st centuries, traditional Mexican textiles have been collected and promoted by museums such as the Museo Textil de Oaxaca and the Museo Franz Mayer in Mexico City, and popularized internationally by figures such as Frida Kahlo, who famously wore indigenous garments. There have also been recurring controversies over foreign fashion brands copying indigenous designs, prompting Mexico to pass a 2022 federal law protecting the cultural heritage of indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples.

Techniques and garments

The backstrap loom (telar de cintura) remains the emblematic pre-Hispanic technique, used to weave huipiles — the loose, tunic-like garments worn by indigenous women, whose designs vary distinctively by community, as in Chiapas (e.g., Zinacantán, San Juan Chamula), Oaxaca (e.g., the Tehuana dress of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec), and among the Purépecha of Michoacán. Other characteristic items include the rebozo (a long woven shawl used also as a baby carrier), the sarape or Saltillo blanket, the quechquémetl, and woven belts (fajas).

Natural dyes — indigo for blue, cochineal insects for reds, and the marine snail Plicopurpura pansa for purple among the Mixtec of the Oaxaca coast — are prized survivals of pre-Hispanic technology. Embroidery traditions include the colorful tenangos of Tenango de Doria, Hidalgo (a style developed in the 1960s), the cross-stitch of Yucatán's hipiles, and the floral embroidery of Puebla's china poblana dress.

Contemporary production

Textile production remains an important source of income, especially for indigenous women, organized through cooperatives and sold in markets and through fair-trade channels. Major textile centers include Teotitlán del Valle, Oaxaca (treadle-loom wool rugs, or tapetes), and the highland towns of Chiapas. The sector faces pressure from cheap machine-made imitations, including imports that copy traditional motifs.
