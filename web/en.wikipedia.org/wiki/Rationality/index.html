Rationality


Rationality concerns the quality of reasoning and decision-making.

Rationality is the quality of being guided by or based on reasons. In this regard, a person acts rationally if they have a good reason for what they do, or a belief is rational if it is based on strong evidence. Rationality is central to philosophy, economics, psychology, and cognitive science, and is often contrasted with emotion, superstition, and bias.

Types

Theoretical rationality concerns beliefs: a belief is rational if it is proportioned to the evidence (epistemic rationality). Practical rationality concerns action: an action is rational if it best serves the agent's goals given their beliefs. Instrumental rationality is the efficient pursuit of given goals, while debates about substantive rationality ask whether the goals themselves can be rational or irrational.

Bounded rationality

Herbert A. Simon proposed the concept of bounded rationality: humans decide under constraints of limited information, time, and cognitive capacity, and therefore "satisfice" — choose good-enough options — rather than optimize. Research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky documented systematic cognitive biases that cause deviations from rational norms.

In economics

Classical economic models assume rational agents ("homo economicus") who maximize utility. Behavioral economics relaxes these assumptions to account for observed irrationalities.
