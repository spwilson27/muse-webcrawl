Impossible object

An impossible object (also known as an impossible figure or an undecidable figure) is a type of optical illusion consisting of a two-dimensional drawing that the visual system interprets as a three-dimensional object which could not exist in ordinary Euclidean space. Each part of the figure appears plausible in isolation, but the whole is geometrically inconsistent. Impossible objects are of interest to psychologists, mathematicians, and artists.

The Penrose triangle, the best-known impossible object.

History

The Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd created the first deliberate impossible figures in 1934, including a triangle built from cubes. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher popularised the theme in lithographs such as Ascending and Descending (1960), with its endless staircase, and Waterfall (1961). Independently, the physicist Roger Penrose and his father, the psychiatrist Lionel Penrose, described the impossible triangle and the Penrose stairs in the 1950s; Escher corresponded with them and incorporated their designs into his work.

Famous examples

  • The Penrose triangle (tribar), a triangular beam that appears to join up impossibly.
  • The Penrose stairs, a staircase that ascends forever in a loop.
  • The blivet (poiuyt or devil's tuning fork), a two-pronged object that appears to have three cylindrical prongs at one end.
  • The Necker cube and other ambiguous figures, whose depth interpretation flips.

Construction

Although impossible in ordinary space, sculptures can be built that look like impossible objects from one carefully chosen viewpoint, using warped or disjoint pieces whose true shapes are concealed. Such works stand in science museums and public spaces around the world. Research in visual perception uses impossible figures to probe how the brain assembles local depth cues into a global interpretation of a scene.
