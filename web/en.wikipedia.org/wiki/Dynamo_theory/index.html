Dynamo theory

In physics, the dynamo theory proposes a mechanism by which a celestial body such as Earth or a star generates a magnetic field. The dynamo theory describes the process through which a rotating, convecting, and electrically conducting fluid acts to maintain a magnetic field over astronomical time scales.

Mechanism

A self-sustaining dynamo requires a sea of conducting fluid (in Earth, liquid iron in the outer core), rotation, and an internal heat source driving convection. Moving conductor stretches and twists magnetic field lines: differential rotation winds poloidal field into toroidal field (the ω-effect), while helical convective motions regenerate poloidal field from toroidal field (the α-effect). If the magnetic Reynolds number is large enough, field generation outpaces ohmic decay and the field sustains itself. The theory is governed by the magnetohydrodynamic induction equation.

The geodynamo

Earth's field has existed for at least 3.5 billion years and undergoes irregular reversals of polarity. Numerical simulations (notably the Glatzmaier–Roberts model of 1995) reproduce a dipole-dominated field and spontaneous reversals. Joseph Larmor first proposed a solar dynamo in 1919; Walter Elsasser and Edward Bullard developed the terrestrial theory in the 1940s. Cowling's theorem (1933) proved a purely axisymmetric field cannot be maintained by axisymmetric motion, so real dynamos are inherently three-dimensional.

Applications

Dynamo theory applies to the Sun's 22-year magnetic cycle, to planetary fields such as Jupiter's and Mercury's, and to laboratory liquid-sodium experiments that have achieved self-excitation.
