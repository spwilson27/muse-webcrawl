Congregación

In the history of Spanish colonial America, a congregación (also called a reducción in some contexts) was a resettlement policy under which dispersed Indigenous populations were forcibly concentrated into new, planned towns. Implemented most extensively in New Spain (Mexico) in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, the congregaciones reorganised settlement patterns that had been disrupted by epidemic disease, war, and earlier colonial policies.

Purposes

Colonial authorities and the Church promoted congregación for several overlapping goals: to facilitate evangelisation by gathering converts around a church and resident clergy; to make tribute collection, labour drafts, and administration easier; to "civilise" Indigenous peoples by imposing Spanish-style town life with a grid plan, plaza, church, and cabildo (town council); and to free up land formerly occupied by scattered settlements. The policy was carried out comprehensively in the Valley of Mexico, Michoacán, Oaxaca, and other regions, typically between about 1593 and 1605 under viceregal orders.

Consequences

Resettlement was often traumatic. Forced removals separated communities from ancestral lands, shrines, and fields; crowding in the new towns worsened mortality from epidemic disease; and some congregaciones failed as residents fled back to their original settlements or to remote areas. At the same time, many congregated towns endured and became the basis of present-day Mexican municipalities, and Indigenous communities used the town institutions — cabildos, communal land titles, and cofradías — to defend their interests within the colonial order. The related system of reducciones was applied in the Andes and, in a distinctive mission form, among the Guaraní of Paraguay.
