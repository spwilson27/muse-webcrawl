Nullarbor Plain

The Nullarbor Plain is a vast, flat, arid region of southern Australia straddling the border of South Australia and Western Australia along the Great Australian Bight. Covering roughly 200,000 square kilometres, it is the world's largest single exposure of limestone bedrock. The name derives from the Latin nullus arbor, "no tree", reflecting its almost treeless surface of low bluebush and saltbush scrub.

Geography and geology

The plain is part of the ancient Eucla Basin, formed from marine limestone laid down when the area lay beneath the sea tens of millions of years ago. Its remarkably flat surface ends abruptly in the south at the Bunda Cliffs, which drop up to about 100 metres into the Southern Ocean. Beneath the plain lie extensive cave systems and blowholes, including the Weebubbie and Koonalda caves; fossil finds there include the marsupial lion Thylacoleo.

Crossing the plain

The first European crossing was made by Edward John Eyre in 1840–41. Today the plain is crossed by the Eyre Highway and by the Trans-Australian Railway, which contains the longest straight stretch of railway line in the world — 478 kilometres without a curve. Roadhouses spaced along the highway serve travellers on the roughly 1,200-kilometre treeless traverse.

Climate and ecology

The climate is arid to semi-arid, with hot summers and scant, unreliable rainfall. Wildlife includes southern hairy-nosed wombats, dingoes, wedge-tailed eagles, and large numbers of feral camels. Much of the South Australian portion is protected in the Nullarbor Regional Reserve and national parks.
