Administrative law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Administrative law is the division of law that governs the activities of executive branch agencies of government. It concerns the powers and procedures of administrative agencies — rulemaking, adjudication and enforcement of regulatory agendas — and the means by which citizens can challenge agency action, principally through judicial review of its legality.

Content

Because legislatures cannot legislate for every detail, they delegate: agencies issue delegated legislation and regulations (on environment, food safety, finance, broadcasting and much else), grant licences and benefits, and conduct hearings through tribunals. Administrative law disciplines this power with requirements of legality, procedural fairness (notice and a hearing), reasonableness or proportionality of decisions, and openness (freedom-of-information laws, consultation duties). In the United States the Administrative Procedure Act (1946) is foundational; in the United Kingdom, judicial review doctrines of illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety frame the field; France maintains a separate administrative court system headed by the Conseil d'État.

Significance

Often called the law of the modern regulatory and welfare state, administrative law mediates most citizens' routine contact with government — taxes, benefits, planning, immigration — and is a principal arena for debates about technocracy, accountability and the "administrative state" itself.
