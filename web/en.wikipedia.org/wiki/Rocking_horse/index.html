Rocking horse

TypeChildren's toy
MaterialsWood (traditionally), later metal, plastic, plush
Origins17th-century England (bow rocker form)

A rocking horse is a child's toy, usually in the shape of a horse, mounted on rockers similar to those of a rocking chair. A rider sits astride it and rocks back and forth, imitating the motion of riding. Rocking horses developed from earlier hobby horses and became a nursery staple in the 18th and 19th centuries, especially in Britain and the United States.

History

The earliest rocking horses date from the early 17th century, when the horse was mounted on a convex "bow rocker" — a single curved base like a boat hull, which required a large floor area and could tip or "travel" across the room. Victorian makers produced elaborately carved and painted "dapple grey" horses with real horsehair manes and tails, leather saddles and bridles; firms such as F. H. Ayres of London and Lines Bros. became famous. In 1878–1880 the American Philip Marqua of Cincinnati patented the safety stand or "swing iron" horse, which swings on an A-frame trestle with parallel irons, taking less space and greatly reducing tipping — this design largely replaced bow rockers, especially in America.

Construction

Traditional rocking horses were carved from laminated pine or other woods, coated in gesso, and painted, often in a realistic dappled-grey finish; the best examples were made to order in sizes from small nursery models to extra-large horses intended for older children. Antique examples by Ayres, Lines (Sportiboy), and others are highly collectible; specialist restorers and modern makers continue the craft.

Cultural presence

The rocking horse appears throughout children's literature, from the Skin Horse in The Velveteen Rabbit (1922) to Paul in D. H. Lawrence's story "The Rocking-Horse Winner" (1926). A giant rocking horse in Gumeracha, South Australia — part of a complex with a toy factory — is one of Australia's "big things" tourist attractions.
