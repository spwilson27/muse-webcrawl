Proton–proton chain

The proton–proton chain, also commonly referred to as the p–p chain, is one of two known sets of nuclear fusion reactions by which stars convert hydrogen to helium. It dominates in stars with masses less than or equal to that of the Sun, while the CNO cycle dominates in more massive stars.

Overview

In general, proton–proton fusion can occur only if the temperature (kinetic energy) of the protons is high enough to overcome their mutual Coulomb repulsion. The theory that proton–proton reactions are the basic principle by which the Sun and other stars burn was first worked out by Arthur Eddington in the 1920s and refined by Hans Bethe and others in the 1930s.

The PP I branch

The first step involves the fusion of two hydrogen nuclei (protons) into deuterium, releasing a positron and an electron neutrino as one proton converts into a neutron. The positron immediately annihilates with an electron, releasing energy as gamma rays. The deuterium nucleus then fuses with another proton to form helium-3, and finally two helium-3 nuclei fuse to give helium-4 plus two protons. The net result converts four protons into one helium-4 nucleus, two positrons, two electron neutrinos and about 26.7 MeV of energy.

Other branches

At higher temperatures the PP II and PP III branches become significant, passing through beryllium-7 and (rarely) boron-8, whose decay produces the high-energy neutrinos detected by experiments such as Sudbury Neutrino Observatory and Borexino. In the Sun, the PP I branch accounts for about 83 per cent of reactions.

Timescales

The first fusion step is extremely slow — an average proton in the Sun's core waits about 9 billion years before fusing — because it depends on the weak interaction converting a proton to a neutron during the brief encounter. This slowness is what gives the Sun its long, stable main-sequence lifetime.
