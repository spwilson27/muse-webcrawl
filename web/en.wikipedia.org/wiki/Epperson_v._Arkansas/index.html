Epperson v. Arkansas

Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97 (1968), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that states may not prohibit the teaching of the theory of evolution in public schools. The Court ruled that Arkansas's 1928 anti-evolution statute violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment because its purpose was to protect a particular religious view.

Background

After the 1925 Scopes trial, Arkansas voters adopted a law making it unlawful for a teacher in a state-funded school "to teach the theory or doctrine that mankind ascended or descended from a lower order of animals". In 1965, Little Rock biology teacher Susan Epperson challenged the law after being expected to use a new textbook containing a chapter on evolution. The Arkansas Supreme Court upheld the statute in a terse two-sentence opinion.

Decision

In a unanimous opinion by Justice Abe Fortas, the Court struck down the law: "The overriding fact is that Arkansas' law selects from the body of knowledge a particular segment which it proscribes for the sole reason that it is deemed to conflict with a particular religious doctrine." The decision ended the era of statutory bans on teaching evolution and set the stage for later cases striking down "balanced treatment" and creation-science laws, such as Edwards v. Aguillard (1987).
