An aqueduct is a watercourse constructed to carry water from a source to a distant distribution point. In modern engineering, aqueducts may be large pipelines, canals or tunnels; in historical terms the word often refers to the monumental arcade bridges built by the Roman Empire to maintain a gentle gradient across valleys.

Ancient aqueducts

Early aqueducts were built by the Assyrians, Egyptians and the peoples of the Indus valley. The Romans perfected the form: by the 3rd century BC the Aqua Appia supplied Rome, and eventually eleven aqueducts delivered over a million cubic metres of water daily to the city, carried largely underground but raised on arched bridges — arcades — where the terrain demanded, as at the Pont du Gard in France and the Aqueduct of Segovia in Spain.

Modern aqueducts

Modern systems such as the California Aqueduct and the Catskill Aqueduct move water hundreds of kilometres to cities and farms, using siphons, pumping stations and lined canals. Navigable aqueducts carry canal boats across rivers and are treated separately as bridge structures.
