In the mathematical field of graph theory, the Petersen graph is an undirected graph with 10 vertices and 15 edges. It is a small graph that serves as a useful example and counterexample for many problems in graph theory. The Petersen graph is named after Julius Petersen, who in 1898 constructed it as the smallest bridgeless cubic graph that cannot be edge-coloured with three colours.

The graph is commonly drawn as a pentagon with a five-point star (pentagram) inside it, with corresponding vertices joined. It can also be described as the Kneser graph KG5,2: its vertices are the ten 2-element subsets of a 5-element set, with edges joining disjoint subsets.

Properties

The Petersen graph is cubic (3-regular) and strongly regular. It is the smallest snark: a bridgeless cubic graph that is not 3-edge-colourable. Although it has a Hamiltonian path, it is not Hamiltonian — deleting any vertex leaves a Hamiltonian graph, so it is hypohamiltonian. Its automorphism group is the symmetric group S5, of order 120.

History

Petersen published the graph in 1898, although A. B. Kempe had described essentially the same configuration a dozen years earlier in connection with the four-colour problem. Donald Knuth has called it "a remarkable configuration that serves as a counterexample to many optimistic predictions about what might be true for graphs in general".
