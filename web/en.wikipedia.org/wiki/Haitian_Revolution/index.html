Haitian Revolution
Date: 14 August 1791 – 1 January 1804
Location: Saint-Domingue (Haiti)
Result: Haitian victory; abolition of slavery; independence from France
Key figures: Toussaint Louverture, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, André Rigaud, Henri Christophe

The Haitian Revolution (French: Révolution haïtienne) was a successful insurrection by enslaved Africans and their descendants against French colonial rule in Saint-Domingue, then France's richest colony. Beginning in 1791, the uprising culminated in the elimination of slavery and the founding of the Republic of Haiti on 1 January 1804. It is the only slave revolt in history that led to the founding of a state, and it is generally considered the most successful slave rebellion in the Americas.

Background

By 1789 Saint-Domingue produced roughly half of the world's sugar and a large share of its coffee on plantations worked by some half a million enslaved Africans, who vastly outnumbered the white colonists and free people of colour combined. Conditions were exceptionally brutal, and the death rate among the enslaved was so high that the labour force had to be constantly replaced by new arrivals from Africa. News of the French Revolution of 1789, with its Declaration of the Rights of Man, destabilised the colony's racial hierarchy: free people of colour demanded equal rights, white planters sought autonomy, and the enslaved seized the opening.

The uprising

According to tradition, a Vodou ceremony at Bois Caïman on 14 August 1791, led by the priest Dutty Boukman and the priestess Cécile Fatiman, launched the rebellion. In the last week of August the enslaved rose across the Northern Plain, burning plantations and killing colonists. Among the leaders who emerged was Toussaint Louverture, a freedman who built a disciplined army, at times allied with Spain against France.

In 1793 the French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax proclaimed the abolition of slavery in Saint-Domingue to rally the insurgents to the Republic; the National Convention extended abolition to all French colonies on 4 February 1794. Louverture switched his allegiance to France, defeated British and Spanish forces, and by 1801 had become governor-general of a largely autonomous Saint-Domingue, promulgating a constitution that abolished slavery forever while retaining a nominal link to France.

Independence

Napoleon Bonaparte, determined to restore slavery and French authority, sent a large expedition under his brother-in-law Charles Leclerc in 1801–02. Louverture was arrested in 1802 and deported to France, where he died at Fort de Joux in April 1803. French brutality and attempts to disarm the population drove former allies together; under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, and with yellow fever devastating the French army, the insurgents won the decisive Battle of Vertières near Cap-Haïtien on 18 November 1803. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines proclaimed the independence of the new state, restoring the Taíno name Ayiti — Haiti, "land of high mountains".

Legacy

The revolution sent shock waves through the Atlantic world: it destroyed France's American colonial ambitions and contributed to Napoleon's decision to sell Louisiana to the United States in 1803, while terrifing slave-holding societies and inspiring the enslaved elsewhere. France refused to recognise Haitian independence until 1825, and then only in exchange for a large indemnity, whose servicing drained Haiti's treasury for more than a century. The United States did not recognise Haiti until 1862. Historians including C. L. R. James, author of The Black Jacobins (1938), have emphasised the revolution's place in the history of abolition and universal human rights.
