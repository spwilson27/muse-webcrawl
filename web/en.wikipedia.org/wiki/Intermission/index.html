Intermission

An intermission, also known as an interval in British usage, is a recess between parts of a performance or production, such as a theatrical play, opera, concert, or film screening. Typically lasting 10 to 25 minutes, it allows the audience to leave their seats, use facilities, and buy refreshments, and gives performers and stage crew time to rest, change costumes, or reset scenery. In British theatre the interval is also a traditional point for selling ice cream.

Audiences use the interval to stretch, talk, and buy refreshments.

History and practice

The convention grew with multi-act plays and long operas in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; entr'acte music — pieces performed between acts — is named from this practice. In cinema, intermissions were built into long roadshow presentations of epic films such as Lawrence of Arabia and 2001: A Space Odyssey, often with overture and entr'acte music, and they remain customary in Indian cinema, where films are commonly structured in two halves around the interval. Many modern Western cinemas and shorter plays dispense with intermissions entirely, and some productions deliberately run without one to preserve tension.

In drama

Playwrights design acts around the interval, often ending the pre-intermission portion on a climax or revelation. A bell or announcement typically recalls the audience before the performance resumes. Streaming and recorded media have borrowed the term for built-in breaks, though these are increasingly rare.
