Friedreich's ataxia

Friedreich's ataxia (FRDA or FA) is an autosomal-recessive genetic disease that causes progressive damage to the nervous system, resulting in gait disturbance (ataxia), impaired coordination, and often heart disease and diabetes. It is the most common inherited ataxia, affecting roughly 1 in 50,000 people. The condition is named after the German physician Nikolaus Friedreich, who described it in the 1860s.

Cause

Friedreich's ataxia is caused by an expanded GAA trinucleotide repeat in the first intron of the FXN gene, which reduces production of the mitochondrial protein frataxin. Frataxin deficiency disrupts iron–sulfur cluster biogenesis, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, iron accumulation, and oxidative damage, particularly in dorsal root ganglia, the spinal cord, the cerebellum, and cardiac muscle.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin in childhood or adolescence with unsteady gait and frequent falls, progressing to dysarthria (slurred speech), loss of deep tendon reflexes, impaired vibration and position sense, and weakness; most patients require a wheelchair within 10–15 years of onset. Non-neurological features include hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (a leading cause of death), scoliosis, pes cavus (high-arched feet), and diabetes mellitus in a substantial minority.

Treatment

There is no cure; management is supportive, with physical therapy, treatment of cardiac disease and diabetes, and surgical correction of scoliosis or foot deformities. In 2023 omaveloxolone (Skyclarys) became the first drug approved in the United States specifically for Friedreich's ataxia, for patients aged 16 and older. Antioxidants such as idebenone have been studied with mixed results.
