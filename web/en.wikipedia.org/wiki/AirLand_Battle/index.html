AirLand Battle was the operational doctrine of the United States Army during the final decade of the Cold War. Published in the 1982 edition of Field Manual 100-5, Operations, and refined in 1986, it replaced the brief "Active Defense" doctrine of 1976 and oriented the Army toward offensive, maneuver-oriented warfare against the numerically superior forces of the Warsaw Pact in Europe.

Development

After the Vietnam War, the Army re-focused on the defense of Western Europe. The 1976 FM 100-5, drafted under General William DePuy, emphasized attrition and defending forward positions. Critics argued it was too defensive and ignored Soviet echelonment — the practice of attacking in successive waves. Under General Donn A. Starry at the Training and Doctrine Command, planners developed a concept that would strike enemy follow-on echelons deep behind the front while the close battle was fought, using integrated Army and Air Force firepower.

Core tenets

The doctrine rested on four tenets: initiative, agility, depth, and synchronization. It divided the battlefield into close, deep, and rear operations. Deep operations — long-range artillery, attack aviation, and Air Force interdiction supported by new systems such as the MLRS, the AH-64 Apache, and JSTARS — aimed to delay, disrupt, and destroy second-echelon formations before they could reach the main battle. AirLand Battle also underwrote the "Big Five" modernization programs, including the M1 Abrams tank and the M2 Bradley fighting vehicle.

Influence and combat use

AirLand Battle shaped NATO planning in the 1980s and was closely related to the alliance's Follow-On Forces Attack concept. Its principles were widely credited with informing the coalition ground campaign in the 1991 Gulf War, where deep strikes and rapid maneuver destroyed Iraqi forces in Kuwait and southern Iraq in a 100-hour ground offensive. After the Cold War the doctrine was superseded by concepts of full-spectrum and full-dimensional operations in the 1993 and later editions of FM 100-5, but AirLand Battle remains a landmark in the development of modern maneuver warfare and joint operations.
