Marco Polo

[Image: miniature from The Travels of Marco Polo showing the Polo brothers received at the court of Kublai Khan]

Marco Polo (c. 1254 – 8 January 1324) was a Venetian merchant, explorer and writer who travelled through Asia along the Silk Road between 1271 and 1295. His travels are recorded in The Travels of Marco Polo (also known as Il Milione, "The Million"), a book that described to Europeans the wealth and great size of China and its capital, and other Asian cities and countries, giving the West its most detailed account of the Mongol world.

The journey

Marco set out at seventeen with his father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo, Venetian merchants who had already visited the court of Kublai Khan. Travelling overland through Persia, the Pamirs and the Gobi Desert, they reached the Khan's summer court at Shangdu in 1275. Marco entered Kublai's service, reporting that he learned several languages and was sent on missions across China and as far as Burma; his fascination with paper money, coal, salt production and the imperial post system fills the book.

Return and the book

In 1291 the Polos left China by sea, escorting a Mongol princess to her betrothed in Persia, and reached Venice in 1295 after a two-year voyage. Captured by the Genoese at the Battle of Curzola (1298), Marco dictated his account to fellow prisoner Rustichello da Pisa. Published in Franco-Italian and rapidly translated, the work was a bestseller of the manuscript age, though many readers doubted its wonders and nicknamed it Il Milione for its reputed exaggerations. On his deathbed, tradition says, Marco insisted: "I have not told half of what I saw."

Legacy

Modern scholarship generally accepts the core of Marco's account as genuine — his descriptions align with Chinese records in striking detail — while noting omissions such as the Great Wall and tea. His book shaped European cartography and geography for centuries and helped inspire Christopher Columbus, whose annotated copy survives.
