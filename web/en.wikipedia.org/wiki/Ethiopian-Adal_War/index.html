Ethiopian–Adal War

Date1529 – 1543
LocationEthiopian Highlands, Horn of Africa
ResultEthiopian–Portuguese victory; restoration of the Ethiopian Empire
CombatantsEthiopian Empire and Portugal vs. Adal Sultanate and Ottoman allies
CommandersDawit II, Gelawdewos, Cristóvão da Gama; Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, Nur ibn Mujahid

The Ethiopian–Adal War (1529–1543) was a military conflict between the Christian Ethiopian Empire and the Muslim Adal Sultanate, supported by the Ottoman Empire. Led by Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, Adal forces conquered most of Ethiopia, but a joint Ethiopian–Portuguese army ultimately defeated them after Ahmad's death in battle, restoring the empire.

Background

For centuries the Ethiopian Solomonid emperors and the Muslim sultanates of the lowlands had alternated between trade and war. In the 1520s the charismatic imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmed Gragn", "the left-handed") united the Somali and Harari forces of Adal, obtained Ottoman muskets and cannon, and declared jihad against the empire of Emperor Dawit II (Lebna Dengel).

Course of the war

Ahmad's victory at Shimbra Kure in 1529 opened the highlands to conquest. Over the following decade his armies overran most of Ethiopia, sacking churches and monasteries including Lalibela's region, and Dawit II died in 1540 while resisting from the mountains. Ethiopia appealed to Portugal, and in 1541 an expedition of some 400 musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama landed at Massawa. After initial successes, da Gama was defeated and killed at the Battle of Wofla in 1542. The decisive battle came at Wayna Daga on 21 February 1543, where Emperor Gelawdewos and the surviving Portuguese defeated the Adal army and Ahmad was killed. Deprived of his leadership, the invasion collapsed, though his widow Bati del Wambara and nephew Nur ibn Mujahid continued resistance from Harar.

Consequences

The war devastated Ethiopia and Adal alike, weakening both and leaving the highlands open to the migrations of the Oromo people that reshaped the region's demography. The conflict is recounted in the Ethiopian royal chronicles and in the Adal chronicle Futuh al-Habasa ("The Conquest of Abyssinia") by Arab Faqih.
