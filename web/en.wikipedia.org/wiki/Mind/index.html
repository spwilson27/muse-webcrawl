Mind

The mind is the totality of psychological phenomena including thought, perception, sensation, emotion, memory, imagination and will, usually contrasted with the body and the brain. It is the aspect of intellect and consciousness experienced subjectively, and the stream of consciousness in which sense impressions and mental phenomena are continually changing.

The mind-body problem

The relationship between the mind and the physical body — in particular the brain — is the mind-body problem, one of the central problems in philosophy. René Descartes defended substance dualism, holding that mind (res cogitans) and matter (res extensa) are distinct kinds of substance. Most contemporary philosophers and neuroscientists favor some form of physicalism, holding that mental phenomena are identical with, or arise from, physical processes in the brain. Other positions include property dualism, functionalism, and panpsychism.

Mental faculties

Traditionally, the mind's activities are divided into faculties such as cognition (thinking, reasoning, memory), affect (feeling and emotion), and conation (will and motivation). Cognitive science studies these processes experimentally, while psychoanalysis and other schools of psychology have proposed models of mental structure, such as Freud's division into id, ego and superego.

Philosophy of mind

The philosophy of mind asks what consciousness is, why some physical systems are conscious, and whether machines could have minds. Landmark contributions include Alan Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), and John Searle's Chinese room argument against strong artificial intelligence.
