VHS

The Video Home System (VHS) is a standard for consumer-level analog video recording on tape cassettes, introduced in Japan by JVC on September 9, 1976, and in the United States in 1977. VHS won the videotape format war against Sony's Betamax and became the dominant home video format of the 1980s and 1990s, used both for recording television and for the pre-recorded movie rental market.

Technical details

A VHS cassette holds two spools of half-inch magnetic tape. Video is recorded by helical scanning with two rotating heads, writing slanted tracks across the tape. Standard-play (SP) speed gives about two hours on a T-120 cassette in NTSC regions; slower LP and EP/SLP speeds extend recording time at reduced quality. Enhancements included hi-fi stereo audio, VHS-C compact cassettes for camcorders, and the higher-resolution S-VHS variant (1987).

Format war and dominance

Although Betamax offered slightly better picture quality, VHS offered longer recording times, simpler and cheaper machines, and — crucially — wide licensing to other manufacturers, backed by Matsushita (Panasonic). Video rental stores stocked what customers owned, reinforcing VHS's lead; by the late 1980s it controlled the overwhelming majority of the market.

Decline

DVD, launched in 1997, overtook VHS rentals in the early 2000s. The last major Hollywood VHS release came in 2006, and Funai, the last manufacturer of VCRs, ended production in July 2016, closing the format's forty-year run.
