Georg Büchner

Georg Büchner

Born: 17 October 1813, Goddelau, Grand Duchy of Hesse

Died: 19 February 1837, Zürich, Switzerland

Notable works: Danton's Death, Woyzeck, Lenz

Karl Georg Büchner (17 October 1813 – 19 February 1837) was a German dramatist and writer of prose and revolutionary pamphlets. Despite a life cut short at 23, he is considered one of the most important figures in German literature, and the country's most prestigious literary award, the Georg Büchner Prize, bears his name.

Life

Büchner was born in Goddelau near Darmstadt, the son of a doctor, and studied medicine at Strasbourg and Giessen. At Giessen he founded a secret Society for the Rights of Man and in 1834 co-wrote the revolutionary pamphlet The Hessian Courier, which opened with the famous slogan "Peace to the huts! War on the palaces!" Wanted by the police, he fled to Strasbourg, then took a lectureship in comparative anatomy at the University of Zürich, where he died of typhus in 1837.

Works

His drama Danton's Death (1835), about the French Revolution, was the only work published in his lifetime. The novella Lenz (1836) is a pioneering psychological portrait of the Sturm und Drang poet Jakob Michael Reinhold Lenz. His final play, Woyzeck (1836–37), left unfinished, depicts a poor soldier driven to murder; it is regarded as a forerunner of modernist and Expressionist theatre, and Alban Berg adapted it as the opera Wozzeck. He also wrote the comedy Leonce and Lena.

Legacy

Büchner's works were largely forgotten until the late 19th century, when naturalist and later Expressionist writers rediscovered him. His fragmentary, episodic style and bleak view of historical forces made him a precursor of modern drama.
