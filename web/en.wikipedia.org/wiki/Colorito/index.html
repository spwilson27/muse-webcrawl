Colorito is a style or method in painting, closely associated with the Venetian school of the Italian Renaissance, in which colour — applied in layered, often glazed oil paint — is the principal means of defining form, light and spatial atmosphere, in contrast to the Florentine emphasis on disegno (drawing and line). In the celebrated Renaissance debate between the two approaches, Venetian colorito stood for a painterly, sensuous handling in which forms emerge from tonal and chromatic relationships rather than from preliminary contour drawing.

Origins and technique

Venice's access to pigments through its maritime trade — ultramarine from Afghanistan, red lakes, verdigris and fine oils — encouraged a colour-rich practice. Venetian painters such as Giovanni Bellini adopted and refined oil painting (associated traditionally with the influence of Antonello da Messina), exploiting slow-drying oils to blend tones directly on the canvas and to build luminous surfaces through translucent glazes over lighter underpainting. The damp lagoon light and the mosaic tradition of St Mark's Basilica are also often cited as shaping the Venetian sensitivity to shimmering colour.

Disegno versus colorito

The critic and biographer Giorgio Vasari, himself Florentine, praised Michelangelo's disegno while finding Venetian painters deficient in drawing — a judgment that framed Venetian art as the triumph of colour over design. Later writers reversed the hierarchy, treating Titian's late, loose brushwork as a liberation of painting. Tintoretto reputedly sought to combine "the drawing of Michelangelo and the colour of Titian". The debate became a template for later oppositions such as the Poussinistes and Rubénistes in 17th-century France.

Influence

Colorito influenced painters far beyond Venice, including Rubens and, through the painterly tradition, the Romantics and Impressionists, who prized colour and visible brushwork over academic line.
