Country house

A classic English country house set in landscaped parkland.

A country house is a large house or mansion in the countryside, historically the seat of a landed family of the gentry or aristocracy. In Britain especially, the country house formed the centre of a landed estate and of local social and political life from the 16th century until the early 20th century. Famous examples include Blenheim Palace, Chatsworth House, Castle Howard, Longleat and Petworth House.

History

Country houses developed from fortified manor houses and castles as internal warfare declined under the Tudors, allowing owners to build for comfort and display rather than defence. The great age of country house building ran from the mid-17th to the late 19th century, producing houses in Palladian, Neoclassical, Gothic Revival and many other styles, often set in parks designed by landscape gardeners such as Capability Brown and Humphry Repton. The houses were supported by large staffs of domestic servants and functioned as power bases, employers and venues for house parties, balls and political entertaining.

Decline

Agricultural depression in the late 19th century, the introduction of death duties in 1894, and the losses of the First World War undermined the economics of country house life. During the 20th century more than a thousand British country houses were demolished, and many others were converted to schools, hotels, flats or offices, or transferred to the National Trust and opened to the public.

Elsewhere

Analogous institutions include the French château, the German Schloss, Russian estate houses, and the plantation houses and great estates of the United States. The term "country house" is also used more loosely for any large rural residence.
