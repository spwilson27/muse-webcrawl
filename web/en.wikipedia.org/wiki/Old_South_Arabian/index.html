Old South Arabian (also called Epigraphic South Arabian or Ṣayhadic) is the group of four closely related extinct languages — Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic and Hadramitic — spoken and written in the kingdoms of ancient Yemen from about the late 2nd or early 1st millennium BC until the 6th century AD. Some 10,000 inscriptions survive, from monumental temple dedications to tiny graffiti and wooden-stick documents recording contracts and letters.

The languages

Sabaic, the language of the kingdom of Saba (Sheba), has by far the largest corpus and the longest history, and remained in use under the later Himyarite kings. Minaic was used by the Minaean merchants whose caravans carried frankincense and myrrh northward; Minaic inscriptions have been found as far away as Egypt and the Greek island of Delos. Qatabanic and Hadramitic served the kingdoms of Qataban and Hadramawt respectively. The languages are distinguished by, among other things, different forms of the causative prefix and of suffixed pronouns.

Script

The Ancient South Arabian script (Musnad) is a consonantal alphabet of 29 signs, written right-to-left in monumental texts (or boustrophedon in the earliest period), with words separated by vertical strokes. Its regular, geometric letter forms were carved on stone and bronze; a cursive form was inked on wooden sticks and palm ribs for everyday records. The script died out with the coming of Islam, but its relative survives in the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) writing system, which developed from a South Arabian-derived alphabet carried across the Red Sea.

Decline

With the collapse of the incense trade, the invasions of Aksum and Persia, and the rise of Islam in the 6th–7th centuries, the Old South Arabian languages were replaced by Arabic. Their modern linguistic relatives in Yemen and Oman — the Modern South Arabian languages such as Mehri and Soqotri — are not descendants of the epigraphic languages but a separate branch.
