Golem effect

The Golem effect is a psychological phenomenon in which lower expectations placed upon individuals — either by supervisors or by the individuals themselves — lead to poorer performance by the individual. It is the negative counterpart of the Pygmalion effect, in which higher expectations produce better performance. Both are forms of the self-fulfilling prophecy.

Origin of the term

The effect was named by researchers Babad, Inbar, and Rosenthal in 1982, after the golem, a creature of Jewish folklore brought to life from inanimate matter. Whereas Pygmalion, the mythical sculptor, fell in love with his creation and brought out its best, a golem's creators feared it would act destructively; similarly, the Golem effect describes expectations that undermine.

Mechanisms

Low expectations are communicated through subtle cues: less challenging assignments, less feedback, less warmth, and fewer opportunities to perform. Subordinates who perceive low expectations may internalise them, reducing effort and confidence, which in turn confirms the supervisor's original belief. Research has documented the effect in classrooms and workplaces, and it has been linked to bias against stigmatised groups.
