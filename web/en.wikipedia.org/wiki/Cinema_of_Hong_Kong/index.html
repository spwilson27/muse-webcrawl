Cinema of Hong Kong

The cinema of Hong Kong (Chinese: 香港電影) is one of the three major threads in the history of Chinese-language cinema, alongside the cinema of China and the cinema of Taiwan. For decades Hong Kong was the third largest motion picture industry in the world (after India and the United States) and the second largest exporter, famous worldwide for its martial arts and action films. Its major stars — Bruce Lee, Jackie Chan, Chow Yun-fat, Jet Li, and Michelle Yeoh — became global figures.

Hong Kong's neon streets became an iconic film backdrop.

History

Film production in Hong Kong began in the early twentieth century, including the 1909 short Stealing a Roasted Duck. After the Second World War, émigré filmmakers from mainland China built a flourishing industry in both Cantonese and Mandarin. The Shaw Brothers studio industrialised production in the 1960s, and in the 1970s Golden Harvest's kung fu films, led by Bruce Lee, conquered world markets. The late 1970s and 1980s "New Wave" brought directors such as Tsui Hark and Ann Hui, while John Woo's "heroic bloodshed" films (A Better Tomorrow, 1986; The Killer, 1989) redefined action cinema.

Decline and co-productions

The industry contracted sharply in the 1990s under pressure from piracy, the Asian financial crisis, and the approach of the 1997 handover. Production shifted increasingly toward co-productions with mainland China. Wong Kar-wai's art films (In the Mood for Love, 2000) and the Infernal Affairs trilogy (2002–2003) won international acclaim even as local output fell far below its peak of more than 300 films a year.
