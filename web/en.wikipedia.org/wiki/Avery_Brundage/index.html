Avery Brundage

Avery Brundage (September 28, 1887 – May 8, 1975) was an American sports administrator, athlete and art collector who served as the fifth president of the International Olympic Committee (IOC) from 1952 to 1972. A competitor at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics, and for decades the dominant figure in American amateur sport, Brundage was a fervent defender of strict amateurism and of keeping politics out of the Games — positions that repeatedly brought him into controversy.

Early life and athletic career

Born in Detroit and raised in Chicago, Brundage studied civil engineering at the University of Illinois, where he excelled in track and field. At the 1912 Stockholm Olympics he contested the pentathlon and decathlon, events both won by Jim Thorpe. He became a successful construction contractor, building a personal fortune, and from 1928 headed the Amateur Athletic Union and then the United States Olympic Committee.

Olympic administration

As US Olympic chief Brundage opposed a boycott of the 1936 Berlin Games, arguing that sport should remain separate from politics; he was elected to the IOC that year. After serving as vice-president, he succeeded Sigfrid Edström as IOC president in 1952. His twenty-year tenure saw the Games grow in scale and television importance; he resisted commercialisation, fought "shamateurism" by state-supported Eastern bloc athletes while expelling individual Westerners for small infractions, and faced crises including the South African apartheid question (South Africa was excluded from 1964), the 1968 Mexico City protests by Tommie Smith and John Carlos — whom he had expelled — and Rhodesia's status.

Munich 1972 and retirement

At the 1972 Munich Olympics, after Palestinian terrorists murdered eleven members of the Israeli team, Brundage insisted that "the Games must go on", a stance praised by some and condemned by others. He retired later that year, succeeded by Lord Killanin, and died in Garmisch-Partenkirchen in 1975.

Art collection

Brundage amassed one of the world's great collections of Asian art, much of it bequeathed to San Francisco, where it forms the core of the Asian Art Museum, often called the Brundage Collection.
