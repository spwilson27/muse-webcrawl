Freedom of expression

Freedom of expression is recognised as a fundamental human right.

Freedom of expression is the right to communicate one's opinions and ideas without fear of government retaliation or censorship. It is recognised in Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and encompasses freedom of speech, freedom of the press, and access to information. The concept is closely related to, but broader than, freedom of speech, covering expression through any medium, including art, writing, and digital communication.

Scope and limits

Most legal systems accept some limits on expression, such as laws against incitement to violence, defamation, obscenity, and hate speech, though the boundaries differ widely between countries. Under international human rights law, restrictions must be provided by law, pursue a legitimate aim, and be necessary and proportionate. In the United States, the First Amendment provides especially strong protection, while European law, under Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, permits a somewhat broader range of restrictions.

Rationale

Philosophers such as John Stuart Mill argued that free expression is essential for the discovery of truth, democratic self-government, and individual self-fulfilment: suppressing an opinion robs society either of truth or of the clearer understanding that comes from contesting error.
