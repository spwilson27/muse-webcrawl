Peter Wolynes

Peter Guy Wolynes (born April 21, 1943) is an American theoretical chemist and physicist. He is the D. R. Bullard-Welch Foundation Professor of Chemistry at Rice University, having previously held chairs at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign and the University of California, San Diego. Wolynes is best known for his work on the theory of protein folding, in particular the energy landscape theory and the principle of minimal frustration, and for foundational contributions to the statistical mechanics of glasses and liquids.

Education and career

Wolynes received his B.A. in chemistry from Indiana University in 1964 and his Ph.D. in chemical physics from Harvard University in 1969, working under John H. van Vleck.[1] After postdoctoral work at MIT with John Deutch, he joined the faculty of Harvard, then moved to the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, where he spent the bulk of his career. He later moved to the University of California, San Diego, and in 2011 accepted the Bullard-Welch Foundation Professorship at Rice University in Houston, Texas.

Research

Wolynes's research concerns the statistical mechanics of complex systems. With José Onuchic and others he developed the energy landscape theory of protein folding, which pictures folding as diffusion on a funnel-shaped energy landscape, and formulated the principle of minimal frustration: evolved proteins have sequences whose interactions are minimally conflicting, so folding is guided rather than frustrated like a random heteropolymer. His earlier work with David Chandler and Hans Andersen on liquid-state theory, and with Peter Debye Award recognition, established quantitative theories of molecular liquids and the glass transition. He has also contributed to theories of electron transfer, biomolecular dynamics, and systems biology networks.

Awards and honors

Wolynes was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1991 and to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. He received the American Chemical Society's Peter Debye Award in Physical Chemistry in 2004 and the Joseph O. Hirschfelder Prize, among other honors.
