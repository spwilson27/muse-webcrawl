Civilian review board

A civilian review board, in the United States, is a body of civilians, typically appointed by a mayor or city council, that reviews complaints against police officers and other public-safety officials, and in some jurisdictions investigates incidents itself, issues findings, and recommends discipline or policy changes. Civilian review is one form of civilian oversight of law enforcement, alongside independent police auditors, inspectors general, and ombudsman offices.

History

Calls for civilian review grew in the twentieth century as police professionalisation removed discipline from public view. Early boards were created in the 1940s and 1950s, but most were short-lived and advisory. A proposed strengthened review board in New York City was defeated in a 1966 referendum after a campaign by the police union, an episode often cited in the history of the movement. From the 1970s onward, and accelerating after high-profile cases of police violence in the 1990s and 2010s, many large American cities adopted or expanded civilian oversight agencies.

Models of review

Scholars and the National Association for Civilian Oversight of Law Enforcement (NACOLE) distinguish several models:

  • Review of internal investigations — the board reviews the completed file of the police internal-affairs investigation and recommends findings.
  • Independent investigation — civilian investigators conduct their own investigation of complaints, as in New York's Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB), made fully civilian in 1993, and San Francisco's Department of Police Accountability.
  • Auditor/monitor models — a professional auditor reviews internal investigations for thoroughness and fairness and reports publicly on patterns.
  • Appellate models — complainants dissatisfied with internal findings may appeal to the civilian body.

Boards vary widely in their subpoena power, budgets, staffing, and whether their disciplinary recommendations are binding or merely advisory; in most jurisdictions final discipline rests with the police chief or commissioner.

Effectiveness debates

Supporters argue that civilian review increases public trust, deters misconduct, and produces policy recommendations that internal systems overlook. Critics, including many police unions, argue that review boards lack investigative expertise, duplicate internal processes, or endanger officer morale; some academic studies find modest effects on sustained complaint rates and caution that structure and powers matter more than formal existence. The National Advisory Commission and later reform movements have generally recommended some form of external oversight as part of accountability systems.

Notable agencies

  • Civilian Complaint Review Board (New York City)
  • Civilian Office of Police Accountability (Chicago), successor to the Independent Police Review Authority
  • Department of Police Accountability (San Francisco)
  • Office of Police Accountability (Seattle)
  • Community Police Review Agency (Oakland)
