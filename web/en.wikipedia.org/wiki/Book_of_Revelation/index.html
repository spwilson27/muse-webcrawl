The Book of Revelation (also called the Apocalypse of John) is the final book of the New Testament and of the Christian Bible. Written in Koine Greek, its title is derived from the first word of the text, apokalypsis, meaning "unveiling" or "revelation". The author names himself "John" and states that he was on the island of Patmos when he received the visions it records; tradition identified him with John the Apostle, though most modern scholars regard the author — usually called John of Patmos — as a distinct figure.

Contents

After a prologue and letters addressed to seven churches of Asia (Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea), the book unfolds a sequence of visionary cycles: the Lamb opening the seven seals (releasing, among others, the Four Horsemen), seven trumpets, and seven bowls of God's wrath poured out on the earth. It depicts cosmic conflict between God and Satan, the Beast whose number is 666, the fall of "Babylon the Great", the binding of Satan for a thousand years, the Last Judgment, and finally the creation of a new heaven and new earth with the New Jerusalem descending from heaven.

Interpretation

Four main schools of interpretation have developed: preterism, which reads most prophecies as fulfilled in the first century, especially in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70; historicism, which maps the visions onto the course of church history; futurism, which places most events in an end-time still to come (the basis of dispensationalism and the rapture theology popularised in modern evangelicalism); and idealism, which treats the book as a timeless allegory of good against evil. Mainstream scholarship generally reads Revelation as apocalyptic literature addressed to persecuted or pressured Christians in Roman Asia Minor, using coded symbolism — its "Babylon" understood as Rome.

Authorship and canonicity

The book's vivid Greek differs markedly from the Gospel of John, leading scholars since Dionysius of Alexandria (3rd century) to doubt common authorship. Its canonicity was long contested, especially in the Eastern churches, and Martin Luther initially ranked it among the Antilegomena, though it was ultimately retained in Protestant Bibles. It is the only book of the New Testament not read aloud in the liturgy of the Eastern Orthodox Church, despite being canonical.
