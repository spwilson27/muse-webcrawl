Salvinia

Salvinia is a genus of free-floating aquatic ferns in the family Salviniaceae, comprising about a dozen species found in still or slow-moving fresh waters in tropical and warm-temperate regions. The genus is named for the 17th-century Italian naturalist Anton Maria Salvini. Unlike most ferns, Salvinia has no true roots; each plant consists of a horizontal stem bearing leaves in whorls of three: two round, hairy floating leaves and one finely divided submerged leaf that functions like a root.

Description

The upper surfaces of the floating leaves are covered with distinctive eggbeater-shaped (trichome) hairs that repel water and trap air, making the leaves strongly water-repellent and buoyant — a surface structure known as the "Salvinia effect," studied for biomimetic drag-reducing and oil-repelling coatings. Reproduction is mainly vegetative by fragmentation; the plants are heterosporous, producing sporocarps beneath the water.

Invasive species

Giant salvinia, Salvinia molesta, native to South America, is one of the world's worst aquatic weeds. Spread largely through the aquarium and ornamental-pond trades, it forms mats that double in days, choking lakes and waterways in Africa (famously Lake Kariba), Asia, Australia and the southern United States, depleting oxygen and blocking navigation, irrigation and fishing. Biological control with the salvinia weevil (Cyrtobagous salviniae) has proved highly successful in many regions.
