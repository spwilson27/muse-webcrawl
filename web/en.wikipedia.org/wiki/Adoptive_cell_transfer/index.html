Adoptive cell transfer

Adoptive cell transfer (ACT) is the transfer of cells into a patient. The cells may originate from the patient (autologous) or from another individual (allogeneic). In cancer immunotherapy, immune cells — usually T cells — are isolated, expanded and often genetically engineered outside the body, then returned to the patient to attack the tumour. The approach was pioneered in the 1980s by Steven Rosenberg and colleagues at the US National Cancer Institute.

Approaches

Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy expands T cells harvested from a patient's own tumour and reinfuses them after lymphodepleting chemotherapy; it produced durable responses in metastatic melanoma and gained FDA approval (lifileucel) in 2024. CAR T-cell therapy engineers T cells to express a chimeric antigen receptor targeting a surface antigen such as CD19; products including tisagenlecleucel and axicabtagene ciloleucel have been approved for B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas since 2017. TCR-engineered therapy gives T cells a new T-cell receptor able to recognise intracellular tumour antigens presented by HLA molecules. ACT is also used outside oncology, for example in treating virus infections in immunocompromised patients.

Benefits and risks

ACT can produce complete, durable remissions in some heavily pretreated patients, but it is complex and expensive to manufacture for each patient, and solid tumours have proved harder to treat than blood cancers. Principal toxicities include cytokine release syndrome and immune-effector-cell-associated neurotoxicity (ICANS), which require specialised management.
