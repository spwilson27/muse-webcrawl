The Gospel of Nicodemus, also known as the Acts of Pilate (Latin: Acta Pilati), is an apocryphal gospel claiming to be an official report of the trial, crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus, supposedly written by Nicodemus, the Pharisee who appears in the Gospel of John. It was one of the most widely read Christian texts of the Middle Ages, though it is not part of the biblical canon.

Contents

The work has two parts. The first, the Acts of Pilate proper, expands the trial before Pontius Pilate with testimonies from those healed by Jesus and a strong exculpation of Pilate, shifting blame to the Jewish authorities. The second part, the Descensus ad Inferos ("Descent into Hell"), narrates Christ's harrowing of Hell between his death and resurrection: the breaking of the gates of Hades, the defeat of Satan, and the release of Adam and the righteous dead of the Old Testament, as told by two men raised from the dead.

Influence

The Gospel of Nicodemus shaped medieval devotion and art enormously: the Harrowing of Hell became a standard scene in iconography and mystery plays, and details such as the naming of the penitent thief (Dismas), the soldiers Longinus, and Veronica's veil entered Christian tradition through it. Its Latin recensions circulated in hundreds of manuscripts, and it was among the earliest apocrypha printed.
