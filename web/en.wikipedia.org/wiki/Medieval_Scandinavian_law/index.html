Medieval Scandinavian law

Medieval Scandinavian law, also called North Germanic law, was the legal tradition of Scandinavia in the Middle Ages. It began as orally transmitted customary law, recited at assemblies (things) by a lawspeaker (lögsögumaður), and was progressively written down from the 12th and 13th centuries in provincial law codes (landskapslagar) for regions such as Uppland, Södermanland, Västergötland, Jutland, Zealand, Scania, and the Norwegian provinces.

Origins and the thing

Early Scandinavian law was not made by kings but preserved by memory and proclaimed at the thing, the local and regional assembly of free men. A lawspeaker was elected to recite the law aloud over a cycle of years, ensuring the community knew its rules. Disputes were settled through compensation payments (bót), oath-helpers, outlawry, and, in earlier periods, trial by ordeal or combat. The Icelandic Commonwealth's Althing, founded around 930 AD, is the best-documented example, and Iceland's law was first written down in the winter of 1117–18.

Written codes

Notable surviving codes include:

  • Grágás — the law collection of the Icelandic Commonwealth
  • Gulaþingslög and Frostaþingslög — Norwegian provincial laws
  • Jyske Lov (Law of Jutland, 1241), given by Valdemar II of Denmark
  • Skånske lov (Scanian Law), among the earliest Danish provincial laws
  • Äldre Västgötalagen (Older Westrogothic Law, c. 1220), the earliest Swedish provincial law
  • Upplandslagen (Law of Uppland, promulgated 1296), prepared under Birger Persson

National codification

From the 13th century, strengthening monarchies replaced provincial laws with national codes: Magnus VI of Norway issued a national law (Landslov) in 1274, one of the first nationwide codes in medieval Europe, and Magnus Eriksson of Sweden promulgated a national law around 1350. Danish law remained provincial until Christian V's Danish Code of 1683.

Characteristics

Medieval Scandinavian law is notable for detailed tariffs of fines for injuries, strong protections for inherited farmland (óðal in Norway), collective kin responsibility, and relatively prominent legal rights for women in inheritance and property compared with many contemporary European systems, though women remained under guardianship in many matters. Church influence introduced canon-law provisions on marriage, oaths, and sanctuary.
