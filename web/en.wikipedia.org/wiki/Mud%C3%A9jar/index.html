Mudéjar

Mudéjar (from Arabic mudajjan, "tamed" or "domesticated") were Muslims who remained in Christian-ruled Spain after the Reconquista rather than emigrating or converting. The term is especially used today for the distinctive architectural style — Mudéjar art — produced in the Christian kingdoms of the Iberian Peninsula from the 12th to the 16th centuries, combining Islamic techniques and ornament with Romanesque, Gothic and Renaissance forms.

History

As Christian kingdoms expanded into al-Andalus, capitulation treaties initially allowed Muslim communities to keep their religion, laws and property, in return for tribute. Conditions hardened over the centuries; following forced conversions in the 16th century, the Moriscos — converted descendants of Mudéjars — were expelled from Spain in 1609–1614.

Architecture

Mudéjar buildings make characteristic use of brick, glazed tiles (azulejos), horseshoe and multifoil arches, carved plasterwork, and wooden coffered ceilings (artesonado). Outstanding examples include the Alcázar of Seville, the synagogues of Toledo, and the towers and cathedral of Teruel, whose Mudéjar architecture is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Legacy

The style influenced colonial architecture in Latin America and was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries as Neo-Mudéjar, visible in bullrings, railway stations and exhibition pavilions in Spain.
