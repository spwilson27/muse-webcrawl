The Masoretic Text (abbreviated MT or 𝕄; Hebrew: נוסח המסורה, Nusakh Ham'mas'sora) is the authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the 24 books of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) in Rabbinic Judaism. The Masoretic Text defines the Jewish canon and its precise letter-text, and also defines the biblical books, their division into chapters and verses, the vocalization of the text, and the accentuation known as cantillation.

The MT was primarily copied, edited, and distributed by a group of Jewish scholars known as the Masoretes between the 7th and 10th centuries CE. It contains marginal notes (the Masorah) recording textual details and guarding against copying errors. The oldest complete manuscript of the Masoretic Text is the Leningrad Codex, dated to about 1008 CE; the Aleppo Codex (c. 920 CE) is older but incomplete.

Origins and history

Following the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, a single textual tradition of the Hebrew Bible gradually became dominant. The Dead Sea Scrolls (3rd century BCE – 1st century CE) show that several textual families once circulated; the proto-Masoretic family already predominated among them. The Masoretes of Tiberias, most famously the Ben Asher family, developed systems of vowel points (niqqud) and cantillation marks (te'amim) to fix the pronunciation and interpretation of the consonantal text.

The Masorah

The Masoretic notes are traditionally divided into the Masorah parva (small Masorah, written in the side margins) and the Masorah magna (large Masorah, in the upper and lower margins). They count letters, words, and verses, note unusual spellings, and mark the middle of books and of the Torah. Two special features are the ketiv ("what is written") and qere ("what is read"), where tradition prescribes reading a word differently from its written form.

Comparison with other versions

The MT differs in places from the Greek Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch, in wording, in the order and length of passages, and in some books — Jeremiah, for example, is about one-eighth shorter in the Septuagint. Most modern Bible translations of the Old Testament are based principally on the Masoretic Text, consulting the Septuagint and Dead Sea Scrolls where the MT appears corrupt or unclear.
