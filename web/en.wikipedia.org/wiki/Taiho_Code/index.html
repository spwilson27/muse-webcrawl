The Taihō Code (大宝律令, Taihō-ritsuryō) was a comprehensive legal and administrative code promulgated in Japan in 701–702, named after the Taihō era of Emperor Monmu. Modelled on the codes of Tang-dynasty China, it formally established the ritsuryō system: a centralised imperial bureaucracy governed by two interlocking bodies of law—the ritsu (penal code) and the ryō (administrative code). It is a landmark in Japanese history, marking the consolidation of Japan as a centralised state ruled under codified law.

Contents

The code organised the government into the Department of State (Daijō-kan) and the Department of Divinities (Jingikan), with eight ministries beneath them. It set out court ranks and offices, household registration, a land-allotment system (handen-shūju) under which rice land was periodically redistributed, taxation in grain, cloth and labour, and provincial administration under appointed governors. Its provisions were drawn up by a commission that included Prince Osakabe and Fujiwara no Fuhito, who had also worked on the earlier, partly lost Asuka Kiyomihara Code of 689.

Survival and successor

The full text of the Taihō Code does not survive; its contents are reconstructed from later commentaries, principally the 9th-century Ryō no Shūge, and from quotations in other works. In 718 the code was revised as the Yōrō Code, which—though promulgated only in 757—largely reproduced the Taihō provisions and is known in much fuller detail. Together the two codes provided the formal framework of the Japanese state for centuries, even as actual power shifted to regents, cloistered emperors and eventually warrior governments.
