Sensible heat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sensible heat — heat exchanged that changes temperature, as opposed to latent heat, which changes phase.

Sensible heat is heat exchanged by a body or thermodynamic system in which the exchange of heat changes the temperature of the body or system, while leaving some macroscopic variables of the body unchanged, such as volume or pressure. The term dates to the early caloric era of thermodynamics and was used by Joseph Black; the heat is called "sensible" because its effect can be sensed — measured with a thermometer — unlike latent heat, which is absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature.

Calculation

The sensible heat transferred to a body of mass m with specific heat capacity c, undergoing a temperature change ΔT without changing phase, is Q = mcΔT. For fluids in motion the corresponding energy balance is written in terms of enthalpy differences, and in heating, ventilation, and air conditioning engineering the sensible heat load of a space is computed from airflow rates and temperature differences, then combined with the latent load (moisture) to give the total cooling load; the ratio of the two is the sensible heat ratio of the process or equipment.

Applications

The distinction is fundamental in meteorology and building science. In the atmosphere, sensible heat flux from the ground — driven by the temperature difference between surface and air — warms the planetary boundary layer and drives convection, contrasting with the latent heat flux carried by evaporated water; their ratio is the Bowen ratio. In HVAC design, air conditioners must remove both forms of heat, and equipment sensible heat ratios determine how well a system dehumidifies as it cools.
