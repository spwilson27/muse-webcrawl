Ammonia

Ammonia (NH₃) is a colourless, pungent gas of nitrogen and hydrogen, lighter than air and extremely soluble in water, whose solution is a weak base. It is one of the world's most produced chemicals because it is the starting point for nearly all synthetic nitrogen fertilisers.

Ammonia is synthesised by the Haber–Bosch process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃), over an iron catalyst at high pressure and about 450 °C; the process, developed by Fritz Haber and industrialised by Carl Bosch in the years before the First World War, is credited with feeding roughly half of humanity. In the Ostwald process ammonia is oxidised to nitric acid.

Properties and uses

Ammonia's sharp smell (from smelling salts to farmyards) comes from its volatility; it was an early refrigerant and remains one in large industrial plants. In the body, excess ammonia is converted to urea; fish excrete ammonia directly.

In nature

Lightning and nitrogen-fixing bacteria and legume root nodules supply natural ammonia compounds; industrial fixation now rivals the natural cycle, with major effects on waterways through fertiliser run-off.
