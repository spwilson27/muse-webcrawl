Cucurbita moschata is a species of winter squash originating in the Americas. It includes some of the world's most widely grown squash cultivars — butternut squash, certain crooknecks and cheese pumpkins, the Dickinson pumpkin used for most canned pumpkin in North America, and the Caribbean calabaza. The species is notable among cultivated Cucurbita for its tolerance of hot, humid, lowland-tropical conditions.

Butternut squash, the best-known cultivar of C. moschata.

Description

Plants are vigorous annual vines (or semi-bush in some modern cultivars) with hairy stems and broad lobed leaves, often mottled with white along the veins. Diagnostic features include a seed stalk (peduncle) that is hard, angled and conspicuously flared where it joins the fruit, and seeds with a distinctive wavy, often dirty-tan margin. Fruits vary enormously — from the tan, bell-shaped butternut to large, ribbed pumpkins — with deep orange, sweet, finely textured flesh that generally keeps well in storage.

Origin and domestication

Archaeological evidence from Mexico (the Tehuacán valley) indicates C. moschata was domesticated by about 10,000 years ago, among the earliest cultivated plants in the Americas; the primary centre of diversity lies in lowland northern South America and Central America. It spread widely after the Columbian exchange and is now grown across the tropics and warm-temperate world, including Asia and Africa, often under local names (e.g. Japanese kabocha types are largely C. maxima, but many Asian "pumpkins" are C. moschata). The species hybridises readily with C. argyrosperma and partially with other cultivated squash species.

Cultivation and uses

C. moschata is valued for its resistance to the squash vine borer (in butternut's solid-stemmed types) and its productivity in hot climates. Fruits are eaten cooked in soups, pies, casseroles and roasted dishes; flowers and young leaves are eaten in some cuisines, and the seeds (pepitas) are roasted as snacks or pressed for oil. Canned "pumpkin" purée in the United States is made predominantly from Dickinson field pumpkins of this species rather than from C. pepo jack-o'-lantern types.
