The tuba is the largest and lowest-pitched musical instrument in the brass family. As with all brass instruments, the sound is produced by buzzing the lips into a mouthpiece, and the instrument is a long tube coiled into an upright oval form, terminating in a large, upward-facing bell. It first appeared in the mid-nineteenth century and is a standard member of the modern orchestra, concert band, and brass band.

History

The tuba was patented in 1835 in Berlin by Wilhelm Friedrich Wieprecht and Johann Gottfried Moritz, building on earlier experiments with valved brass bass instruments, including the ophicleide (a keyed instrument) and the bass horn. Earlier ancestors include the Roman buccina and the serpent. Richard Wagner commissioned the ring of Wagner tubas for the Ring cycle, a related but distinct hybrid instrument played by horn players.

Types and sizes

Tubas are built in several pitches, most commonly F, E♭, B♭, and C (contrabass tubas). Instruments typically have three to six valves, either piston or rotary. The smaller euphonium is sometimes called the "tenor tuba". The sousaphone, with its wrap-around body and forward-facing detachable bell, was developed for American marching bands.

Role in ensembles

In the orchestra there is usually a single tuba, which anchors the bass of the brass section. In concert bands and British-style brass bands it supplies the foundation of the harmony. Despite its size, the tuba has a notable solo and chamber repertoire.

Repertoire

Major works featuring the tuba include Vaughan Williams's Tuba Concerto (1954, the first concerto for the instrument), concertos by Edward Gregson and John Williams, and prominent orchestral parts in Prokofiev's Symphony No. 5, Mahler's symphonies, and Strauss's Ein Heldenleben.
