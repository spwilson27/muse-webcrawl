Asset-backed security

An asset-backed security (ABS) is a financial security whose value and income payments are derived from, and collateralised (or "backed") by, a specified pool of underlying assets. The pool of assets is typically a group of small and illiquid assets which are unable to be sold individually. Pooling the assets allows them to be sold to general investors, a process called securitization, making the risk of investing in the underlying assets diversified.

Structure

In a typical transaction, a lender or originator sells a pool of receivables to a bankruptcy-remote special-purpose vehicle (SPV). The SPV issues securities, usually divided into tranches of different seniority and credit rating, and uses the cash flows from the pool to pay interest and principal to investors. Credit enhancements — such as overcollateralisation, reserve accounts, or subordination — are used to protect senior tranches.

Types

Common collateral types include auto loans and leases, credit card receivables, student loans, equipment loans, and home-equity loans. Securities backed by residential mortgages are usually called mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and treated as a separate category.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

Asset-backed securities — in particular those backed by subprime mortgages, and collateralised debt obligations built from them — were central to the 2008 financial crisis, as falling house prices caused widespread defaults and losses, and the complexity and opacity of the structures made the location of risk difficult to assess. Post-crisis regulation introduced risk-retention ("skin in the game") requirements and simpler, more transparent securitisation standards.
