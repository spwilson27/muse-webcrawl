The Roman army was the armed force deployed by the Romans throughout the duration of ancient Rome, from the Roman Kingdom (c. 753 BC) to the fall of Constantinople in AD 1453. Over some 2,200 years it evolved from a citizen militia of the city of Rome into the professional legionary system of the Republic and Empire, and eventually into the very different armies of late antiquity and Byzantium.

Republican army

Early Rome fielded conscripted citizen levies organized as maniples. The reforms traditionally credited to Gaius Marius (c. 107 BC) opened recruitment to the landless poor, standardized equipment — the gladius sword, pilum javelin and scutum shield — and organized the legion into cohorts of about 480 men, creating a professional standing force loyal increasingly to its generals, a shift with profound political consequences.

Imperial army

Augustus fixed the army at about 28–30 legions of roughly 5,000 men each, supported by an equal number of non-citizen auxiliary troops (infantry cohorts and cavalry alae) who earned citizenship on discharge after 25 years. Soldiers served in permanent frontier fortresses linked by military roads; the army also built roads, bridges and frontier works such as Hadrian's Wall, and its engineers were famous. Late reforms under Diocletian and Constantine divided forces between mobile field armies (comitatenses) and frontier troops (limitanei).
