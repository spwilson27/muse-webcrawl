TORCH syndrome refers to a group of infections transmitted from mother to child during pregnancy or childbirth (vertically transmitted infections) that can cause a similar pattern of congenital disease. The acronym was coined in 1971 and stands for:

  • T — Toxoplasma gondii (toxoplasmosis)
  • O — Other agents, including Treponema pallidum (syphilis), varicella-zoster virus, parvovirus B19, HIV and Zika virus
  • R — Rubella virus
  • C — Cytomegalovirus
  • H — Herpes simplex virus

Signs and symptoms

Because the pathogens infect the developing fetus, the resulting conditions share overlapping features, although severity varies greatly and many infected infants appear normal at birth. Common findings include intrauterine growth restriction, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, thrombocytopenia with petechiae or purpura ("blueberry muffin baby"), anaemia, microcephaly, intracranial calcifications, chorioretinitis and sensorineural hearing loss. Characteristic patterns can point to a specific agent: periventricular calcifications suggest cytomegalovirus, while diffuse intracranial calcifications, hydrocephalus and chorioretinitis form the classic triad of congenital toxoplasmosis; cataracts, cardiac defects and deafness characterise congenital rubella syndrome.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines maternal history and serology with infant testing — pathogen-specific IgM, PCR on blood, urine or cerebrospinal fluid, and ophthalmological and audiological examination. Treatment is agent-specific: spiramycin or pyrimethamine–sulfadiazine for toxoplasmosis, penicillin for congenital syphilis, ganciclovir or valganciclovir for symptomatic congenital cytomegalovirus, and acyclovir for neonatal herpes. Prevention relies on vaccination (rubella), food and hygiene precautions in pregnancy, and antenatal screening programmes.
