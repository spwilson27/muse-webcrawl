In human genetics, a human Y-chromosome DNA haplogroup is a haplogroup defined by specific variations in the non-recombining portions of DNA from the male-specific Y chromosome (Y-DNA). Because the Y chromosome is passed essentially unchanged from father to son (except for accumulated mutations), Y-DNA haplogroups reveal direct paternal-line ancestry over thousands of years and are widely used in population genetics, anthropology, forensics and genetic genealogy.

Nomenclature and the phylogenetic tree

The Y Chromosome Consortium's 2002 naming system labels the twenty primary branches A through T by letter, with subclades indicated by alternating numbers and letters (e.g. R1b1a1...), supplemented since then by SNP-based names published by organisations such as ISOGG and commercial labs. The root of the tree — the most recent common patrilineal ancestor of all living men, dubbed "Y-chromosomal Adam" — is placed in Africa, with age estimates of roughly 200,000–300,000 years for the deepest divergences; the rare basal lineage A00, identified in 2013, pushed the estimated tree depth appreciably further back.

Major haplogroups and populations

Patterns of distribution reflect ancient migrations: haplogroup E (especially E1b1a) is common across Africa and E1b1b around the Mediterranean; R1a is frequent in Eastern Europe and Central/South Asia while R1b dominates Western Europe; J is associated with the Middle East and the Neolithic spread of farmers; O prevails in East and Southeast Asia; Q is the principal lineage of Indigenous peoples of the Americas and parts of Siberia; and N extends across northern Eurasia. Coalescence (TMRCA) dating of these clades links them with events such as the out-of-Africa dispersal (~50–70 kya), the Bantu expansion, Neolithic farming dispersals and Bronze Age steppe migrations.

Uses

Y-DNA testing underlies surname projects and ancestry testing, forensic lineage markers, and research reconstructions of patrilineal descent — famous (and debated) cases include the proposed "Genghis Khan" star-cluster lineage in haplogroup C and the Jefferson–Hemings Y-DNA study. Because the Y represents only a single line of a person's vast ancestry, geneticists caution against treating it as a measure of overall ethnic identity.
