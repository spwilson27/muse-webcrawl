Speakers' Corner

Speakers' Corner
LocationNorth-east corner of Hyde Park, London, near Marble Arch
EstablishedTradition from 1872 (Parks Regulation Act)
Known forFree public speaking and debate

A Speakers' Corner is an area where open-air public speaking, debate, and discussion are allowed. The original and best known is in the north-east corner of Hyde Park in London, England. Historically, speakers there have included Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels, Vladimir Lenin, George Orwell, and Marcus Garvey, alongside countless religious preachers, eccentrics, and hecklers.

History

The site stands near the former Tyburn gallows, where condemned prisoners were permitted a final speech. In 1855 riots in Hyde Park protesting a Sunday Trading Bill helped establish the tradition of public demonstration there. The Reform League's demonstrations of 1866–1867, pressing for working-class suffrage, led to the Parks Regulation Act 1872, which formalized the right to hold meetings and speak in the park. By custom, a speaker may talk on any subject, so long as the speech is lawful; police attend but rarely intervene.

Tradition

Speaking traditionally takes place on Sunday mornings and afternoons. Orators traditionally stand on a chair, ladder, or sometimes an actual soapbox — the origin of the term "soapboxing." Heckling the speaker is an accepted and expected part of the exchange, and the cut and thrust of debate is the attraction as much as the speeches themselves.

Other Speakers' Corners

The concept has spread worldwide: Speakers' Corners exist in other British cities and in countries including Australia (the Domain, Sydney), Canada, the Netherlands, Singapore (Hong Lim Park, the only place where public protest is permitted), and Trinidad and Tobago. In 1999 the Speakers' Corner Trust was founded to promote free expression and establish new corners.
