Hedonic regression

Hedonic regression, also called hedonic demand theory, is a revealed preference method of estimating the demand for a good, or equivalently its value to consumers. It decomposes the item being researched into its constituent characteristics and obtains estimates of the contributory value of each characteristic.

In hedonic regression, the price of a differentiated good is modelled as a function of its attributes. For example, the price of a house may be regressed on the number of bedrooms, floor area, distance to the city centre, school quality and neighbourhood characteristics; the estimated coefficients then represent the implicit market prices of those attributes.

History

The method is commonly traced to Andrew Court's 1939 work on automobile prices and to Zvi Griliches, who applied hedonic methods to automobiles in the 1960s. Sherwin Rosen's 1974 paper, "Hedonic Prices and Implicit Markets", provided the influential theoretical framework in which hedonic prices arise from the interaction of buyers and sellers in markets for differentiated products.

Applications

Hedonic regression is widely used by statistical agencies to construct quality-adjusted price indices, such as the consumer price index, where changes in product characteristics must be separated from pure price changes. It is also used in real estate appraisal, environmental economics (for example, to estimate the value of clean air or proximity to amenities) and in valuing ecosystem services.

Criticism

Critics note that results can be sensitive to the choice of functional form and included variables, that omitted attributes can bias estimates, and that the method requires large datasets with substantial variation in characteristics.
