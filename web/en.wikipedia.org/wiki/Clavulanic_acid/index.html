Clavulanic acid

Clavulanic acid
Class: β-lactamase inhibitor
Source: Streptomyces clavuligerus
Combination products: Co-amoxiclav (with amoxicillin); timentin (with ticarcillin)
Administration: Oral, intravenous

Clavulanic acid is a β-lactam drug that functions as a mechanism-based β-lactamase inhibitor. Although it has negligible antimicrobial activity of its own, it is combined with penicillin-group antibiotics to overcome antibiotic resistance in bacteria that secrete β-lactamase enzymes.

History

Clavulanic acid was discovered in the mid-1970s by scientists at Beecham Pharmaceuticals screening cultures of the bacterium Streptomyces clavuligerus. Its name derives from that species. Combined with amoxicillin, it was marketed as co-amoxiclav (brand names include Augmentin), one of the most widely prescribed antibiotics in the world.

Mechanism of action

Many bacteria produce β-lactamases, enzymes that hydrolyse the β-lactam ring of penicillins and destroy their activity. Clavulanic acid resembles a β-lactam substrate and binds irreversibly to the active site of these enzymes — a "suicide inhibition" — sacrificing itself so that the partner antibiotic survives to kill the bacterium.

Medical use

Clavulanate combinations are used for infections caused by β-lactamase-producing organisms, including sinusitis, otitis media, urinary tract infections, skin infections and animal bites. Common adverse effects are gastrointestinal, particularly diarrhoea; clavulanate is also associated, rarely, with cholestatic hepatitis.
