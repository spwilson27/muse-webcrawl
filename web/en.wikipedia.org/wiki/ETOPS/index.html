ETOPS (pronounced "ee-tops") is an acronym for a set of aviation rules that permit an aircraft to fly routes that take it farther than a specified flying time from an adequate diversion airport. Originally it stood for Extended-range Twin-engine Operational Performance Standards and applied to twin-engine airliners on overwater routes. It allowed twins to operate routes previously restricted to three- and four-engine types, and is credited — or blamed — for the displacement of trijets and quadjets by large twins on most long-haul services. Under current FAA usage ETOPS is redefined as "ExTended OPerationS" and the framework extends beyond twins in some jurisdictions; ICAO uses the term EDTO (Extended Diversion Time Operations).

History

Post-war rules (notably the FAA's 1953 "60-minute rule") confined twin-engine aircraft to within 60 minutes of a diversion field, which barred them from most transoceanic routings. As jet reliability improved, exceptions accumulated: the first ETOPS approval, ETOPS-120, went to a TWA Boeing 767 in 1985, and ETOPS-180 followed in 1988–89 for types such as the 767 and Airbus A300-600 and A310, opening direct transatlantic and transpacific twin operations. Each extension rested on statistical evidence of very low in-flight engine shutdown rates for approval fleets.

Requirements

ETOPS approval is granted jointly to an airframe–engine combination and to each operator. Requirements include demonstrated engine reliability (target in-flight shutdown rates on the order of 0.02 per 1,000 hours for 180-minute approval), redundant systems with ETOPS-specific maintenance rules (no identical maintenance on both engines by the same technician on the same visit, special pre-departure checks), fire-suppression endurance, APU reliability, crew training, and dispatch planning that keeps equal-time points and fuel for worst-case diversion and decompression scenarios within the approved time.

Ratings

Approvals are expressed in minutes of single-engine diversion time: 75, 90, 120, 138, 180, 207, 240, 330 and 370 minutes exist. ETOPS-180 covers the overwhelming majority of the world's surface within range of some airport; the Boeing 777 was the first type designed around "ETOPS at entry into service" and later gained 330-minute approval; the Airbus A350 XWB holds approvals up to 370 minutes, enabling nearly any routing on Earth, while polar and South Pacific operations drove the highest ratings.

Effect on aircraft design

Because ETOPS made economical twins legal on long oceanic sectors, airlines abandoned most trijets (L-1011, DC-10, MD-11) and progressively many quadjets on passenger routes. Wags in the industry long expanded the acronym as "Engines Turn Or Passengers Swim". Modern rules have largely converged: the FAA's 2007 rule applies ETOPS-type requirements to all extended operations, and only remote polar routes still effectively require special provisions rather than more engines.
