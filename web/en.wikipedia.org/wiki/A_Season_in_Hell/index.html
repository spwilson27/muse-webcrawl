A Season in Hell (French: Une Saison en Enfer) is an extended prose poem written and published by French poet Arthur Rimbaud in 1873. It was the only work Rimbaud published himself, printed in Brussels in a small run at his own expense when he was eighteen years old, after the collapse of his relationship with Paul Verlaine.

Contents

The work is a confessional, hallucinatory sequence in nine sections, framed by a short introduction ("Once, if my memory serves me well, my life was a feast where every heart was open...") and a farewell ("Adieu"). Sections such as "Bad Blood", "Night in Hell", "Delirium I: The Foolish Virgin" and "Delirium II: Alchemy of the Word" recount Rimbaud's poetic ambitions, his rejection of European civilisation and Christianity, and his attempt to become a "seer" (voyant) through a "long, immense and reasoned derangement of all the senses".

Reception and legacy

Rimbaud, unable to pay the printer in full, left most of the edition in storage, and the work was effectively lost until copies resurfaced in 1901. A Season in Hell is now regarded as a foundational text of Symbolism and modernist literature, and a major influence on the Surrealists, the Beat Generation and musicians including Jim Morrison and Patti Smith. Rimbaud abandoned literature altogether by about the age of twenty-one.
