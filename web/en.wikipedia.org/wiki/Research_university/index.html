Research university

A research university or research-intensive university is a university in which research is a central part of its mission, alongside teaching. Such institutions typically offer extensive doctoral programmes, employ faculty expected to publish original scholarship, and receive substantial competitive funding from government agencies and industry.

History

The modern research university emerged in 19th-century Germany, where Wilhelm von Humboldt's model — embodied in the University of Berlin (founded 1810) — united teaching and research and granted academic freedom. The model spread to the United States with Johns Hopkins University (1876) and was greatly expanded after the Second World War by large-scale federal research funding.

Characteristics and classification

In the United States, the Carnegie Classification designates universities with the highest levels of research activity as "R1: Doctoral Universities – Very high research activity". Research universities host laboratories, research institutes, university presses and often teaching hospitals. Graduate students and postdoctoral researchers perform much of the day-to-day research.

Criticisms and debates

Critics argue that the research emphasis can divert resources and faculty attention from undergraduate teaching, and that publication and grant pressures create perverse incentives. Defenders reply that research universities drive innovation, train the research workforce and anchor regional economies; technology transfer from university laboratories is credited with major contributions in computing, biotechnology and medicine.
