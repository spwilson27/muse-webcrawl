Box-office bomb

[Image: An empty cinema auditorium]

A box-office bomb, box-office flop, or simply flop, is a film that is unprofitable or considered highly unsuccessful during its theatrical run. Although any film for which the production budget, marketing, and distribution costs exceed the revenue it generates after revenue sharing can be considered a bomb, the term is generally reserved for major studio releases expected to turn a profit that fail to do so by a significant margin.

Economics

Studios typically receive only a portion of theatrical grosses — roughly half in North America and less in many international markets — so a film generally needs to gross at least twice its production budget to approach profitability from ticket sales alone, before accounting for marketing costs, which for tentpole releases can rival the production budget. Ancillary revenue from home video, streaming licences, and television sales can soften or occasionally reverse a theatrical loss.

Notable examples

Among the most famous box-office bombs are Cleopatra (1963), whose costs nearly bankrupted 20th Century Fox despite high grosses; Heaven's Gate (1980), whose failure ended United Artists as an independent studio; Ishtar (1987); Cutthroat Island (1995), long listed by Guinness World Records as the largest box-office loss; John Carter (2012), which prompted a write-down of about $200 million by Disney; and Mars Needs Moms (2011), one of the largest inflation-adjusted losses of all time.

Causes

Cited causes include excessive production costs, cost overruns, poor release timing, competition from other films, weak marketing, negative word of mouth and reviews, changing audience tastes, and external events such as the COVID-19 pandemic's disruption of theatrical attendance. Some films initially deemed flops later become profitable or acclaimed as cult classics, including Blade Runner (1982) and The Shawshank Redemption (1994).
