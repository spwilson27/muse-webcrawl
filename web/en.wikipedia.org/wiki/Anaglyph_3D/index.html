Anaglyph 3D

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anaglyph 3D is the stereoscopic 3D effect achieved by means of encoding each eye's image using filters of different (usually chromatically opposite) colours, typically red and cyan. Anaglyph 3D images contain two differently filtered coloured images, one for each eye. When viewed through the "color-coded" anaglyph glasses, each eye sees one of the two images, and the visual cortex of the brain fuses them into the perception of a three-dimensional scene or composition.

History

The first method to produce anaglyph images was developed in 1852 by Wilhelm Rollmann in Leipzig, who used red and blue lines on a black background; in 1858 Joseph D'Almeida projected red/green anaglyph lantern slides in Paris. The first printed anaglyphs appeared in the 1890s, and anaglyph motion pictures enjoyed a boom beginning in 1952 with films such as Bwana Devil and House of Wax.

How it works

A stereo pair of images is combined so that the view for one eye is recorded in the red channel and the view for the other eye in the green/blue (cyan) channels. Cellophane or gel filters of matching colours over each eye block the unwanted image. Because each eye receives only part of the colour information, perceived colour fidelity is reduced, and retinal rivalry ("ghosting") can occur when colours leak through the filters.

Applications

Anaglyphs are cheap to produce and distribute, and have been used in 3D comics, magazine illustrations, television broadcasts, scientific imaging (including NASA imagery of Mars), and home video releases. Modern variants such as "ColorCode" (amber/blue) and optimised "dubois" anaglyphs improve colour reproduction.
