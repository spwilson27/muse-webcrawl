Pontius Pilate (Latin: Pontius Pilatus) was the fifth governor of the Roman province of Judaea, serving under Emperor Tiberius from about AD 26/27 to 36/37. He is best known as the official who presided over the trial of Jesus and ordered his crucifixion. Pilate's governorship is attested outside the New Testament by the Jewish writers Philo and Josephus, by Tacitus, and by the "Pilate Stone", a limestone inscription found at Caesarea Maritima in 1961 that names him as prefect of Judaea.

Governorship

Pilate resided mainly at Caesarea, coming to Jerusalem at festivals when unrest was likely. Josephus and Philo describe a series of provocations and confrontations: the introduction of imperial standards into Jerusalem by night, the use of Temple funds to build an aqueduct, and the violent suppression of protests; Philo called him inflexible, merciless and obstinate, and accused him of repeated executions without trial. His tenure ended after he attacked a gathering of armed Samaritans at Mount Gerizim; the Samaritan council complained to the legate of Syria, Vitellius, who sent Pilate to Rome to answer to the emperor. Tiberius died in AD 37 before or around Pilate's arrival, and Pilate's later life is unknown to history.

Trial of Jesus

According to the four Gospels, the Jerusalem authorities brought Jesus before Pilate on charges of sedition. Pilate questioned him, reportedly found no capital offence under his own judgement, and — in accounts that differ in detail — yielded to pressure from the priestly authorities and crowd, releasing the prisoner Barabbas instead and sentencing Jesus to crucifixion. Matthew's Gospel adds Pilate's wife's dream and the hand-washing scene ("I am innocent of this man's blood"), from which comes the idiom "wash one's hands of". The Apostles' and Nicene creeds date the crucifixion "under Pontius Pilate" (Latin: sub Pontio Pilato), anchoring the event in secular history.

Legacy

Literature and theology have treated Pilate ambivalently: as coward or villain in Western tradition, yet he is venerated as a saint and martyr in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Later legends (such as the apocryphal Acts of Pilate) supplied him with a wife, Procula, and various fates; his figure recurs in works from medieval mystery plays to Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita.
