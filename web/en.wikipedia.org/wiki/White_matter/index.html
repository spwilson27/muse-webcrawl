Part ofCentral nervous system

FunctionRapid transmission of signals between brain regions and the body

White matter refers to areas of the central nervous system (CNS) that are mainly made up of myelinated axons, also called tracts. Long thought to be passive tissue, white matter actively coordinates communication between different brain regions. Its white colour derives from the lipid-rich myelin sheaths that insulate the axons of neurons, allowing nerve impulses to travel rapidly by saltatory conduction.

Structure

White matter is composed of bundles of axons coated with myelin, together with supporting glial cells. In the brain, white matter lies mainly beneath the grey matter cortex, connecting cortical areas to one another and to subcortical structures; large bundles such as the corpus callosum link the two cerebral hemispheres. In the spinal cord the arrangement is reversed: white matter surrounds the central grey matter.

White matter tracts are commonly grouped into three types: commissural tracts (connecting corresponding areas of the two hemispheres), association tracts (connecting regions within one hemisphere), and projection tracts (connecting the cortex with lower centres such as the spinal cord and brainstem).

Function

White matter modulates the distribution of action potentials, acting as a relay and coordinating communication between different brain regions. It is important for learning and for fast processing, because conduction speed along myelinated axons determines the timing of signals across distributed networks.

Clinical significance

Damage to white matter occurs in a variety of conditions. In multiple sclerosis, immune-mediated inflammation destroys myelin in the CNS. Leukoaraiosis (white matter hyperintensities seen on MRI) is associated with ageing, small-vessel disease, and increased risk of stroke and dementia. Traumatic axonal injury, leukodystrophies, and some infections also primarily affect white matter.
