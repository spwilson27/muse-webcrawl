Menarche (/mɪˈnɑːrki/) is the first menstrual cycle, or first menstrual bleeding, in female humans. It is the culminating event of puberty in girls, signalling (though not guaranteeing) fertility. Menarche usually occurs between the ages of 10 and 16, with an average of about 12–13 years in most developed countries, and typically follows about two years after the start of breast development (thelarche).

Physiology

Menarche is the outcome of maturation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: pulsatile release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone stimulates the pituitary to secrete LH and FSH, driving ovarian follicle growth and oestrogen production, which in turn builds up the endometrium. Early cycles after menarche are frequently anovulatory and irregular; regular ovulatory cycles usually become established within one to two years. Body weight and fat percentage influence timing — a critical level of body mass appears necessary for menarche to occur — as do genetics, nutrition, chronic illness and intensive athletic training, which can delay it.

Timing and trends

Average age at menarche fell markedly in Europe and North America during the 19th and 20th centuries — from around 16–17 years in the mid-1800s to about 12.5–13 by the late 20th century — a "secular trend" attributed mainly to improved nutrition and health in childhood. The trend has largely levelled off in affluent countries. Inheritance is a strong predictor: mothers' and daughters' ages at menarche are closely correlated.

Cultural and social aspects

In many societies menarche is marked by rituals or celebrations — such as the quinceañera in Latin America, the Japanese seijin-shiki customs in modern form, or traditional puberty rites in parts of Africa, South Asia and among Indigenous peoples — while in others it is treated as a private matter. Health education around menarche remains an important public-health topic, particularly where menstruation carries stigma or where girls lack access to sanitary products.
