Agricultural extension

Agricultural extension
PurposeApplying scientific research and new knowledge to agricultural practices through farmer education
Key institutionsLand-grant universities, ministries of agriculture, NGOs

Agricultural extension is the application of scientific research and knowledge to agricultural practices through farmer education. Generally, agricultural extension can be defined as the "delivery of information inputs to farmers", and the role of extension workers as intermediaries between researchers and farmers encourages the adoption of new techniques and varieties, helps farmers solve problems, and feeds farmers' experiences back to research institutions.

History

Modern extension is usually dated from nineteenth-century developments in Britain and Ireland, where itinerant lecturers advised farmers during the potato famine era, and from the growth of agricultural societies. In the United States, the Morrill Acts (1862, 1890) created land-grant colleges, the Hatch Act (1887) funded agricultural experiment stations, and the Smith–Lever Act of 1914 established the Cooperative Extension Service, a nationwide system linking the USDA, universities, and county agents. Similar national services were created across the world in the twentieth century and played a major role in spreading the high-yielding varieties and practices of the Green Revolution.

Methods and approaches

Extension methods include farm visits, demonstration plots and field days, farmer field schools, training-and-visit systems, radio and mobile-phone advisory services, and participatory approaches in which farmers experiment jointly with researchers. Critics of top-down "transfer of technology" models argue for pluralistic, demand-driven systems in which public agencies, private companies, cooperatives, and NGOs all supply advice, and farmers' own knowledge is valued alongside laboratory science.
