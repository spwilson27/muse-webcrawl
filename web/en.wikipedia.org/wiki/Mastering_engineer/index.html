Mastering engineer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mastering engineer is an audio professional who performs the final creative and technical stage of audio production — mastering — preparing a finished mix for distribution. The mastering engineer takes the mixed stereo (or multichannel) recording and applies equalisation, compression, limiting, stereo enhancement and level adjustments so that the material plays back consistently across systems and formats, sequences the tracks of an album, sets the gaps and fades between them, and produces the production master from which copies are made.

Work and tools

Mastering is done in an acoustically treated room with accurate monitoring. Tools include precision equalisers, compressors, limiters, de-essers, noise reduction, and metering for loudness standards (such as integrated LUFS targets used by streaming services). The engineer also fixes minor defects, applies dithering when reducing bit depth, embeds metadata such as ISRC codes, and creates format-specific masters — separate optimised masters for streaming, CD and vinyl are now routine, since vinyl cutting imposes constraints on bass and level.

For vinyl, the mastering engineer (or cutting engineer) uses a lathe to cut a lacquer master disc, translating the signal into groove geometry; historically this role evolved from the transfer engineers who cut masters directly in the early recording industry.

Role in production

Mastering is distinct from mixing: the mix engineer balances individual tracks, while the mastering engineer works on the overall stereo mix with fresh ears and an objective room, providing a final quality-control check before manufacture or release. Noted mastering engineers include George Peckham, Bob Ludwig, Bernie Grundman and Emily Lazar. The pursuit of loudness in mastering was a central issue in the "loudness war" of the CD era; loudness normalisation by streaming platforms has since moderated the incentive to over-limit.
