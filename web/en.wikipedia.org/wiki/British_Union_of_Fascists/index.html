British Union of Fascists

The British Union of Fascists (BUF) was a British fascist political party formed in 1932 by Oswald Mosley. It changed its name to the British Union of Fascists and National Socialists in 1936 and, in 1937, to the British Union. It was banned by the British government in 1940 during the Second World War.

History

Oswald Mosley, a former Conservative and Labour MP, founded the New Party in 1931 after leaving Labour. Following a visit to Italy and meetings with Benito Mussolini, Mosley merged the New Party with several smaller fascist groups to form the BUF in October 1932. The party adopted the blackshirt uniform, the Roman salute, and the symbol of a flash of lightning within a circle.

The BUF initially attracted support from the Daily Mail, whose proprietor Lord Rothermere published the headline "Hurrah for the Blackshirts!" in January 1934. Support collapsed, however, after the violence of the Olympia rally in June 1934, when stewards brutally beat anti-fascist protesters. Membership, which may have peaked at around 50,000, fell sharply, and Rothermere withdrew his backing.

The East End and Cable Street

From 1935 the BUF increasingly targeted Jewish communities in the East End of London with antisemitic campaigning. A planned march through the area on 4 October 1936 was blocked by an estimated 100,000 or more anti-fascist demonstrators at the Battle of Cable Street, and the march was abandoned after clashes with police. Parliament responded with the Public Order Act 1936, which banned political uniforms and strengthened police powers over marches.

Decline and ban

Despite a revival in the late 1930s, the BUF never won a seat in Parliament. After the outbreak of the Second World War, Mosley and around 740 leading members were interned under Defence Regulation 18B in May 1940, and the party was formally banned that July.
