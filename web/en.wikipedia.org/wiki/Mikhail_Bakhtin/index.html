Mikhail Mikhailovich Bakhtin (1895–1975).

Mikhail Mikhailovich Bakhtin (1895–1975) was a Russian philosopher, literary critic and theorist whose work on language, literature and culture became highly influential in the humanities after his writings were rediscovered and translated in the later twentieth century.

Life

Born in Oryol, Bakhtin studied at Odessa and Petrograd and was associated with an intellectual circle that included Valentin Voloshinov and Pavel Medvedev; the authorship of some works published under their names remains disputed. Arrested in 1929 and exiled to Kazakhstan, he later taught in Saransk and lived for long periods in obscurity before his work was championed by younger scholars in the 1960s.

Key concepts

In Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics Bakhtin developed the idea of the polyphonic novel, in which a plurality of independent voices and consciousnesses interact dialogically rather than being subordinated to a single authorial viewpoint. His study Rabelais and His World analysed folk humour and the carnivalesque — the temporary inversion of hierarchies in carnival culture. Other durable concepts include dialogism, heteroglossia (the coexistence of distinct speech varieties within a language), and the chronotope, the intrinsic connectedness of temporal and spatial relationships in literature.

Influence

Bakhtin's ideas have shaped literary theory, linguistics, anthropology, education research and cultural studies, and terms such as "dialogic" and "carnivalesque" are now standard critical vocabulary.
