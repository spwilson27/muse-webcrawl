Western Neo-Aramaic (ܐܪܡܝܬ, arāmīṯ) is a modern Aramaic language spoken in three villages in the Anti-Lebanon Mountains of western Syria: Maaloula, al-Sarkha (Bakh'a), and Jubb'adin, about 60 kilometres northeast of Damascus. It is the sole surviving remnant of the Western Aramaic languages, which once included the Aramaic spoken in Roman-era Palestine, and is regarded as the closest living relative of the language spoken by Jesus.

Overview

The three villages differ religiously: Maaloula's Aramaic speakers are Christians (Melkite Greek Catholic and Greek Orthodox), while Jubb'adin and al-Sarkha are Muslim. The dialects of the three villages are mutually intelligible but noticeably distinct, with Maaloula's the best documented.

The language is endangered. It lacks official status, is not traditionally written (speakers are literate in Arabic), and intergenerational transmission has weakened, a decline accelerated by the Syrian civil war, during which Maaloula was occupied by armed groups in 2013–2014 and many residents displaced. In al-Sarkha, the language is generally considered to have died out, with the last fluent speakers passing away in the 2010s.

Characteristics

Western Neo-Aramaic preserves features lost in the Northeastern Neo-Aramaic languages but has also undergone heavy Arabic influence in vocabulary, and its phonology has shifted substantially from Old Aramaic. It is written by scholars in a modified Syriac script or Latin transcription. The linguist Werner Arnold produced the standard modern documentation of the Maaloula dialect, including a grammar and dictionary.
