Lowndes County is a county in the south-central part of the U.S. state of Alabama, in the region known as the Black Belt. As of the 2020 census, the population was 10,311. Its county seat is Hayneville. The county is named in honour of William Lowndes, a U.S. congressman from South Carolina. Though one of Alabama's poorer counties, Lowndes occupies a celebrated place in American history: lying between Selma and Montgomery, it was a battleground of the civil rights movement, where the Lowndes County Freedom Organization of 1965–66 — whose black panther emblem inspired the Black Panther Party — pioneered independent Black political organising in the rural South.

History

Lowndes County was created in 1830 from parts of Montgomery, Dallas and Butler counties, on land ceded by the Creek people. Its fertile black prairie soil made it prime cotton country, and by 1860 enslaved African Americans formed a large majority of the population. That Black majority persisted, yet after Reconstruction the county was ruled by a white minority through violence and disfranchisement; it was notorious as "Bloody Lowndes" for the lynchings and intimidation that kept Black voter registration at effectively zero for decades — in 1965, although roughly four-fifths of residents were Black, not a single Black citizen was registered to vote.

Civil rights movement

In 1965 organisers of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), led locally by figures including Stokely Carmichael and courted by long-time local activists, built a registration campaign that, facing manipulation by the county Democratic Party, led in 1966 to an independent political party, the Lowndes County Freedom Organization (LCFO). Its ballot symbol, required for largely illiterate voters, was a black panther — an emblem soon adopted by the Black Panther Party founded in Oakland, California. The Selma to Montgomery marches of 1965 also crossed the county on U.S. Route 80, where marchers camped at a site in Lowndes sponsored by local families; Viola Liuzzo was murdered by Klansmen on that highway after the final march. The National Park Service's Selma to Montgomery National Historic Trail and the Lowndes Interpretive Center near White Hall commemorate these events, and in 2024 the Equal Justice Initiative opened its Freedom Monument Sculpture Park partly drawing on this history in nearby Montgomery.

The county remains poor and overwhelmingly African American, and has drawn national attention in recent years for failing sanitation infrastructure — many homes rely on straight-pipe sewage disposal — which federal civil-rights investigations and a 2023 environmental justice settlement with Alabama's health department sought to address.

Geography and communities

According to the U.S. Census Bureau, the county has a total area of 725 square miles (1,880 km²), of which 716 square miles is land, bounded by the Alabama River to the north. Communities include Hayneville, Fort Deposit, Mosses, Gordonville, Letohatchee, Lowndesboro and White Hall.
