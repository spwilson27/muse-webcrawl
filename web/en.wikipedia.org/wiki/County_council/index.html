County council

A county council is the elected administrative body governing an area known as a county. The term is used in several countries with systems derived from English local government, including the United Kingdom and the Republic of Ireland.

England

County councils were first established in England by the Local Government Act 1888, taking over many administrative functions previously exercised by unelected magistrates in quarter sessions. They initially covered administrative counties, which excluded the largest towns designated as county boroughs.

Following the Local Government Act 1972, county councils became the upper tier of a two-tier system in most of England, responsible for services such as education, social services, highways, fire and rescue, libraries, waste disposal and strategic planning, while district councils handled more local services. Since the 1990s, some areas have replaced the two tiers with unitary authorities, and several county councils have been abolished or merged, most recently in reforms creating new unitary councils in counties such as Northamptonshire, Cumbria and North Yorkshire.

County councils are elected every four years. Day-to-day leadership is provided by a council leader and cabinet, or in some cases a directly elected mayor.

Wales and Scotland

In Wales, county councils were replaced in 1996 by unitary principal councils, many of which retain "county council" in their names. In Scotland, county councils existed from 1890 until 1975, when they were replaced by regional and district councils.

Ireland

In the Republic of Ireland, county councils are the principal local authorities for each county, responsible for housing, roads, planning and environmental services. Following reforms in 2014, their number stands at 26, alongside three city councils and two city and county councils.
