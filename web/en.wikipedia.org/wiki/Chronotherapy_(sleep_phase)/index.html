Chronotherapy (sleep phase)

Chronotherapy
SpecialtySleep medicine, psychiatry
UsesDelayed sleep phase disorder, non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder, jet lag, shift work disorder
MethodProgressive shifting of bedtime/wake time; timed light exposure and melatonin

Chronotherapy, in the context of sleep medicine, is a behavioural treatment for circadian rhythm sleep disorders that works by deliberately shifting the patient's sleep–wake schedule — typically by progressively delaying (or, less commonly, advancing) bedtime — until the desired schedule is reached, then maintaining it rigidly. The term is also used for the timed administration of light, melatonin and medication in accordance with the body's circadian phase.

[Image: A chart of a sleep schedule being progressively delayed around the clock]

History

The classic delaying protocol was described by Charles Czeisler and colleagues in 1981 for a patient with delayed sleep phase syndrome whose sleep onset had drifted to the early morning hours. Because it is physiologically easier to delay sleep than to advance it, the patient's bedtime was moved later by about three hours each day, rotating around the clock over roughly a week until the target schedule was attained.

Procedure

In the original form of chronotherapy:

  • Each successive "night", the patient goes to bed several hours later than the previous night (e.g. 4 a.m., then 7 a.m., then 10 a.m., and so on).
  • The rotation continues until the desired bedtime (e.g. 11 p.m.) is reached.
  • The new schedule is then enforced strictly, with fixed bed and wake times every day.

Modern practice more often uses smaller daily shifts combined with bright-light therapy on waking, avoidance of evening light, and low-dose melatonin in the evening to advance the circadian phase directly.

Risks

Chronotherapy requires considerable discipline and free time. There are case reports of patients developing non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder after rotating protocols, particularly those with underlying vulnerability, and the treatment is generally undertaken under medical supervision.

Other uses of the term

"Chronotherapy" also refers to timing chemotherapy and other drug treatments to circadian rhythms (chronopharmacology), and to therapeutic wake therapy (sleep deprivation) combined with light and sleep phase advance for depression.
