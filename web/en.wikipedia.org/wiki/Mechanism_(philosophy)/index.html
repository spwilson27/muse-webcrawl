Mechanism (philosophy)

Mechanism is the belief that natural wholes (principally living things) are similar to complicated machines or artifacts, composed of parts lacking any intrinsic relationship to each other. Historically, mechanism in philosophy has taken two main forms: a metaphysical thesis about the nature of reality (all phenomena can be explained in terms of matter in motion) and, in contemporary philosophy of science, a thesis about explanation—that scientific explanations describe mechanisms: entities and activities organized so as to produce a phenomenon.

Early modern mechanism

Mechanism arose in the 17th-century Scientific Revolution. Thinkers such as René Descartes, Pierre Gassendi, Robert Boyle, and Thomas Hobbes held that nature operates like clockwork, explainable by the size, shape, and motion of corpuscles. Descartes treated animals as automata ("beast machines") and the material world as res extensa governed by mechanical laws, though he exempted the human mind as a separate substance. The clock became the era's emblem of an intelligible, law-governed universe, and mechanistic thinking framed debates about determinism (as in Laplace's demon) and the existence of God as a cosmic clockmaker.

Vitalism and its decline

Mechanism was long opposed by vitalism, which held that living organisms possess a non-physical life force. Vitalism declined as biochemistry advanced—often dated from Friedrich Wöhler's synthesis of urea in 1828—and as molecular biology explained heredity and metabolism in physical terms, though philosophers debate whether such explanations eliminate or merely relocate questions about organization and function.

New mechanism in philosophy of science

Since the 1990s, the "new mechanists"—including Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver (the "MDC account"), William Bechtel, and Stuart Glennan—have argued that much of science, especially biology and neuroscience, explains by discovering mechanisms. On the MDC definition, "mechanisms are entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or set-up to finish or termination conditions." Mechanistic explanation is typically multi-level, and discovering a mechanism involves decomposition (identifying parts and operations) and localization (assigning them to structures), as well as recomposition to show how the organized whole produces the phenomenon.
