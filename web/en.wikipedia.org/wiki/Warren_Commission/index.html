Warren Commission

The President's Commission on the Assassination of President Kennedy, known informally as the Warren Commission, was established by President Lyndon B. Johnson on 29 November 1963 to investigate the assassination of President John F. Kennedy in Dallas, Texas, on 22 November 1963. It was chaired by Chief Justice Earl Warren.

Members and method

Besides Warren, the commission included Senators Richard Russell Jr. and John Sherman Cooper, Representatives Hale Boggs and Gerald Ford, former CIA director Allen Dulles, and former World Bank president John J. McCloy. Over ten months it heard 552 witnesses and reviewed evidence from the FBI, CIA and Secret Service.

Findings

Its 888-page report, published in September 1964, concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone in assassinating Kennedy, that Jack Ruby acted alone in killing Oswald two days later, and that there was no credible evidence of a domestic or foreign conspiracy. The "single-bullet theory" — that one bullet struck both Kennedy and Governor John Connally — was central to reconciling the shots fired with Oswald's rifle.

Reception and later investigations

The report has been intensely debated ever since. The House Select Committee on Assassinations (1979) concluded that Kennedy was "probably assassinated as a result of a conspiracy", while subsequent reviews, including the 1992 Assassination Records Review Board, released further documents without overturning the commission's core finding. The commission's records remain a touchstone in American political culture.
