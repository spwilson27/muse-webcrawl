Penalty shoot-out (association football)

A penalty shoot-out (previously known as kicks from the penalty mark) is a tie-breaking method in association football, in which two teams each take turns taking shots from the penalty mark, with the goal defended only by the opposing team's goalkeeper. Each team has five shots which must be taken by different players; the team that makes more successful kicks is declared the victor. Shoot-outs finish as soon as one team has an insurmountable lead. If scores are level after five pairs of shots, the shoot-out progresses to sudden death.

A penalty shoot-out is normally used only in knock-out competitions where a winner is required, when scores remain level after regulation time and, usually, extra time. Unlike games decided by a penalty kick awarded during play, shoot-out kicks are not counted as goals and do not affect players' goal tallies.

Procedure

The referee tosses a coin to decide which goal is used, and again to decide which team shoots first. Only players on the pitch at the end of the match (or temporarily absent with permission) are eligible. Each kick is taken from the penalty mark, 11 metres from the goal, and once taken the kicker may not play the ball again. The goalkeeper must stay on the goal line until the ball is kicked but may move along it. Teams may reduce their numbers to equalise if the other team has lost a player to injury or dismissal.

History

Before shoot-outs, drawn knockout matches were resolved by replays, play-off matches, or drawing of lots. The shoot-out was proposed in the late 1960s — credit is often given to Israeli official Yosef Dagan after Israel lost a 1968 Olympic quarter-final by lot — and was adopted by IFAB in 1970. The first major tournament decided by a shoot-out was the Euro 1976 final, won by Czechoslovakia over West Germany, featuring Antonín Panenka's famous chipped penalty. The FIFA World Cup final was first decided this way in 1994, when Brazil beat Italy.

Statistics and psychology

Across professional shoot-outs roughly three-quarters of kicks are scored. Research has examined the advantage of shooting first — historically about 60% of shoot-outs were won by the team kicking first — prompting trials of an "ABBA" order. Shoot-outs are frequently described as a lottery, and they have produced some of football's most dramatic moments, including the 2005 UEFA Champions League final and the 2022 World Cup final.
