Legacy system

[Image: A mainframe installation]

In computing, a legacy system is an old method, technology, computer system, or application program "of, relating to, or being a previous or outdated computer system", yet still in use. Often referencing a system as "legacy" means that it paved the way for the standards that would follow it. This can also imply that the system is out of date or in need of replacement, while continuing to fulfil the purpose for which it was created.

Overview

The word "legacy" is heavily used among information technology professionals to describe systems that, while old, still handle critical business functions. Legacy systems may be built on obsolete hardware (for example mainframes and minicomputers), written in older programming languages such as COBOL, Fortran or PL/I, use flat-file or hierarchical databases, and operate in batch mode rather than interactively. Their documentation is often incomplete, and the staff who understand them may be few or retired.

Problems and risks

Organisations keep legacy systems because they work, embody decades of accumulated business rules, and would be costly and risky to replace. However, they pose problems: hardware and vendor support may be withdrawn; security patches may be unavailable; integrating them with modern systems, web services and data formats is difficult; and maintenance costs grow as expertise becomes scarce. The Year 2000 problem is a famous example of the risks embedded in legacy code.

Modernisation approaches

Common strategies include rehosting (moving the software to modern hardware or the cloud, sometimes via emulation), replatforming, wrapping the system with APIs so newer applications can use it, incremental replacement (the "strangler fig" pattern), and full re-engineering or replacement with packaged software. Brownfield development describes building new systems that must interoperate with legacy ones.
