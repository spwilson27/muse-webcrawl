Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) is the official and national language of Indonesia. It is a standardised variety of Malay, an Austronesian language that had served for centuries as the lingua franca of maritime Southeast Asia. Indonesian was proclaimed the language of national unity in the Youth Pledge of 28 October 1928 and was made official with independence in 1945. Although only a minority of Indonesians speak it as a first language, it is used by the great majority as a second language and is the medium of government, education, and national media across an archipelago with more than 700 living local languages.

Indonesian is the language of public life across Indonesia.

History

The language is based on the Malay of the Riau–Johor region, long used in trade and Islamic scholarship. Dutch colonial administrators used Malay in dealings with the population, and the colonial spelling system of Van Ophuijsen (1901) standardised its Latin orthography. After independence, spelling reforms — notably the 1972 Ejaan Yang Disempurnakan (EYD) — brought Indonesian orthography closer to that of Malaysian Malay. The language absorbed extensive vocabulary from Dutch, Sanskrit, Arabic, Portuguese, and regional languages such as Javanese and Sundanese, and today borrows heavily from English.

Grammar and features

Indonesian is not a tonal language and has relatively simple morphology: nouns do not inflect for gender or case, plurals are often marked by reduplication, and verbs are not conjugated for tense, which is expressed by context and auxiliary words such as sudah (already) and akan (will). An extensive system of affixes — prefixes such as me-, di-, ber-, and ter- and the suffixes -kan and -i — builds derivations, and voice distinctions differ from European active/passive patterns. Formal written Indonesian differs considerably from the colloquial speech of cities such as Jakarta.

Relationship with Malay

Indonesian and Malaysian Malay are mutually intelligible standard varieties of the same language, differing mainly in vocabulary influences (Dutch versus English) and some spelling and idiom. Their speakers often liken the relationship to that between different national standards of a major world language.
