Prospero is the protagonist of William Shakespeare's play The Tempest. The rightful Duke of Milan, he was deposed by his usurping brother Antonio, aided by Alonso, King of Naples, and set adrift at sea with his infant daughter Miranda. They survived on a remote island, where Prospero — a scholar who had neglected government for his library and the study of magic — enslaved the island's native inhabitant Caliban and bound the spirit Ariel to his service. Twelve years later, when his enemies sail near the island, Prospero raises the storm of the play's title to bring them ashore and work his restoration.

Role in the play

Prospero functions almost as a stage manager within the play: he narrates the backstory, directs Ariel's spectacles (including the masque of Act 4), and engineers the reconciliation of the final act. Having achieved his ends, he famously chooses forgiveness over vengeance — "the rarer action is / In virtue than in vengeance" — renounces his magic, breaks his staff and drowns his book, frees Ariel, and prepares to return to Milan, where "every third thought shall be my grave". His epilogue, spoken directly to the audience, asks for their applause to set him free, and is one of Shakespeare's most discussed passages.

Interpretations

Critics have read Prospero as a figure of the artist, and even of Shakespeare himself bidding farewell to the stage (the play was long considered Shakespeare's last solo work). Postcolonial readings focus on his domination of Caliban and Ariel, interpreting the play through European colonisation of the Americas. Others emphasise Prospero's flaws — his irascibility, his treatment of his servants, his manipulation of Miranda and Ferdinand's courtship — against the idealised image of the wise magus. Famous stage Prosperos include John Gielgud, Derek Jacobi and Patrick Stewart; the role has also been reimagined for Helen Mirren as "Prospera" in Julie Taymor's 2010 film.

Other uses of the name

Prospero's name has been given to a small irregular moon of Uranus, to the British satellite Prospero X-3 (1971), and to characters and starships across literature and science fiction, including the planet Prospero in the Warhammer 40,000 universe and the library ship in The Expanse (via the play's influence).
