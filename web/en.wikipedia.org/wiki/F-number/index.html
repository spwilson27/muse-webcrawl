F-number

The f-number of an optical system, such as a camera lens, is the ratio of the system's focal length to the diameter of the entrance pupil ("clear aperture"). It is also known as the focal ratio, f-ratio, or f-stop, and is written as f/ followed by the number (for example f/2.8). It is a dimensionless number that quantifies how much light a lens gathers: the smaller the f-number, the larger the aperture, the more light reaches the sensor or film, and the shallower the depth of field. A lens with a low minimum f-number is called a "fast" lens.

Definition

N = f / D

Because image brightness depends on the area of the aperture, successive "full stops" differ by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.414: each full stop halves the light reaching the focal plane. Standard full-stop values are f/1, f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16, f/22, and f/32; cameras commonly also mark half- and third-stop increments.

Effects

Aperture choice controls exposure together with shutter speed and sensor sensitivity (ISO), and it affects depth of field and diffraction. Wide apertures (small f-numbers) give shallow depth of field, used to isolate subjects in portraiture; narrow apertures (large f-numbers) maximise depth of field for landscapes but beyond about f/16–f/22 diffraction progressively softens the image. The T-stop, used in cinematography, measures actual light transmission and so accounts for absorption in the lens elements.

History

Several incompatible aperture numbering systems were used in the nineteenth century before the f-number system, based directly on the focal ratio, became standard in the early twentieth century. Very fast lenses have reached f/0.7 (the Zeiss Planar 50 mm f/0.7 made for NASA and used by Stanley Kubrick), while smartphone lenses are commonly around f/1.6–f/2.4 and pinhole cameras may exceed f/100.
