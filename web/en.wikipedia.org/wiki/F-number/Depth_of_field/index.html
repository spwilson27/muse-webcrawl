In optics, especially photography and cinematography, depth of field (DOF) is the distance between the nearest and farthest objects in a scene that appear acceptably sharp in an image. Although a lens can focus precisely at only one distance, sharpness declines gradually on either side of the focused distance, so within the depth of field the unsharpness is imperceptible under normal viewing conditions.

Factors affecting depth of field

Depth of field is determined by the aperture (f-number), the focal length of the lens, the distance to the subject, and the size of the image format or sensor, which sets the acceptable circle of confusion. Stopping down to a larger f-number increases depth of field; focusing on a more distant subject increases it; wider-angle lenses yield more of it at the same framing distance. For a given subject magnification and f-number, depth of field is approximately the same regardless of focal length — a fact often used to explain why small-sensor cameras produce deep-focus images.

Near and far limits

For a subject at distance s, with hyperfocal distance H, the near and far limits of acceptable sharpness are approximately:

Dnear = s(H − f) / (H + (s − 2f)),    Dfar = s(H − f) / (H − s)

The hyperfocal distance is the focus distance that places the far limit at infinity, maximising depth of field for landscape work. Roughly one third of the depth of field lies in front of the plane of focus and two thirds behind it at typical distances.

Creative use

Shallow depth of field is a classic portrait technique for isolating a subject from its background, while deep focus — keeping foreground and background simultaneously sharp — was a hallmark of directors such as Orson Welles and cinematographer Gregg Toland, and of the f/64 Group of photographers (named for a very small aperture) who prized overall sharpness. Modern techniques to extend depth of field include focus stacking, in which multiple images at different focus distances are combined in software.
