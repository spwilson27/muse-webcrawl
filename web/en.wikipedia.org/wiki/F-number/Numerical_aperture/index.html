In optics, the numerical aperture (NA) of an optical system is a dimensionless number that characterizes the range of angles over which the system can accept or emit light. It is defined as:

NA = n sin θ

where n is the refractive index of the medium in which the lens is working and θ is the maximum half-angle of the cone of light that can enter or exit the lens. Numerical aperture is the standard measure of the light-gathering power and resolving ability of microscope objectives and of the acceptance cone of optical fibres.

Microscopy

For a microscope objective, resolution improves as NA increases: by the Abbe criterion, the smallest resolvable distance is approximately d = λ / (2 NA), where λ is the wavelength of light. In air (n ≈ 1.0) NA cannot exceed 1.0 and practical dry objectives reach about 0.95. Immersion oil with a refractive index around 1.515, placed between the specimen and the objective, raises practical NA to about 1.4, and specialised objectives reach roughly 1.6. High-NA objectives also gather more light and have shallower depth of field, making them brighter but harder to focus.

Relation to f-number

For a lens imaging a distant object in air, the numerical aperture of the image space is approximately NA ≈ 1 / (2N), where N is the f-number. Thus an f/1.4 lens corresponds to an NA of about 0.36, and the fastest photographic lenses approach NA 0.5 only in the limit of f/1.0.

Optical fibres

In a step-index optical fibre, NA = √(n₁² − n₂²), where n₁ and n₂ are the refractive indices of core and cladding. It sets the acceptance angle for light entering the fibre and is typically about 0.1–0.3 for telecommunications fibres and higher for multimode fibres used over short distances.
