In optics, an aperture is a hole or opening through which light travels. More specifically, the aperture of an optical system determines the cone of rays that can enter and reach the image plane: it governs how much light the system gathers, how much of the scene appears in focus (depth of field), and the degree to which the image is softened by diffraction. In a camera, the aperture is usually an adjustable iris diaphragm inside the lens.

Aperture stop and pupil

The aperture stop is the physical opening that limits the brightness of the image; in many systems it is the rim of a lens element or a dedicated diaphragm. The entrance pupil is the image of the aperture stop as seen from the front of the system, and its diameter — the effective aperture — is what matters for light gathering. The size of the aperture relative to the focal length f is expressed by the f-number:

N = f / D

where D is the entrance pupil diameter. A "fast" lens has a large maximum aperture (small f-number) and admits more light, allowing faster shutter speeds.

Effects in photography

Opening the aperture (a smaller f-number) increases exposure, decreases depth of field, and can isolate a subject against a blurred background; stopping down increases depth of field and often sharpness up to the point where diffraction softens the image. Aperture is one of the three elements of the exposure triangle, together with shutter speed and sensitivity (ISO). Aperture also shapes out-of-focus highlights (bokeh) and, at small settings with many-bladed irises, can produce starburst patterns around bright lights.

Other uses

The term is used across optics and engineering: the aperture of a telescope or radio dish determines its collecting area and resolution; synthetic aperture radar builds a large effective aperture from a moving antenna; and in microscopy the numerical aperture of an objective measures its ability to gather light and resolve detail.
