In photography, exposure value (EV) is a number that represents a combination of a camera's shutter speed and f-number such that all combinations that yield the same exposure have the same EV. The concept was developed by the German shutter manufacturer Friedrich Deckel in the 1950s and underlies the APEX system of exposure measurement. It allows photographers to think of scene brightness and camera settings on a single logarithmic scale.

Definition

At ISO 100, exposure value is defined as:

EV = log2( N² / t )

where N is the f-number and t is the exposure time (shutter speed) in seconds. EV 0 corresponds to an exposure of 1 second at f/1.0. Each increase of one EV halves the exposure. For other film speeds or ISO settings, the EV for a scene shifts by the difference in speed expressed in stops: a setting that is correct at ISO 100 for EV 11, for example, is correct at ISO 400 for a scene two stops brighter.

Typical values

  • EV −4 to −2: night scenes under a quarter moon or aurora
  • EV 0 to 3: dim interiors, night city scenes
  • EV 6 to 8: bright interiors, overcast dusk
  • EV 11: sunsets, shade on a sunny day
  • EV 14: partly cloudy daylight
  • EV 15: bright sunshine on light subjects — the basis of the "Sunny 16 rule"
  • EV 16: bright sunshine on snow or white sand

Use in cameras

Light meters report scene luminance as an EV, and many cameras of the 1960s and 1970s (notably Hasselblad and Rollei models with coupled aperture–shutter rings) let the photographer set an EV directly, the aperture and shutter rings turning together so that all paired combinations on that EV could be selected freely. Exposure compensation is also conventionally specified in EV units. In digital photography the closely related light value (LV) and the concept of "stops" remain in everyday use.
