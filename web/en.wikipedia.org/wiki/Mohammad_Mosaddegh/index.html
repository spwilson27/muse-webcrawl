Mohammad Mosaddegh (Persian: محمد مصدق, romanised: Mohammad Mosaddeq; 16 June 1882 – 5 March 1967) was an Iranian politician, author and lawyer who served as the 35th prime minister of Iran from 1951 to 1953. He nationalised the Iranian oil industry, which had been under British control through the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, and introduced social and political reforms. His democratically elected government was overthrown in the 1953 Iranian coup d'état orchestrated by the United Kingdom's MI6 and the United States' CIA (Operation Ajax), which restored the absolute power of Mohammad Reza Pahlavi.

Early life and career

Born into a wealthy landowning family in Tehran — his father was a finance minister and his mother a Qajar princess — Mosaddegh entered government service as a young man, studied in Paris at Sciences Po, and earned a doctorate in law from the University of Neuchâtel in Switzerland, becoming the first Iranian to do so. He served in numerous posts: finance minister, foreign minister, governor, and deputy in the Majlis (parliament). He opposed the 1919 Anglo-Persian Agreement, resisted Reza Shah's authoritarianism — retiring from politics during much of his reign — and returned to parliament in 1944, where he championed the "negative equilibrium" policy of refusing oil concessions to all foreign powers.

Premiership and oil nationalisation

In 1949 Mosaddegh founded the National Front coalition. After the assassination of Prime Minister Haj Ali Razmara in March 1951, the Majlis passed Mosaddegh's bill nationalising the oil industry and elected him prime minister. Nationalisation — and the creation of the National Iranian Oil Company — made him a hero to Iranian nationalists and to anti-colonial movements worldwide, but provoked a British embargo and blockade that crippled oil exports and the economy. Britain took the case to the International Court of Justice at The Hague, which ruled in Iran's favour in 1952. That year, after the Shah refused Mosaddegh control of the war ministry, Mosaddegh resigned; mass protests (the uprising of 21 July 1952, "30 Tir") forced his reinstatement with expanded powers. Time magazine named him its Man of the Year for 1951.

1953 coup and later life

Relations with the Shah deteriorated, and Mosaddegh's coalition fractured, losing clerical support under Ayatollah Kashani and facing Tudeh Party unrest. On 19 August 1953 (28 Mordad), after an initially failed attempt, CIA- and MI6-backed plots, paid crowds and army units loyal to the Shah overthrew his government; General Fazlollah Zahedi became prime minister. The United States publicly acknowledged its role in 2013 with the release of declassified CIA documents. Mosaddegh was tried by a military court for treason, imprisoned for three years, and then kept under house arrest at his estate in Ahmadabad until his death on 5 March 1967; he was buried there quietly to prevent his grave becoming a rallying point.

Legacy

Mosaddegh remains an emblem of Iranian nationalism, constitutionalism and resistance to foreign domination. The coup's legacy — entrenching the Shah's authoritarian rule and a generation's distrust of the United States and Britain — is widely cited as a contributing cause of the 1979 Iranian Revolution.
