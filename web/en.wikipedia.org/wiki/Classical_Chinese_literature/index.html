Classical Chinese literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical Chinese literature comprises the written works of China composed in Classical Chinese (wenyan), the literary language used from the Zhou dynasty through the early 20th century. It encompasses poetry, prose, historiography, and philosophy of exceptional continuity and influence.

Early classics

The foundation is the Five Classics (wujing): the Book of Songs (Shijing), Book of Documents (Shujing), Book of Rites (Liji), Book of Changes (Yijing), and Spring and Autumn Annals (Chunqiu), traditionally associated with Confucius. The Shijing, China's oldest poetry collection, contains 305 poems dating to the 11th–7th centuries BCE.

Philosophical prose

The Warring States period produced the masters' texts: the Analects, Mencius, Daodejing, and Zhuangzi — works of terse, aphoristic brilliance that defined Chinese thought. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (c. 94 BCE) established the biographical model of Chinese historiography.

Poetry

Tang poetry represents the classical summit: Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei in regulated verse (lüshi) and quatrains (jueju). Song-dynasty ci lyrics by Su Shi and Li Qingzhao extended the lyric range.

Later prose and the vernacular turn

The Tang-Song "ancient prose" (guwen) movement of Han Yu and Liu Zongyuan championed clarity against parallel prose. By the Ming and Qing, vernacular novels — Journey to the West, Water Margin, Dream of the Red Chamber — joined the canon, though the examination system kept wenyan dominant until 1919.
