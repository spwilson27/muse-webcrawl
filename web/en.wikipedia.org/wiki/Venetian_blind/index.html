Venetian blind

A Venetian blind is a type of window blind made of horizontal slats, usually of metal, plastic, or wood, that can be tilted to control the amount of light entering a room, raised or lowered as a whole, and fully retracted to the top of the window.

Horizontal aluminium Venetian blinds on an office window.

Description

The slats of a Venetian blind are suspended on ladder cords. A tilt wand or cord rotates the slats through nearly 180 degrees, allowing fine control of light and privacy, while a lift cord raises the blind so the slats stack at the top. Common slat widths range from about 16 mm ("micro" blinds) to 50 mm or more; wide wooden slats are sometimes called plantation blinds.

History

Slatted window coverings were used in ancient Egypt and China, made from reeds and bamboo. The modern design is associated with Venice, whose merchants traded with the Middle East, though similar blinds were known in Persia — hence the French name persiennes. Venetian blinds were patented in London in 1769 by Edward Bevan, and in the United States John Hampton patented a version in 1841 in New Orleans. They became widespread in offices and homes in the 20th century with the introduction of aluminium slats.

Materials and safety

Modern blinds are made from aluminium, PVC, faux wood, or real wood. Looped cords on blinds have been identified as a strangulation hazard for small children, leading many countries to require cordless designs, breakaway cords, or warning labels.
