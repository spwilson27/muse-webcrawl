Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation

Formed1968
JurisdictionUnited States federal courts
Members7 federal judges
Authority28 U.S.C. § 1407

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML), often called the MDL Panel, is a special body within the United States federal judiciary that decides whether civil actions pending in different federal districts involving common questions of fact should be consolidated and transferred to a single district for coordinated pretrial proceedings.

History and authority

Congress created the panel in 1968 under 28 U.S.C. § 1407, responding to complex nationwide litigation such as antitrust and electrical equipment cases. The Chief Justice of the United States appoints seven circuit and district judges to serve on the panel; no two members may be from the same circuit.

Procedure

Any party, or the panel on its own initiative, may move to centralize related cases. If the panel finds transfer will serve convenience and the just and efficient conduct of litigation, it assigns the cases to a transferee judge who handles discovery, motions, and often bellwether trials. Cases not settled or dismissed are remanded to their original districts for trial under Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss.

Well-known MDLs have included asbestos, opioids, Volkswagen emissions, and pelvic mesh litigation. The panel sits several times a year in locations around the United States.

Criticism

Multidistrict litigation has been praised for efficiency and criticized for delaying individual trials, concentrating settlement pressure, and limiting appellate review of pretrial rulings.
