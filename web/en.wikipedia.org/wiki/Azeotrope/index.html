Azeotrope

An azeotrope (/əˈziːəˌtroʊp/) or a constant heating point mixture is a mixture of two or more liquids whose proportions cannot be changed by simple distillation. This happens because when an azeotrope is boiled, the vapour has the same proportions of constituents as the unboiled mixture.

Vapour–liquid equilibrium diagram of a positively deviating azeotropic mixture

Behaviour

Because the vapour and liquid compositions are identical at the azeotropic point, the mixture boils at a constant temperature, like a pure substance. Each azeotrope has a characteristic boiling point. The boiling point of a positive azeotrope is lower than that of any of its constituents, whereas the boiling point of a negative azeotrope is higher than that of any of its constituents.

Azeotropes arise from non-ideal liquid mixtures that deviate from Raoult's law. Positive deviation produces a minimum-boiling azeotrope; negative deviation produces a maximum-boiling azeotrope.

Examples

ComponentsBoiling pointComposition
Ethanol / water78.1 °C95.6% ethanol by mass
Hydrochloric acid / water110 °C20.2% HCl by mass
Nitric acid / water120.5 °C68% HNO3 by mass

The ethanol–water azeotrope is why simple fractional distillation cannot produce ethanol purer than about 95.6%; further dehydration requires methods such as molecular sieves or azeotropic distillation with an entrainer.

Separation

Azeotropic mixtures can be separated by techniques that alter the equilibrium, including pressure-swing distillation, extractive distillation, and the addition of a third component (an entrainer) that forms a new, lower-boiling azeotrope carrying one component overhead.

Etymology

The term was coined in 1911 by John Wade and Richard William Merriman from the Greek a- (no), zein (to boil), and tropos (turning) — "no change on boiling."
