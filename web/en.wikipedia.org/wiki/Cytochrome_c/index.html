Cytochrome c

Cytochrome c is a small heme protein found loosely associated with the inner membrane of the mitochondrion, where it functions as an essential electron carrier in the electron transport chain. It is a highly conserved protein of about 104 amino acids, studied since the early 20th century and important both in bioenergetics and in programmed cell death.

Role in the electron transport chain

Cytochrome c shuttles single electrons between Complex III (the cytochrome bc1 complex) and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). Its heme iron cycles between the Fe(II) and Fe(III) states as it accepts and donates electrons. Because it is water-soluble and mobile in the intermembrane space, it links the two membrane-bound complexes, contributing to the proton gradient used to synthesise ATP.

Role in apoptosis

When a cell receives apoptotic signals, cytochrome c is released from the mitochondria into the cytosol, where it binds the adaptor protein Apaf-1 and, with dATP, forms the apoptosome. This activates caspase-9 and the downstream caspase cascade that executes programmed cell death. Release of cytochrome c, controlled by Bcl-2 family proteins, is a key commitment point in the intrinsic apoptosis pathway.

Evolutionary significance

The amino acid sequence of cytochrome c changes very slowly over evolutionary time and is nearly identical among closely related species. Comparing its sequence across organisms was one of the earliest molecular approaches to phylogeny: human cytochrome c differs from chimpanzee by zero residues, from horse by about a dozen, and from yeast by several dozen — differences that broadly mirror evolutionary relationships.
