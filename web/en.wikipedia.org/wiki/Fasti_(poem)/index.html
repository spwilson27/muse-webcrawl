The Fasti (Latin for "the calendar") is a Latin poem by the Roman poet Ovid, published around AD 8. It is a six-book work, written in elegiac couplets, on the structure and festivals of the Roman calendar. Each book corresponds to one month, from January through June, and explains the origins of Roman religious festivals, astronomical lore, and associated myths and customs. A second half, covering July through December, was never completed, apparently because of Ovid's exile to Tomis.

Content

The poem opens with a proem dedicating the work to Germanicus. For each month Ovid gives a calendrical framework, interviews gods such as Janus, Mars, and Flora about the festivals in their honour, and retells myths — including the rape of Proserpina, the tale of Anna Perenna, and the story of Lucretia. The poem mixes antiquarian learning with wit and erotic playfulness characteristic of the poet.

Influence

The Fasti is an important source for modern knowledge of Roman religion and the Roman calendar, preserving details of festivals that would otherwise be lost. It influenced later poets and remained widely read through the Middle Ages and Renaissance.
