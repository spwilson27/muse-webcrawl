Normal mapping

In 3D computer graphics, normal mapping is a texture mapping technique used to fake the lighting of bumps and dents on a surface without adding geometric detail. It is an extension of bump mapping: instead of a greyscale height map, a normal map stores, in each texel's RGB channels, the components of a surface normal vector, which the renderer uses when computing lighting.

Tangent-space normal maps

The most common type, tangent-space normal maps, are recognisable by their predominantly blue appearance: the RGB values encode the X, Y and Z components of normals relative to the surface's tangent frame. Object-space normal maps instead store normals in model coordinates and appear more colourful. Because the geometry itself is unchanged, silhouettes remain smooth even when surface shading looks highly detailed.

Creation and use

Normal maps are typically "baked" from a high-polygon sculpted model onto a low-polygon game-ready model, transferring apparent detail. The technique became widespread in video games in the mid-2000s, notably with titles such as Doom 3 (2004), and remains standard in real-time rendering alongside displacement and parallax mapping techniques.
