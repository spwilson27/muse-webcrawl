Sandplay therapy is a form of psychotherapy in which clients create three-dimensional scenes or pictures in a tray of sand using miniature objects and figures. Developed in Switzerland in the 1950s by Dora Kalff, a student of C. G. Jung, the method is grounded in Jungian analytical psychology and is used with children, adolescents and adults as a non-verbal, symbolic means of expressing inner experience.

History

Sandplay has its roots in the "World Technique" created in the 1920s by the British paediatrician and child psychotherapist Margaret Lowenfeld, who found that children communicated their inner states readily when given small toys and a tray of sand. Inspired by the writer H. G. Wells's floor games with his sons, Lowenfeld's technique spread internationally.

Dora Kalff encountered Lowenfeld's work in the 1950s, studied with Jung in Zurich, and combined the sand tray with Jungian theory — in particular the ideas of archetypes, the collective unconscious and individuation. She coined the term "sandplay" to distinguish her Jungian approach and founded the International Society for Sandplay Therapy (ISST) in 1985.

Method

The standard equipment is a shallow wooden tray (traditionally about 72 × 57 × 7 cm) whose interior is painted blue to suggest water or sky, partly filled with sand. The client is offered a large collection of miniatures — people, animals, buildings, vehicles, natural objects, religious and fantasy figures — and invited simply to "make something" in the sand, including shaping the sand itself and using water.

The therapist acts mainly as a silent, attentive witness who provides what Kalff called a "free and protected space". The finished scene is not interpreted on the spot; instead, in Kalff's approach, interpretation is delayed, often until a whole series of trays has been made, on the theory that the psyche heals and organises itself through the symbolic process. The scene is photographed for the record and then dismantled.

Applications and evidence

Sandplay is used in work on trauma, grief, anxiety, attachment difficulties and developmental issues, and because it does not depend on verbal expression it is popular in child therapy and cross-cultural practice. Systematic reviews have reported positive effects in a number of studies, especially with children, though the research base remains smaller and less rigorous than for mainstream therapies.
