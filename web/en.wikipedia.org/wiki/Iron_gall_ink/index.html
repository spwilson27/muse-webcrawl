Iron gall ink

Iron gall ink (also known as common ink, oak gall ink or iron gall nut ink) is a purple-black or brown-black ink made from iron salts and tannic acids from vegetable sources, principally oak galls. It was the standard writing and drawing ink in Europe from roughly the 5th century to the 19th century, and remained in widespread use into the 20th century.

Preparation

Traditional recipes combine crushed oak galls (rich in gallotannic acid) with iron(II) sulphate (green vitriol), water and gum arabic as a binder and suspending agent. The fresh ink is pale; on exposure to air the iron tannate oxidises to a dark, water-resistant pigment that binds into the fibres of parchment or paper, making the writing exceptionally durable and difficult to erase.

Use

Iron gall ink was used for countless landmark documents and artworks — medieval manuscripts, Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks, Bach's scores, and the drawings of Rembrandt and Van Gogh. Official registries in some countries specified it well into modern times.

Ink corrosion

The same chemistry that makes the ink permanent also attacks its support: excess iron(II) ions and acidity catalyse the degradation of paper and parchment, a phenomenon called ink corrosion or "ink burn", in which letters brown, crack and eventually fall out of the sheet. Conservation treatments include washing, deacidification and treatment with calcium phytate.
