In statistics, an outlier is a data point that differs significantly from other observations. An outlier may indicate variability in a measurement, a heavy-tailed distribution, a distinct subpopulation, or experimental error; the last possibility is often — though controversially — grounds for excluding the point from analysis.

Causes

Outliers arise from several sources: instrument malfunction or recording mistakes; data drawn from a genuinely heavy-tailed process (as with city sizes, incomes, or earthquake magnitudes, where extreme values are expected); mixtures of populations, such as a dataset combining measurements from two different regimes; and rare but real phenomena, where the outlier is the most interesting observation in the data — the classic example being the Antarctic ozone hole, whose early satellite readings were nearly discarded as instrument errors.

Identifying outliers

There is no rigid mathematical definition, but several conventions are widely used. In Tukey's box plot rule, points lying more than 1.5 interquartile ranges beyond the first or third quartile are flagged. Other approaches standardise values as z-scores, or apply formal tests for a single outlier such as Grubbs's test or Dixon's Q test. In multivariate and machine-learning contexts, methods such as Mahalanobis distance, local outlier factor, and isolation forests generalise the idea to many dimensions, where univariate rules fail.

Working with outliers

Because outliers can badly distort non-robust statistics — a single bad point can drag a mean or a least-squares regression line far off course — analysts choose among several strategies: verify the point and correct or remove proven errors; accommodate the data with robust statistics such as the median, trimmed means, or robust regression; transform the data (for instance, taking logarithms); or model the extreme values explicitly, as in extreme value theory for risk and engineering. Simply deleting inconvenient outliers without justification is considered poor practice and, in scientific reporting, a form of selective presentation.
