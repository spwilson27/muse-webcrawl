Henry VI (6 December 1421 – 21 May 1471) was King of England from 1422 to 1461 and again from 1470 to 1471, and claimed King of France from 1422 to 1453. The only child of Henry V, he succeeded to the throne at nine months of age, the youngest person ever to become king of England. His long minority and, later, his periods of mental illness shaped a reign marked by the loss of England's French territories and the outbreak of the Wars of the Roses.

Reign

Governed by regents in his youth, Henry was crowned king of France in Paris in 1431 under the terms of the Treaty of Troyes. His marriage to Margaret of Anjou (1445) accompanied unpopular concessions to France, and by 1453 England had lost all its French possessions except Calais. In that year Henry suffered a complete mental breakdown — sometimes attributed to schizophrenia or catatonic depression, possibly inherited from his grandfather Charles VI of France — and Richard, Duke of York was made Protector. Rivalry between the Lancastrian court and the Yorkists erupted into civil war in 1455.

Deposition and death

Henry was deposed in 1461 by Edward IV, captured in 1465, and briefly restored in 1470–71 (the "Readeption"). After the Lancastrian defeats at Barnet and Tewkesbury, where his son Edward of Westminster was killed, Henry died in the Tower of London on 21 May 1471, almost certainly murdered. He founded Eton College and King's College, Cambridge, whose chapel is a masterpiece of late Gothic architecture. Shakespeare devoted three plays to his reign.
