Planing (boat)

Planing
TypeHigh-speed hydrodynamic regime
Support of weightMainly hydrodynamic lift
Typical craftPowerboats, dinghies, surfboards, seaplane floats

Planing is the mode of operation of a waterborne craft in which its weight is supported mainly by hydrodynamic lift, rather than by the hydrostatic buoyancy that supports a displacement hull. When a boat planes, it rises up and skims over the water's surface, greatly reducing drag from the hull and allowing much higher speeds.

Mechanics

As speed increases, pressure builds on the flat or V-shaped aft surfaces of the hull, lifting the bow and then most of the hull out of the water. The transition through the drag-heavy "hump" or semi-displacement speed is the main obstacle; beyond it, wetted area and wave-making fall sharply. Planing surfaces must be relatively flat and wide — hence the typical hard-chine, flat-bottomed or shallow-V runabout hull. In Froude-number terms, planing corresponds to high values (roughly above 1.0 by volume-based conventions).

Applications

Most small motorboats, personal watercraft, racing powerboats, sailing dinghies and windsurfers are designed to plane. Surfboards and wakeboards plane without engines, using wave or towing power. Seaplanes and flying boats use stepped hulls to break suction and reach planing speed for take-off. Naval and workboats may use semi-planing ("semi-displacement") hulls that plane only partially.

Behaviour and hazards

Planing hulls ride harshly in chop and can exhibit instabilities such as porpoising (rhythmic pitching) and chine walking. Trim tabs and engine trim are used to control running angle. At very high speed in rough water the hull may slam dangerously, so ocean-going fast craft often use deep-V hulls that trade efficiency for seakeeping.
