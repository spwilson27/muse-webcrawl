Bach cantata

The term Bach cantata refers to the cantatas composed by Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750). Bach composed church cantatas for the Lutheran liturgy across his career — above all in Leipzig, where from 1723 he produced several annual cycles of cantatas for the Sundays and feast days of the church year — as well as secular cantatas for civic and private occasions. Around 200 church cantatas survive, and many more are known to have been lost.

Cantata cycles

In his first years in Leipzig Bach composed cantatas at a remarkable rate, performing a new work nearly every week. The first Leipzig cycle (1723–24) largely reworked earlier pieces; the second, the chorale cantata cycle (1724–25), based each work on a Lutheran hymn; further cycles followed more intermittently. The Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis (BWV) catalogue numbers church cantatas BWV 1–224.

Structure and texts

A typical Leipzig church cantata opens with a substantial chorus, continues with alternating recitatives and arias for solo voices, and closes with a four-part chorale. Texts combine Bible quotations, free madrigalesque poetry, and hymn stanzas. Librettists included Picander (Christian Friedrich Henrici) and, for many earlier works, Erdmann Neumeister. Movements were scored for the modest forces available at the Thomaskirche and Nikolaikirche, though Bach's writing is often virtuosic.

Secular cantatas

Bach's secular cantatas, such as the Coffee Cantata (BWV 211) and the Peasant Cantata (BWV 212), were written for collegium musicum concerts, weddings, birthdays, and homage to rulers. Bach frequently recycled their music in sacred works, a practice known as parody.

Legacy

The cantatas form the largest single part of Bach's output and are central to the modern Bach revival; complete recorded cycles have been undertaken by conductors including Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt, John Eliot Gardiner, Philippe Herreweghe, and Masaaki Suzuki.
