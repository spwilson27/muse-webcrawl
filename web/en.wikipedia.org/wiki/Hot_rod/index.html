Hot rod

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hot rods are typically American cars that have been rebuilt or modified with large engines for speed and acceleration, and with distinctive appearances for style. The term is also used for the culture — including specialised magazines, shows, and racing — that grew up around such cars from the 1930s onward.

Origins

Hot rodding began in Southern California in the 1930s, where enthusiasts modified cheap, plentiful cars such as the Ford Model T and Model A, stripping fenders and running boards and "hopping up" their engines — fitting high-compression heads, multiple carburettors, and later the Ford flathead V8 — for racing on dry lake beds such as Muroc and El Mirage. After the Second World War, returning servicemen with mechanical skills drove a boom, and organised drag racing emerged, leading to the founding of the National Hot Rod Association (NHRA) in 1951.

Typical features

Classic hot rods are often based on pre-1949 cars, especially the 1932 Ford ("Deuce"), and feature lowered suspension, chopped roofs, channelled bodies, exposed or supercharged V8 engines, wide rear tyres, and elaborate paintwork including flames and pinstriping. The related "rat rod" style deliberately embraces a rough, unfinished look.

Culture

Hot rodding became a defining element of post-war American youth culture, documented in magazines such as Hot Rod (founded 1948), films, and music. Modern street rods continue the tradition with contemporary engines, brakes, and comforts while preserving period styling.
