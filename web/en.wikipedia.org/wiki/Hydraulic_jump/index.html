Hydraulic jump

A hydraulic jump is a phenomenon in open-channel flow in which rapidly flowing, shallow (supercritical) water abruptly transitions to slowly flowing, deeper (subcritical) water. The transition is accompanied by intense turbulence, a surface roller, air entrainment, and a significant loss of kinetic energy. Hydraulic jumps occur naturally in rivers and are deliberately engineered below dam spillways and sluice gates to dissipate energy that would otherwise erode the channel bed.

Theory

The jump is governed by conservation of momentum: for a horizontal rectangular channel, the depths before and after the jump (the sequent or conjugate depths) are related by the Bélanger equation, in which the downstream depth depends on the upstream depth and the upstream Froude number. Flow approaching the jump must have a Froude number greater than 1 (supercritical); downstream flow is subcritical. The greater the upstream Froude number, the taller and more energetic the jump and the larger the fraction of energy dissipated — as much as 45–70 per cent for strong jumps.

Classification

Engineers classify jumps by the upstream Froude number: undular jumps (Fr 1–1.7), in which the surface rises in a series of standing waves with little turbulence; weak jumps (1.7–2.5); oscillating jumps (2.5–4.5), whose jet fluctuates and generates damaging waves; steady jumps (4.5–9.0), which are well-balanced and dissipate energy efficiently; and strong jumps (above 9.0), which are rough and highly dissipative. The everyday example seen in a kitchen sink, where a falling stream spreads out and the film suddenly thickens, is a circular hydraulic jump.

Applications

Hydraulic jumps are used in water engineering to dissipate energy in stilling basins below spillways and weirs, protecting downstream channels from scour; to mix chemicals in water treatment; to aerate water; to increase the weight on aprons to prevent uplift; and in desalination and irrigation works. First studied scientifically by Giorgio Bidone in the early 19th century and analysed by Jean-Baptiste Bélanger, the hydraulic jump remains a standard topic in fluid mechanics.
