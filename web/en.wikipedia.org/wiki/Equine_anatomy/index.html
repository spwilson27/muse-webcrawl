Equine anatomy

Equine anatomy describes the gross and microscopic structures of the horse and other equids. Because horses are large domesticated animals kept for riding, work and sport, their anatomy has been studied for centuries, and a specialised vocabulary — the "points of the horse" — is used to describe external landmarks such as the poll, crest, withers, croup, stifle, hock and fetlock.

External anatomy

The head includes the muzzle, nostrils, ears and eyes set laterally for a wide field of vision. The neck joins the trunk at the withers, the ridge between the shoulder blades whose height is the standard point for measuring a horse's height in hands. The back runs from the withers to the loins and croup, ending at the dock where the tail attaches. The barrel encloses the thorax and abdomen, and the four legs end in single hooves — horses are odd-toed ungulates that bear weight on the third digit of each foot.

Musculoskeletal system

The horse's skeleton comprises roughly 205 bones (excluding the variable number in the tail). The limbs are adapted for speed and economical standing: ligaments and tendons such as the suspensory apparatus allow the horse to rest standing with little muscular effort. Powerful muscles of the hindquarters drive locomotion, while the forelimbs bear about 60 percent of body weight. Lameness arising from the musculoskeletal system is one of the most common veterinary problems in horses.

Digestive system

Horses are herbivorous hindgut fermenters. The small stomach (about 8–15 litres) leads to a small intestine where enzymatic digestion occurs, and then to the greatly enlarged caecum and colon, where microbes ferment fibrous plant material. Horses cannot vomit, which makes them vulnerable to certain forms of colic — abdominal pain from gastrointestinal disturbance — a leading cause of death in domestic horses. An adult horse has 36–44 teeth, adapted for grazing, with a toothless gap (the bars) where the bit sits.

Other systems

The respiratory system includes large nostrils and an obligate nasal breathing arrangement; horses cannot breathe through the mouth. The cardiovascular and integumentary systems support sustained exercise, with sweating as the main means of heat loss. The hoof, a modified nail of keratin, grows continuously and protects the coffin bone within.
