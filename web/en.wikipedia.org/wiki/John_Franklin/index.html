Sir John Franklin (16 April 1786 – 11 June 1847) was a British Royal Navy officer and Arctic explorer. After serving in the Napoleonic Wars and the War of 1812, he led two overland expeditions to the Arctic coast of Canada and mapped large stretches of it. Appointed to command HMS Erebus and HMS Terror in 1845, he set out to traverse the last unnavigated section of the Northwest Passage; the expedition was never seen again by Europeans.

Early career

Franklin entered the navy at 14, fought at Copenhagen (1801) and Trafalgar (1805), and was wounded at New Orleans (1815). In 1818 he commanded the brig Trent on an attempt toward the North Pole via Spitsbergen.

Overland expeditions

The Coppermine expedition (1819–1822) mapped the Arctic coast eastward from the Coppermine River but degenerated into a starving retreat in which 11 of 20 men died — Franklin became famous as "the man who ate his boots". A second expedition (1825–1827) down the Mackenzie River mapped over a thousand miles of coastline with far better preparation and no loss of life.

Van Diemen's Land

Knighted in 1829, Franklin served as Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania) from 1837 to 1843, promoting education and reform, before being recalled amid disputes with local officials.

The lost expedition

In May 1845 Franklin sailed with 129 officers and men in Erebus and Terror, superbly equipped ships with steam engines and three years' provisions. The ships wintered at Beechey Island, then became trapped in ice off King William Island. A note found in 1859 — the only written record — reported Franklin's death on 11 June 1847 and that 105 survivors had abandoned the ships in April 1848 to walk south; all perished. Decades of searches, driven by his widow Lady Jane Franklin, charted much of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. The wrecks of Erebus and Terror were finally located in 2014 and 2016.
