Wood gas

Wood gas is a fuel gas produced by the thermal gasification of wood or other biomass in a wood gasifier. It consists chiefly of combustible carbon monoxide (roughly 20%) and hydrogen (about 15–20%), together with small amounts of methane and large proportions of inert nitrogen and carbon dioxide drawn from the air blast; its heating value is low, around 4–6 MJ/m³. Wood gas can fuel internal combustion engines, stoves and furnaces, and was used on a large scale for vehicles in Europe during the Second World War when petroleum was rationed.

Production

In a gasifier, wood is pyrolysed and partially burned with a restricted supply of air. In the classic Imbert downdraft design, air enters above a constriction ("throat"); the hot charcoal bed below reduces CO₂ and steam to CO and H₂ — the endothermic Boudouard and water-gas reactions. The raw gas is hot and laden with tar, so vehicle systems pass it through coolers, cyclones and filters (sometimes using wood chips or cloth) before it reaches the carburettor, where it is mixed with additional air. Roughly 2.5–3 kg of dry wood substitutes for about a litre of petrol, at the cost of a 30–50% loss of engine power.

History

Producer gas from coal was widely used in industry from the 19th century. Georges Imbert's practical wood gasifier (1920s) popularised vehicle use. During the Second World War roughly a million producer-gas vehicles operated in Europe — in Germany, France, Sweden and Finland especially — including buses, trucks, tractors and private cars fitted with bulky rear or trailer-mounted generators (German: Holzvergaser). Interest revived briefly in the 1970s oil crises; the US Federal Emergency Management Agency published plans for a simplified "stratified downdraft" gasifier in 1989 for emergency use. In North Korea wood-gas trucks remain a common sight owing to chronic fuel shortages.

Modern uses

Today wood gasifiers are used for off-grid electricity and combined heat and power from biomass, in survivalist and maker communities, and in some developing regions. Modern designs address tar cracking and gas cleaning to protect engines. Because the carbon released is biogenic, wood gas is often presented as approximately carbon-neutral, though sustainability depends on feedstock sourcing and the high toxicity of the carbon monoxide content makes the gas dangerous to handle.
