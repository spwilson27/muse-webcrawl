Altitudinal zonation

Altitudinal zonation (or elevational zonation) in mountainous regions describes the natural layering of ecosystems that occurs at distinct elevations because of varying environmental conditions. Conditions such as temperature, humidity, soil composition, and solar radiation change with altitude and determine which plants and animals can thrive in each zone.

Zones

A typical sequence from low to high elevation in temperate mountains includes foothill or colline forest, montane forest, subalpine forest, the treeline ecotone (with stunted krummholz trees), alpine tundra or meadow above the treeline, and, at the highest elevations, the nival zone of permanent snow and bare rock. In the tropics, zones include lowland rainforest, montane rainforest, cloud forest (with persistent mist and epiphytes), subalpine shrubland (such as the Andean páramo or East African afroalpine zone), and alpine desert or tundra.

Factors

Temperature typically falls with height at an environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per kilometre. Temperature limits many species' ranges and defines the treeline, which often corresponds to a growing-season mean temperature near 6–7 °C. Precipitation commonly increases with elevation up to a level of maximum precipitation, then decreases; cold air drainage, aspect (sun-facing versus shaded slopes), wind exposure, and the mass elevation effect (Massenerhebungseffekt), by which treelines are higher on large massifs than on isolated peaks, further modify the zones.

Study and significance

Altitudinal zonation parallels latitudinal zonation from the equator to the poles, an observation classically described by Alexander von Humboldt in the Andes. Because zones are compressed into short horizontal distances, mountains are important natural laboratories for ecology and for studying how species may shift their ranges under climate change.
