Disc brake

A disc brake is a type of brake that uses calipers to squeeze pairs of pads against a disc (rotor) to create friction that slows rotation of a shaft or wheel. Disc brakes generally dissipate heat better than drum brakes and offer more consistent stopping performance, especially in wet conditions and under repeated heavy use.

Design

The rotor is typically cast iron, sometimes drilled or slotted to aid cooling and gas escape, and may be ventilated with internal vanes. The caliper may be of fixed or floating design and houses pistons actuated hydraulically (in cars and motorcycles) or mechanically (common on bicycles). Brake pads use friction materials formulated for the application; squeal, dust and fade are managed through pad compounds, shims and rotor design.

History and use

Disc brakes were developed in Britain around the turn of the 20th century and patented by Frederick Lanchester in 1902, but became practical for production cars in the 1950s, notably with the Citroën DS (1955) and through racing successes such as Jaguar's Le Mans wins. They are now standard on the front wheels of almost all cars, increasingly on all wheels, and are used on motorcycles, bicycles, trains and aircraft.
