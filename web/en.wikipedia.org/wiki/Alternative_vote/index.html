Alternative vote

Alternative vote (instant-runoff voting)
TypeRanked, single-winner electoral system
Used inAustralia (House of Representatives), Ireland (presidential), various U.S. cities and states
Also calledInstant-runoff voting (IRV); ranked-choice voting (U.S.)

The alternative vote (AV), known in the United States as instant-runoff voting or ranked-choice voting, is an electoral system in which voters rank candidates in order of preference rather than marking a single choice. If no candidate wins a majority of first preferences, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and their ballots are transferred to the next preference marked on each, repeating until one candidate holds a majority of the votes remaining in the count.

History and use

The method was devised in the 1870s by the American architect William Robert Ware and developed by the English lawyer Thomas Hare's contemporaries in proportional-representation debates. It has been used for Australia's House of Representatives since 1919 (where compulsory full preferential voting applies), for presidential elections in Ireland, in Papua New Guinea, and increasingly in the United States — in Maine and Alaska statewide, and in cities including San Francisco and New York City. A 2011 UK referendum on adopting AV for Westminster elections was rejected by 68% to 32%.

Properties and debate

Supporters argue AV ensures the winner has broad support, removes the "spoiler effect" and the need for tactical voting between similar candidates, and encourages candidates to appeal beyond their base for second preferences. Critics note AV is not proportional, can still produce landslides disproportional to vote shares, is non-monotonic (ranking a candidate higher can in theory hurt them), and — in its exhaustive-ballot counting — can eliminate a centrist compromise candidate early ("centre squeeze"). In practice, AV usually elects the same winner as plurality voting in most districts, with outcomes differing in a minority of competitive seats.
