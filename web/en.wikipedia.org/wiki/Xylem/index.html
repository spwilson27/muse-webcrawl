Xylem

Xylem is one of the two types of transport tissue in vascular plants (the other being phloem). The word derives from the Greek xylon, "wood" — indeed, the best-known form of xylem is wood. Its basic function is to transport water and dissolved minerals from the roots up to the stems and leaves, and it also provides mechanical support. Water transport in the xylem is a passive process powered mainly by transpiration pull and explained by the cohesion–tension theory: evaporation from leaves places the continuous water columns under negative pressure (tension).

Structure

Xylem consists of conducting cells called tracheary elements — tracheids and vessel elements — which are dead and hollow at maturity, with lignified secondary walls that may be thickened in annular, spiral, scalariform, or pitted patterns. Tracheids, found in nearly all vascular plants, are long tapered cells that conduct water through pits. Vessel elements are shorter and wider, joined end-to-end into vessels, and are characteristic of flowering plants; water moves between them through perforations. Xylem also contains fibres for support and living parenchyma cells involved in storage and lateral (ray) transport.

Primary and secondary xylem

Primary xylem is formed during primary growth from the procambium; in stems it is arranged in vascular bundles. In woody plants, the vascular cambium produces secondary xylem inward — accumulating as wood — and secondary phloem outward. Each growing season's secondary xylem forms a growth ring, the basis of dendrochronology. In older stems the inner, darker, non-conducting xylem is called heartwood, while the younger, functional outer xylem is sapwood.

Water transport

Water columns in xylem are vulnerable to cavitation and embolism, especially under drought or freeze–thaw cycles; plants mitigate this with narrow conduits, pit membranes that limit air spread, and refilling mechanisms. Root pressure, generated by osmotic uptake of ions, can push water upward modestly and causes guttation in some herbs.
