Georgian polyphony

Georgian polyphony is the traditional vocal polyphonic music of Georgia, one of the world's oldest polyphonic singing traditions. It is characterized by three-voice singing with close, often dissonant harmonies, drones, and striking regional diversity. In 2001 UNESCO proclaimed Georgian polyphonic singing a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and it was inscribed on the Representative List in 2008.

Characteristics

Georgian polyphony is usually sung by three voices: a top voice carrying the melody, a second voice harmonizing closely against it, and a bass providing a drone or slow-moving foundation. The harmony makes prominent use of seconds, fourths, fifths, and sevenths that Western-trained ears may hear as sharply dissonant; many scholars believe the tradition preserves features of European music older than the development of functional tonality. Some songs employ the krimanchuli, a virtuosic yodel-like ornament sung by the top voice, particularly in the western region of Guria.

Regional styles

Styles differ markedly between regions. Eastern Georgia (Kakheti) is known for long table songs with a sustained drone bass and two ornamented solo voices, sung at the traditional feast, the supra. Western regions such as Guria and Samegrelo feature faster, more complex interplay of all three voices. Svaneti, in the high Caucasus, preserves some of the most dissonant singing, associated with round dances and ancient ritual.

Genres and setting

The repertoire includes work songs (such as the threshing song Naduri, sung with field hammers), wedding and funeral songs, healing songs, war songs, and lullabies. Sacred polyphony developed in Georgian Orthodox chant, with three voices symbolizing the Trinity. Perhaps the most famous Georgian song is the hymn Shen Khar Venakhi ("Thou Art a Vineyard"), dedicated to the Virgin Mary.

Modern era

A recording of the song Chakrulo was included on the Voyager Golden Record sent into space in 1977. Ensembles such as the Rustavi Choir and Mtiebi have carried the tradition internationally, and choirs devoted to Georgian singing now exist in many countries.
