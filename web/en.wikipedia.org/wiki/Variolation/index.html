Variolation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Variolation was an early method of immunisation against smallpox: material from a smallpox sore or scab was deliberately introduced into a healthy person's skin or nose to induce a (usually) milder infection and lasting immunity. The word comes from variola, the Latin name of the smallpox virus.

History

The practice was used for centuries in China and India and was current in the Ottoman Empire when Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, wife of the British ambassador, had her children inoculated and promoted the practice in Britain from 1718–1721. In Boston in 1721, Cotton Mather — who had learned of it from Onesimus, an enslaved African man — championed variolation during an epidemic, with physician Zabdiel Boylston performing the inoculations amid fierce controversy.

Risks and replacement

Variolation worked — recipients' death rate was far below that of natural smallpox — but it killed roughly 1–2% of those treated and could itself seed outbreaks, since recipients were contagious. It was displaced after 1796 by Edward Jenner's much safer cowpox vaccination, and was eventually banned in many countries once vaccination spread.
