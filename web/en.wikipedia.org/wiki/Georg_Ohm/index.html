Georg Simon Ohm (16 March 1789 – 6 July 1854) was a German physicist and mathematician. He is best known for Ohm's law, the fundamental relationship between voltage, current and electrical resistance. The SI unit of electrical resistance, the ohm (Ω), is named in his honour.

Early life and career

Ohm was born in Erlangen, the son of a locksmith who taught his children mathematics and physics to a high standard. He entered the University of Erlangen in 1805 but, short of money, left to teach in Switzerland. He returned, completed his doctorate in 1811, and spent years as a poorly paid teacher of mathematics and physics, including at the Jesuit Gymnasium in Cologne, where he began his electrical experiments.

Ohm's law

In 1827 Ohm published Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet ("The Galvanic Circuit Investigated Mathematically"), presenting his law that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance: V = IR. Initially the work was received coldly in Germany — the minister of education reportedly declared that a professor who preached such heresies was unworthy to teach science — and Ohm resigned his post.

Recognition

Recognition came first from abroad: the Royal Society awarded him the Copley Medal in 1841. He became professor at the Polytechnic School of Nuremberg in 1833 and finally, in 1852, full professor of experimental physics at the University of Munich, two years before his death. His brother Martin Ohm was a noted mathematician.
