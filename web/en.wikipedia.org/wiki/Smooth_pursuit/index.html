Smooth pursuit

Smooth pursuit describes a type of eye movement in which the eyes remain fixated on a moving object. It is one of two ways that visual animals can voluntarily shift gaze, the other being saccadic eye movements. Pursuit differs from the vestibulo-ocular reflex, which only occurs during movements of the head and serves to stabilise gaze on a stationary object.

Most people are unable to initiate smooth pursuit without a moving visual signal; attempts to follow an imagined moving target result in a series of saccades instead. The pursuit system matches eye velocity to target velocity, typically with a gain close to 1 for targets moving below about 30 degrees per second; faster or unpredictable targets require catch-up saccades to correct position errors.

Neural control

Motion signals from the retina are analysed in visual cortex, notably area MT (middle temporal) and MST, which encode the speed and direction of the target. These areas project via the frontal eye fields and the pontine nuclei to the cerebellum (flocculus, ventral paraflocculus and dorsal vermis), which calibrates pursuit gain and generates the motor command relayed through brainstem ocular motor nuclei to the eye muscles. Pursuit is also predictive: after brief practice with a periodic target motion, the eyes can begin moving before the target and continue smoothly even when the target is briefly hidden.

Development and clinical aspects

Smooth pursuit emerges in infancy over the first months of life and improves through childhood. It is degraded by fatigue, alcohol and many sedative drugs. Abnormal pursuit — reduced gain with frequent catch-up saccades ("cogwheeling") — occurs in cerebellar and parietal lesions, in Parkinson's disease, and has been extensively studied in schizophrenia, where pursuit deficits are a candidate endophenotype. Clinicians test pursuit at the bedside by asking the patient to follow a slowly moving finger.

Optokinetic response

Pursuit works together with the optokinetic system, which responds to motion of the whole visual field (as when watching scenery from a train) with slow following movements interrupted by resetting saccades — optokinetic nystagmus.
