Coal tar

Coal tar is a thick dark liquid which is a by-product of the production of coke and coal gas from coal. It is a type of creosote and a complex mixture of hundreds of organic compounds, chiefly aromatic hydrocarbons. It has both medical and industrial uses; refined fractions provide feedstocks for dyes, drugs, explosives, and road surfacing.

Production

Coal tar is obtained by cooling the gas formed during the destructive distillation of coal to approximately 1,100 °C. The tar condenses from the gas stream and is separated from the aqueous ammonia liquor. Distillation of coal tar yields a series of fractions—light oil, middle oil, heavy oil, and anthracene oil—leaving a residue of pitch.

Industrial significance

Coal tar was the raw material of the nineteenth-century organic chemical industry. In 1856 the eighteen-year-old William Henry Perkin, attempting to synthesize quinine, oxidized aniline derived from coal tar and produced mauveine, the first synthetic dye, launching the aniline dye industry. Benzene, naphthalene, phenol, and anthracene from coal tar became the starting points for pharmaceuticals, saccharin, aspirin, and TNT.

Medical use

Crude coal tar preparations have been used for more than a century in dermatology to treat psoriasis and eczema; they reduce scaling and itching and are still found in medicated shampoos and ointments.

Health hazards

Coal tar contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, several of which are carcinogenic. In 1775 Percivall Pott linked soot exposure to scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps, one of the first occupational cancers identified. Coal tar itself is classified as carcinogenic to humans, and its use in road surfacing has declined in favour of bitumen.
