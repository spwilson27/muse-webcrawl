The Archaic period in North American archaeology is the second stage of cultural development proposed in Gordon Willey and Philip Phillips's 1958 scheme, spanning roughly 8000 to 1000 BC. It follows the Paleo-Indian period and describes societies of hunter-gatherers who adapted to post-glacial environments after the extinction of the megafauna.

Characteristics

Archaic peoples used ground-stone axes and grinding tools, the atlatl (spear-thrower), and a broad-spectrum diet of deer, fish, shellfish, nuts and seeds. Over time they developed regional traditions, semi-sedentary settlements, long-distance exchange in copper and shell, and monumental earthworks such as Poverty Point in Louisiana. In the Eastern Woodlands and Mexico, late Archaic communities began domesticating squash, sunflower, maize and other crops.

Subdivisions

Archaeologists divide the period into Early, Middle and Late Archaic, with timing varying between the Eastern Woodlands, Plains, Great Basin, Southwest and Arctic regions.
