Eadweard Muybridge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Born: Edward James Muggeridge, 9 April 1830, Kingston upon Thames, England

Died: 8 May 1904, Kingston upon Thames

Known for: Chronophotography; motion studies; the zoopraxiscope

Eadweard Muybridge (9 April 1830 – 8 May 1904), born Edward James Muggeridge, was an English photographer known for his pioneering studies of motion and early projection of moving images. Working mainly in California, he used banks of cameras triggered in sequence to record people and animals in movement, work that laid foundations for the motion picture.

Early career

Muybridge emigrated to the United States in the 1850s and, under the name "Helios", became a celebrated landscape photographer of the American West, producing celebrated views of Yosemite and working as an official photographer for the US government and the Central Pacific Railroad.

The horse in motion

Railroad magnate Leland Stanford hired Muybridge to settle the question of whether a galloping horse ever has all four hooves off the ground. In 1878, at Palo Alto, Muybridge's sequential photographs of the mare Sallie Gardner proved that it does — a landmark moment in photography, published around the world. He developed the zoopraxiscope, a device that projected his sequences as moving images, and toured with lectures. At the University of Pennsylvania (1884–87) he produced Animal Locomotion, some 100,000 images of humans and animals in motion, which remains a standard reference for artists and scientists. His work directly influenced Étienne-Jules Marey, Thomas Edison and the development of cinema.

Personal life

In 1874 Muybridge shot and killed Harry Larkyns, his wife's lover, and was acquitted by a jury on grounds of justifiable homicide. He spent his final years in England and died in 1904 in his birthplace, Kingston upon Thames.
