Maianthemum is a genus of flowering plants in the family Asparagaceae (subfamily Nolinoideae). The genus includes about 39 species of herbaceous perennials distributed across the temperate Northern Hemisphere — North America, Europe, and Asia — with a few species extending into the mountains of Central America. Plants grow from spreading rhizomes and are characteristic of moist woodland habitats. Well-known species include the false Solomon's seals of North America and the Eurasian May lily.

Description

Species are rhizomatous herbs with unbranched or branched stems bearing alternate, often broad leaves. Flowers are small, white or greenish, with six tepals (in species formerly placed in Smilacina) or four tepals (in species formerly placed in Maianthemum in the strict sense, such as the May lily). The fruit is a berry, green when immature and ripening to red or, in some species, speckled or dark. The flowers are pollinated by insects, and the berries are eaten by birds and mammals.

Taxonomy

The genus was established by Friedrich Heinrich Wiggers in 1780, taking its name from the Latin Maius (May) and the Greek anthemon (flower), referring to the spring flowering of the type species, Maianthemum bifolium. The genus was long restricted to a few northern species with four-petalled flowers, while the larger American and Asian plants with six tepals were placed in the genus Smilacina. Morphological and molecular studies showed the two groups were not separable, and in modern classifications (including APG IV) Smilacina is treated as a synonym of Maianthemum.

Notable species

  • Maianthemum bifolium — May lily, widespread in European and Asian woodlands.
  • Maianthemum racemosum — feathery false Solomon's seal, North America.
  • Maianthemum stellatum — starry false Solomon's seal, North America and Asia.
  • Maianthemum canadense — Canada mayflower, eastern North America.
  • Maianthemum dilatatum — false lily of the valley, Pacific coasts of North America and Asia.

Uses

Some species are grown as woodland garden ornamentals for their foliage, spring flowers, and berries. Indigenous peoples of North America used various species medicinally and, in some accounts, as food, although the berries of some species are reported to be mildly purgative and the plants are not generally recommended for consumption.
