The Hashemite Kingdom of Iraq was a state located in the Middle East from 1932, when it gained formal independence from the British Mandate, until 1958, when the monarchy was overthrown in the 14 July Revolution. The kingdom was ruled by the Hashemite dynasty, installed under British auspices after the First World War.

Background and mandate

After the Ottoman Empire's defeat in World War I, Britain received a League of Nations mandate over Mesopotamia. Following the Iraqi revolt of 1920, the Cairo Conference of 1921 selected Faisal ibn Husayn — a son of the Sharif of Mecca who had been expelled from Syria by the French — as king. Faisal I was crowned on 23 August 1921. The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930 paved the way for formal independence, and Iraq joined the League of Nations as an independent state on 3 October 1932, though Britain retained air bases and considerable influence.

The independent kingdom

Faisal I died in 1933 and was succeeded by his son Ghazi, whose reign saw the first military coup in the modern Arab world (1936) and growing nationalist agitation. Ghazi died in a car accident in 1939, leaving the four-year-old Faisal II on the throne under the regency of Prince 'Abd al-Ilah. During the Second World War, the Rashid Ali al-Gaylani government of 1941 attempted to align with the Axis, prompting the Anglo-Iraqi War and a second British occupation. Iraq subsequently became a founding member of the United Nations and the Arab League in 1945.

Decline and fall

Oil revenues expanded after the 1950 revenue-sharing agreement, financing development projects under long-serving prime minister Nuri al-Said, but wealth was unevenly shared and the regime was widely seen as a British client. Iraq joined the Baghdad Pact in 1955, and in February 1958 united with Jordan in the Arab Federation. On 14 July 1958, Free Officers led by Abd al-Karim Qasim overthrew the monarchy; King Faisal II, the former regent, and Nuri al-Said were killed, and a republic was proclaimed.
