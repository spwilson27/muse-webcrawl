Andromeda paradox

Different observers' planes of simultaneity can slice distant events weeks apart.

The Andromeda paradox is a thought experiment in special relativity concerning the relativity of simultaneity. It is a version of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, presented by Roger Penrose in his book The Emperor's New Mind (1989), intended to draw out the philosophical consequences of Einstein's theory — in particular, its apparent support for a "block universe" in which past, present, and future all equally exist.

The argument

Two people passing each other on the street have slightly different planes of simultaneity — different sets of distant events that count as happening "now" for each of them. Because the Andromeda Galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away, this tiny difference in orientation is amplified enormously: events in Andromeda that one passer-by counts as simultaneous with the meeting can be days or weeks apart from the events the other counts as simultaneous. Penrose's example imagines an Andromedan fleet launched toward Earth: for one pedestrian the decision to launch may already have been made days ago, while for the other the fleet's council has not yet even met.

Interpretation

Philosophers C. W. Rietdijk (1966) and Hilary Putnam (1967) had argued that relativity leaves no room for a privileged present: if what is real depends on an observer's frame, and all frames are equally valid, then future events must be as real as past ones — the doctrine of eternalism. Critics respond that the argument confuses simultaneity conventions with ontology, or that quantum mechanics and a proper account of becoming may avoid the conclusion. The paradox remains a standard illustration in discussions of the philosophy of time.
