Unix philosophy

The Unix philosophy, originated by Ken Thompson, is a set of cultural norms and philosophical approaches to minimalist, modular software development. It is based on the experience of the leading developers of the Unix operating system. Early Unix developers were important in bringing the concepts of modularity and reusability into software engineering practice, spawning a "software tools" movement.

Formulations

Doug McIlroy, inventor of Unix pipes, summarized the philosophy as follows:

"Write programs that do one thing and do it well. Write programs to work together. Write programs to handle text streams, because that is a universal interface."

In their 1978 Bell System Technical Journal paper and in The Unix Programming Environment (1984), Brian Kernighan and Rob Pike emphasized simplicity, clarity and generality. Peter H. Salus's A Quarter-Century of Unix (1994) distilled three principles: write programs that do one thing well; write programs that work together; write programs that handle text streams. Eric S. Raymond's The Art of Unix Programming (2003) expanded the philosophy into seventeen rules, including the Rule of Modularity ("build simple parts connected by clean interfaces"), the Rule of Clarity, the Rule of Composition, the Rule of Least Surprise, and "when in doubt, use brute force."

Related principles

The philosophy is closely associated with the KISS principle ("keep it simple, stupid"), the idea that "everything is a file," the pipe-and-filter architecture that lets the output of one program become the input of another, and the preference for plain text as a universal data format. It stands in contrast to monolithic design, reflected in the joking counter-slogan "Worse is better" and, later, in critiques such as The UNIX-HATERS Handbook (1994).

Influence

The philosophy shaped the GNU tools, the POSIX standard, scripting culture, and microservice architectures in modern computing, which recast "do one thing well" at the level of network services.
