Electrophilic addition

Electrophilic addition
Reaction typeAddition reaction
Typical substratesAlkenes and alkynes
Key intermediateCarbocation (or cyclic halonium ion)
RegiochemistryMarkovnikov's rule (usually)

In organic chemistry, an electrophilic addition reaction is an addition reaction where a chemical compound containing a double or triple bond is broken, and two new single bonds are formed. The driving force is the attack of an electron-seeking species (an electrophile, E⁺) on the electron-rich π bond, producing a carbocation intermediate that is then attacked by a nucleophile.

Mechanism

The reaction typically proceeds in two steps. First, the π electrons of the alkene attack the electrophile, forming a new σ bond and leaving a positively charged carbocation on the other carbon. Second, a nucleophile donates an electron pair to the carbocation, completing the addition across the former double bond. When the electrophile is a halogen, a cyclic halonium ion forms instead, leading to anti addition stereochemistry.

Typical reactions

  • Hydrohalogenation: addition of HBr or HCl across a double bond, following Markovnikov's rule — the hydrogen attaches to the carbon already bearing more hydrogens.
  • Halogenation: addition of Br₂ or Cl₂ to give vicinal dihalides.
  • Acid-catalysed hydration: addition of water to form alcohols, via a carbocation.
  • Oxymercuration and hydroboration–oxidation: routes to Markovnikov and anti-Markovnikov alcohols respectively.

Markovnikov's rule

The orientation of addition is usually predicted by Markovnikov's rule, rationalised by the relative stability of the carbocation intermediate: the electrophile adds so as to place the positive charge on the more substituted carbon, which is stabilised by hyperconjugation and inductive effects. Exceptions arise with HBr in the presence of peroxides, which proceeds by a radical mechanism (the peroxide effect).
