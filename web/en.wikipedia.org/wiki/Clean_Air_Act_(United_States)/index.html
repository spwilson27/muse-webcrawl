The Clean Air Act is the primary federal law governing air emissions in the United States. Enacted in 1963 and substantially expanded in 1970 under President Richard Nixon, it authorizes the EPA to set National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for six criteria pollutants: particulate matter, ozone, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide, and lead.

Major amendments

  • 1970: technology-forcing vehicle standards cutting auto emissions by 90%; state implementation plans (SIPs).
  • 1977: Prevention of Significant Deterioration rules for clean-air regions.
  • 1990: cap-and-trade program for acid rain (SO2 allowances) and regulation of 187 hazardous air pollutants — signed by George H. W. Bush.

Impact

Between 1970 and 2020, aggregate US emissions of the six criteria pollutants fell by roughly 78% even as GDP tripled. Studies credit the acid rain program with achieving SO2 cuts at a fraction of projected cost, influencing emissions trading design worldwide.

Greenhouse gases

In Massachusetts v. EPA (2007), the Supreme Court held that carbon dioxide is an "air pollutant" under the Act, leading the EPA to regulate greenhouse gases.
