Kurdish nationalism

Kurdish nationalism is a political movement that seeks self-determination, autonomy, or independence for the Kurds, an ethnic group native to Kurdistan, a mountainous region divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria. The Kurds are often described as the largest ethnic group without a state of their own.

Origins

Early expressions of Kurdish identity appeared in works such as the Sharafnama (1597) and the poetry of Ahmad Khani, whose epic Mem û Zîn (1692) lamented Kurdish disunity. Modern nationalism emerged in the late Ottoman Empire, with uprisings led by tribal chiefs such as Sheikh Ubeydullah in 1880, and after World War I, when the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) contemplated a Kurdish state — a prospect removed by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

Developments by country

In Iraq, Kurdish rebellions under Mustafa Barzani led to an autonomy agreement in 1970, renewed conflict, and eventually the Kurdistan Regional Government after 1991; an independence referendum in 2017 voted overwhelmingly for independence but was rejected by Baghdad. In Turkey, the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK), founded by Abdullah Öcalan in 1978, waged an armed insurgency from 1984. In Iran, the short-lived Republic of Mahabad (1946) remains a symbol. In Syria, Kurdish-led forces established an autonomous administration in Rojava during the civil war.

Symbols

The Kurdish flag — red, white and green with a golden sun of twenty-one rays — and the celebration of Newroz, the spring new year, are central symbols of the movement.
