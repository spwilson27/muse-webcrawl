Waterwheel

A waterwheel is a machine for converting the energy of flowing or falling water into useful forms of power, often in a watermill. A waterwheel consists of a wheel with paddles or buckets arranged on the outside rim forming the driving surface, and a horizontal or vertical axle.

Types

The principal types are distinguished by the point at which water strikes the wheel. In an undershot wheel the current strikes the bottom of the wheel; in a breastshot wheel water strikes at mid-height; in an overshot wheel water fills buckets near the top, giving the highest efficiency, typically 60–85 percent. Pitchback and reverse overshot variants also exist. Vertical wheels predominate in the European tradition, while horizontal wheels (the "Greek" or "Norse" type) drive millstones directly without gearing.

History

Waterwheels appeared in the Hellenistic world by the 3rd century BCE and spread through the Roman Empire for grinding grain, sawing, fulling cloth, and powering forge hammers. By the Middle Ages they were the principal industrial prime mover in Europe; the Domesday Book of 1086 lists more than 5,000 mills in England. Waterwheels began to be displaced by water turbines and steam engines in the 19th century, though they survive in heritage mills and small hydro applications.

Mechanics

Power depends on the flow rate and head of water. Pit wheels, wallower gears, and great spur wheels in mills converted the wheel's slow rotation into the higher speeds needed by millstones and other machinery.
