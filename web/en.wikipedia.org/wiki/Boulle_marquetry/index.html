Boulle marquetry

Boulle marquetry is a type of rich marquetry, or inlay process, developed by the French cabinetmaker André Charles Boulle (1642–1732), cabinetmaker to Louis XIV. It involves veneering furniture with tortoiseshell inlaid with brass, pewter, and other metals in elaborate designs of arabesques and scrollwork.

Boulle marquetry
Named after: André Charles Boulle
Period: late 17th–18th century
Materials: Tortoiseshell, brass, pewter, ebony

Technique

Sheets of tortoiseshell and brass were glued together in a packet and cut with a fret saw from a pattern, so that the brass design and the shell design were mirror counterparts. The brass-inlaid shell, called première partie, and the shell-inlaid brass, contre partie, could furnish two pieces from one cutting. The dark shell was often backed with red or coloured foil to enrich its figure.

Boulle and Versailles

Boulle supplied commodes, desks, clocks, and cabinets for Versailles and the great aristocratic houses of Paris. His workshop in the Louvre set the standard for French luxury furniture, and his name became a generic term — "Buhl work" in English — for this kind of inlay.

Later use

Boulle work was imitated throughout the 18th and 19th centuries, especially in Napoleon III furniture, often using shell substitutes. Because the materials expand at different rates, original Boulle surfaces are notoriously fragile and a major challenge for conservators.
