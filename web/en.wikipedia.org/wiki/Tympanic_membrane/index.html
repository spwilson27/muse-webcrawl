Tympanic membrane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The tympanic membrane, commonly called the eardrum, is a thin, cone-shaped membrane that separates the external ear from the middle ear in humans and other tetrapods. Its function is to transmit sound vibrations from the air to the ossicles inside the middle ear, and then to the oval window in the fluid-filled cochlea.

Anatomy

The membrane is roughly 8–10 mm in diameter and less than 0.1 mm thick. It consists of three layers: an outer layer of skin continuous with the ear canal, a middle fibrous layer that gives it stiffness, and an inner layer of mucous membrane. The handle of the malleus, the largest of the ossicles, is attached to its inner surface and pulls its centre inward, forming the umbo.

Parts

The membrane is divided into the larger, taut pars tensa and the smaller, flaccid pars flaccida (Shrapnell's membrane) above the lateral process of the malleus.

Clinical significance

The eardrum may be perforated by infection (otitis media), trauma, or sudden pressure changes (barotrauma). Most small perforations heal spontaneously, but larger ones may require surgical repair by tympanoplasty. Examination with an otoscope reveals the membrane's characteristic cone of light reflection.
