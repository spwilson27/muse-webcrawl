Jerusalem Light Rail

The Jerusalem Light Rail is a light rail system serving Jerusalem, Israel. Its first line, the Red Line, opened to passengers on 19 August 2011 after years of construction delays, and runs roughly 13.9 kilometres (8.6 miles) from Pisgat Ze'ev in the north-east of the city, through the city centre along Jaffa Road, to Mount Herzl in the south-west. The line has 23 stations and uses low-floor Alstom Citadis trams.

History

The light rail was planned as the backbone of a new public-transport network for Jerusalem, intended to relieve severe traffic congestion in the city centre. Construction of the Red Line began in 2002 under the CityPass consortium, which held a 30-year concession to build and operate the line. The project became notorious for delays, cost overruns and disputes between the consortium, the Jerusalem municipality and the Israeli government; Jaffa Road was dug up for years, badly affecting businesses along the route. Trial running began in 2010 and passenger service, initially free of charge, started in August 2011; full fare collection began that December.

A prominent feature of the line is the Chords Bridge (Bridge of Strings), a cable-stayed bridge designed by Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava at the western entrance to the city, which carries the trams between the central bus station area and Herzl Boulevard.

Extensions and new lines

The Red Line has been extended at both ends: north-eastwards beyond Pisgat Ze'ev towards Neve Yaakov, and south-westwards from Mount Herzl to the Hadassah Ein Kerem hospital campus. Two further lines form the rest of the planned network: the Green Line, linking the Hebrew University campuses at Mount Scopus and Givat Ram via the city centre and the southern neighbourhood of Malha, and the Blue Line, running from Ramot in the north to Gilo in the south. After disputes with the original operator, operation of the Red Line passed in 2021 to a new concessionaire, Cfir, a partnership of the Israeli company Shapir and the Spanish builder CAF, which also supplies new rolling stock for the extensions.

Ridership and criticism

The Red Line carries well over 100,000 passengers on a typical weekday and is credited with reviving Jaffa Road and the city centre. The project has also attracted international criticism because the line serves Israeli settlements in East Jerusalem, including Pisgat Ze'ev and French Hill, which most of the international community considers illegal under international law; the French companies Veolia and Alstom faced boycott campaigns over their early involvement. Trains and stations in Jerusalem have occasionally been targeted in stoning attacks and, during periods of unrest, the service through the Shuafat area has been suspended.
