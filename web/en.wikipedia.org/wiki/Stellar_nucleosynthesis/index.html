Stellar nucleosynthesis

In astrophysics, stellar nucleosynthesis is the creation of chemical elements by nuclear fusion reactions within stars. Stellar nucleosynthesis has occurred since the original creation of hydrogen, helium and lithium during the Big Bang. As a predictive theory, it yields critical clues to stellar evolution, explaining observed elemental abundances and the energy that powers stars. It was placed on firm theoretical ground by Fred Hoyle's work and the landmark 1957 B²FH paper by E. Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William A. Fowler and Hoyle.

Hydrogen burning

Stars begin life fusing hydrogen into helium. Low-mass stars such as the Sun use the proton–proton chain, in which four protons ultimately combine to form a helium-4 nucleus, releasing positrons, neutrinos and energy. In more massive stars the CNO cycle dominates: carbon, nitrogen and oxygen nuclei act as catalysts in a sequence that likewise converts hydrogen to helium. The energy released sustains the star against gravitational collapse for millions to billions of years.

Helium burning and beyond

When core hydrogen is exhausted, the core contracts and heats until helium fusion ignites: the triple-alpha process fuses three helium-4 nuclei into carbon-12, and further captures produce oxygen-16. Successive stages in massive stars burn carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon in concentric shells, each stage hotter and shorter-lived than the last. The sequence ends at iron and nickel, whose nuclei are the most tightly bound: fusion beyond iron consumes energy rather than releasing it.

Neutron capture processes

Elements heavier than iron are built mainly by neutron capture. In the slow s-process, occurring in asymptotic giant branch stars, neutrons are captured one at a time with beta decays in between, building elements up to lead and bismuth. The rapid r-process, now associated with supernovae and neutron star mergers, floods nuclei with neutrons, producing about half of the heavy element isotopes including gold and platinum.

Explosive nucleosynthesis

When a massive star's iron core collapses, the ensuing core-collapse supernova briefly reaches temperatures that forge additional elements and disperses the star's enriched material into the interstellar medium, where it becomes the raw material for later generations of stars and planets.
