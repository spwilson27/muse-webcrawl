Tartan

Tartan (Scottish Gaelic: breacan) is a patterned cloth consisting of criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands in multiple colours, woven in a simple twill. Tartans originated in woven wool, but are now made in many materials, and are particularly associated with Scotland and Scottish clans. The pattern of a tartan is called a sett; kilts almost always have tartan patterns.

Construction

A tartan is woven with a pre-dyed warp and weft in a 2/2 twill. The sequence of coloured threads, the threadcount, is identical in warp and weft, so the pattern appears the same on both sides; where warp and weft of the same colour cross, the colour is pure, and where different colours cross, a half-tone mixture results.

History

Patterned checked cloth has been worn in Scotland since at least the 3rd–4th centuries AD (the Falkirk tartan). Tartan became emblematic of Highland dress, and after the Jacobite rising of 1745 the Dress Act of 1746 banned Highland dress (except for soldiers of Highland regiments) until its repeal in 1782. In the 19th century, fuelled by romanticism and royal patronage under Queen Victoria, specific setts became firmly attached to particular clans and regiments — a system partly codified, and partly invented, during that period.

Modern use

Today thousands of tartans are recorded in the Scottish Tartans Register, including clan, military, district, corporate and commemorative designs. Tartan features in Highland dress, fashion, and ceremonies such as Tartan Day in North America and Australasia.
