Zoosemiotics

Zoosemiotics is the study of animal communication and sign processes in non-human animals. As a branch of biosemiotics, it examines how animals use signs — sounds, gestures, odours, colours, and displays — to convey information to members of their own and other species, and how those signs are produced, perceived, and interpreted.

History

The term was introduced in 1963 by the semiotician Thomas Sebeok, who argued that the study of animal communication should be integrated into the general science of signs founded by Charles Sanders Peirce. Zoosemiotics drew on the work of Jakob von Uexküll, whose concept of the Umwelt — the perceptual world particular to each species — emphasized that signs exist only in relation to an organism's sensory and functional capacities.

Scope

Research topics include the waggle dance of honeybees, alarm calls in vervet monkeys that distinguish predator types, whale song, chemical signalling in ants, and the visual displays of birds and cephalopods. Zoosemioticians distinguish communication within a species from interactions between species, and study the functions of signs — from mating and territory defence to deception and mimicry. The field has contributed to debates about whether animal signalling constitutes a form of language and about the ethics of how humans interpret animal behaviour.
