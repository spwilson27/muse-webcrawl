Fifth Party System

Fifth Party System
Era: c. 1932/1936 – 1968/1980
Dominant coalition: New Deal (Democratic)
Key figure: Franklin D. Roosevelt
Preceded by: Fourth Party System (Progressive Era)

The Fifth Party System, also called the New Deal Party System, is the period of American political history that began with the realigning elections of the 1930s and is generally dated from about 1932 (or 1936) to the late 1960s or 1970s. Political scientists use the "party system" framework to describe eras in which a stable coalition and a characteristic set of issues dominate national politics; the Fifth System was defined by the Democratic New Deal coalition assembled under President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

The New Deal coalition

In response to the Great Depression, Roosevelt united urban workers and organised labour, white Southerners, African Americans (who migrated in large numbers from the Republican Party of Lincoln), Catholics and other ethnic voters, farmers, intellectuals, and the urban machines behind an activist federal government. The realigning elections of 1932 and 1936 gave Democrats landslide victories and durable congressional majorities, and the central policy cleavage of the era was the size and role of the welfare and regulatory state created by the New Deal.

Politics of the era

Republicans, competitive mainly when they nominated moderate candidates such as Dwight D. Eisenhower (who accepted most of the New Deal), were the minority party for most of the period. The coalition's internal contradiction — its dependence on both segregationist white Southerners and Black voters — was papered over for a generation. It began to fracture over civil rights in the 1960s: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, passed by Democratic administrations, drove the white South toward the Republicans, while the Vietnam War split the party's liberal wing.

End of the system

Scholars debate when the Fifth Party System ended: candidates include 1968 (George Wallace's third-party run and Richard Nixon's southern strategy), 1980 (Ronald Reagan's election and the consolidation of a conservative Republican coalition), or a gradual "dealignment" rather than a clean realignment into a Sixth Party System. What is clear is that the New Deal alignment, built in the crisis of the 1930s, shaped American politics for roughly four decades.
