Reverse slope defence

A reverse slope defence is a military tactic in which defending troops are positioned just behind the crest of a hill or ridge, on the slope facing away from the enemy. The position shields the defenders from direct fire and observation, and forces an attacker who crests the hill to silhouette themselves against the skyline while coming into close range of the defence.

Advantages

The reverse slope protects troops from long-range direct fire and from artillery observed from the enemy's side, and it conceals the strength and disposition of the defence. Attackers cresting the ridge lose the support of their own direct-fire weapons and expose themselves suddenly at short range, where the defenders' weapons are most effective.

Historical use

The tactic is closely associated with the Duke of Wellington, who used it repeatedly in the Peninsular War and at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815, where his infantry sheltered behind the ridge of Mont-Saint-Jean. Reverse slope positions were also widely used in trench warfare and remain part of modern defensive doctrine.
