Homothetic transformation

In mathematics, a homothety (or homothecy, or homothetic transformation) is a transformation of an affine space determined by a point S called its center and a nonzero number k called its ratio, which sends point M to a point N by the rule that the vector from S to N equals k times the vector from S to M. In other words, it fixes S, and sends each point M to another point N located on the line SM but at a distance |k| times as far from S, on the same side if k > 0 and on the opposite side if k < 0.

Properties

Homotheties preserve angles, parallelism, and ratios of distances, mapping any figure to a similar figure; when |k| > 1 the image is enlarged, and when 0 < |k| < 1 it is shrunk. The composition of two homotheties with different centers is either another homothety or a translation. Circles map to circles, and any two circles are related by homotheties whose centers lie on the line joining their centers — a fact used in solving geometric problems and in Monge's theorem.

Applications

The concept underlies scaling in Euclidean geometry and appears in the study of similar figures, pantographs, perspective drawing, and the homothety centers of inscribed and circumscribed circles of triangles, such as the Feuerbach hyperbola context and homothety at the centroid mapping a triangle to its medial triangle with ratio −1/2.
