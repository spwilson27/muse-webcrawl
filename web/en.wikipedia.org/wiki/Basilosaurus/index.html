Basilosaurus


Basilosaurus cetoides skeleton

Basilosaurus (meaning "king lizard") is a genus of large, extinct archaeocete whale that lived during the late Eocene, roughly 41 to 35 million years ago, in the Tethys Sea and the shallow seas covering what is now the southeastern United States and Egypt. One of the earliest fully aquatic whales, it was a top predator of its ecosystem.

Description and discovery

Basilosaurus had an extremely elongated, serpentine body — the trunk vertebrae being much lengthened — reaching about 15–20 metres (49–66 ft) in the larger species B. cetoides. Its head was relatively small for its size and its brain small compared with modern whales; it lacked the melon organ used for echolocation by modern toothed whales, but possessed external hind limbs, about 0.6 metres long with a knee and toes, far too small for walking and perhaps used as claspers during mating.

Fossils found in Alabama and Louisiana in the 1830s were at first interpreted as those of a giant sea serpent or reptile — hence the name "king lizard", proposed by Richard Harlan. Richard Owen recognised the remains as mammalian and proposed the name Zeuglodon, but priority preserved Basilosaurus. Albert Koch notoriously assembled a composite "Hydrarchos" sea-serpent skeleton from multiple specimens. Rich fossil beds at Wadi El Hitan (Whale Valley) in Egypt preserve hundreds of skeletons. Basilosaurus is the state fossil of both Mississippi and Alabama.
