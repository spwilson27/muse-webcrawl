Square-free integer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a square-free integer (or squarefree integer) is an integer that is divisible by no square number other than 1. That is, in its prime factorisation no prime factor occurs more than once; equivalently, an integer n is square-free if and only if for every prime p dividing n, p does not divide n/p. For example, 10 = 2 × 5 is square-free, but 18 = 2 × 3 × 3 is not, because 9 = 3² divides it. The number 1 is conventionally counted as square-free.

Characterisations

An integer n is square-free if and only if the Möbius function μ(n) is non-zero; in that case μ(n) = ±1 according to whether the number of prime factors is even or odd. Every integer can be written uniquely as the product of a square-free integer and a perfect square, which underlies many arguments in analytic number theory.

Distribution

The proportion of square-free integers among the first N integers tends to 6/π² ≈ 0.6079 as N grows — so a randomly chosen large integer has about a 61% chance of being square-free. This constant is 1/ζ(2), the reciprocal of the value of the Riemann zeta function at 2. The error term in this count is connected with deep questions about the zeta function; the still-unproved Mertens conjecture, had it been true, would have given particularly sharp bounds.

Applications and generalisations

Square-free numbers classify algebraic objects: quadratic fields correspond to square-free integers (their discriminants), and square-free monomials appear in combinatorics and algebraic geometry. The notion generalises to square-free elements of unique factorisation domains and to square-free polynomials, where a polynomial is square-free when it has no repeated irreducible factor — testable by computing the greatest common divisor with its derivative.
