ALGOL 68 (short for Algorithmic Language 1968) is an imperative programming language that was conceived as a successor to the ALGOL 60 programming language, designed to facilitate a wider range of applications and to supply a language whose specification could be formally precise. It was defined by the IFIP Working Group 2.1 and described in the 1968 Report on the Algorithmic Language ALGOL 68, edited by Adriaan van Wijngaarden and colleagues.

Design

ALGOL 68 introduced a systematically orthogonal design: a small set of primitive concepts — types ("modes"), references, arrays with flexible bounds, structures, unions, procedures — that could be combined freely, together with operator definitions, concurrency primitives (semaphores and parallel clauses), and a powerful system of coercions (automatic conversions such as dereferencing and widening). Its syntax distinguished bold "stropped" keywords in the strict language from representations used in actual hardware.

Definition and reception

The language was specified using a two-level formalism, the van Wijngaarden grammar, which made the report famously difficult to read — C. A. R. Hoare, a committee member who dissented, called aspects of it incomprehensible, and Edsger Dijkstra was also a prominent critic. A minority report objected to the language's complexity. Nevertheless, many of its ideas were influential: its type system and mode declarations influenced later languages, its case expressions and operator precedences entered the mainstream, and features such as returning values from conditional expressions foreshadowed modern practice. Stephen Bourne, author of the Bourne shell, drew its syntax in part from ALGOL 68.
