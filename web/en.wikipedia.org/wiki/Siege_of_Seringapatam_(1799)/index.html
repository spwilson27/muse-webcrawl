Siege of Seringapatam (1799)

The siege of Seringapatam was the final confrontation of the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, fought between the British East India Company, supported by the Nizam of Hyderabad, and the Kingdom of Mysore under Tipu Sultan. The siege of Tipu's island capital, Seringapatam (modern Srirangapatna, on the Kaveri River in Karnataka), lasted from 5 April to 4 May 1799 and ended with the storming of the fortress and the death of Tipu Sultan, decisively extending British dominance over southern India.

Background

Tipu Sultan, ruler of Mysore, had fought the British in a series of wars alongside his father Hyder Ali. Suspected of seeking an alliance with Revolutionary France, he was attacked in 1799 by a large Company army under General George Harris advancing from Madras, accompanied by a Bombay force under General Stuart, which defeated Tipu's army at Seedaseer, and by troops of the Nizam. Tipu fell back on his capital and was invested in early April.

The siege and storming

Seringapatam stood on an island in the Kaveri, strongly fortified at its western end. Harris established batteries on the west bank, and after weeks of bombardment a breach was opened in the north-western wall. On 4 May 1799 the storming party, commanded by Major-General David Baird — who had once been held prisoner in the city by Tipu — crossed the river in daylight and carried the breach in a rapid assault. Tipu Sultan was killed fighting at one of the gateways; his body was found under a pile of the slain. The city was subjected to looting before order was restored.

Aftermath

Mysore was partitioned: the Company annexed coastal and border districts, the Nizam received territory (later partly ceded to the British), and a reduced Mysore state was restored to the Hindu Wodeyar dynasty in the person of the child Maharaja Krishnaraja Wodeyar III, under a subsidiary alliance. Colonel Arthur Wellesley, the future Duke of Wellington, was appointed to command the garrison and district; the siege was among his early Indian experiences. Tipu's treasures, including his famous tiger automaton "Tipu's Tiger", were taken to Britain. The victory is commemorated by the Seringapatam medal, and the storming long figured in British imperial imagination; it also features in fiction, including Bernard Cornwell's novel Sharpe's Tiger.
