Vitiligo is a chronic autoimmune disorder that causes patches of skin to lose their pigment. The patches occur when melanocytes, the cells that produce melanin, are destroyed by the immune system or cease to function. The resulting pale patches can appear anywhere on the body and are more noticeable in people with darker skin; hair growing in affected areas may also turn white.

Cause and classification

The exact cause is multifactorial, combining genetic susceptibility with autoimmune and oxidative-stress mechanisms; onset is often triggered by stress, sunburn or skin injury (the Koebner phenomenon). Vitiligo is associated with other autoimmune diseases such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis and type 1 diabetes. The most common form, non-segmental (generalized) vitiligo, is roughly symmetric, while segmental vitiligo affects one side or segment of the body and tends to appear earlier and stabilize.

Treatment

There is no cure, but treatments can slow progression and sometimes restore pigment. Options include topical corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, narrowband ultraviolet B phototherapy, and the topical JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib cream, approved for repigmentation in 2022. Surgical grafting can help stable segmental cases, while complete depigmentation of remaining skin is occasionally chosen for very extensive disease. Sunscreen is recommended because affected skin burns easily.

Society and culture

Vitiligo is not contagious or life-threatening, but it can have significant psychological effects, particularly in cultures where it carries stigma. High-profile figures with the condition, including model Winnie Harlow, have increased public awareness.
