The Baltic Way or Baltic Chain of Freedom was a peaceful political demonstration on 23 August 1989 in which approximately two million people joined hands to form a human chain about 690 kilometres (430 mi) long across the three Baltic states—Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania—then occupied republics of the Soviet Union. The chain linked the capitals Tallinn, Riga, and Vilnius.

Background

The date marked the 50th anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, whose secret protocols assigned the Baltic states to the Soviet sphere, leading to their annexation in 1940—an annexation never recognised by most Western states. During the Singing Revolution, the popular fronts of the three nations coordinated a protest demanding public acknowledgement of the pact and restoration of independence.

The demonstration

At 7 p.m. local time on 23 August 1989, participants joined hands for 15 minutes along a route traced through cities, towns, and countryside, assigned in advance by organisers with the aid of radio broadcasts. In Lithuania the chain also included a branch to honour victims of deportations. The event drew worldwide media coverage as a symbol of nonviolent resistance. Moscow denounced the protest, but within months Lithuania declared restored independence (11 March 1990); all three states regained full independence in 1991.

Legacy

UNESCO inscribed the documentary heritage of the Baltic Way in the Memory of the World Register in 2009. The event inspired later human chains, including Catalonia's Via Catalana (2013) and the Hong Kong Way (2019).
