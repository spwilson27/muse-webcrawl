Vale tudo (Portuguese pronunciation: [ˈvali ˈtudu]; English: anything goes) is a Brazilian full-contact combat sport tradition with very few rules, in which fighters from different martial arts disciplines compete against one another. Emerging in the 1920s, vale tudo became the direct forerunner of modern mixed martial arts (MMA).

History

A precursor of vale tudo appeared in Brazilian circuses and variety shows in the 1920s, where fighters of different styles were matched against each other. The tradition became closely associated with the Gracie family, who promoted "Gracie Challenge" matches to demonstrate the effectiveness of Brazilian jiu-jitsu. In 1959 the television programme Heróis do Ringue ("Heroes of the Ring") brought vale tudo to a national audience, though broadcasts were curtailed after a serious injury in a televised bout led to the show's cancellation.

During the 1960s–1980s vale tudo thrived in Rio de Janeiro gyms and arenas, often with a fierce rivalry between Brazilian jiu-jitsu practitioners and fighters of luta livre, a Brazilian submission wrestling style. Bouts were fought bare-knuckle, frequently in a ring, and commonly ended by knockout, submission or referee stoppage.

Influence

The style-versus-style spectacle of vale tudo inspired the creation of the Ultimate Fighting Championship (UFC) in the United States in 1993, co-founded by Rorion Gracie. As MMA adopted unified rules in the 2000s, traditional vale tudo events became rarer in Brazil, although promotions using permissive rulesets—such as Japan's PRIDE Fighting Championships, which allowed soccer kicks and knees to a grounded opponent—carried on aspects of the vale tudo ethos.
