Body donation
Donated bodies are central to anatomical education
PurposeMedical education, research, surgical training
RecipientsMedical schools, university anatomy programmes

Body donation, also known as whole body donation, is the donation of the whole body after death for research and education. Donated bodies are used by medical students, physicians, and other health professionals to study anatomy, practise and develop surgical techniques, and conduct research. Body donation is distinct from organ donation, in which specific organs or tissues are transplanted into living recipients.

Process

Prospective donors typically register during life with a medical school, university anatomy department, or a state anatomical board, giving written consent; next of kin may also be able to donate a deceased relative's body in some jurisdictions. After death, the body is transported to the receiving institution, screened for infectious diseases (such as HIV or hepatitis) that would prevent acceptance, and embalmed. Remains may be used for a period ranging from several months to a few years, after which they are usually cremated and the ashes returned to the family or interred in a memorial plot, often with a service of thanksgiving attended by students and families.

Legal framework

In the United States, the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act (first adopted 1968, revised since) governs anatomical gifts. In the United Kingdom, donation is regulated under the Human Tissue Act 2004, overseen by the Human Tissue Authority, and requires the donor's own written consent. Acceptance is never guaranteed: institutions may decline bodies due to factors such as certain infections, major trauma, obesity, autopsy, or lack of storage capacity.

Ethics and public attitudes

Ethical practice emphasises informed, voluntary consent and respectful treatment; many anatomy departments hold memorial ceremonies honouring donors, sometimes called "silent teachers" in East Asian traditions, where students learn gratitude alongside anatomy. Historically, the scarcity of legally available bodies drove grave robbing and the Anatomy Act 1832 in Britain — modern donation programmes depend on public trust and altruism.
