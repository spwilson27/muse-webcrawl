Nonsense

Nonsense is a form of communication — in speech, writing or any other symbolic system — that lacks coherent meaning. It may be unintentional, arising from error or misunderstanding, or deliberate, as in nonsense literature, comedy, ritual and certain forms of poetry. The study of nonsense touches on linguistics, philosophy of language, logic and literary criticism.

Types

  • Literary nonsense — a genre that balances elements of sense and non-sense to produce playful, often humorous effects. Its best-known practitioners are Edward Lear, whose Book of Nonsense (1846) popularised the limerick, and Lewis Carroll, whose poem "Jabberwocky" (in Through the Looking-Glass, 1871) uses invented words within perfectly grammatical English sentences.
  • Phonetic nonsense — strings of sounds with no lexical meaning, such as glossolalia or scat singing.
  • Nonsense in philosophy — logical positivists such as A. J. Ayer held that statements which are neither analytic nor empirically verifiable are literally nonsensical; Ludwig Wittgenstein argued that many philosophical problems arise from nonsense generated by misunderstanding the logic of language.
  • Nonsense syllables — in experimental psychology, Hermann Ebbinghaus used meaningless syllables (consonant–vowel–consonant combinations) to study memory and the forgetting curve without interference from prior associations.

In science

The term appears in technical compounds: in molecular biology, a nonsense mutation is a point mutation that converts a codon into a stop codon, prematurely terminating protein synthesis; nonsense-mediated decay is the cellular surveillance pathway that degrades such faulty messenger RNAs. In genetics education, "nonsense" DNA was once used loosely for non-coding sequences, a usage now largely avoided.
