Decision tree

A decision tree is a decision support tool that uses a tree-like model of decisions and their possible consequences, including chance event outcomes, resource costs and utility. It is one way to display an algorithm that only contains conditional control statements. Decision trees are commonly used in operations research, decision analysis, and in machine learning, where a decision tree model predicts the value of a target variable based on several input variables.

Structure

A decision tree consists of internal nodes representing tests on attributes, branches representing the outcomes of tests, and leaf nodes representing class labels or probability distributions. Square nodes conventionally denote decisions, circles denote chance events, and triangles denote endpoints. Trees can be drawn with the root at the top or at the left; for decision support the tree is often drawn left to right so that many branches can be read comfortably.

Machine learning

In data mining and machine learning, decision tree learning constructs a tree from labelled training examples. Algorithms such as ID3, C4.5, C5.0 and CART (Classification and Regression Trees) grow trees by recursively partitioning data to maximise information gain or minimise impurity measures such as the Gini index or entropy. Trees are prone to overfitting, countered by pruning and by ensemble methods such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees, which combine many trees for substantially improved accuracy.

Advantages and disadvantages

Decision trees are simple to understand and interpret, require little data preparation, and can handle both numerical and categorical data. However, small variations in the training data can produce very different trees, and very deep trees may model noise rather than structure.
