Spawn is a fictional superhero/antihero appearing in American comic books published by Image Comics, created by artist and writer Todd McFarlane. The character first appeared in Spawn #1 (May 1992), one of the flagship launches of Image Comics, and its first issue sold 1.7 million copies. The series has run for more than 350 issues, making Spawn one of the longest-running creator-owned superhero titles, and has spawned numerous spin-offs, a 1997 live-action film, an acclaimed HBO animated series (1997–1999), and one of the most successful action-figure lines in the industry through McFarlane Toys.

Fictional biography

Lieutenant Colonel Albert "Al" Simmons, a highly decorated Marine and CIA assassin, was murdered by his own agency — burned to death on the orders of his superior, Jason Wynn, by his colleague Chapel. In Hell, Simmons made a pact with the demon Malebolgia: he would serve as a Hellspawn, a general in Hell's army, in exchange for seeing his wife Wanda once more. Returned to Earth five years later with necroplasmic powers, a living symbiotic costume, and a "power counter" (9,999 units) that, once exhausted, would condemn him to Hell permanently, Spawn instead turned against his masters, protecting New York's homeless in Rat City and fighting both Heaven's and Hell's agents — including the demonic clown the Violator and the angelic warrior Angela.

Publication and influence

McFarlane wrote and drew the early issues, with guest scripts by Alan Moore, Neil Gaiman, Dave Sim and Frank Miller in the celebrated #8–11 run; Gaiman's creation of Angela and the medieval Spawn later produced a major lawsuit confirming creators' rights in work-for-hire disputes. The book's dark tone, McFarlane's artwork and its creator-ownership model made Spawn a defining title of 1990s comics. Key later arcs include Greg Capullo's long run as artist, the death of Malebolgia, the Armageddon storyline, and the 2019 "Spawn Universe" expansion, as Spawn #300–301 surpassed Cerebus as the longest-running independent comic book series.
