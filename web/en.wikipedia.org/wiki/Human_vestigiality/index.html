Human vestigiality


The human appendix, a commonly cited vestigial structure.

In the context of human evolution, human vestigiality involves those traits (such as organs or behaviors) occurring in humans that have lost all or most of their original function through evolution. Although structures usually called "vestigial" may have a minor function or none at all, evidence for their evolutionary histories comes from comparative anatomy and the fossil record.

Examples

Classic examples include the vermiform appendix, the coccyx (tailbone), wisdom teeth, the plica semilunaris of the eye, the arrector pili muscles that produce goose bumps, and the palmaris longus muscle, which is absent in a portion of the population. Behavioral vestiges include the grasp reflex in infants and hiccups, thought to be inherited from amphibian ancestors. Modern research has found secondary functions for some structures — for example, the appendix may serve as a reservoir for beneficial gut bacteria — prompting debate over how "vestigial" they truly are.

History of thought

Charles Darwin discussed vestigial structures in The Descent of Man (1871) as evidence for evolution. In 1893, anatomist Robert Wiedersheim published a list of 86 human vestigial structures, many of which have since been shown to have functions.
