Panopea (bivalve)

Panopea is a genus of large marine bivalve molluscs in the family Hiatellidae, commonly known as geoducks (pronounced "gooey-duck"). Members of the genus are distinguished by their very long fused siphons, which cannot be fully retracted into the shell, and by their habit of burrowing deeply into sand and mud in the intertidal and subtidal zones. The best-known species is the Pacific geoduck, Panopea generosa, one of the largest burrowing clams in the world and among the longest-lived animals, with individuals recorded at more than 165 years old.

Species

Recognized species include Panopea generosa (Pacific geoduck, native to the Pacific Northwest coast of North America), Panopea globosa (Cortés geoduck, from the Gulf of California), Panopea glycimeris (European waters), Panopea japonica (Japanese geoduck), Panopea smithae, and Panopea zelandica (New Zealand geoduck, or "king clam"). Several fossil species are also known, and the genus has a long geological history.

Biology and fisheries

Geoducks settle on the seabed as juveniles and burrow to depths of about a metre; adults are essentially sessile and feed by filtering plankton through their long siphons, which extend to the sediment surface. They are broadcast spawners with high fecundity. Pacific geoducks support valuable commercial dive fisheries in Washington State and British Columbia and are farmed aquaculturally; the meat, especially the siphon, is prized in East Asian cuisine, where it is eaten as sashimi and in hot pot. Because of their longevity and slow growth, wild stocks are carefully managed.
