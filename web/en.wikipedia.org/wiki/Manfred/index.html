Manfred

Manfred is a name and a title shared by several notable subjects. This page outlines the most prominent ones.

Manfred, King of Sicily

Manfred (1232 – 26 February 1266) was the last Hohenstaufen King of Sicily, reigning from 1258 until his death at the Battle of Benevento in 1266. An illegitimate son of Emperor Frederick II, he acted as regent for his nephew Conradin before taking the crown himself. His court at Palermo was a centre of poetry and learning, and he patronized the Sicilian School of poets. Pope Urban IV and Charles of Anjou opposed him; Manfred was killed fighting Charles's army, and Dante placed him in Purgatory in the Divine Comedy, where he recounts how his excommunicated soul may still be saved.

Manfred by Lord Byron

Manfred is a dramatic poem ("a closet drama") by Lord Byron, written in 1816–17 and published in 1817. Set in the Bernese Alps, it concerns a Faustian nobleman, Manfred, tormented by guilt over the death of his beloved Astarte, who summons spirits seeking forgetfulness and oblivion but ultimately rejects their authority and dies defiant. The work is a landmark of the Romantic "Byronic hero" and inspired Schumann's Manfred overture (from his Op. 115 incidental music) and Tchaikovsky's Manfred Symphony (1885).

Other uses

  • Manfred is a German given name, borne by figures such as Manfred von Richthofen, the First World War flying ace known as the "Red Baron".
  • Manfred is also the name of a symphony by Tchaikovsky and incidental music by Robert Schumann, both based on Byron's poem.
