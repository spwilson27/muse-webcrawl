Show trial

Show trial
TypeJudicial proceeding used as propaganda
Defining featuresPredetermined verdict; public confession; didactic purpose
Famous examplesMoscow Trials (1936–38); Slánský trial (1952); Gang of Four trial (1980–81)

A show trial is a public trial in which the guilt or innocence of the accused is predetermined by the authorities, and the proceedings serve primarily as propaganda — to intimidate opponents, legitimise repression, and instruct the public in an official narrative — rather than to determine the facts of a case. The term is most associated with the judicial spectacles of 20th-century authoritarian regimes.

Characteristics

Show trials typically feature extravagant, often implausible charges; confessions extracted by coercion, threats or torture and rehearsed before trial; the absence of a genuine defence; and heavy publicity, including mass media coverage and organised public demands for severe punishment. The defendants frequently confess to fantastic conspiracies and beg for the penalties the state intends to impose anyway.

Historical examples

The most infamous show trials were the Moscow Trials of 1936–1938 during Stalin's Great Purge, in which Old Bolsheviks such as Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin confessed to treason and espionage in proceedings overseen by prosecutor Andrey Vyshinsky; most were executed. The model was exported to post-war Eastern Europe: the Rajk trial in Hungary (1949), the Slánský trial in Czechoslovakia (1952) and the staged trial of Cardinal Mindszenty all followed the pattern of scripted confession and predetermined sentence.

The Nazi regime's People's Court under Roland Freisler humiliated defendants in filmed proceedings, especially after the July 1944 plot against Hitler. In China, the trial of the Gang of Four in 1980–81 is sometimes described as a show trial, though conducted to close the Cultural Revolution with a political verdict within legal forms. The 1930s Soviet trials also inspired Arthur Koestler's novel Darkness at Noon, a classic exploration of the psychology of the confessing defendant.
