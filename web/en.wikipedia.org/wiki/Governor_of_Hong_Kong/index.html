The Governor of Hong Kong was the representative of the British monarch in Hong Kong during the territory's period as a British colony, from 1843 until the transfer of sovereignty to China in 1997. The governor was the head of the colonial government, presided over the Executive Council and — for most of the period — the Legislative Council, and served as commander-in-chief of the British Forces in Hong Kong.

History

The office was created following the Treaty of Nanking (1842), by which Hong Kong Island was ceded to Britain. Sir Henry Pottinger, who had negotiated the treaty, became the first governor on 26 June 1843, when the Letters Patent of 1843 — the colony's first constitutional document — were proclaimed. The Letters Patent and the accompanying Royal Instructions defined the governor's powers and were amended several times, notably in 1917 and during the constitutional reforms of the 1980s and 1990s.

In all, 28 men held the office, although some governors served more than one term, so there were 25 individual appointments excluding acting governors. The longest-serving governor was Sir Edward Youde's predecessor, Sir Murray MacLehose, whose tenure from 1971 to 1982 saw major social reforms, including the establishment of the Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC) in 1974 and the massive public housing programme. Sir Mark Young was governor both before and after the Japanese occupation of 1941–1945, during which the office was suspended and the territory administered by the Imperial Japanese Army.

Powers and duties

The governor possessed wide executive authority: he assented to (or reserved) all legislation, appointed judges and officials, granted pardons, and could dissolve the Legislative Council. By convention he acted on the advice of the Executive Council in most matters. The governor lived at Government House on Government Hill in Central, and used Fanling Lodge as a summer retreat. His flag was the Union Flag defaced in the centre with the Hong Kong coat of arms on a white disc, and the vice-regal salute and the hymn "God Save the King" (or Queen) were associated with the office.

The last governor

The 28th and final governor was Chris Patten, a former Conservative Party chairman and cabinet minister, who took office on 9 July 1992. Patten introduced controversial democratic reforms to the Legislative Council elections of 1995, which Beijing denounced as a violation of the Sino-British Joint Declaration; the elected council was replaced on the handover by a provisional legislature. Patten departed Hong Kong aboard the royal yacht Britannia after the midnight handover ceremony on 30 June–1 July 1997. The office was succeeded by that of the Chief Executive of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, first held by Tung Chee-hwa.

Notable governors

  • Sir Henry Pottinger (1843–1844) — first governor.
  • Sir John Bowring (1854–1859) — scholar-governor associated with the Arrow incident.
  • Sir Richard MacDonnell (1866–1872) — legalised gambling briefly and founded the Hong Kong Mint.
  • Sir Frederick Lugard (1907–1912) — founded Hong Kong University (1911).
  • Sir Mark Young (1941, 1946–1947) — surrendered Hong Kong to Japan and returned after the war.
  • Sir Alexander Grantham (1947–1957) — oversaw post-war recovery and resettlement housing after the Shek Kip Mei fire.
  • Sir Murray MacLehose (1971–1982) — longest-serving; founded the ICAC and the Ten-year Housing Programme.
  • Sir David Wilson (1987–1992) — governor during the Tiananmen Square crisis and the airport core programme.
