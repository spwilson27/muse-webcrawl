Political philosophy is the study of fundamental questions about the state, government, politics, liberty, justice and the enforcement of a legal code by authority. It asks what makes a government legitimate, what rights individuals hold against it, how goods and burdens should be distributed, and what forms of political organisation are best.

History

In the West the discipline begins with Plato's Republic and Aristotle's Politics, which classified constitutions and asked what the best city would be. Medieval political thought, from Augustine to Aquinas, placed political authority within a divinely ordered cosmos. Early modern thinkers such as Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau recast the state in terms of the Social contract: political obligation grounded in the (actual or hypothetical) consent of individuals leaving a state of nature.

Modern debates

The 19th century added liberalism, conservatism, socialism and anarchism as rival frameworks, and contested questions of empire, nationalism and suffrage. In the 20th century, John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) revived systematic normative theory with his account of "justice as fairness", answered by libertarian, communitarian, feminist and multicultural critics. Contemporary political philosophy addresses global justice, climate obligations, migration, and the just distribution of the benefits of technology.
