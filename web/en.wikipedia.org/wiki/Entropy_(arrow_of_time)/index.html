Entropy (arrow of time)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The arrow of time is the concept that time flows in a single, unidirectional direction, from the past toward the future. Of all the asymmetries that define this arrow — we remember the past but not the future, causes precede their effects, the universe expands — the one most firmly rooted in physics is the thermodynamic arrow of time, which is grounded in the second law of thermodynamics: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases over time.

Irreversible processes, such as a breaking cup, define the everyday arrow of time.

The problem

The fundamental equations of physics — Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations, the Schrödinger equation, and general relativity — are very nearly symmetric under time reversal: any process they describe can, in principle, run backwards without violating them. Yet macroscopic experience is strikingly irreversible. Heat flows from hot to cold, gases expand to fill their containers, and eggs scramble but never unscramble. The question of how this temporal asymmetry emerges from time-symmetric laws is known as the problem of the arrow of time, and entropy is central to its resolution.

Entropy and statistical mechanics

In the statistical mechanics of Ludwig Boltzmann, entropy measures the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) compatible with a system's macroscopic state: S = kB ln Ω. High-entropy macrostates vastly outnumber low-entropy ones, so a system starting in a low-entropy state will, simply by probability, evolve toward higher entropy — not because the laws forbid the reverse, but because the reverse is fantastically improbable. On this account, the second law is statistical: entropy decreases are possible in principle, but for macroscopic systems they are so rare as to be unobservable.

The past hypothesis

Probability alone does not explain why entropy was lower yesterday than today; if the universe had begun in a typical (high-entropy) state, the thermodynamic arrow would not exist. Cosmologists therefore invoke a boundary condition sometimes called the Past Hypothesis: the universe began, at the Big Bang, in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, and the entire observable history of increasing entropy — star formation, biological evolution, memory, and causation — unfolds as the universe moves away from that special beginning. Why the early universe had such low entropy, particularly in its gravitational degrees of freedom, remains an open question in cosmology.

Other arrows

Philosophers and physicists distinguish several arrows of time. The psychological arrow is our sense that we remember the past and anticipate the future; it is generally thought to derive from the thermodynamic arrow, since forming a memory is itself an entropy-increasing process. The causal arrow — causes precede effects — and the cosmological arrow, defined by the expansion of the universe, are likewise often traced back to entropy. A separate, much weaker asymmetry arises in particle physics from the violation of time-reversal symmetry in certain weak-interaction decays (via CP violation), though this is considered far too feeble to account for everyday irreversibility.

"The law that entropy always increases holds, I think, the supreme position among the laws of Nature." — Arthur Eddington, The Nature of the Physical World (1928)
