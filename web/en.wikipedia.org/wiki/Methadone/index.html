Methadone, sold under the brand names Dolophine and Methadose among others, is a synthetic opioid agonist used for chronic pain and also to treat opioid use disorder. It is used to treat dependence on other opioids such as heroin, in programs known as methadone maintenance treatment, where a single daily dose replaces shorter-acting opioids and suppresses withdrawal and craving without producing the same degree of euphoria.

Medical uses

Opioid use disorder

Methadone maintenance is an evidence-based treatment for opioid dependence. It is dispensed through specially licensed clinics in many countries. By occupying opioid receptors for 24 hours or longer, methadone reduces illicit opioid use, overdose deaths and transmission of infectious diseases.

Pain

Methadone is also used for severe chronic pain, including cancer pain and neuropathic pain, in part because it also acts as an NMDA receptor antagonist. Its long and variable half-life makes dosing complex, and conversion from other opioids requires care.

Adverse effects

Side effects include sedation, constipation, nausea, sweating and sexual dysfunction. Serious risks include respiratory depression and overdose, particularly when combined with alcohol or benzodiazepines, and prolongation of the QT interval, which can cause torsades de pointes. Tolerance and physical dependence develop with use, though in maintenance treatment doses are stable.

History

Methadone was developed in Germany in 1937–1939 by scientists at IG Farben, including Max Bockmühl and Gustav Ehrhart, while searching for a synthetic analgesic that would be easier to manufacture than morphine. It was introduced into the United States in 1947 by Eli Lilly under the name Dolophine. Its use for opioid dependence was pioneered in the 1960s by Vincent Dole and Marie Nyswander at Rockefeller University.
