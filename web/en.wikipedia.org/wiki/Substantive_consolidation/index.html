Substantive consolidation

Substantive consolidation is a doctrine of United States bankruptcy law under which a bankruptcy court merges the assets and liabilities of two or more related debtors — typically a parent corporation and its subsidiaries, or affiliated companies — into a single estate for purposes of distribution to creditors. Unlike ordinary procedural consolidation (joint administration) of cases, substantive consolidation disregards the separate legal identities of the consolidated entities.

Effects

When entities are substantively consolidated, their assets are pooled, intercompany claims and guarantees between them are extinguished, claims filed against more than one entity are treated as a single claim against the combined estate, and all creditors of all entities share pro rata in the pooled assets. The result is equitable in some cases but can harm creditors who relied on the credit of one particular entity — for example, lenders to a healthy subsidiary may see their recovery diluted by the debts of an insolvent affiliate.

Legal standards

Federal courts of appeal apply varying tests. Influential formulations include the standard of In re Auto-Train Corp. (D.C. Cir. 1987), which asks whether there is substantial identity between the entities and whether consolidation is necessary to avoid harm or realize a benefit, balanced against the harm to objecting creditors. The Second Circuit's In re Augie/Restivo test focuses on whether creditors treated the entities as a single economic unit and whether their affairs are so entangled that consolidation benefits all creditors. The Third Circuit in In re Owens Corning (2005) emphasized respect for corporate separateness, limiting the doctrine to exceptional cases such as where entities disregarded separateness prepetition or where assets and liabilities are hopelessly commingled.

Because of its drastic redistribution effects, substantive consolidation is described by courts as an extraordinary, last-resort remedy, and it is distinct from piercing the corporate veil under state law, though the underlying concerns about disregarded corporate formality overlap.
