Lady Godiva (died between 1066 and 1086), in Old English Godgifu, "god gift", was a late Anglo-Saxon noblewoman who is relatively well documented as the wife of Leofric, Earl of Mercia, and a patron of various churches and monasteries.

Today she is mainly remembered for a legend dating back to at least the 13th century, in which she rode naked — covered only by her long hair — through the streets of Coventry to gain a remission of the oppressive taxation that her husband, Leofric, imposed on his tenants.

Historical figure

Godiva was the wife of Leofric, Earl of Mercia, one of the most powerful magnates of late Anglo-Saxon England under Cnut and Edward the Confessor. Together they founded and endowed the Benedictine monastery at Coventry in 1043. She also made grants to Stow St Mary in Lincolnshire, Evesham Abbey and other houses. She is one of the few women named as a landholder in Domesday Book, and she retained lands after the Norman Conquest for some years, one of very few Anglo-Saxons to do so.

The legend

According to the legend, first recorded by Roger of Wendover in the early 13th century — some 150 years after her death — Godiva pleaded with her husband to relieve the heavy tolls on the people of Coventry. Leofric replied that he would do so if she rode naked through the market-place. She issued a proclamation ordering all townspeople to stay indoors and shut their windows, then rode through the town clothed only in her long hair. Leofric kept his word and abolished the tolls.

The figure of "Peeping Tom", a tailor who disobeyed the proclamation and was struck blind (or dead), was added to the story in later centuries. Most historians regard the ride as legend rather than fact, although Godiva herself was entirely real.

Legacy

Coventry has celebrated the legend in processions since the 17th century. A statue of Lady Godiva stands in the city centre, unveiled in 1949, and the Godiva Clock in Broadgate shows her ride hourly, watched by Peeping Tom. She has been the subject of paintings by John Collier and others, poems by Tennyson, and the Belgian chocolate company Godiva took its name from her.
