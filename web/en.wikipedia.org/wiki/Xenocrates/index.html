Xenocrates

Xenocrates of Chalcedon (Greek: Ξενοκράτης; c. 396/5 – 314/3 BCE) was a Greek philosopher, mathematician and the third head (scholarch) of Plato's Academy, succeeding Speusippus in 339/8 BCE and leading the school for 25 years. He was a pupil of Plato and a companion of Aristotle, with whom he left Athens after Plato's death before returning to the Academy.

Philosophy

Xenocrates systematised Platonic doctrine, dividing philosophy into the three branches — logic, physics and ethics — that became standard in Hellenistic schools. He identified Plato's principles of the One and the Indefinite Dyad with mathematical structures, treating soul as a "self-moving number", and ordered reality into a hierarchy of gods, daemons and humans; his daimonology influenced later Middle Platonism and religious thought. Diogenes Laërtius preserves a list of his many writings and anecdotes emphasising his gravity, incorruptibility and austerity, which made him a moral exemplar but reportedly a dull lecturer.

Influence

Through his pupil Polemo and his writings on ethics (including works on happiness and virtue), Xenocrates helped shape the Old Academy's emphasis on moral philosophy and the doctrine that virtue is central to, though not alone sufficient for, happiness.
