Graphic novel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A graphic novel's spread: cinematic panels of a memoir in black and white, gutters doing the work of chapters]

A graphic novel is a long-form work of comics, narrative in sequential art, published as a book. The term — popularised from the late 1970s (Will Eisner's A Contract with God, 1978, carried it on its cover) — is partly a form, partly a marketing and dignity claim: book-length comics, whether fiction, memoir, history or journalism, shelved and reviewed as literature.

The form's modern canon was established in the 1980s–90s: Art Spiegelman's Maus (serialised 1980–91; Pulitzer Prize 1992, the first for comics), Alan Moore and Dave Gibbons's Watchmen (1986–87), Frank Miller's The Dark Knight Returns (1986) and Neil Gaiman's The Sandman; its expansion since includes Marjane Satrapi's Persepolis (2000), Alison Bechdel's Fun Home (2006) and the children's and YA comics boom (Raina Telgemeier) that made graphic novels the bookstore's growth engine.

Comics as literature

The graphic novel's critical fight was won in stages — Maus's Pulitzer and special citation, Watchmen's place on Time's 100 novels list, the academic field of comics studies, the National Book Award longlists — on the argument that the gutter (the space between panels, where readers construct time) is a literary device and drawing a prose. Memoir proved the breakout genre: Spiegelman, Satrapi, Bechdel, David B. and Craig Thompson turned the form inward with results no reviewer now condescends to.

The market

Book-format comics outsell the monthly pamphlet format that birthed them; school and library adoption (and the manga tsunami alongside) made the children's graphic novel the industry's centre of gravity. 'Graphic novel' remains contested inside comics — some creators prefer 'comic book' undefensively — but as a shelf, a syllabus category and a publishing programme, the term and the form are permanent.
