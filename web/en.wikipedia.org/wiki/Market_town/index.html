Market town

A market town is a settlement that obtained by custom or royal charter, in the Middle Ages, a market right, which allowed it to host a regular market; this distinguished it from a village. In modern usage the term describes a town that remains a commercial and social centre for a surrounding rural area, even when the original charter market has diminished.

History

From roughly 1200 onwards, English and Welsh kings and lords granted hundreds of market charters, and market towns became the engine of local trade: farmers sold produce, and in return bought manufactured and imported goods. Many towns took their names from the function — Market Harborough, Market Drayton, Market Weighton. Market crosses, market halls and wide market squares remain characteristic features.

Features

Market towns traditionally possessed a weekly market day, annual fairs, a corn exchange or market hall, coaching inns, and later banks, solicitors and auction marts serving the agricultural hinterland. They occupied a middle rank in the settlement hierarchy, larger than villages but smaller than county towns and cities.

Modern use

Since the late twentieth century many markets have contracted under pressure from supermarkets and online retail, but farmers' markets and local food movements have revived some. In Ireland, Germany (Markt), Scandinavia and elsewhere, similar legally defined market settlements historically existed. The sea-borne trade of the maritime republics began from towns of this type grown to city scale, and Sarajevo's Markale is a surviving urban market of the same tradition.
