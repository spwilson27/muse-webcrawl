Geothermal heating

Geothermal heating is the direct use of geothermal energy for heating applications. Humans have taken advantage of geothermal heat for cooking and for space heating since ancient times. More recently, geothermal systems — both district heating networks drawing on hot aquifers and ground-source heat pumps exploiting the stable temperature of the shallow ground — have become a significant low-carbon heating technology.

Direct use

In direct-use systems, hot water from geothermal reservoirs or springs is piped to buildings, greenhouses, aquaculture ponds, and processes such as food drying and snow melting. Where water temperatures are moderate (roughly 30–90 °C), it may be used directly in radiators, underfloor systems, or through heat exchangers. Iceland is the world leader in direct use: about 90 percent of Icelandic buildings are heated with geothermal water, principally through the district heating systems of Reykjavík and other towns. Other major users include China, Turkey, the United States, and France, where the Paris Basin's Dogger aquifer has supplied district heating since the 1970s.

Historically, geothermal bathing goes back at least to the Romans, who used hot springs at sites such as Bath, England, and the chaudesaignes in France; district heating began in the 14th century at Chaudes-Aigues, France, and in Boise, Idaho in the 1890s.

Ground-source heat pumps

A geothermal heat pump (also called a ground-source heat pump) transfers heat between a building and the ground or groundwater, whose temperature a few metres below the surface remains nearly constant year-round (typically 10–16 °C in temperate climates). In winter the pump upgrades this low-grade heat to warm the building; in summer the cycle reverses, rejecting heat to the ground. Heat is exchanged through closed loops of pipe buried in boreholes or trenches, or open loops using well water. Typical coefficients of performance of 3 to 5 mean three to five units of heat are delivered per unit of electricity consumed, making such systems among the most efficient heating technologies available, though installation (especially drilling) is costly.

Advantages and limitations

Geothermal heating has very low operating emissions, is unaffected by weather, and provides a baseload heat supply. Limitations include geographic dependence on suitable geology for direct use, high capital cost, possible scaling and corrosion from mineralized waters, and the need for reinjection of spent fluids to sustain reservoirs.
