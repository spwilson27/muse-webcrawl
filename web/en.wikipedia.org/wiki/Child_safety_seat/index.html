Child safety seat

A child safety seat, sometimes called an infant safety seat, child restraint system, child seat, baby seat, car seat, or booster seat, is a seat designed specifically to protect children from injury or death during vehicle collisions. Most commonly these seats are purchased and installed by car owners, and are a legal requirement for transporting children in many jurisdictions.

Types

  • Infant seats — rear-facing only, typically used from birth until the child outgrows them at roughly 9–16 kg (20–35 lb); many double as carriers with a detachable base.
  • Convertible seats — can be used rear-facing for infants and toddlers and converted to forward-facing for older children.
  • Combination seats — forward-facing seats with a harness that can later be used as boosters.
  • Booster seats — position the child so that the vehicle's lap and shoulder belt fit properly; used until the adult seat belt fits, typically at a height of about 145 cm (4 ft 9 in).

Safety and guidance

Safety authorities such as the American Academy of Pediatrics and the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration recommend that children ride rear-facing as long as possible, within the seat manufacturer's height and weight limits, because rear-facing seats best protect the head, neck and spine of infants and toddlers in a frontal crash. Harnesses should be snug, with the chest clip at armpit level, and children under 13 are advised to ride in the rear seat.

Modern seats attach using the vehicle's seat belt or dedicated anchor systems: LATCH (Lower Anchors and Tethers for Children) in the United States and ISOFIX in Europe and other markets (UN Regulation No. 44 and the newer i-Size Regulation No. 129). Studies consistently find that correctly used child restraints reduce the risk of death for infants by roughly 71% and for toddlers by about 54% in passenger cars, though misuse — loose installation, incorrect harness routing — remains common.

History and regulation

The earliest car seats for children, dating to the 1930s, were booster devices intended to help children see out rather than to protect them. Purpose-built restraint designs appeared in the 1960s; Leonard Rivkin's 1966 design and Britax and other manufacturers' early seats led to regulation. The U.S. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 213 has governed child restraints since 1971, and all U.S. states had child-passenger safety laws by 1985. Comparable standards exist in the European Union, Australia, Japan and elsewhere, though age and size thresholds vary.
