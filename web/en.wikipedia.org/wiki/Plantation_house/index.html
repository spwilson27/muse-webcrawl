Plantation house


A plantation house in the American South

A plantation house is the main house of a plantation, often a substantial farmhouse built and occupied by the plantation owner. In the Southern United States, plantation houses of the antebellum period are among the region's most recognizable buildings, frequently designed in the Greek Revival or Federal styles with columned porticoes, symmetrical facades, and wide galleries adapted to the hot climate. The term is also used for great houses on plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil, and other slaveholding societies.

Architecture

Plantation houses ranged from modest farmhouses to grand mansions such as those along Louisiana's River Road. Typical features included raised basements for ventilation and flood protection, hipped or gabled roofs, dormers, and detached kitchens and other outbuildings, including slave quarters whose survival provides essential evidence of the lives of enslaved people. Construction used local materials such as brick, cypress, and, in Louisiana, bousillage.

History and legacy

The plantation system depended on enslaved labour, and modern interpretation at preserved plantation sites increasingly centres the experiences of the enslaved. Many houses were destroyed in the American Civil War or fell into ruin afterward; others survive as museums and historic sites, some listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
