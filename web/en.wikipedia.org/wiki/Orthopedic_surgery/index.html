Orthopedic surgery

Orthopedic surgery (or orthopaedics) is the branch of surgery concerned with conditions involving the musculoskeletal system. Orthopedic surgeons use both surgical and nonsurgical means to treat musculoskeletal trauma, spine diseases, sports injuries, degenerative diseases, infections, tumours and congenital disorders. The word comes from the Greek orthos ("straight") and paidion ("child"), coined by Nicholas Andry in 1741 for the correction of childhood deformities.

Scope and subspecialties

Subspecialties include joint replacement (arthroplasty), sports medicine, hand surgery, spinal surgery, foot and ankle surgery, musculoskeletal oncology, pediatric orthopedics and orthopedic trauma. Among the most frequently performed procedures are knee and hip arthroplasty, arthroscopic surgery of the knee and shoulder, spinal fusion, fracture fixation, carpal tunnel release and rotator cuff repair.

History

Early orthopedics centred on correcting skeletal deformities in children with braces and manipulation. Jean-André Venel founded the first orthopedic institute in 1780. The field was transformed in the 20th century by antisepsis and anaesthesia, internal fixation techniques, and Sir John Charnley's development of modern low-friction total hip replacement in the 1960s. The AO Foundation (founded 1958) systematized fracture care, and arthroscopy — building on the work of Kenji Takagi and Masaki Watanabe — made minimally invasive joint surgery routine.

Training and practice

Orthopedic surgeons typically complete medical school followed by about five years of residency in orthopedic surgery and often a one- or two-year fellowship in a subspecialty. Much of modern practice is non-operative — fractures managed in casts, exercise therapy, injections — with surgery reserved for cases where conservative treatment fails or is inappropriate. Rehabilitation and physiotherapy are integral to outcomes.
