Henry Molaison

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry Gustav Molaison (February 26, 1926 – December 2, 2008), known widely as H.M., was an American memory disorder patient whose case profoundly advanced the scientific understanding of memory. After bilateral removal of much of his medial temporal lobes — including most of the hippocampus — to treat epilepsy in 1953, Molaison developed severe anterograde amnesia: he could no longer form new long-term declarative memories.

The operation and its aftermath

Molaison's epilepsy, possibly worsened by a childhood bicycle accident, resisted medication. Neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville performed an experimental bilateral medial temporal lobe resection. The seizures lessened, but Molaison was left unable to commit new events to long-term memory, while his short-term and working memory remained intact.

Scientific significance

Studied for decades by neuropsychologist Brenda Milner and later Suzanne Corkin at McGill and MIT, H.M. demonstrated that the hippocampus is essential for forming new declarative memories, that short-term and long-term memory are distinct, and — through his ability to learn motor skills such as mirror tracing without remembering the practice — that procedural memory depends on different brain structures. The 1957 Scoville and Milner paper on his case is one of the most cited in neuroscience.

Later life

Molaison lived with relatives and later in a nursing home in Connecticut, participating in research for over fifty years. His identity was protected during his lifetime; after his death in 2008 his brain was preserved and sectioned for study, and his full name became widely known.
