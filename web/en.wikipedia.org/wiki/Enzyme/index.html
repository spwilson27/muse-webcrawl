Enzyme

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An enzyme is a biological catalyst — almost always a protein (occasionally an RNA molecule, a ribozyme) — that speeds up a specific chemical reaction in living organisms without being consumed by it. Enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions, in many cases by factors of millions, and life as we know it depends on them: metabolism, DNA replication, digestion and blood clotting are all enzyme-driven.

Enzyme names usually end in -ase (amylase, polymerase, lactase). Each enzyme binds its substrate at an active site whose shape and chemistry complement the reaction — the older "lock and key" picture refined into induced fit.

Regulation and conditions

Enzymes work best in narrow ranges of temperature and pH and are regulated by inhibitors, activators, and feedback from their products; many require cofactors such as metal ions or vitamins. This regulation lets cells control metabolic pathways precisely, switching production up or down with demand.

Uses

Enzymes are used in detergents (proteases, lipases), food production (rennet in cheese, amylases in brewing), medicine (thrombolytics, digestive aids) and industry. Enzyme defects cause metabolic diseases — a missing enzyme in phenylketonuria, for example, lets phenylalanine accumulate to toxic levels.
