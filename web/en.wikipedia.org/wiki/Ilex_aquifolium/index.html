Ilex aquifolium, the holly, European holly, or sometimes Christmas holly, is a species of flowering plant in the family Aquifoliaceae, native to western and southern Europe, northwest Africa, and southwest Asia. It is an evergreen tree or shrub with glossy, dark green, variably spined leaves and bright red drupes that ripen in autumn and persist through winter.

Description

The tree grows 10–25 m tall with a trunk up to about 40–80 cm in diameter. Leaves on lower branches typically bear sharp spines, an adaptation against browsing animals, while leaves higher in the crown are often smooth-edged — an example of heterophylly. Plants are dioecious, with separate male and female trees; the small white flowers are insect-pollinated, and only female plants bear the red berries, which are mildly toxic to humans but an important winter food for birds.

Cultivation and uses

European holly is widely planted as an ornamental hedge and specimen tree, and numerous cultivars exist, including variegated forms. Cut branches with berries are a traditional Christmas decoration in Europe, and holly held symbolic significance in Celtic and Roman festivals. The dense, white, fine-grained wood has been used for inlay, turnery, and engraving blocks. In western North America the species has become invasive, spreading into native forests.
