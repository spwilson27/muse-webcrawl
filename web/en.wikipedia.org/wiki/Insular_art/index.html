Insular art

A carpet page from an Insular gospel book, dense with interlace and animal ornament.

Insular art, also known as Hiberno-Saxon art, is the style of art produced in the post-Roman history of the British Isles, especially Ireland and Anglo-Saxon England, from about 600 to 900 AD. The term "Insular" — from the Latin insula, island — reflects the shared culture of the Irish Sea zone. Its supreme achievements are illuminated manuscripts whose pages dissolve into abstract fields of interlace, spirals, and stylised beasts.

Origins

The style fused Celtic La Tène spiral ornament, Germanic animal interlace from Anglo-Saxon metalwork, and Mediterranean motifs arriving with missionary manuscripts. Monasteries such as Iona, Lindisfarne, and Kells were its workshops; portable objects — brooches, chalices like the Ardagh Chalice, and gospel books — spread the style.

Manuscripts

The Book of Durrow (c. 650–700), the Lindisfarne Gospels (c. 700–720), and the Book of Kells (c. 800) culminate the tradition: evangelist portraits framed in riotous ornament, "carpet pages" of pure decoration, and the great Chi Rho monogram page at Matthew 1:18. After Viking raids disrupted the monasteries, the tradition's influence flowed into Anglo-Saxon and Carolingian art, and its interlace vocabulary revived in the 19th-century Celtic Revival.
