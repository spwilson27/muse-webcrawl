Ctenophora

Ctenophora (commonly known as comb jellies) is a phylum of marine invertebrates that inhabit waters worldwide. They are notable for their groups of cilia they use for swimming (known as "combs"), and they are the largest animals to swim with the help of cilia. Adults range from a few millimetres to over a metre in size, and most are predators feeding on small planktonic animals.

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Ctenophora (Eschscholtz, 1829)
Classes: Tentaculata, Nuda
Species: approx. 150–200 described

Anatomy

Ctenophores have soft, gelatinous, mostly transparent bodies with biradial symmetry. Eight meridional rows of fused ciliary plates ("combs") beat in sequence to propel the animal, producing shimmering rainbow effects as they diffract light. Most species possess a pair of long, retractable feeding tentacles bearing colloblasts — adhesive cells unique to the phylum — rather than the stinging nematocysts of jellyfish. Many species are bioluminescent.

Ecology

Comb jellies are voracious predators of zooplankton, fish eggs and larvae. The introduced species Mnemiopsis leidyi caused severe ecological and economic damage in the Black Sea in the 1980s after arriving in ballast water, later spreading to the Caspian and European seas, a famous case of an invasive marine species.

Evolutionary position

The phylogenetic placement of Ctenophora has been controversial. Some genomic studies suggest ctenophores may be the sister group to all other animals (the "Ctenophora-sister" hypothesis), which would imply that nervous systems and muscles evolved independently in comb jellies or were lost in sponges and placozoans.
