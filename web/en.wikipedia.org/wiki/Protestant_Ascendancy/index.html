Protestant Ascendancy

The Protestant Ascendancy refers to the political, economic, and social domination of Ireland between the 17th and early 20th centuries by a small Anglican (Church of Ireland) landed aristocracy, most of whose members were of English and Scottish origin. The phrase came into common use in the 1780s–90s during debates over Catholic emancipation, and is often credited to Sir Boyle Roche.

Origins

The Ascendancy's position rested on the confiscation of Catholic-owned land during the plantations of Ireland and the Cromwellian settlement, and was entrenched after the Williamite War (1689–91) by the Penal Laws, which restricted Catholics' rights to own land, hold public office, vote, and practice their religion openly. By the mid-18th century, Catholics — the large majority of the population — owned only a small fraction of the land.

Character and decline

The Ascendancy class produced many of Ireland's 18th-century cultural and political figures, including Jonathan Swift, Edmund Burke, Oliver Goldsmith, and Henry Grattan, whose parliament achieved legislative independence in 1782. Catholic relief acts from 1778 onwards gradually dismantled the penal system; emancipation followed in 1829, and the Church of Ireland was disestablished in 1869. Land Acts from the 1880s to 1903 transferred most land to tenant farmers, and the upheavals of 1916–1923 ended the class's political dominance; many "big houses" were abandoned or burned during the revolutionary period.

The term has since broadened in usage to describe Protestant political dominance in Northern Ireland in the 20th century, and it remains a charged phrase in Irish historical debate.
