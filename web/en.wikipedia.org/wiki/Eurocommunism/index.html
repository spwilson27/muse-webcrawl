Eurocommunism

Eurocommunism was a trend in the communist parties of Western Europe during the 1970s and early 1980s that sought independence from the Soviet Union and a democratic, pluralist road to socialism within parliamentary systems. Its principal exponents were the Italian Communist Party (PCI) under Enrico Berlinguer, the Spanish Communist Party (PCE) under Santiago Carrillo, and, more ambivalently, the French Communist Party (PCF) under Georges Marchais.

Programme

Eurocommunist parties accepted electoral democracy, freedom of the press and opposition parties, rejected the dictatorship of the proletariat and the Soviet model, and criticised the 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia; Berlinguer later condemned the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan and the suppression of Solidarity in Poland. Carrillo's book Eurocommunism and the State (1977) gave the movement its theoretical statement. In Italy, Berlinguer proposed a "historic compromise" with Christian Democracy, and the PCI reached 34.4 percent of the vote in 1976.

Decline

The trend faded in the 1980s: the PCF retreated into orthodoxy and decline, the PCE split, and after 1989 the PCI dissolved itself into a post-communist social-democratic party. Eurocommunism is generally seen as a transitional stage between orthodox communism and modern European social democracy, and as evidence of the Cold War's hold loosening on Western European politics.
