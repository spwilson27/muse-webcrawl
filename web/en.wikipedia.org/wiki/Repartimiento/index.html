Repartimiento

Repartimiento (Spanish pronunciation: [repaɾtiˈmjento]) was a colonial forced labour system imposed by the Spanish Crown in Spanish America and the Spanish Philippines. Under the system, Spanish authorities allotted indigenous communities to provide labour for mines, farms, ranches and public works for a set period each year, with workers nominally entitled to wages.

Origins

The repartimiento developed from the earlier encomienda system, under which individual Spaniards received grants of tribute and labour from indigenous communities. As criticism of encomienda abuses mounted — notably from Bartolomé de las Casas — the Crown sought a system administered by royal officials rather than private encomenderos. The New Laws of 1542 restricted the encomienda, and repartimiento arrangements were gradually formalised during the later sixteenth century.

Operation

In New Spain (Mexico), a rotating draft called the repartimiento de labores required each indigenous community to supply a quota of adult male workers — often around 2 to 4 percent of the tributary population per week — for agriculture and public works, while mining drafts took workers for longer periods. In Peru, the analogous system was the mita, adapted from an Inca labour obligation and reorganised by Viceroy Francisco de Toledo in the 1570s, which famously supplied labour to the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica.

Abuses and decline

Although the law required payment of wages and limited the duration and distance of service, in practice officials and employers frequently exceeded quotas, withheld pay and kept workers beyond their terms, causing severe hardship and population decline. The Crown abolished agricultural repartimiento in New Spain in 1632, though mining drafts persisted longer, and variants of the system survived into the eighteenth century before final abolition with independence-era reforms.
