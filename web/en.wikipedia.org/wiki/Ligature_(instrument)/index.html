Ligature (instrument)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ligature is a device which holds a reed onto the mouthpiece of a single-reed instrument such as a saxophone or clarinet. It secures the flat side of the reed against the table of the mouthpiece while allowing the tip and edges of the reed to vibrate freely as the player's breath passes over it. Many clarinettists and saxophonists consider the ligature an important part of their instrument's tone and response, and a wide variety of designs are marketed.

Design and materials

The earliest ligatures were simply lengths of string or cord wrapped around the mouthpiece and reed — a method still used by some classical clarinettists, particularly in Germany, who wind waxed cord by hand. Modern ligatures are most often made of metal (nickel, brass, silver or gold-plated), in a band that is tightened by one or two screws; others are made of leather, fabric or synthetic materials with a single screw or a ratchet. Some designs contact the reed only at a few points, or hold it by a plate or rails, in order to minimise damping. Inverted ligatures place the screw on top, away from the reed, so that the band's smooth inner surface presses on the reed.

Effect on sound

Manufacturers and players claim that ligature material and design influence tone colour, articulation and ease of response, although controlled tests generally find the differences subtle compared with the effects of the reed and mouthpiece themselves. The ligature's undisputed practical roles are to hold the reed firmly and evenly, to avoid warping it, and to allow quick reed changes.
