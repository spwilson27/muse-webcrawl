A negative interest rate occurs when a central bank (or, by extension, bond markets) sets a nominal policy interest rate below zero, so that depositors pay to hold money at the central bank rather than receiving interest on it. The policy, once considered purely theoretical, was implemented by several major central banks in Europe and Japan in the 2010s to fight deflation and stimulate economic activity after conventional cuts had reached the zero lower bound.

History

Denmark's Nationalbank first took its certificate-of-deposit rate below zero in July 2012 to defend the krone's peg to the euro. The European Central Bank cut its deposit facility rate to −0.10% in June 2014, ultimately reaching −0.50% in 2019. The Swiss National Bank imposed −0.75% in January 2015 as it abandoned the franc's exchange-rate floor, Sweden's Riksbank took its repo rate negative in 2015, and the Bank of Japan adopted a −0.1% rate on a portion of reserves in January 2016 (a policy it retained until 2024). At the 2019 peak, roughly US$17 trillion of bonds worldwide traded at negative yields, meaning buyers were guaranteed a small loss if they held the bonds to maturity.

Rationale and transmission

Negative rates are intended to penalise banks for holding excess reserves, encouraging them to lend instead, and to push down market interest rates and the exchange rate, easing financial conditions. In practice, most central banks "tiered" the system so that only part of banks' reserves was charged, protecting bank profitability. Retail deposit rates rarely went below zero, as banks feared customers would withdraw cash, though some corporate and institutional deposits were charged.

Criticism

Critics argue that negative rates squeeze bank margins and pension funds, distort asset prices and risk-taking, punish savers, and lose effectiveness the longer they persist (the "reversal rate" hypothesis). Supporters point to evidence that they eased credit conditions and helped avert deflation in the euro area. Most adopting central banks exited negative-rate policy during the inflation surge of 2022–2024.
