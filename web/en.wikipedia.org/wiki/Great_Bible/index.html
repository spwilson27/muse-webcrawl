Great Bible

The Great Bible of 1539 was the first authorised edition of the Bible in English, authorised by King Henry VIII of England to be read aloud in the church services of the Church of England. The Great Bible was prepared by Myles Coverdale, working under commission from Thomas Cromwell, Secretary to the King and Vicar General.

Production

The translation drew heavily on the work of William Tyndale — his New Testament and his translations of the Pentateuch and Jonah — revised by Coverdale and supplemented from the Latin Vulgate and other versions for the portions Tyndale had not translated. Printing began in Paris in 1538, but charges of heresy led to the seizure of the sheets; the work was completed in London in April 1539.

The Bible's name comes from its sheer size: it was a huge folio volume, roughly 16.5 inches tall, intended to stand chained in churches. Its title page, designed in the circle of Hans Holbein the Younger, shows Henry VIII handing the Word of God down to Cromwell and Archbishop Cranmer, and through them to the clergy and laity.

Legacy

Royal injunctions required every parish church in England to provide "one book of the Bible of the largest volume in English". The Great Bible went through several editions between 1539 and 1541. Much of its text survived into the King James Version, and its Psalter remained in the Book of Common Prayer for centuries. See also Coverdale Bible.
