Income effect

In economics, the income effect is the change in demand for a good or service caused by a change in a consumer's purchasing power resulting from a change in real income. This change in real income can result from a change in the consumer's nominal income, or from a change in the price of the good itself. Together with the substitution effect, the income effect explains how a price change alters the quantity of a good demanded — a decomposition known as the Slutsky equation.

Explanation

When the price of a good falls, the consumer's real income effectively rises: the same money income now buys more than before. How the consumer responds depends on the type of good. For a normal good, the increase in purchasing power leads the consumer to buy more of it, so the income effect reinforces the substitution effect. For an inferior good, higher real income leads the consumer to buy less of it, so the income effect works against the substitution effect.

In the standard analysis using indifference curves and a budget constraint, a price change produces a total effect that can be decomposed in two ways: the Hicksian decomposition (holding utility constant) and the Slutsky decomposition (holding purchasing power constant). In both, the movement along the original indifference curve or to a parallel budget line isolates the substitution effect, while the shift to a new indifference curve at the new relative prices captures the income effect.

Giffen goods

In the extreme case of a Giffen good — a strongly inferior good that absorbs a large share of the consumer's budget — the income effect of a price rise can outweigh the substitution effect, so that demand rises when price rises, apparently violating the law of demand. The classic (if disputed) example is a staple food such as bread or rice in a very poor household: when its price rises, the household can no longer afford more expensive foods and must buy even more of the staple.

Income–consumption curve

Tracing the consumer's optimal bundles as income varies at constant prices yields the income–consumption curve. Projecting this relationship for a single good gives the Engel curve, which shows how demand for a good changes with income. Goods whose Engel curves slope upward are normal goods; those sloping downward over some range are inferior goods.
