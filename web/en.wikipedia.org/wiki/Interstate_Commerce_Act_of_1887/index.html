Interstate Commerce Act of 1887


The Act was the first federal law to regulate the railroad industry.

The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 is a United States federal law, enacted on February 4, 1887, that created the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) to regulate the railroad industry and later trucking. It was the first federal law designed to regulate an industry, and it responded to decades of complaints by farmers and shippers about discriminatory and extortionate railroad freight rates.

Provisions

The Act required that railroad rates be "reasonable and just" and prohibited rate discrimination in the form of rebates to large shippers and higher charges for short hauls than long hauls over the same line. Railroads were required to publish their tariffs and to file them with the new commission. Pooling arrangements between competing railroads were banned.

Background

The law followed state-level Granger laws of the 1870s and the Supreme Court's decision in Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway Co. v. Illinois (1886), which held that states could not regulate interstate rail traffic, creating a regulatory gap only Congress could fill.

Legacy

The early ICC was weakened by adverse court rulings, but Congress strengthened it with the Elkins Act (1903) and Hepburn Act (1906), the latter giving it power to set maximum rates. The ICC became the model for later federal regulatory agencies before being abolished in 1995, its remaining functions transferred to the Surface Transportation Board.
