Cost-of-living adjustment

A cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) is an increase in income — wages, salaries, pensions, or social benefits — that keeps pace with the cost of living, usually measured by a consumer price index. COLAs are intended to preserve purchasing power against inflation.

Social Security

The best-known COLA is that applied to United States Social Security benefits. Automatic annual adjustments were legislated in 1972 and first paid in 1975; before then, increases required acts of Congress. The adjustment is based on the percentage increase in the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) from the third quarter of the previous year to the third quarter of the current year. If prices do not rise, no COLA is paid, though benefits are never reduced. The size of the adjustment fluctuates with inflation: it reached 11.2 percent in 1981 during the Great Inflation and 8.7 percent for 2023 following the post-pandemic price surge.

Other applications

Federal and military pensions, Supplemental Security Income, and many state retirement systems include COLA provisions, sometimes capped or tied to investment returns. In the private sector, COLA clauses were common in mid-20th-century union contracts — the 1948 General Motors–UAW agreement was a famous early example — but became rarer as inflation fell. Multinational employers and governments also pay cost-of-living allowances to staff posted to expensive locations.

Criticism and debate

Economists debate whether standard price indexes overstate inflation because of substitution and quality changes, which has led to proposals to base COLAs on the lower chained CPI. Critics counter that indexes for the elderly (such as the experimental CPI-E), who spend more on health care and housing, may rise faster than the general index, so switching formulas would effectively cut real benefits.
