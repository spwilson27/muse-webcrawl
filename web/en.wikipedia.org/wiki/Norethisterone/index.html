Norethisterone, also known as norethindrone and sold under many brand names, is a progestin medication used in birth control pills, menopausal hormone therapy, and for the treatment of gynaecological disorders. It was the first highly orally active progestogen to be synthesised, in 1951 at Syntex in Mexico City by a team including Carl Djerassi, Luis Miramontes and George Rosenkranz, and it became the progestin component of the first combined oral contraceptives.

Medical uses

Norethisterone is used alone (the "mini-pill") and in combination with an estrogen such as ethinylestradiol in combined oral contraceptives. At higher doses it is used to treat abnormal uterine bleeding, endometriosis, dysmenorrhoea and premenstrual syndrome, and to postpone menstruation. The long-acting ester norethisterone enanthate is given by injection as a contraceptive in some countries. It is also used in hormone therapy for menopausal symptoms and, in low doses, for transgender hormone therapy in some settings.

Pharmacology

Norethisterone is an agonist of the progesterone receptor and a weak agonist of the androgen and estrogen receptors. It is a derivative of testosterone (19-nortestosterone) and retains some androgenic activity, which accounts for side effects such as acne and adverse effects on cholesterol at high doses. Orally, it undergoes first-pass metabolism; a small fraction is converted to ethinylestradiol, which may contribute to its estrogenic effects.

History

Synthesised in 1951, norethisterone was introduced for medical use by 1957 and became the progestin in Enovid and other early oral contraceptives approved from 1960, making it one of the most historically significant drugs of the 20th century. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
