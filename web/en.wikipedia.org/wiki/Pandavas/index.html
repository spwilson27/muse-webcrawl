Pandava

The Pandavas (Sanskrit: पाण्डवाः, Pāṇḍavāḥ) are the five heroic sons of Pandu in the Hindu epic Mahabharata: Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva. Acknowledged sons of Pandu — who was cursed to die if he touched his wives — they were in fact fathered by gods upon Pandu's wives Kunti and Madri through a mantra that invoked the deities: Yudhishthira by Dharma (Yama), Bhima by Vayu, Arjuna by Indra, and the twins Nakula and Sahadeva by the Ashvins.

Story

Raised at Hastinapura alongside their cousins the Kauravas, the Pandavas excelled in warfare and virtue, arousing the jealousy of the eldest Kaurava, Duryodhana. After surviving the attempt to burn them in the house of lac, the brothers won Draupadi at her svayamvara and, sharing her as wife, received the barren land of Khandavaprastha, which they transformed into the splendid capital Indraprastha. Yudhishthira's gambling match against Shakuni cost them their kingdom, their freedom, and Draupadi's honour, and condemned them to twelve years of exile and a thirteenth year incognito.

When Duryodhana refused to return their share of the kingdom, the dispute culminated in the eighteen-day Kurukshetra War, in which the Pandavas, guided by Krishna, defeated the Kauravas at terrible cost. Yudhishthira ruled Hastinapura for decades before the brothers renounced the world, ascending toward heaven in the epic's closing books; only Yudhishthira, accompanied by a faithful dog, reached the summit alive. The blind king Dhritarashtra, father of the Kauravas, stands at the moral centre of the conflict they fought.
