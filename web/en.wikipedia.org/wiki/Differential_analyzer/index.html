Differential analyser

The differential analyser is a mechanical analogue computer designed to solve differential equations by integration, using wheel-and-disc mechanisms to perform the integration. It was one of the first advanced computing devices to be used operationally.

History

The concept of using progressive integration to solve differential equations was first proposed in 1876 by James Thomson, brother of Lord Kelvin, who had explored the idea of an integrator based on a disc, globe and cylinder. A working, general-purpose differential analyser was built at MIT under the direction of Vannevar Bush and became operational in 1931. Bush's machine used mechanical integrators interconnected by shafts; the whole system was driven by electric motors.

Operation

Variables in the equation were represented by the rotations of shafts. Integrators, adders and multipliers were connected according to the structure of the problem, and torque amplifiers (adapted from the Nieman design) allowed one shaft to drive others without slippage. Results were drawn by a pen on a plotting table or read from counters. Setting up a problem could take days, but the machine could then integrate equations that were intractable by hand, including ballistics and electrical network problems used during the Second World War.

Legacy

Differential analysers were built in the United States, Britain, Norway, the Soviet Union and Japan. They were central to early scientific computing until displaced by electronic digital computers in the 1950s, though they influenced the development of analog computing and control theory.
