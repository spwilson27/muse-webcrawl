The Latvian Legion (Latvian: Latviešu leģions) was a military formation of the German Waffen-SS created during the Second World War, consisting primarily of Latvian conscripts. Formed from 1943, its core comprised the 15th and 19th Waffen Grenadier Divisions of the SS, which fought on the Eastern Front against the Soviet Union, ending the war in the Courland Pocket and in Germany.

Formation

After the Soviet occupation of Latvia in 1940–41 and the German occupation that followed, Berlin authorised a Latvian volunteer formation in early 1943; as manpower needs grew, recruitment became conscription, mobilising successive age classes of Latvian men. Many conscripts saw service against the USSR — which had deported tens of thousands of Latvians in 1941 — as defence of their homeland rather than support for Nazi ideology, though the formation was formally part of the Waffen-SS and took an oath to Hitler.

Service

The two divisions fought in Russia and then, from 1944, in the defence of Latvia itself against the returning Red Army. The 19th Division held in the Courland Pocket until the German capitulation in May 1945; the 15th Division ended the war in Germany, where many of its men surrendered to the Western Allies.

Postwar assessment and commemoration

The Nuremberg Tribunal declared the SS a criminal organisation but exempted persons who had been conscripted and had not committed crimes; the U.S. Displaced Persons Commission in 1950 ruled that the Baltic Waffen-SS units were distinct in purpose from the German SS. The Legion was formed after the main phase of the Holocaust in Latvia, and its units as such were not convicted of war crimes, though the broader question of Latvian collaboration remains historically debated. Annual commemorations of the Legion in Riga on 16 March are politically controversial.
