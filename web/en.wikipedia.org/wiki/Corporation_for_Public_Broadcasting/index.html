The Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB) is an American publicly funded non-profit corporation created by Congress in 1967 to promote and fund public broadcasting in the United States. It was established by the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967, signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson, which declared that public broadcasting should serve the public interest and be shielded from political pressure.

The CPB does not produce programming itself and owns no stations. Instead, it distributes federal appropriations — more than 70 percent of them directly to local public television and radio stations — and funds the development of educational and cultural programming. It played a founding role in the creation of the Public Broadcasting Service (PBS) in 1969 and National Public Radio (NPR) in 1970.

Funding

The CPB receives an annual appropriation from Congress, typically authorised two years in advance to insulate funding decisions from immediate political pressures. Its appropriation in recent years has been in the region of $525–535 million per year. The CPB allocates the bulk of television funds to local stations through Community Service Grants, with a smaller portion supporting national programming and system infrastructure; radio funding follows a similar pattern.

Governance

The corporation is governed by a board of directors appointed by the President of the United States and confirmed by the Senate, serving six-year terms; by law, no more than five of the nine directors may belong to the same political party. The CPB's statutory duties include ensuring objectivity and balance in programming, soliciting diverse sources of funding, and helping stations extend the reach of public broadcasting to underserved audiences.
