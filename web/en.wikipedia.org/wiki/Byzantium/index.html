Byzantium

Byzantium (Greek: Byzantion) was an ancient Greek city on the European side of the Bosphorus, at the entrance to the Black Sea, occupying the site of the historic peninsula of modern Istanbul. Tradition dates its founding by colonists from Megara, led by the legendary Byzas, to around 657 BC.

Its superb strategic and commercial position — a sheltered harbour (the Golden Horn), control of trade between the Aegean and Black seas, and defensible landward walls — made it wealthy and fought-over. It was besieged by Philip of Macedon, taken by Rome, destroyed by Septimius Severus in 196 AD and rebuilt by him. In 330 AD Constantine the Great refounded it as Constantinople, 'New Rome' — after which the old name faded, and much later gave historians the label 'Byzantine Empire' for the medieval eastern Roman state.

Site

The acropolis of Byzantium stood near today's Topkapi Palace. The city's land walls ran along roughly the line later occupied by the Theodosian Walls. Little of the Greek city survives visibly, buried under Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman Constantinople.
