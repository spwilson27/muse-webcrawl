Water well

A water well is an excavation or structure created in the ground by digging, driving, boring, or drilling to access groundwater in underground aquifers. The well water is drawn up by a pump, or using containers, such as buckets that are raised mechanically or by hand. Wells are among the oldest human constructions for obtaining water, dating back thousands of years.

Types

Dug wells are excavated by hand or machine to relatively shallow depths and lined with stone, brick, or concrete rings. Driven wells are made by driving a small-diameter pipe into shallow water-bearing sand or gravel. Drilled wells, made with rotary or percussion drilling rigs, can reach depths of hundreds or thousands of metres and are cased with pipe and screens to keep water flowing and contaminants out.

Construction and safety

A modern drilled well typically includes a casing, a well screen, a gravel pack, sanitary seals, and a submersible or surface pump. Proper siting and sealing are essential to prevent contamination from surface water, septic systems, and agricultural chemicals. Contaminated wells have historically been vectors of waterborne disease, as in the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak traced by John Snow to a polluted London well.

History

Hand-dug wells date to the Neolithic era; some of the oldest known, in Cyprus and the Jezreel Valley, are around 8,000–10,000 years old. Wells were central to settlement in arid regions and remain vital: hundreds of millions of people worldwide depend on groundwater wells for drinking water.
