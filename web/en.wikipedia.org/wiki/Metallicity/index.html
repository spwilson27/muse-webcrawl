Metallicity

In astronomy, metallicity is the abundance of elements present in an object that are heavier than hydrogen and helium. Most of the normal physical matter in the Universe is either hydrogen or helium, and astronomers use the word "metal" conveniently to describe all other elements collectively. For example, a nebula rich in carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and neon would be referred to as "metal-rich" in astrophysical terms even though those elements are non-metals in chemistry.

Measurement

Metallicity is commonly expressed as [Fe/H], the logarithm of the ratio of a star's iron abundance to that of the Sun:

[Fe/H] = log10(NFe/NH)star − log10(NFe/NH)Sun

Thus a star with [Fe/H] = 0 has the Sun's iron abundance, while one with [Fe/H] = −1 has one-tenth as much iron relative to hydrogen. Iron is used because it is common and its spectral lines are easy to measure. The overall metal mass fraction is often denoted Z, with hydrogen fraction X and helium fraction Y satisfying X + Y + Z = 1. For the Sun, Z ≈ 0.014.

Stellar populations

Metallicity underlies the stellar population classification: young, metal-rich stars like the Sun are Population I; older, metal-poor stars in the galactic halo and globular clusters are Population II; and the hypothetical first generation of stars, born from metal-free primordial gas, are Population III. Metals are produced by stellar nucleosynthesis and dispersed by supernovae and stellar winds, so each successive generation of stars is born from more metal-enriched gas.

Significance

Metallicity affects a star's structure, opacity, color, and lifetime; it also correlates with the likelihood of hosting planets, particularly giant planets. In galaxies, metallicity gradients and the mass–metallicity relation provide clues to galactic formation and evolution.
