Waterhouse stop

A set of Waterhouse stops: interchangeable plates with different-sized holes.

A Waterhouse stop is an early form of photographic aperture control: a thin metal plate, usually brass, pierced by a circular hole of a specific diameter, which is inserted into a slot in a camera lens barrel to set the aperture. The photographer carried a set of stops of different sizes and swapped them as needed. The device is named after the English inventor John Waterhouse, who introduced it in 1858.

Use

Before the invention of the iris diaphragm, Waterhouse stops were the standard way to regulate exposure and depth of field in wet-plate and dry-plate cameras, process lenses and large-format lenses. Each stop's hole diameter corresponded to a particular f-number, although early stops were often marked in the proprietary "uniform system" (U.S.) rather than the modern f-stop series. Because the stop was fixed in size, exposure adjustment was stepwise rather than continuous. The system remained common in process and enlarging lenses well into the 20th century, and survives in the pinhole lenses and some specialty optics of today, as well as in the language of "stops" in exposure measurement.
