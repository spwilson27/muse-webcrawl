Unified Modeling Language

The Unified Modeling Language (UML) is a general-purpose visual modeling language in the field of software engineering. It is intended to provide a standard way to visualize the design of a system. UML was created by Grady Booch, Ivar Jacobson, and James Rumbaugh at Rational Software in the 1990s, drawing on their earlier object-oriented analysis and design methods, and was later adopted as a standard by the Object Management Group (OMG).

A UML class diagram, one of the structure diagrams defined by the language.

History

Before UML, object-oriented design was fragmented among competing notations such as Booch's method, Jacobson's Object-Oriented Software Engineering (OOSE), and Rumbaugh's Object Modeling Technique (OMT). The three authors — often called the "Three Amigos" — unified their approaches at Rational Software. UML 1.0 was submitted to the OMG in 1997 and adopted that year. UML 2.0, a major revision, was adopted in 2005; the current version, UML 2.5.1, was published in 2017. UML is also an ISO standard (ISO/IEC 19503).

Diagrams

UML 2 defines fourteen types of diagrams, divided into two categories:

Structure diagrams

  • Class diagram
  • Component diagram
  • Composite structure diagram
  • Deployment diagram
  • Object diagram
  • Package diagram
  • Profile diagram

Behavior diagrams

  • Activity diagram
  • Communication diagram
  • Interaction overview diagram
  • Sequence diagram
  • State diagram
  • Timing diagram
  • Use case diagram

Modeling

UML models can be used descriptively, to document an existing system, or prescriptively, as blueprints for building one. UML is a modeling language, not a development methodology, though it is commonly used with processes such as the Rational Unified Process. Extensions called profiles adapt UML for specific domains; the OMG standard SysML (Systems Modeling Language) is derived from UML for systems engineering.

Criticism

UML has been criticized for being large and complex, with many diagrams rarely used in practice, and for imprecision in parts of its semantics. Nevertheless it remains the most widely taught and recognized visual notation for object-oriented software design.
