Pozzolana

Pozzolana or pozzuolana, from the Italian pozzolana, is a broad class of siliceous and aluminous materials which, in themselves, possess little or no cementitious value but which will, in finely divided form and in the presence of water, react chemically with calcium hydroxide at ordinary temperature to form compounds possessing cementitious properties. It is named after the town of Pozzuoli near Naples, in the shadow of Mount Vesuvius, where volcanic ash with these properties was quarried in antiquity.

History

The Romans used pozzolana mixed with lime to make hydraulic concrete (opus caementicium) that could set underwater. It was a key ingredient in structures such as the Pantheon — whose unreinforced concrete dome remains the largest in the world — and in harbours, breakwaters, aqueducts, and baths across the Roman world. Vitruvius described the material and its properties in De architectura.

Sources

Natural pozzolanas include volcanic ashes and tuffs, pumice, diatomaceous earth, and calcined clays and shales. Artificial (manufactured) pozzolanas include industrial by-products such as fly ash from coal combustion, silica fume from silicon production, blast-furnace slag, and metakaolin produced by calcining kaolin clay.

Use in modern cement

Pozzolanas are widely used as supplementary cementitious materials in Portland-pozzolana cements. Their use reduces the amount of energy-intensive clinker required, lowers carbon dioxide emissions per tonne of cement, consumes less water, and typically improves long-term strength, durability, and resistance to sulfate attack and alkali–silica reaction, at the cost of slower early strength gain.

The pozzolanic reaction

The pozzolanic reaction is the chemical reaction that occurs in Portland cement upon the addition of pozzolans. Reactive silica and alumina in the pozzolan combine with calcium hydroxide released by cement hydration to form additional calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H), the principal binding phase in concrete.
