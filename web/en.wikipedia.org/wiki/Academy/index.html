Academy

An academy is an institution of secondary or tertiary higher learning, or a learned society of scholars and experts. The name originates with Plato's school in the grove of Akademos near Athens, founded around 387 BC and often regarded as the first institution of higher learning in the Western world. Plato's Academy continued, with interruptions, for nearly nine centuries until it was closed under the Byzantine emperor Justinian in AD 529.

Learned societies

From the Renaissance onward, "academy" came to denote societies of scholars, artists, and scientists established to promote a field: the Accademia dei Lincei in Rome (1603), the Royal Society in London (1660), the French Academy of Sciences (1666), and the Académie Française (1635), which regulates the French language. Many nations maintain national academies — such as the United States National Academies or the Chinese Academy of Sciences — that advise governments and confer prestige on elected members.

Schools

In education, the word is used for many kinds of school. In England, academies are state-funded schools independent of local authority control, a usage introduced in the 2000s. In the United States, "academy" historically named many secondary schools (such as Phillips Academy) and today also denotes specialized institutions such as military academies (West Point, Sandhurst) and police or fire academies that train particular professions.

Art academies

Academies of art, beginning with Florence's Accademia di Belle Arti (1563), institutionalized art training and exhibitions and gave rise to the term "academic art" for the styles they promoted; their juried salons dominated European art life for centuries before modernist movements reacted against them.
