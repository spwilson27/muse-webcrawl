Numerical weather prediction

Numerical weather prediction (NWP) uses mathematical models of the atmosphere and oceans to predict the weather based on current weather conditions. Though first attempted manually in the 1920s, it was not until the advent of computer simulation in the 1950s that numerical weather predictions produced realistic results. A number of global and regional forecast models are run in different countries worldwide, using current weather observations relayed from radiosondes, weather satellites and other observing systems as inputs.

Mathematical models based on the same physical principles can be used to generate either short-term weather forecasts or longer-term climate predictions; the latter are widely applied for understanding and projecting climate change.

History

The first attempt at forecasting the weather numerically was made by the British mathematician Lewis Fry Richardson in his 1922 work Weather Prediction by Numerical Process; hand computations for a single six-hour forecast took weeks and failed. In 1950, a team led by Jule Charney, Philip Thompson, and others produced the first successful computerised forecast on the ENIAC, using a simplified barotropic model. Operational numerical prediction began in Sweden (1954) and the United States (1955). John von Neumann's Institute for Advanced Study group in Princeton was central to the early theory.

Model physics

Models solve the primitive equations — the equations of motion, continuity, thermodynamics, and state — on a three-dimensional grid covering the model domain. Processes smaller than the grid spacing, such as cloud formation, radiation, and convection, are represented by parameterisations. Global models such as the American GFS, the ECMWF's IFS, and the UK Met Office Unified Model produce forecasts for the whole Earth, while limited-area models such as the WRF and HRRR run at finer resolution over regions.

Initialisation and data assimilation

Because the atmosphere is chaotic, forecast skill depends critically on the initial analysis. Data assimilation techniques, including three- and four-dimensional variational methods (3D-Var, 4D-Var) and ensemble Kalman filters, blend observations with a short previous forecast to produce the starting state.

Ensemble forecasting

To account for uncertainty in both initial conditions and model physics, forecast centres run ensemble forecasts: many simulations with slightly perturbed starts. The spread of the ensemble indicates forecast confidence and allows probabilistic products such as the probability of precipitation.

Chaos and predictability

Edward Lorenz's discovery of sensitive dependence on initial conditions ("the butterfly effect") established a practical predictability limit of roughly two weeks for mid-latitude weather, even as model skill has steadily improved — modern five-day forecasts are as accurate as three-day forecasts were a generation ago.
