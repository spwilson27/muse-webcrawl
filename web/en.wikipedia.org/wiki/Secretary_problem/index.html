Secretary problem

Under the optimal strategy, the probability of hiring the best applicant approaches 1/e ≈ 37%.

The secretary problem is a problem that demonstrates a scenario involving optimal stopping theory. It is also known as the marriage problem, the sultan's dowry problem, and the best choice problem. The problem has been studied extensively in applied probability, statistics, and decision theory.

Formulation

The problem can be stated as follows: an administrator interviews applicants for a secretarial position one at a time, in random order. Each applicant can be ranked relative to those already seen, but rejected applicants cannot be recalled. After each interview the administrator must decide immediately whether to hire that applicant and stop, or reject them and continue. The goal is to maximise the probability of selecting the single best applicant overall.

Optimal strategy

The optimal strategy is a stopping rule of the following form: interview and reject the first r − 1 applicants, letting them set a benchmark; then hire the first subsequent applicant who is better than all those seen before. The optimal cutoff is at approximately n/e applicants, where n is the total number of applicants and e is the base of the natural logarithm. With this rule, the probability of selecting the best applicant tends to 1/e ≈ 0.3679 as n grows large — a surprisingly high chance given that the decision-maker has so little information. The same constant governs related "look-then-leap" rules in many sequential decision problems.

History and variants

The problem circulated in the 1950s and was popularised by Martin Gardner in Scientific American in 1960. Numerous variants have been analysed, including versions with known numbers of applicants, multiple choices, costs for interviewing, and objectives such as minimising the expected rank of the chosen applicant rather than maximising the probability of picking the very best.
