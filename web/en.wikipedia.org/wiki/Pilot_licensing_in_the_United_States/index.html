Pilot licensing in the United States

Pilot licensing in the United States is administered by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, principally Part 61. The FAA issues pilot certificates, which do not expire, though exercising their privileges requires meeting recency-of-experience rules and holding an appropriate medical certificate or, for some operations, a driver's licence under BasicMed.

Certificate grades

Grades include student pilot, sport pilot, recreational pilot, private pilot, commercial pilot, and airline transport pilot (ATP). The ATP certificate, required to fly for scheduled airlines, demands 1,500 hours of flight time (with reduced pathways for military and university-trained pilots) since a 2013 rule change prompted by the Colgan Air Flight 3407 accident.

Categories, classes, and ratings

Certificates carry ratings for category (airplane, rotorcraft, glider, lighter-than-air), class (single-engine land, multi-engine sea, and so on), and type (specific large or turbojet aircraft). An instrument rating permits flight in instrument meteorological conditions.

Certification process

Applicants log training with authorized instructors, pass FAA knowledge tests, and pass a practical test — the checkride — with an FAA inspector or designated pilot examiner, following the Airman Certification Standards.
