Classified information in the United States

The United States government classification system is established under Executive Order 13526 and classifies information requiring protection against unauthorised disclosure into three levels: Confidential, Secret and Top Secret.

U.S. classification levels
Confidential — damage
Secret — serious damage
Top Secret — exceptionally grave damage
Governing order: E.O. 13526 (2009)

Levels and markings

Classification is based on the damage disclosure could cause to national security. Above Top Secret, access is further limited by Sensitive Compartmented Information (SCI) and Special Access Programs (SAPs); additional markings include NOFORN (not releasable to foreign nationals) and ORCON. "Restricted Data" on nuclear weapons is classified under the Atomic Energy Act — "born secret".

Clearances and handling

Access requires a security clearance, a need-to-know, and approved facilities (SCIFs). Unauthorised disclosure may be prosecuted under the Espionage Act of 1917; notable cases involve the Pentagon Papers, Edward Snowden and Chelsea Manning.

Declassification

Most classified records are automatically declassified after 25 years, subject to exemptions, under oversight of the Information Security Oversight Office and the National Declassification Center. Critics argue the system overclassifies millions of documents. See Classified information.
