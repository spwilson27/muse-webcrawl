Józef Klemens Piłsudski (5 December 1867 – 12 May 1935) was a Polish statesman who is widely regarded as the founder of the independent Second Polish Republic. He served as Chief of State from 1918 to 1922, led Poland to victory over Soviet Russia in the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921, and after the May Coup of 1926 remained the dominant figure in Polish politics until his death, ruling through the Sanation movement while mostly holding the posts of Minister of Military Affairs and twice Prime Minister.

Early life and socialist activism

Piłsudski was born into a landowning Polish-Lithuanian family at Zułów, near Vilnius. In 1887 he was arrested by Tsarist authorities for alleged involvement in a plot to assassinate Alexander III (in which Lenin's brother Aleksandr Ulyanov was executed) and exiled to Siberia for five years. On his return he became a leading figure in the Polish Socialist Party (PPS), editing its underground newspaper Robotnik and arguing that Polish independence was a precondition of socialism. Arrested again in 1900, he escaped from a St Petersburg hospital and later built paramilitary organisations in Austrian Galicia — the Union of Active Struggle and the Riflemen's Associations.

First World War and independence

In August 1914 Piłsudski's riflemen crossed into Russian Poland as the nucleus of the Polish Legions fighting alongside Austria-Hungary. In 1917 he refused to swear an oath of loyalty to the Central Powers and was imprisoned by the Germans at Magdeburg, which burnished his patriotic credentials. Released in November 1918, he returned to Warsaw and, on 11 November 1918, was entrusted with command of the army and the task of forming a government; as Chief of State he presided over the reunification of the partitioned Polish lands.

Polish–Soviet War

Piłsudski promoted a federation concept (Międzymorze) of states between Germany and Russia. In 1920 his alliance with Ukraine's Symon Petliura and the Kyiv offensive triggered a Soviet counteroffensive that reached the gates of Warsaw. In August 1920 Polish forces won the decisive Battle of Warsaw — the "Miracle on the Vistula" — in which Piłsudski's counterstroke from the Wieprz River played the central role. The Peace of Riga (1921) fixed Poland's eastern border.

May Coup and later years

Disgusted by political instability and having retired in 1923, Piłsudski seized power in the May Coup of 1926, in which several hundred people were killed. Thereafter he ruled behind the scenes through loyal governments, increasingly restricting the opposition — most notoriously in the imprisonment of opposition leaders at Brest in 1930. In foreign policy he signed non-aggression pacts with the Soviet Union (1932) and Nazi Germany (1934), seeking to balance between the two. He died of liver cancer in Warsaw on 12 May 1935 and was buried in the crypt of Wawel Cathedral in Kraków; his heart was interred in his mother's grave at Rasos Cemetery in Vilnius. His legacy remains central, and contested, in Polish historical memory.
