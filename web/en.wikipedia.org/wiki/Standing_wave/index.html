Standing wave

In physics, a standing wave, also known as a stationary wave, is a wave that oscillates in time but whose peak amplitude profile does not move in space. It can form when two waves of the same frequency travel in opposite directions and interfere, or when a wave is confined in a medium — as in a vibrating string or a resonant cavity — so that it reflects back on itself.

Description

A standing wave produced by superposing the waves y = A sin(kx − ωt) and y = A sin(kx + ωt) has the form y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). Points called nodes remain permanently at rest, spaced half a wavelength apart, while midway between them antinodes oscillate with maximum amplitude. Unlike a travelling wave, a standing wave does not transport energy along its length; energy oscillates locally between kinetic and potential forms.

Examples

Musical instruments depend on standing waves: a guitar or violin string fixed at both ends vibrates at a fundamental frequency and harmonics, and the air columns of wind instruments form standing waves with nodes and antinodes at their ends. Standing waves also occur in microwave ovens (causing hot and cold spots in food), in laser cavities, in seiches in lakes and harbours, and in electron orbitals in atoms, which are described by standing matter waves. On transmission lines, reflections create partial standing waves, measured by the standing wave ratio (SWR) used in radio engineering.

Resonance

Strong standing waves arise at the resonant frequencies of a confined system, when the medium's length equals an integer number of half-wavelengths (for nodes at both ends). This phenomenon is the physical basis of resonance in acoustics, optics, and quantum mechanics.
