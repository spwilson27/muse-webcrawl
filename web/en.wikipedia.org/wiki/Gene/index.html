Gene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, a gene is a segment of DNA (or, in some viruses, RNA) that is transcribed to produce a functional product — typically a protein, via a messenger RNA, though some genes produce functional RNA molecules directly. Genes are the molecular units of heredity: they are passed from parents to offspring and, together with the environment, determine an organism's traits.

The term was introduced in 1909 by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen, who also coined genotype and phenotype. The classical picture of genes as beads on a chromosomal string was refined in the molecular era: a gene includes regulatory sequences as well as the coding region, most eukaryotic genes contain non-coding introns that are spliced out of the transcript, and overlapping and alternatively spliced genes make the definition elastic. The Human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes.

Structure and expression

A typical protein-coding gene has a promoter where transcription begins, exons that survive into the mature messenger RNA, and introns that are removed by splicing. The mRNA is then translated by ribosomes into a chain of amino acids, following the genetic code. Gene expression is regulated at many levels, allowing the same genome to produce the very different cells of a multicellular body.

Alleles and variation

Alternative versions of a gene at the same locus are called alleles. New alleles arise by mutation; their spread or loss by natural selection and drift is the substance of evolution. Dominant and recessive inheritance patterns, first worked out by Gregor Mendel in peas, describe how alleles in a diploid organism combine to produce a trait.
