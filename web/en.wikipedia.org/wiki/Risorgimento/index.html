The Risorgimento (Italian for "Resurgence"), also known as the unification of Italy, was the 19th-century political and social movement that in 1861 resulted in the consolidation of different states of the Italian Peninsula into a single state, the Kingdom of Italy. Inspired by the rebellions in the 1820s and 1830s against the outcome of the Congress of Vienna, the unification process was precipitated by the Revolutions of 1848, and reached completion in 1871 after Rome was designated the capital of the new kingdom.

The Risorgimento is conventionally divided into phases: the liberal and republican agitation associated with Giuseppe Mazzini and his Young Italy movement; the diplomatic and military leadership of Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, prime minister of the Kingdom of Sardinia; and the popular military exploits of Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose Expedition of the Thousand in 1860 conquered Sicily and Naples.

Background

After the fall of Napoleon, the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) restored the patchwork of Italian states, most of them under Austrian Habsburg influence: the Kingdom of Lombardy–Venetia, the duchies of Parma, Modena and Tuscany, the Papal States and the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. Only the Kingdom of Sardinia, ruled by the House of Savoy, retained genuine independence. Secret societies such as the Carbonari plotted insurrection, and revolts erupted in Naples and Piedmont in 1820–1821 and in the central duchies in 1831, but all were suppressed with Austrian help.

The Revolutions of 1848

The revolutionary wave of 1848 swept the peninsula. Venice under Daniele Manin and Rome under a triumvirate including Mazzini proclaimed republics. Charles Albert of Sardinia declared war on Austria, beginning the First Italian War of Independence, but was defeated at Custoza (1848) and Novara (1849) and abdicated in favour of his son Victor Emmanuel II. The French expedition of 1849 crushed the Roman Republic, restoring Pius IX.

Cavour and the Second War of Independence

As prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia from 1852, Cavour modernised his state, sought French support, and in the secret Plombières agreement of 1858 secured Napoleon III's alliance against Austria. In the Second Italian War of Independence (1859) Franco-Piedmontese forces won at Magenta and Solferino; the armistice of Villafranca ceded Lombardy to Piedmont, and in 1860 plebiscites annexed Tuscany, Parma, Modena and the Romagna.

Garibaldi and the Expedition of the Thousand

In May 1860 Garibaldi sailed from Genoa with about a thousand red-shirted volunteers, landing at Marsala in Sicily. Victories at Calatafimi, Palermo and Milazzo brought Sicily under his control; crossing to the mainland, he took Naples and defeated Bourbon forces at the Volturno. He then handed the south to Victor Emmanuel II at Teano. On 17 March 1861 the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed, with Turin as its capital.

Completion: Venice and Rome

Venetia was acquired after the Third Italian War of Independence in 1866, fought in alliance with Prussia against Austria. Rome remained under papal rule protected by a French garrison until the Franco-Prussian War withdrew it; Italian troops breached Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, and Rome became the capital in 1871. Territories still unredeemed — Trentino and Trieste — were not gained until after the First World War, a grievance that nourished Italian irredentism.

Historiography

Later historians have debated whether unification was a national revolution or, in Antonio Gramsci's phrase, a "passive revolution" accomplished by elites with limited popular participation. The phrase "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians," attributed to Massimo d'Azeglio, captures the enduring problem of forging a national identity in the new state.
