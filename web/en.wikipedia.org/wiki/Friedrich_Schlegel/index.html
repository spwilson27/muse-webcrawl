Karl Wilhelm Friedrich Schlegel (10 March 1772 – 12 January 1829) was a German poet, literary critic, philosopher, philologist and Indologist. With his elder brother August Wilhelm Schlegel, he was one of the leading figures of Jena Romanticism and a founder of modern literary criticism.

Life and work

Schlegel was born in Hanover, the youngest of seven children. He studied law at Göttingen and Leipzig but abandoned it for literature. In Jena he joined the circle around his brother and sister-in-law Caroline, together with Novalis, Ludwig Tieck and Schelling. With August Wilhelm he founded the journal Athenaeum (1798–1800), the chief organ of early German Romanticism, in which he published his celebrated Fragments.

His fragmentary novel Lucinde (1799), partly inspired by his relationship with Dorothea Veit, whom he later married, scandalised contemporaries with its celebration of free love and its experimental form. His dialogue On the Language and Wisdom of the Indians (1808) helped found comparative philology and Indology in Europe, promoting the idea of a kinship between Sanskrit and the European languages.

Later years

In 1808 Schlegel and his wife converted to Roman Catholicism. He moved to Vienna, entered Austrian state service under Metternich, and became an increasingly conservative thinker, editing the journal Concordia and lecturing on the philosophy of history and of life. He died in Dresden in 1829 while preparing a lecture series.

Ideas

Schlegel coined or popularised several enduring concepts: Romantic irony, the idea of literature as "progressive universal poetry" that would reunite all genres, and the fragment as a literary form complete in itself "like a hedgehog". His theory of irony — the author's simultaneous creation and negation of the work — influenced Kierkegaard, who took Schlegel as the subject of his dissertation, and much later literary theory.
