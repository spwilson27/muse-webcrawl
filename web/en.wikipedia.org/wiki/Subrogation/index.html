Subrogation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Subrogation
FieldInsurance law, equity
EffectOne party acquires another's rights of recovery after paying their loss

Subrogation is the assumption by a third party (such as a second creditor or an insurance company) of another party's legal right to collect damages or a debt. It is a legal doctrine whereby one person is entitled to enforce the subsisting or revived rights of another for one's own benefit. A right of subrogation typically arises by operation of law, but can also arise by statute or by agreement.

The most common example is in insurance: when an insurer pays its insured for a loss caused by a third party, the insurer is "subrogated" to the insured's rights and may sue the third party in the insured's place to recover what it paid. The doctrine prevents the insured from recovering twice for the same loss and ensures the party ultimately responsible bears the cost.

Types

  • Equitable (legal) subrogation – arising by operation of law from the equities of the situation.
  • Conventional subrogation – arising from an express agreement, such as a clause in an insurance policy or loan document.
  • Statutory subrogation – created by legislation, as in workers' compensation schemes where the paying fund may pursue the negligent third party.

Applications

Beyond insurance, subrogation appears in suretyship (a guarantor who pays the principal's debt acquires the creditor's rights), in real-estate finance (a lender paying off an earlier mortgage may be subrogated to its priority), and in government benefits. Insurers routinely pursue subrogation in motor collisions, property fires, and product-liability cases. In some jurisdictions and lines of insurance, the made whole doctrine limits the insurer's recovery until the insured has been fully compensated.

Waiver of subrogation

Contracts—especially construction contracts and commercial leases—often contain mutual waivers of subrogation, in which each party's property insurer agrees not to pursue the other party for insured losses, allocating risk to insurance rather than litigation.
