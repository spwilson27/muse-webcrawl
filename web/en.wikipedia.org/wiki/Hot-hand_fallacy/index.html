Hot-hand fallacy

The hot-hand fallacy (also known as the "hot hand phenomenon" or "hot hand") is the belief that a person who has experienced success with a random event has a greater chance of further success in additional attempts. The concept is often applied to sports, particularly basketball: players and fans may believe that a player who has made several shots in a row is "on a streak" and more likely to make the next shot.

Research

The phenomenon was named in a 1985 paper by Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone and Amos Tversky, who examined basketball shooting data and concluded that streaks were consistent with chance: the probability of making a shot did not significantly increase after previous successes. The "hot hand" was thus characterised as a cognitive illusion — an example of the belief in the law of small numbers and the clustering illusion.

Later research complicated this picture. Some studies of controlled shooting found a small genuine hot-hand effect, and a 2018 re-analysis by Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo identified a subtle statistical bias in the original methodology (selection bias in computing conditional probabilities), which, when corrected, suggested the effect may be larger than originally estimated. The topic remains an active area of research in behavioural economics and sports analytics.

Related concepts

The hot-hand fallacy is closely related to the gambler's fallacy, the opposite belief that a run of one outcome makes the opposite outcome more likely in random events.
