Microscopy

Microscopy is the technical field of using microscopes to view objects and areas of objects that cannot be seen with the naked eye (objects not within the resolution range of the normal eye). There are three well-known branches: optical, electron, and scanning probe microscopy, along with the emerging field of X-ray microscopy.

Optical microscopy

Optical (light) microscopy uses visible light and a system of lenses to magnify images of small samples. Techniques include bright-field, dark-field, phase contrast (developed by Frits Zernike), differential interference contrast, and fluorescence microscopy, in which fluorescent dyes or proteins label specific structures. The diffraction limit restricts resolution to roughly 200 nanometres, though super-resolution methods (STED, PALM/STORM) have overcome this barrier, earning the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy uses a beam of electrons instead of light. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) passes electrons through an ultrathin specimen to achieve atomic-scale resolution, while the scanning electron microscope (SEM) scans a focused beam across a surface to produce detailed three-dimensional images. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) revolutionized structural biology and was recognized by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Scanning probe microscopy

Scanning probe techniques build images by rastering a sharp probe over a surface: the scanning tunneling microscope (STM, 1981) images conductive surfaces at atomic resolution, and the atomic force microscope (AFM, 1986) measures forces between tip and sample, working on insulators and in liquid.

History

Compound microscopes appeared around 1600 in the Netherlands. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's single-lens microscopes of the 1670s revealed bacteria and other microorganisms, while Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) popularized the instrument and coined the word "cell".
