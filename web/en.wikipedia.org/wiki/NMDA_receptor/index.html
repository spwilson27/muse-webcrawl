The N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) is a glutamate receptor and ion channel found in neurons. It is one of the main molecular devices by which neural activity — including learning and memory — is controlled in the brain, and it plays a central role in synaptic plasticity, long-term potentiation (LTP), and excitotoxicity.

Structure and activation

NMDA receptors are heterotetramers typically composed of two obligatory GluN1 subunits together with GluN2 (A–D) and/or GluN3 subunits. Uniquely among glutamate receptors, activation requires the simultaneous binding of two agonists: glutamate at the GluN2 subunits and a co-agonist, glycine or D-serine, at the GluN1 subunits. The channel is also blocked in a voltage-dependent manner by Mg2+ ions at resting membrane potentials; postsynaptic depolarisation expels the Mg2+ block, so the receptor acts as a coincidence detector, opening only when presynaptic glutamate release coincides with postsynaptic activity. When open, the channel is highly permeable to Ca2+, whose influx triggers the intracellular cascades underlying LTP and long-term depression.

Pharmacology

The receptor takes its name from the synthetic agonist N-methyl-D-aspartate. Competitive antagonists such as APV (AP5) block the glutamate site in research. Non-competitive channel blockers include the dissociative anaesthetics ketamine and phencyclidine (PCP), the research tool MK-801 (dizocilpine), and the clinically used drug memantine, prescribed for Alzheimer's disease.

Clinical significance

Excessive NMDA receptor activation causes excitotoxic neuronal death implicated in stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative disease. Conversely, receptor hypofunction is a leading hypothesis of schizophrenia, based on the psychotomimetic effects of PCP and ketamine. Autoimmune anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, in which antibodies target the GluN1 subunit, causes severe psychiatric and neurological symptoms. Alterations in the receptor are also implicated in chronic pain, depression, and epilepsy.
