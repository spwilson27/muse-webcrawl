Banu Musa

The Banū Mūsā ("Sons of Moses") were three Persian brothers of ninth-century Baghdad — Muḥammad, Aḥmad, and al-Ḥasan ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir — who became leading scholars, engineers, and patrons of learning at the Abbasid court. Their father, Mūsā ibn Shākir, had been an astrologer (and, in some accounts, a reformed highwayman) in the service of the caliph al-Ma'mun, who took charge of the boys' education after their father's death.

Works

The brothers' most famous composition is the Kitāb al-Ḥiyal (Book of Ingenious Devices, c. 850), a catalogue of about one hundred automata and mechanical contrivances: trick pitchers and self-trimming lamps, automatic fountains, a mechanical grab for retrieving objects from the seabed, and what is considered the earliest programmable machine — an automatic flute player driven by interchangeable pinned cylinders. Their mathematical works include the Book on the Measurement of Plane and Spherical Figures, a major development of Archimedean geometry, and treatises on the astrolabe and on conic sections.

Patronage

Wealthy from court salaries and estates, the Banu Musa financed expeditions to Byzantine territory to obtain Greek manuscripts, maintained translators such as Thabit ibn Qurra and Hunayn ibn Ishaq in their orbit, and made their house a centre of the Graeco-Arabic translation movement associated with Baghdad's House of Wisdom. They were also adept court politicians, surviving the favour and disfavour of successive caliphs while feuding famously with the philosopher al-Kindi.
