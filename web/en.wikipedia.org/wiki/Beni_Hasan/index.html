Beni Hasan (also written Bani Hasan, from Arabic بني حسن) is an ancient Egyptian cemetery site on the east bank of the Nile, about 20 kilometres south of the modern city of Minya in Middle Egypt. It is famous for its rock-cut tombs of the Middle Kingdom, whose painted scenes of daily life, wrestling, warfare and foreign visitors are among the most informative surviving sources for Egyptian society of the second millennium BC.

Site

The necropolis occupies cliffs above the floodplain at the edge of the eastern desert. The site takes its name from a nearby modern village associated with the Arab tribe of Bani Hasan. The principal tombs were cut for the nomarchs — the hereditary governors — of the Oryx nome (the 16th nome of Upper Egypt), whose capital was at Hebenu, near modern Zawiet el-Amarna.

Tombs

Of the 39 numbered upper tombs, most date to the 11th and 12th Dynasties. Four are large and richly decorated:

  • Baqet III (BH15) — 11th Dynasty nomarch; its walls carry hundreds of figures of wrestlers in paired poses, a siege scene, and craftsmen at work.
  • Khety (BH17) — son of Baqet; repeats the wrestling scenes and shows military training.
  • Amenemhat (BH2) — nomarch under Senusret I, with scenes of a military expedition and a fortress siege.
  • Khnumhotep II (BH3) — nomarch under Amenemhat II and Senusret II; celebrated for its autobiographical inscription, its procession of 37 Asiatics (Aamu) bringing eye-paint, and fine paintings of birds and desert hunts.

The famous scene of Asiatic visitors in the tomb of Khnumhotep II is often discussed in connection with the movement of Levantine peoples into Egypt in the Middle Kingdom. Below the cliff tombs lies a lower cemetery with hundreds of shaft burials of the First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, and there are also smaller New Kingdom and Late Period tombs.

Other remains

South of the main necropolis stands the rock temple known as the Speos Artemidos, cut under Hatshepsut and dedicated to the lion goddess Pakhet. In the Graeco-Roman period the area continued in use, and a Coptic church and hermitages were later established among the tombs.

Exploration

The tombs were recorded by the Napoleonic expedition, by John Gardner Wilkinson, and systematically by Percy Newberry for the Egypt Exploration Fund in the 1890s, whose publication remains fundamental. In recent decades the Australian Centre for Egyptology (Macquarie University) has re-recorded the decorated tombs.
