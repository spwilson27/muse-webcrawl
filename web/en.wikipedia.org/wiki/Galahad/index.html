Sir Galahad is a knight of King Arthur's Round Table and one of the central figures of the Grail quest in Arthurian legend. The illegitimate son of Sir Lancelot and Elaine of Corbenic, he is portrayed as the purest and most perfect of knights — the one destined to succeed where all others failed and to achieve the Holy Grail.

Origins

Galahad is a comparatively late addition to the Arthurian tradition, first appearing in the thirteenth-century Lancelot-Grail (Vulgate) Cycle. Earlier Grail romances, such as Chrétien de Troyes' Perceval, had given the quest to Perceval; the prose cycles introduced Galahad as a new, spiritually flawless hero, conceived when Elaine, daughter of the Grail king Pelles, tricked Lancelot into her bed by magical disguise.

The Grail quest

At Camelot, Galahad takes the Siege Perilous — the seat at the Round Table reserved for the Grail knight and fatal to any other — and draws a sword from a floating stone, marks of his destiny. In the Quest for the Holy Grail he journeys with Perceval and Bors, undergoes tests of purity and charity, heals the Maimed King with blood from the Grail, and at Sarras beholds the Grail's full mysteries, after which he dies in ecstasy and his soul is carried to heaven. Galahad's red-cross shield, once belonging to Joseph of Arimathea, is among his emblems.

Later treatments

Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485) transmitted Galahad's story to English literature, and Victorian writers and artists — Tennyson in "Sir Galahad" and the Idylls of the King, and the Pre-Raphaelites — made him an emblem of chaste, selfless purity. His name became proverbial for an impeccably virtuous man ("a perfect Galahad").
