Glacial landform

Glacial landforms are landforms created by the action of glaciers. Most glacial landforms present today were created by the movement of large ice sheets during the Quaternary glaciations. Some areas, such as the Alps and western North America, still bear evidence of glaciation that occurred millions of years ago, and some glaciers continue to reshape the landscape today.

As glaciers expand, they accumulate snow and ice, and flow outward under their own weight. Glaciers erode the underlying rock by abrasion and plucking, and transport the debris they pick up, depositing it elsewhere when the ice melts. These erosional and depositional processes produce a wide variety of distinctive landforms.

Erosional landforms

Erosional landforms include cirques (armchair-shaped hollows at the heads of glacial valleys), arêtes (sharp ridges between two cirques), pyramidal peaks or horns (such as the Matterhorn), U-shaped valleys or glacial troughs, truncated spurs, hanging valleys, fjords (drowned glacial troughs), and rock drumlins or roches moutonnées. Striations and polish on bedrock record the direction of ice flow.

Depositional landforms

Depositional landforms include moraines (ridges of unsorted debris called till), drumlins (elongated hills of till), eskers (winding ridges of sand and gravel deposited by subglacial streams), kames, kettle lakes, and outwash plains (sandurs) formed by meltwater sorting sediment beyond the ice margin. Erratics — large boulders transported far from their source rock — are also characteristic.
