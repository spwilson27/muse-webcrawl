Lakshmibai, the Rani of Jhansi (born Manikarnika Tambe; 1828 – 18 June 1858), was an Indian queen, the Maharani consort of the princely state of Jhansi in Maratha-ruled northern India from 1843 to 1853. She was one of the leading figures of the Indian Rebellion of 1857, in which she fought against the British East India Company, and she became a national heroine and icon of resistance in the Indian independence movement.

Early life and marriage

Manikarnika, nicknamed Manu, was born in Varanasi to a Maharashtrian Karhada Brahmin family and was raised at the court of the exiled Peshwa Baji Rao II at Bithoor, where she was educated alongside Nana Sahib and Tantia Tope, and — unusually for a girl of her time and station — trained in riding, fencing and martial arts. In 1842 she married Maharaja Gangadhar Rao Newalkar of Jhansi and took the name Lakshmibai.

Her only son died in infancy. In 1853, shortly before his death, the Maharaja adopted a son, Damodar Rao, as heir. After the Maharaja's death, the Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, refused to recognise the adoption and annexed Jhansi under the Doctrine of Lapse, which held that a princely state without a recognised natural heir reverted to the Company. Lakshmibai's appeals to London failed, and the annexation became one of the immediate grievances behind the coming rebellion.

The Rebellion of 1857

When the rebellion reached Jhansi in June 1857, the garrison mutinied and the British residents who had taken refuge in the fort were massacred after surrendering — an event for which Lakshmibai's responsibility remains disputed. She took control of Jhansi, raised troops and governed the state. In March 1858 a British force under Sir Hugh Rose besieged Jhansi; she defended the city for two weeks and then escaped by night, famously, to join Tantia Tope at Kalpi.

After the fall of Kalpi, the rebels seized the fortress city of Gwalior, whose ruler fled to the British. Rose advanced on Gwalior, and Lakshmibai was killed in action on 17–18 June 1858 during the fighting near the city. Rose, her opponent, reportedly called her "the most dangerous of all Indian leaders" and, in an often-quoted phrase, "the only man among the rebels".

Legacy

Rani Lakshmibai occupies a central place in Indian national memory: statues of her on horseback stand in many cities, and she is celebrated in ballads, textbooks, novels and films as a symbol of courage and resistance. Subhadra Kumari Chauhan's Hindi poem "Jhansi ki Rani" (1930) made her story known to generations of schoolchildren.
