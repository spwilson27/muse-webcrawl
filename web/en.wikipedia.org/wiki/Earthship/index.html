An Earthship is a style of passive solar house made of natural and upcycled materials, such as earth-packed automobile tyres, cans and bottles, designed to operate largely off the public utility grid. The concept was developed from the 1970s onward by American architect Michael Reynolds and his company Earthship Biotecture, centred near Taos, New Mexico, where a community of experimental earthships — the Greater World Earthship Community — has grown.

Design principles

Earthships are designed around six principles: thermal/solar heating and cooling using thermal mass and passive solar orientation; solar and wind electricity; contained sewage treatment; building with natural and recycled materials; rainwater harvesting with multiple re-use of water through indoor botanical cells; and on-site food production in interior greenhouses. Load-bearing walls of earth-rammed tyres ("earthship bricks") store heat, while a bank of south-facing glass (in the northern hemisphere) warms the interior. Earth tubes and vents regulate temperature without mechanical systems.

Criticism and adoption

Supporters praise earthships' low running costs and resilience; critics point to high labour demands, difficulties meeting building codes designed for conventional construction, performance problems in cloudy or humid climates, and the cost and effort of building to a standard suitable for mortgages and insurance. Earthships have nevertheless been built in many countries, sometimes as disaster-relief structures, and have influenced wider interest in natural building and autonomous housing.
