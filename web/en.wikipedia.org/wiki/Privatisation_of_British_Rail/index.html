The privatisation of British Rail was the process by which ownership and operation of the railways of Great Britain passed from the state-owned operator British Rail to private companies. Effected by the Railways Act 1993 under the Conservative government of John Major, it broke British Rail into more than 100 separate units which were sold or franchised between 1994 and 1997.

The new structure

Track and infrastructure went to Railtrack; passenger services were divided into 25 franchises let to train operating companies (TOCs); freight was sold outright; and rolling stock was transferred to three rolling stock companies (ROSCOs) which leased trains to the TOCs. British Rail had earlier been reorganised into sectors, and the previous decades had seen the network reshaped by the Beeching cuts of the 1960s and modernisation including the InterCity 125.

Effects and controversy

Passenger numbers, which had been in long decline, rose strongly after privatisation, roughly doubling over two decades, and freight volumes also grew; but public subsidy increased substantially, fares rose, and critics attacked the fragmentation of the industry. Railtrack collapsed into administration in 2001 after the Hatfield crash exposed the network's condition, and was replaced in 2002 by Network Rail, a not-for-dividend company later reclassified into the public sector. Franchising was repeatedly reformed, effectively suspended during the COVID-19 pandemic, and in the 2020s the government legislated to bring passenger services back into public ownership under "Great British Railways" as franchises expire.
