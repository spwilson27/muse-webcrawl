Punjabi literature


A manuscript page of Waris Shah's Heer (1766).

Punjabi literature comprises the body of writings in the Punjabi language, produced in the Punjab region of Pakistan and India. Punjabi is written in two scripts: Gurmukhi in Indian Punjab and Shahmukhi (a Perso-Arabic script) in Pakistani Punjab. The literature is especially celebrated for its Sufi poetry, its heroic ballads, and the tragic romances known as qisse.

Early period

The earliest Punjabi writing is associated with the Nath yogis and with Baba Farid (1173–1266), a Sufi saint whose verses (shlokas) were later incorporated into the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy scripture of Sikhism. The Janamsakhis, prose narratives of the life of Guru Nanak, are among the earliest Punjabi prose works. Punjabi verses are found in the Guru Granth Sahib (compiled 1604) by the Sikh Gurus and by Hindu and Muslim saints including Kabir and Namdev.

Sufi poetry and the qissa tradition

Sufi poets such as Shah Hussain, Sultan Bahu, and Bulleh Shah (1680–1757) composed kafis expressing devotion and defiance of religious orthodoxy. The qissa tradition produced long narrative poems of romance and legend: the story of Heer Ranjha was versified several times, most famously by Waris Shah in 1766, whose Heer is regarded as the greatest classic of Punjabi literature. Other popular qisse include Mirza Sahiban, Sohni Mahiwal, and Sassi Punnun.

Modern literature

In the colonial period, Bhai Vir Singh pioneered the modern Punjabi novel and poetry. The 20th century saw the poetry of Amrita Pritam—the first prominent woman in Punjabi literature, known for her poem lamenting the Partition of 1947 ("Ajj Aakhaan Waris Shah Nu")—and novelists such as Nanak Singh and Jaswant Singh Kanwal. The Partition split the Punjabi readership between India and Pakistan, where writers such as Ustad Daman and Ahmed Rahi sustained the tradition. Contemporary Punjabi literature flourishes in both Punjabs and in the diaspora in Canada, Britain, and the United States.
