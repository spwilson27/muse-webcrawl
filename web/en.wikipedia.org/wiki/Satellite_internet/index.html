Satellite Internet

Satellite Internet is Internet access provided through communications satellites, typically in geostationary orbit or, increasingly, in low Earth orbit (LEO) constellations. It allows users in rural and remote areas, as well as ships, aircraft, and vehicles, to connect to the Internet where terrestrial infrastructure such as cable or DSL is unavailable or impractical.

History

Consumer satellite Internet services emerged in the mid-1990s. Early systems such as HughesNet (originally DirecPC) offered one-way service in which downloads arrived by satellite while uploads travelled over a dial-up telephone line. Two-way services, where both directions pass through the satellite, became common in the early 2000s with providers such as HughesNet, ViaSat (Exede), and Eutelsat's Tooway.

Traditional geostationary (GEO) satellites orbit about 35,786 km above the equator. The long round-trip path introduces a latency of roughly 550–600 ms, which is noticeable in interactive applications such as online gaming and video calls, though bandwidth steadily improved with high-throughput satellites using Ka-band spot beams.

Low Earth orbit constellations

Beginning in 2019, SpaceX's Starlink constellation began deploying thousands of small satellites in low Earth orbit at altitudes of a few hundred kilometres. Because the signal path is far shorter, latency falls to roughly 20–40 ms, comparable to terrestrial broadband. Competing LEO systems include OneWeb and Amazon's Project Kuiper. These constellations require electronically steered phased-array user terminals that track satellites as they pass overhead.

Technology

A typical installation consists of a parabolic dish antenna (the VSAT terminal), a satellite modem, and a transceiver. Signals travel from the user's terminal to the satellite, which relays them to a ground station gateway connected to the Internet backbone. Data uses protocols derived from the DVB-S2 standard, with adaptive coding and modulation to cope with rain fade, the attenuation of high-frequency signals by heavy precipitation.

Applications and limitations

Satellite Internet serves rural households, maritime and aviation connectivity, disaster recovery, military communications, and remote industrial sites such as mines and oil platforms. Limitations include higher cost per megabit than terrestrial services, data caps on some GEO plans, weather sensitivity, and, for GEO systems, high latency.
