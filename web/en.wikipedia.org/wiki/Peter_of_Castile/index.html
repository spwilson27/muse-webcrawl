Peter (Spanish: Pedro; 30 August 1334 – 23 March 1369), called the Cruel (el Cruel) or the Just (el Justo), was King of Castile and León from 1350 to 1369. He was the son of Alfonso XI of Castile and Maria of Portugal.

Peter's reign was dominated by conflict with his illegitimate half-brother, Henry of Trastámara, a struggle that became part of the wider Hundred Years' War between England and France. Peter was allied with Edward, the Black Prince of England, while Henry was backed by France.

Reign

Peter ascended the throne at the age of fifteen. His early reign was dominated by his mother and by his favourite, Juan Alfonso de Alburquerque. He became known for harsh treatment of rebellious nobles and for his favouritism toward his mistress, María de Padilla, whose family he advanced.

In 1366 Henry of Trastámara, supported by French mercenary companies under Bertrand du Guesclin, invaded Castile and proclaimed himself king. Peter fled but returned in 1367 with the Black Prince's army, defeating Henry at the Battle of Nájera. Relations between Peter and the Black Prince soon broke down over unpaid costs, and the English withdrew.

Death

In 1369 Peter was besieged by Henry at the castle of Montiel. During negotiations, he was captured and, according to tradition, killed by Henry himself in a personal struggle on 23 March 1369. Henry succeeded him as Henry II, founding the House of Trastámara.

Legacy

Peter's reputation has divided historians: hostile chroniclers, notably Pero López de Ayala, portrayed him as a tyrant, while later writers emphasised his defence of royal authority and his protection of Jewish and Muslim subjects. In Seville he is remembered for building works in the Alcázar.
