Mammalian diving response

The mammalian diving response (also called the diving reflex) is a set of physiological responses to immersion that overrides the basic homeostatic reflexes. It is found in all air-breathing vertebrates but is most pronounced in marine mammals such as seals, otters, dolphins and muskrats. The response optimises respiration and blood distribution so that an animal can stay underwater for long periods.

Components

Three principal changes occur when the face is immersed in cold water:

  • Bradycardia — the heart rate slows dramatically, by 10–30% in humans and up to 90% in diving seals, reducing oxygen consumption.
  • Peripheral vasoconstriction — blood vessels in the limbs and gut constrict, reserving oxygenated blood for the brain, heart and lungs, the organs most sensitive to hypoxia.
  • Blood shift — blood plasma fills the thoracic cavity and lungs as pressure increases with depth, preventing the lungs from collapsing under compression.

In some species, notably deep-diving seals, the spleen also contracts, releasing stored red blood cells into circulation and raising the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.

Trigger and mechanism

The reflex is triggered primarily by cold water contacting receptors in the face, innervated by the trigeminal nerve, and by breath-holding (apnoea). It is mediated by the autonomic nervous system: parasympathetic (vagal) output slows the heart while sympathetic output constricts peripheral vessels.

In humans

In humans the response is modest but measurable, and it forms the physiological basis of competitive freediving. It is also invoked clinically: splashing cold water on the face, or a Valsalva-like manoeuvre, can terminate episodes of supraventricular tachycardia by increasing vagal tone. The reflex is believed to be stronger in infants, and has been discussed in relation to sudden infant death and to survival after prolonged submersion in icy water, particularly in children.
