Knock-and-announce

Under the knock-and-announce rule, officers must knock, announce their identity and purpose, and wait before forcing entry.

Knock-and-announce, in United States law, is the common law principle that police officers executing a search warrant at a home must first knock, announce their identity and purpose, and wait a reasonable time before entering by force. The rule is part of the reasonableness inquiry under the Fourth Amendment, as held by the Supreme Court in Wilson v. Arkansas (1995), which traced the requirement to English common law. Its purposes are to protect life and limb by preventing violent confrontations, to protect property from unnecessary damage, and to safeguard the privacy and dignity of occupants.

Legal framework

In Richards v. Wisconsin (1997), the Supreme Court rejected a blanket exception for felony drug cases but recognised case-by-case "exigent circumstances" and "reasonable suspicion" exceptions, under which a no-knock entry may be justified if announcing would be dangerous, futile, or would allow destruction of evidence. In United States v. Banks (2003), the Court held that a wait of 15–20 seconds could be reasonable where evidence (cocaine) could be quickly destroyed. Conversely, Hudson v. Michigan (2006) held that evidence obtained in violation of the rule is not subject to the exclusionary rule, because the interests protected by knocking are not related to the seizure of the evidence itself.

No-knock warrants and reform

A no-knock warrant expressly authorises entry without announcement. Such warrants, and unannounced "quick-knock" entries, became controversial because of fatal errors and mistaken addresses — notably the 2020 killing of Breonna Taylor in Louisville, Kentucky, during a botched raid, after which many jurisdictions restricted or banned no-knock warrants, and federal policy was changed to limit their use. Critics argue the practice endangers occupants and officers alike; defenders maintain it is sometimes necessary against armed suspects or easily destroyed evidence.

Statutory background

Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 3109) codifies the rule: an officer may break open a door only after giving notice of authority and purpose and being refused admittance. Most states have similar statutes or constitutional provisions.
