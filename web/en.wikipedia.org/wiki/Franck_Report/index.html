Franck Report

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Franck Report of June 1945 was a petition signed by a group of Manhattan Project scientists, principally at the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory, urging that the atomic bomb not be used in a surprise attack on Japan without warning. Named after its lead author, the Nobel laureate James Franck, the report recommended instead that the bomb first be demonstrated before representatives of the United Nations on a desert or uninhabited island, and warned of the political and moral consequences of a sudden first use.

Contents and arguments

The committee — which included Leo Szilard, Glenn Seaborg, and Eugene Rabinowitch among its members and signatories — argued that a surprise atomic attack would sacrifice world public opinion and American moral authority, precipitate a nuclear arms race with the Soviet Union, and make future international control of atomic weapons nearly impossible. It presciently foresaw that the secret of the bomb could not be kept for long and that safety would lie not in secrecy or stockpiles but in international control. The scientists also noted that they had a special responsibility, since knowledge of the weapon existed only through their work.

Reception

The report was delivered to the office of Secretary of War Henry Stimson and considered by the Interim Committee's scientific panel (Oppenheimer, Fermi, Lawrence, and Compton), which reported on 16 June 1945 that it could propose "no technical demonstration likely to bring an end to the war" and saw "no acceptable alternative to direct military use". The Franck Report's recommendations were therefore set aside, though Leo Szilard continued the effort with a separate petition in July signed by dozens of scientists. The report, initially classified, was declassified in 1946 and published in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, becoming a founding document of the scientists' movement for arms control.
