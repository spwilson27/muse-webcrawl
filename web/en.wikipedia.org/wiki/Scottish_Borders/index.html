The Scottish Borders (Scots: the Mairches, Scottish Gaelic: Crìochan na h-Alba) is one of Scotland's 32 council areas, and also the name given to the wider historic region in the south-east of Scotland adjoining the Anglo-Scottish border. The council area, created in 1975 as the Borders region and renamed Scottish Borders in 1996, covers the historic counties of Berwickshire, Peeblesshire, Roxburghshire and Selkirkshire, together with part of Midlothian.

The region is predominantly rural and hilly, drained by the River Tweed and its tributaries. Its towns grew up around the great medieval abbeys of Melrose, Dryburgh, Kelso and Jedburgh and around the textile industries of the Tweed valley, notably knitwear and tweed production centred on Hawick and Galashiels. Historically, the Borders were a contested frontier between the kingdoms of Scotland and England, fought over for centuries and long associated with the Border reivers — raiding clans of the debatable lands.

Geography

The Scottish Borders lie between the Southern Uplands to the west and the North Sea coast to the east. The highest ground is Broad Law (840 m) in the Manor Hills. The River Tweed flows eastward for about 156 km through the region, forming part of the border with England in its lower reaches, and is celebrated for salmon fishing. Principal towns include Galashiels, Hawick (the largest town), Peebles, Kelso, Selkirk, Jedburgh, Melrose, Duns and the fishing port of Eyemouth.

History

The Romans built Dere Street through the area and a major fort at Trimontium near Melrose. After the Roman withdrawal the region passed through Anglian Northumbrian rule before the frontier settled roughly along the Tweed. In the 12th century David I founded the great Border abbeys, which became wealthy centres of the wool trade. From the late 13th century the Wars of Scottish Independence and later centuries of cross-border raiding devastated the region; fortified pele towers and bastle houses dotted the landscape. The union of the crowns in 1603 brought the reivers under control, and the area — renamed the "Middle Shires" — gradually pacified.

Economy and culture

Agriculture — especially sheep farming — and textiles remain important, alongside tourism drawn by Sir Walter Scott's home at Abbotsford, the ruined abbeys, and Common Ridings held each summer in Border towns. The Borders Railway, reopened in 2015 between Edinburgh and Tweedbank, restored rail services to part of the region after the closure of the Waverley Route in 1969. Rugby union is a traditional strength of the area, which produced the Melrose Sevens tournament, where the seven-a-side game was invented in 1883.
