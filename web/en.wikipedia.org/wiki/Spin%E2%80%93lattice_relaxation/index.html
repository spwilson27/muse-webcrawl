Spin–lattice relaxation

Spin–lattice relaxation
Symbol: T1
Type: longitudinal relaxation in NMR/MRI

Spin–lattice relaxation in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the process by which the component of the nuclear magnetization vector parallel to the external magnetic field returns to its equilibrium value. It is characterised by the time constant T1, also called the longitudinal relaxation time.

Mechanism

After a radiofrequency pulse tips the magnetization, excited nuclear spins exchange energy with their surroundings — the "lattice" of surrounding molecules — through fluctuating local magnetic fields produced by molecular tumbling. This energy transfer restores the Boltzmann equilibrium population difference between spin states. Recovery of the longitudinal magnetization is approximately exponential, reaching about 63 percent of its final value after one T1 period.

Dependence

T1 depends on the strength of the main magnetic field, temperature, and the correlation time of molecular motion; relaxation is most efficient when molecular tumbling occurs near the Larmor frequency. In the Bloembergen–Purcell–Pound (BPP) theory, T1 passes through a minimum when the tumbling rate matches the resonance frequency.

Use in MRI

Differences in T1 between tissues generate contrast in T1-weighted images: fat has a short T1 and appears bright, while water and cerebrospinal fluid have long T1 values and appear dark. Gadolinium-based contrast agents shorten T1 of nearby water protons, enhancing vessels and some lesions.
