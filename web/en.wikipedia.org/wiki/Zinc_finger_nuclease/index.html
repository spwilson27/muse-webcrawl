Zinc finger nuclease

Zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) are artificial restriction enzymes generated by fusing a zinc finger DNA-binding domain to a DNA-cleavage domain. Zinc finger domains can be engineered to target specific desired DNA sequences, enabling zinc finger nucleases to target unique sequences within complex genomes. By taking advantage of endogenous DNA repair machinery, these reagents can be used to precisely alter the genomes of higher organisms.

Structure

Each ZFN consists of a chain of typically three to six zinc finger motifs, each recognizing about three base pairs of DNA, fused to the nuclease domain of the FokI restriction enzyme. FokI must dimerize to cut DNA, so ZFNs are used in pairs that flank the target site, doubling the length of the recognized sequence and increasing specificity.

Applications

ZFNs were one of the first practical genome-editing technologies, used to create targeted knockouts and gene modifications in model organisms, plants and human cells, and were the first engineered nucleases to enter clinical trials (for example, targeting the CCR5 gene in T cells as an HIV therapy). They have since been largely superseded in research use by TALENs and especially CRISPR–Cas9, which are easier to design and deploy.
