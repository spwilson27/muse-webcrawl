History of ideas

The history of ideas is a field of research in history that deals with the expression, preservation and change of human ideas over time. It is a supplementary field to intellectual history, though the two are often treated together. The field was defined by the American historian Arthur O. Lovejoy, who founded the History of Ideas Club at Johns Hopkins University and the Journal of the History of Ideas in 1940, and who proposed the analysis of "unit-ideas" — persistent conceptual components such as the Great Chain of Being — as they recombine through history.

The history of ideas traces concepts such as nature, reason and progress across centuries.

Lovejoy and unit-ideas

In The Great Chain of Being (1936), Lovejoy traced the idea of a plenitudinous, hierarchically ordered universe from Plato through medieval scholasticism to Romanticism. His method decomposed doctrines into unit-ideas and followed their persistence and transformation across philosophy, literature, science and religion, crossing disciplinary boundaries that traditional histories respected.

Later developments

The Cambridge School associated with Quentin Skinner and J. G. A. Pocock criticised Lovejoy's approach, arguing that texts must be read in their linguistic and political contexts and that there are no perennial ideas detached from the speech acts of historical agents. Michel Foucault's "archaeology" of knowledge and the German tradition of Begriffsgeschichte (conceptual history), led by Reinhart Koselleck, offered alternative frameworks. The field overlaps with the history of philosophy, the history of science, and cultural history.
