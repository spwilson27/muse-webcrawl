Last Glacial Maximum

The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) was the most recent period in Earth's history when the ice sheets were at their greatest extent, roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago. Vast ice sheets covered much of North America, northern Europe, and Asia, global sea level stood about 120–130 metres lower than today, and the global mean temperature was several degrees colder than the present.

Ice sheets and sea level

The Laurentide Ice Sheet, up to several kilometres thick, covered Canada and the northern United States, while the Fennoscandian sheet buried Scandinavia and much of northern Europe. Water locked in ice lowered sea level so much that land bridges connected Siberia to Alaska (Beringia), Britain to mainland Europe (Doggerland), and mainland Asia to islands of Southeast Asia (Sundaland); Australia and New Guinea formed the single continent of Sahul.

Climate and landscapes

Although ice covered the high latitudes, much of the tropics was cooler and drier, deserts expanded, and a cold, dry mammoth steppe stretched across northern Eurasia supporting mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, and reindeer. Rainforest contracted into refuges, a pattern that shaped modern biodiversity distributions.

Humans and deglaciation

Modern humans lived through the LGM in refuges in southern Europe, Asia, and Beringia; the lowered sea levels enabled or eased migrations, including the peopling of the Americas. From about 19,000 years ago the ice sheets retreated — with interruptions such as the Younger Dryas — in the deglaciation that ushered in the Holocene.
