Russell Alan Hulse (born November 28, 1950) is an American physicist who, together with his thesis advisor Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr., won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1993 "for the discovery of a new type of pulsar, a discovery that has opened up new possibilities for the study of gravitation."

Binary pulsar discovery

While a graduate student at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, Hulse discovered in 1974, using the Arecibo Observatory, the pulsar PSR B1913+16, the first binary pulsar — a pulsar orbiting a companion neutron star. The system's orbital decay, observed over subsequent years, matched the energy loss predicted by general relativity through gravitational radiation, providing the first indirect evidence for gravitational waves. The discovery became a landmark test of Einstein's theory.

Later career

After completing his PhD in 1975, Hulse worked at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory and then at the Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory, where he worked on plasma physics and fusion research, including the Tokamak Fusion Test Reactor. He later joined the University of Texas at Dallas, helping to found its science and mathematics education center.
