The Pāli Canon is the standard collection of scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism, preserved in the Pāli language. Known in Pali as the Tipiṭaka ("Three Baskets"), it is the most complete surviving early Buddhist canon and the doctrinal foundation of Theravada practice in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia. It contains texts held by the tradition to record the teachings of the Buddha and his disciples, transmitted orally for centuries before being committed to writing.

Structure

The Canon is organised into three piṭakas:

  • Vinaya Piṭaka — the rules of monastic discipline for monks and nuns, with accounts of how each rule arose, including the Pātimokkha code recited fortnightly.
  • Sutta Piṭaka — thousands of discourses attributed to the Buddha and close disciples, arranged in five Nikāyas: the Dīgha (long), Majjhima (middle-length), Saṃyutta (connected), Aṅguttara (numerical), and Khuddaka (minor) collections, the last containing beloved texts such as the Dhammapada, Udāna, Sutta Nipāta, Theragāthā, and the Jātaka birth stories.
  • Abhidhamma Piṭaka — seven works of systematic, scholastic analysis of mind and matter, regarded by Theravada as the Buddha's higher teaching.

History

According to Theravada tradition, the teachings were rehearsed at the First Council at Rājagaha shortly after the Buddha's death and transmitted through communal recitation. The canon was brought to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE by Mahinda, and, fearing loss during war and famine, monks wrote it down on palm leaves at Aluvihāra in the 1st century BCE. A great commentarial tradition grew around it, crowned by the 5th-century scholar Buddhaghosa. Successive councils in Myanmar and Thailand revised and standardised the text; the Fifth Council (1871) had it inscribed on 729 marble slabs at Kuthodaw Pagoda in Mandalay — sometimes called "the world's largest book" — and the Sixth Council (1954–56) produced the standard printed edition.

Scholarly assessment

Modern scholars regard the Pali Canon as containing some of the earliest strata of Buddhist literature, while noting that it took its final shape over several centuries and reflects the doctrinal standpoint of the Theravada school. Parallels to many of its suttas survive in the Chinese Āgamas and in Sanskrit and Tibetan sources, allowing comparison of the shared early material.
