Purple (cipher machine)

Purple
Japanese nameType B cipher machine; 97-shiki ōbun inji-ki ("Alphabetical Typewriter Type 97")
Introduced1939 (used by the Foreign Ministry)
MechanismTelephone stepping switches (uniselector switches)
Broken byU.S. Army Signal Intelligence Service, 1940

Purple was the American codename for a Japanese diplomatic cipher machine — officially the Type B cipher machine — used by Japan's Foreign Ministry before and during the Second World War to encipher its most sensitive diplomatic traffic. Unlike the German Enigma, Purple was built not from rotors but from telephone stepping switches. United States cryptanalysts of the Army's Signal Intelligence Service, led by William Friedman and Frank Rowlett, succeeded in breaking the system in 1940 without ever seeing a machine, and the intelligence derived from Purple decrypts was given the codename MAGIC.

Design

Purple replaced the earlier, weaker "Red" machine. It processed the Latin alphabet by dividing it into two groups: six letters (the "sixes") and the remaining twenty (the "twenties"), each group passing through its own cascade of stepping switches — three switches of 25 positions for the twenties and one for the sixes. A plugboard permuted the input alphabet, and daily keys specified the initial switch positions and settings. The machine stepped in a complex motion with a very long period, which the Japanese believed made the system unbreakable.

Breaking Purple

Genevieve Grotjan's insight in September 1940 — noticing a cyclic repetition in the switch behaviour — enabled the SIS team to deduce the machine's logic, after which Leo Rosen built an analogue machine from identical stepping switches. From then on, U.S. and (through exchange) British cryptanalysts could read Japanese diplomatic messages, including instructions to embassies and reports from Japan's ambassador in Berlin. The famous decrypts included the 14-part message breaking off negotiations, delivered to the U.S. government in December 1941. Purple did not carry naval operational traffic (protected by systems such as JN-25), so it gave strategic and diplomatic warning, but not the specific target of the Pearl Harbor attack.

Legacy

No complete Purple machine survives; only fragments remain, one displayed at the National Cryptologic Museum. The breaking of Purple is considered one of the great feats of pure cryptanalysis, since the system was reconstructed from intercepted ciphertexts alone.
