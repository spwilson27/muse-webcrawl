The Butterworth filter is a type of signal processing filter designed to have a frequency response that is as flat as possible in the passband. It is also called a maximally flat magnitude filter. It was first described in 1930 by the British engineer and physicist Stephen Butterworth in his paper "On the Theory of Filter Amplifiers".

Properties

The magnitude response of an n-th order Butterworth low-pass filter falls monotonically, with a roll-off of 20n dB per decade beyond the cutoff frequency, at which the gain is −3 dB regardless of order. Unlike Chebyshev and elliptic filters, the Butterworth response has no ripple in either the passband or the stopband — the price is a gentler transition for a given order. The filter's poles lie evenly spaced on a semicircle in the left half of the s-plane.

Implementation

Butterworth filters are used in analogue electronics (often as cascaded Sallen–Key stages), in digital signal processing via the bilinear transform or impulse invariance, in audio crossovers, anti-aliasing, motion analysis, and control systems. Higher-order designs are usually realised as cascades of second-order sections for numerical stability.
