Umwelt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Umwelt (German for "environment" or "surroundings"; plural: Umwelten) is a concept developed by the Baltic German biologist Jakob Johann von Uexküll (1864–1944) denoting the perceptual world in which an organism exists and acts as a subject. Each species, Uexküll argued, inhabits its own self-centred world shaped by its sensory organs and nervous system; the same physical environment therefore appears radically differently to different animals.

The functional cycle

Uexküll divided the Umwelt into the Merkwelt (perceptual world) — everything an animal can sense — and the Wirkwelt (operational world) — everything it can affect. The two are joined in a "functional cycle" (Funktionskreis): a stimulus perceived triggers an action that changes the stimulus, closing the loop. His famous example is the tick, whose entire world is organised around three signs: the odour of butyric acid from mammalian skin, the warmth of blood, and the feel of hairless skin. Blind and deaf, the tick may wait motionless for years for these cues.

Influence

The Umwelt concept influenced phenomenology (Heidegger discussed Uexküll's work), semiotics — Thomas Sebeok built much of biosemiotics on it — and the philosophy of mind, where it anticipates arguments that subjective experience is structured by an organism's sensory apparatus. Thomas Nagel's question "What is it like to be a bat?" echoes the same insight. In robotics and artificial intelligence, the term is sometimes used for the limited sensor space within which an agent operates.
