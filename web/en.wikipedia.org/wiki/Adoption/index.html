Adoption

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adoption is a process whereby a person assumes the parenting of another, usually a child, from that person's biological or previous legal parent or parents, permanently transferring all rights and responsibilities. Ancient adoption (Roman adoptio, used for succession) differs from the modern child-welfare institution, founded by statutes such as Massachusetts's Adoption of Children Act 1851 (the first modern law, introducing judicial inquiry into fitness and the child's welfare) and England's Adoption of Children Act 1926.

Modern adoption law balances the adopted person's identity interests, birth parents' rights and adoptive parents' security: trends include the decline of closed/sealed records toward openness and contact, the rise of adoption from care (foster systems), step-parent and same-sex adoption (extended by marriage-equality reforms), and intercountry adoption — regulated by the Hague Adoption Convention (1993) against trafficking and improper gain, with sending patterns shifting from South Korea and China (historically the largest programmes) toward domestic care as volumes fell sharply from their 2004 peak (some 45,000 intercountry adoptions worldwide that year, by common estimates). Adult adoption survives for inheritance and family-integration purposes in some systems (notably Japan).
