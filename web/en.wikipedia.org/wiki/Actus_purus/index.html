Actus purus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Actus purus (Latin for "pure act") is a term in scholastic philosophy and theology for the state of being fully actualised, with no unrealised potentiality. In the metaphysics of Aristotle and, most fully, Thomas Aquinas, God is said to be actus purus: pure actuality without any admixture of potency.

Act and potency

Aristotelian metaphysics distinguishes act (what a thing actually is) from potency (what it can become). Ordinary beings are mixtures of both: an acorn is actually an acorn and potentially an oak. Change is the actualisation of a potency. Because whatever is moved is moved by another, Aquinas argued in the First Way of his Five Ways that the chain of movers must terminate in an unmoved mover that is pure act, since anything containing potency would require something else to actualise it.

Theological implications

From God's being actus purus, Aquinas derived the classical attributes of divine simplicity, immutability, eternity and perfection: a being with no unrealised potential cannot change, cannot be divided into parts, and lacks nothing. In God, essence and existence are identical. The doctrine remains central to Thomism and to classical theism more broadly.
