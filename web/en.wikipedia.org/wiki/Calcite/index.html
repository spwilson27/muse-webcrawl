Calcite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Calcite
Composition: Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)
Hardness: 3 on the Mohs scale (reference mineral)
Forms: Limestone, chalk, marble (with aragonite, its polymorph)

Calcite is a carbonate mineral and the most stable polymorph of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. It is the principal mineral of limestone and marble and a major constituent of chalk. On the Mohs scale calcite defines hardness 3; it effervesces ("fizzes") in cold dilute hydrochloric acid, the field test every geology student learns.

Clear calcite (Iceland spar) shows double refraction so strong that text viewed through it appears twice — the phenomenon through which Huygens and others probed the nature of light, and which Viking legend's "sunstone" may have exploited for navigation. Most calcite is biogenic in origin: the shells and skeletons of marine organisms (corals, foraminifera, coccoliths) rain down to form limestones, locking away carbon on geological timescales (see carbon cycling through algae and shells alike).

Cement, agriculture (liming), glass and steel all consume calcite in bulk; speleothems of calcite — stalactites and stalagmites — build cave worlds slowly from dripping water, and their layers archive past climates.
