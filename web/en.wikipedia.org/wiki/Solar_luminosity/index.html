Solar luminosity

SymbolL☉
Value3.828 × 1026 W
DefinitionTotal radiant power output of the Sun

The solar luminosity (L☉) is a unit of radiant flux (power) used by astronomers to measure the luminosity of stars, galaxies, and other celestial objects in terms of the output of the Sun. One solar luminosity equals the Sun's total electromagnetic power output, nominally 3.828 × 1026 watts (3.828 × 1033 erg/s), a value adopted as a nominal conversion constant by the International Astronomical Union in 2015.

Determination

The solar luminosity is computed from the total solar irradiance measured by satellites at Earth — about 1361 W/m² at one astronomical unit — multiplied by the surface area of a sphere of radius 1 AU. The Sun's actual output varies slightly: by roughly 0.1% over the 11-year solar cycle, and by much larger factors over evolutionary timescales. The young Sun was only about 70% as luminous as today (the "faint young Sun" problem), and its luminosity increases by roughly 1% every 100 million years.

Use

Stellar luminosities span an enormous range — from about 10−4 L☉ for the faintest red dwarfs to over 106 L☉ for the most luminous supergiants — making the solar unit convenient for comparison. Luminosity relates to radius and effective temperature through the Stefan–Boltzmann law, L = 4πR²σT4. A related quantity, bolometric magnitude, expresses the same power on a logarithmic scale.
