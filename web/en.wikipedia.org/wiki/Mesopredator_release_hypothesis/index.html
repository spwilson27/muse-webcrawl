Mesopredator release hypothesis

The mesopredator release hypothesis is an ecological theory used to describe the interrelated population dynamics between top predators and mesopredators within an ecosystem, such that when the population of the top predator decreases, the population of the mesopredator increases. The phenomenon arises because apex predators suppress the abundance and behaviour of smaller predators through predation and competition; removing them releases this suppression.

Mechanism and examples

The term was popularised by Soulé and colleagues in 1988 in a study of coastal sage scrub fragments in San Diego, California, where the absence of coyotes (the apex predator) allowed domestic cats, opossums, and raccoons to proliferate, driving local extinctions of scrub-breeding birds. Other widely cited examples include the increase of raccoons and other mid-sized predators following wolf declines, increases in feral cat and fox impacts in Australia, and the expansion of coyotes in North America after the extirpation of wolves — the coyotes in turn suppressing smaller predators such as foxes.

Implications and debate

The hypothesis has major implications for conservation: it suggests that restoring apex predators can cascade through food webs, benefiting prey species and ecosystem processes, while conversely that controlling mesopredators without addressing apex predator loss may fail. However, reviews have found the evidence mixed: mesopredator increases can also be driven by habitat change, human food subsidies, and prey availability, and some studies find weak or context-dependent effects. The hypothesis is closely related to the concept of trophic cascades.
