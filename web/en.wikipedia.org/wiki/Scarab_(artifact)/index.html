Scarab (artifact)

Scarab
OriginAncient Egypt
Periodc. 2000–500 BCE (peak production)
MaterialsSteatite, faience, carnelian, amethyst, lapis lazuli
FunctionSeal, amulet, commemorative object

Scarabs are small carved artifacts in the shape of a dung beetle (Scarabaeus sacer), produced in enormous numbers in ancient Egypt and imitated throughout the ancient Near East and Mediterranean. Typically a few centimetres long, a scarab has a domed back carved as a beetle and a flat base inscribed with names, designs, or hieroglyphic signs; most are pierced lengthwise so they could be strung, worn, or set in a ring.

Religious significance

The scarab was associated with the god Khepri, a form of the sun god, who was believed to roll the sun across the sky as the beetle rolls its ball of dung. Scarabs thus symbolised rebirth, regeneration, and the rising sun, and were among the most popular amulets in Egyptian culture. Large "heart scarabs" inscribed with spells from the Book of the Dead were placed over the hearts of mummies.

Uses

Because their bases could be engraved, scarabs functioned as personal seals for stamping documents, goods, and jar stoppers. Pharaohs also issued large commemorative scarabs recording royal events — most famously the scarab series of Amenhotep III announcing his marriage, lion hunts, and the arrival of a Mitanni princess. Hyksos, Phoenician, and later cultures adopted and adapted the form.
