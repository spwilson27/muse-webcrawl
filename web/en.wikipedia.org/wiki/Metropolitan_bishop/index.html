Metropolitan bishop

In Christian churches with episcopal polity, a metropolitan bishop — or simply metropolitan (Greek: μητροπολίτης, mētropolítēs) — is a bishop who presides over an ecclesiastical province, a grouping of dioceses, with authority over the other (suffragan) bishops in that province. The metropolitan's see is called a metropolis, historically the chief city (metropolis) of a civil province of the Roman Empire.

History

The office emerged in the 3rd and 4th centuries as church organization followed Roman provincial administration. The Council of Nicaea (325) recognized the metropolitan's right to confirm episcopal elections within the province and to convene provincial synods. In the Greek and Slavic Orthodox traditions, "metropolitan" became a rank of honor and jurisdiction between archbishop and patriarch; in some churches it ranks above archbishop, in others below.

In the Roman Catholic Church

A metropolitan archbishop heads a metropolitan archdiocese and the ecclesiastical province composed of it and its suffragan dioceses. His powers are limited and defined by canon law: he may conduct canonical visitations if a suffragan bishop neglects them, appoint diocesan administrators in certain circumstances, and receive appeals from suffragan tribunals. The distinctive insignia of the office is the pallium, a woolen band conferred by the pope. Well-known examples include the metropolitan archbishops of Canterbury in the Anglican Communion, where the Archbishop of Canterbury is "Primate of All England and Metropolitan".

In Eastern Orthodoxy

In the Greek tradition (Ecumenical Patriarchate, Church of Greece), the metropolitan is the ruling bishop of a metropolis. In the Russian Orthodox Church, "metropolitan" is an honorary title of dignity, often granted to archbishops of important sees (e.g., the Metropolitan of St. Petersburg), and since 2011 some head new metropolitanates (mitropolii) grouping several dioceses. In the Orthodox Church in America, "the Metropolitan" is the title of its primate.
