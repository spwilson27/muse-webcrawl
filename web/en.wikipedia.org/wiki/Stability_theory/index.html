Stability theory

In mathematics, stability theory addresses the stability of solutions of differential equations and of trajectories of dynamical systems under small perturbations of initial conditions. The heat equation, for example, is a stable partial differential equation because small perturbations of initial data lead to small variations in temperature at a later time as a result of the maximum principle. More generally, a theorem is stable if small changes in the hypotheses produce small changes in the conclusion.

Stability in dynamical systems

The foundational definitions were given by Aleksandr Lyapunov in his 1892 doctoral thesis. An equilibrium point of a dynamical system is called Lyapunov stable if trajectories that start sufficiently close to it remain arbitrarily close for all future time. It is called asymptotically stable if, in addition, nearby trajectories converge to the equilibrium as time tends to infinity. Lyapunov's second method — the construction of a Lyapunov function, a scalar function that decreases along trajectories — remains a principal tool for proving stability without solving the equations explicitly.

Other uses of the term

"Stability theory" also names a branch of model theory in mathematical logic, initiated by Morley and developed by Shelah, which classifies complete theories according to the number of models they admit and the behaviour of forking and independence. Stable numerical schemes, structural stability in the sense of Andronov and Pontryagin, and stability criteria in control theory (such as the Nyquist and Routh–Hurwitz criteria) are related but distinct topics.
