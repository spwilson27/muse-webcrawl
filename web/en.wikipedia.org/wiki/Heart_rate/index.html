Heart rate

Heart rate is the frequency of the heartbeat, usually measured as the number of contractions of the heart per minute (bpm). It varies with the body's physical needs, including the need to absorb oxygen and excrete carbon dioxide, and is modulated by the autonomic nervous system, hormones, fitness, and health status. A typical resting heart rate for adults is 60–100 bpm; rates persistently below 60 bpm are termed bradycardia and above 100 bpm tachycardia.

Physiology

The heart's rhythm originates in the sinoatrial node, whose intrinsic rate of about 100 bpm is normally restrained by parasympathetic vagal tone. Sympathetic stimulation and circulating catecholamines increase rate and contractility during exercise, stress, or fever (roughly 10 bpm per °C). Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume; during maximal exercise the rate may approach an age-predicted maximum, commonly estimated as 220 minus age, though with wide individual variation.

Resting heart rate and health

Athletes commonly have resting rates of 40–60 bpm owing to increased stroke volume and vagal tone. Epidemiological studies associate a persistently high resting rate with increased cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Resting rates are higher in infancy (100–160 bpm) and decline through childhood.

Measurement

Heart rate can be taken by palpating the radial or carotid pulse, by auscultation, from an electrocardiogram, by pulse oximetry, or by optical sensors in wearable devices. Heart rate variability — the variation in intervals between beats — is used as an index of autonomic balance and recovery.

Abnormalities

Abnormal rhythms (arrhythmias) include atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, and heart block. Sustained resting tachycardia may reflect anaemia, hyperthyroidism, infection, or heart failure; symptomatic bradycardia may require a pacemaker.
