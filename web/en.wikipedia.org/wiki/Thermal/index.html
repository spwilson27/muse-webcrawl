Thermal

ProcessAtmospheric convection
CauseSurface heating and buoyancy
Used byGlider pilots, paragliders, hang gliders, birds
Associated cloudCumulus

A thermal is a column or bubble of rising air produced when the sun heats the ground unevenly and the air close to the surface becomes warmer and less dense than its surroundings. Thermals are the engine of much fair-weather convection and are used by soaring birds and by pilots of gliders, hang gliders and paragliders to gain height without an engine. A cumulus cloud often marks the top of a thermal where rising air cools to saturation.

Formation

On a sunny day, fields, rock faces, towns and slopes warm at different rates. A parcel of air over a hot surface expands and becomes buoyant. If the environmental lapse rate is favourable—temperature falling sufficiently with height—the parcel continues to rise, entraining some surrounding air but retaining enough warmth to ascend. Thermals may detach as discrete bubbles, form streets aligned by wind shear, or feed continuously into a growing cumulus.

The strength of a thermal depends on surface heating, humidity, wind, terrain and the stability of the atmosphere. Light winds allow columns to remain coherent; strong winds can shred them. Cool air over lakes or forests may suppress lift, while dry ploughed land, quarries and south-facing slopes can trigger reliable climbs. In deserts, dust devils are a visible, intense form of surface-driven convection, though not every thermal is dusty or rotating.

Soaring flight

Soaring pilots circle within thermals, balancing turn radius against the strongest lift near the core. The skill lies in centring: adjusting the circle as the column drifts and changes with height. A variometer measures climb and sink, often with audio tones that rise in pitch as lift improves. Cross-country flights link thermals, cumulus clouds, ridge lift and wave systems, converting potential energy gained in climbs into distance during glides.

Birds use the same resource with exquisite economy. Raptors, storks, vultures and pelicans climb in thermals and then glide between them, migrating over land while avoiding long sea crossings where surface heating is weak. Their behaviour reveals thermal structure to attentive human pilots.

Hazards and limits

Thermals create turbulence, especially near the ground and at cloud base. Strong convection can develop into cumulonimbus with lightning, hail and gust fronts. For small aircraft and parachutists, rough thermic conditions near landing areas demand caution. In meteorology, the depth and intensity of thermal convection help forecasters predict cumulus development, boundary-layer growth and the possibility of showers.

In other contexts

The adjective “thermal” more broadly means relating to heat: thermal energy, thermal radiation, thermal expansion, thermal springs and thermal insulation. In meteorology and aviation, however, the noun usually denotes this rising current of air.
