St Davids Cathedral (Welsh: Eglwys Gadeiriol Tyddewi) is an Anglican cathedral in St Davids, Pembrokeshire, Wales, the resting place of Saint David, the patron saint of Wales, and the seat of the Bishop of St Davids. It stands on the site of the monastery founded by David in the 6th century in the valley of the River Alun, deliberately hidden in a hollow so as to escape the notice of raiders.

History

The monastic community was repeatedly attacked by Vikings between 645 and 1097. In 1123 Pope Calixtus II granted St Davids a privilege by which two pilgrimages to St Davids were equal to one to Rome. The present cathedral was begun in 1181 in the Transitional style between Romanesque and Gothic. The central tower collapsed in 1220, and the building was repeatedly repaired and extended, gaining its carved nave ceiling and rood screen in later centuries. Bishop Henry Gower (1328–1347) built much of the adjacent Bishop's Palace, now a picturesque ruin.

The cathedral suffered during the Reformation — when Bishop William Barlow stripped the lead from the palace roof — and again in the Civil War, when Parliamentarian troops destroyed stained glass and the organ. The west front was restored by George Gilbert Scott in the 1860s.

Features

Notable features include the sloping nave floor, the ornate 16th-century Irish oak ceiling, misericords in the choir stalls, the shrine of St David (restored in 2012), and the tomb of Edmund Tudor, father of Henry VII. St Davids is the smallest city in the United Kingdom by population, its status deriving from the cathedral.
