The Palace of Holyroodhouse (typically pronounced "Holy-rood"), commonly referred to as Holyrood Palace, is the official residence of the British monarch in Scotland. Located at the bottom of the Royal Mile in Edinburgh, at the opposite end to Edinburgh Castle, Holyrood has served as the principal royal residence in Scotland since the 16th century, and is a setting for state occasions and official entertaining.

History

The site grew around Holyrood Abbey, founded in 1128 by David I. A guesthouse beside the abbey grew into a royal palace under James IV around 1501, and the surviving north-west tower was built by James V. Mary, Queen of Scots lived at Holyrood from 1561 to 1567; her secretary David Rizzio was murdered in her private apartments in 1566, an event that remains central to the palace's public tours.

The present quadrangular palace, in classical style with baroque detailing, was rebuilt for Charles II between 1671 and 1678 to designs by Sir William Bruce, with interiors by Robert Mylne. After the Union of 1707 the palace was used less by the court, though Bonnie Prince Charlie held court there briefly in 1745. Queen Victoria restored its use, and in the 20th century George V and Queen Mary refurbished it as a working palace; the monarch traditionally spends a week ("Holyrood Week") there each early summer, with investitures, garden parties and audiences of Scottish officials. The State Apartments, including the Great Gallery hung with Jacob de Wet's portraits of Scottish kings, are open to the public.

Grounds

The palace stands in about 10 acres of gardens within Holyrood Park, beneath Arthur's Seat. The King's Gallery at the palace exhibits works from the Royal Collection.
