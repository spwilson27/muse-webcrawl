Bancô (architecture)

A bancô-plastered mud mosque in the Sudano-Sahelian style

Bancô (also spelled banco or banko) is a traditional building material of West Africa: a plaster and mortar made of mud — typically riverbank clay and earth — mixed with organic additives such as straw, rice husks, shea butter residue and sometimes cow dung. It is the characteristic rendering of Sudano-Sahelian mud-brick architecture.

Use and maintenance

Bancô is applied by hand over walls of sun-dried mud brick (ferey) or rammed earth, producing the smooth, sculpted surfaces and rounded forms of buildings such as the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali, the largest mud-brick building in the world. Wooden toron beams protrude from the walls both as structural reinforcement and as scaffolding for repairs. Because rain erodes the plaster, bancô surfaces must be renewed regularly — often annually, in communal festivals such as Djenné's Crépissage de la Grande Mosquée, during which the whole community re-plasters the mosque in a single day. The mixture's organic content improves workability and water resistance, but the need for constant maintenance means bancô architecture survives only where communities actively sustain it; UNESCO-listed towns such as Djenné and Agadez depend on this cycle.
