Space debris

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tracked objects in Earth orbit (visualisation).

Space debris (or space junk) consists of defunct human-made objects in orbit — dead satellites, spent rocket stages, fragments from explosions and collisions, and even tools lost on spacewalks. Tens of thousands of objects larger than 10 cm are tracked from the ground, and millions of smaller, untrackable fragments are estimated to exist, all moving at velocities where even a fleck of paint can damage a spacecraft.

The debris population grew sharply with the 2007 Chinese anti-satellite test that destroyed the FengYun-1C satellite, and the 2009 collision between Iridium 33 and the dead Kosmos 2251 — together creating thousands of new fragments. The International Space Station periodically manoeuvres to dodge tracked debris, and crews have sheltered in their return vehicles during close passes. The feared runaway scenario, in which collisions breed fragments that breed more collisions, is called the Kessler syndrome. Proposed remedies range from stricter disposal rules to active removal missions that grapple or harpoon dead satellites.
