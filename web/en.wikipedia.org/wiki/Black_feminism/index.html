Black feminism

Black feminism holds that sexism, racism, and class oppression are inextricably linked

Black feminism is a philosophy and political movement centered on the experiences of Black women, arguing that sexism, class oppression, and racism are bound together and cannot be addressed in isolation. Black feminists contend that the liberation of Black women entails freedom for all people, since it would require the end of racism, sexism, and class oppression simultaneously. The tradition predates the term itself: the phrase "Black feminism" gained currency in the 1970s, but its ideas reach back to 19th-century abolitionist and women's rights activists.

Early history

In 1851 Sojourner Truth delivered her famous "Ain't I a Woman?" speech at the Women's Rights Convention in Akron, Ohio, challenging both racial and gender exclusions. Maria Stewart, Anna Julia Cooper (whose A Voice from the South appeared in 1892), Ida B. Wells, and Mary Church Terrell carried the tradition into the era of the Black women's club movement. These thinkers insisted that Black women faced a distinctive position shaped by slavery, racism, and patriarchy at once.

The 1970s: naming the movement

Black feminism emerged as an organized movement during the 1970s, as Black women found the mainstream feminist movement dominated by white, middle-class concerns and the Black liberation movement dominated by men. The National Black Feminist Organization was founded in 1973. In 1977 the Boston-based Combahee River Collective issued its influential statement, which coined the term "identity politics" and declared: "If Black women were free, it would mean that everyone else would have to be free since our freedom would necessitate the destruction of all the systems of oppression."

Intersectionality and theory

Legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw formalized the concept of intersectionality in 1989, arguing that Black women's experiences of discrimination cannot be understood through race or gender alone. bell hooks's Ain't I a Woman (1981) and Feminist Theory: From Margin to Center (1984), Angela Davis's Women, Race, & Class (1981), Audre Lorde's Sister Outsider (1984), and Patricia Hill Collins's Black Feminist Thought (1990) are foundational texts. Alice Walker's related concept of "womanism" also emerged from this tradition.

Contemporary Black feminism

Black feminist ideas have shaped 21st-century movements, including Black Lives Matter — founded by Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi — as well as scholarship across sociology, literature, and law. Critiques of mainstream feminism's exclusions remain a central theme.
