Ahmed Sékou Touré (9 January 1922 – 26 March 1984) was a Guinean political leader and African statesman who became the first President of Guinea, governing from independence in 1958 until his death in 1984. A labour organizer who rose through the trade-union movement and the African Democratic Rally, Touré led the "No" campaign in the 1958 referendum on Charles de Gaulle's proposed French Community, making Guinea the only French colony to choose immediate independence.

Rise to power

Touré worked as a postal clerk and became a leading trade unionist, organizing a long postal strike in 1953 and founding the Democratic Party of Guinea (PDG). Elected to the French National Assembly and as mayor of Conakry, he campaigned against the 1958 constitution. France responded to Guinea's independence vote with an abrupt withdrawal of administrators, aid, and equipment, and Touré turned first to Ghana and the socialist bloc for support while proclaiming a policy of "positive neutralism".

Rule

Touré established a one-party socialist state, nationalizing much of the economy — including the important bauxite sector, partly in joint ventures — and ruling through the PDG's mass organizations. His government survived an armed attack at Conakry in 1970 (Operation Green Sea) backed by Portugal. Domestically, his regime became increasingly repressive: thousands of alleged plotters and dissidents were imprisoned or executed, notoriously at Camp Boiro, and large numbers of Guineans fled into exile. In the late 1970s, after market women's protests and economic failure, Touré moderated his policies and improved relations with France and the United States. He died in 1984 in Cleveland, Ohio, while undergoing heart surgery, and was succeeded within days by a military coup led by Lansana Conté.
