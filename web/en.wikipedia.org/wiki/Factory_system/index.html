Factory system

The factory system was a method of manufacturing using machinery and division of labour that spread during the Industrial Revolution. Under the factory system, workers and machines were concentrated in a single location, production was mechanised, and work was organised in shifts supervised by managers — replacing the earlier domestic or "putting-out" system in which artisans worked at home.

Origins

Early examples include the Venetian Arsenal and silk mills such as Lombe's Mill in Derby (1721). Richard Arkwright's water-powered cotton mills at Cromford in the 1770s are often credited as the archetype of the modern factory, combining powered machinery, a disciplined workforce, and continuous production. The system spread from textiles to iron, pottery and other industries.

Characteristics and effects

The factory system brought standardisation, interchangeable parts, and large economies of scale, but also long hours, child labour, dangerous conditions and rigid discipline, prompting labour movements and factory legislation such as the British Factory Acts. It reshaped society by driving urbanisation and creating an industrial working class.

Later developments

Steam power freed factories from water sites; electrification, the assembly line (mass production) and scientific management extended the system in the 20th century, and automation and global supply chains continue its evolution today.
