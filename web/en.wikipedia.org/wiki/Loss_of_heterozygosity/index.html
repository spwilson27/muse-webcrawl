Loss of heterozygosity

Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) is a genetic event in which one of the two alleles at a locus is lost, so that a cell that was heterozygous becomes homozygous or hemizygous for the remaining allele. LOH is a hallmark of cancer genomes and is a principal mechanism by which the second, functional copy of a tumour suppressor gene is eliminated, completing the "two hits" required for its inactivation.

Mechanisms

LOH can arise through several routes:

  • Chromosome loss: nondisjunction eliminates the whole chromosome carrying the normal allele (often followed by reduplication of the mutant chromosome, producing copy-neutral LOH or uniparental disomy).
  • Deletion: a segment of the chromosome carrying the normal allele is deleted.
  • Mitotic recombination: crossing over between homologous chromosomes during mitosis, followed by segregation, yields daughter cells homozygous for everything beyond the crossover point.
  • Gene conversion: a short tract of DNA is copied from one homologue over the other.

Role in cancer

In Alfred Knudson's two-hit model of retinoblastoma, a child inherits one defective copy of the RB1 tumour suppressor; a tumour arises when the remaining normal copy is lost, most often by LOH. The same pattern is seen at many tumour suppressor loci — TP53 (17p), APC (5q), BRCA1 (17q), and others — and genome-wide LOH patterns are used diagnostically, for example LOH at 1p/19q in oligodendroglioma and microsatellite marker analysis of tumours.

Other significance

LOH also occurs in normal and ageing tissues and in genetic imprinting disorders through uniparental disomy, and it is exploited in forensic and population genetics wherever allelic diversity is tracked.
