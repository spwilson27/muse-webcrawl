Cyclin B

Cyclin B accumulates through interphase and is destroyed at mitotic exit.

Cyclin B is a member of the cyclin family of regulatory proteins and a key controller of the cell cycle. Cyclin B binds to and activates the cyclin-dependent kinase CDK1 (also known as Cdc2); the resulting complex, historically called maturation-promoting factor (MPF), drives the cell's entry into mitosis.

Function

Cyclin B is synthesised during S and G2 phases and accumulates in the nucleus. When the cyclin B–CDK1 complex is activated — by removal of inhibitory phosphates by the phosphatase Cdc25 — it phosphorylates hundreds of substrates that bring about chromosome condensation, nuclear envelope breakdown, and spindle assembly. To exit mitosis, cyclin B must be destroyed: it is tagged with ubiquitin by the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) and degraded by the proteasome, inactivating CDK1 and allowing the cell to complete division.

Types

Vertebrates have several B-type cyclins, principally cyclin B1 and cyclin B2 (with a B3 in some groups), which differ in localisation and partly in function. Cyclin B1 is the essential mitotic cyclin in mammals; its misregulation, like that of other cell-cycle proteins, is frequently observed in cancer cells, where checkpoint controls are compromised.
