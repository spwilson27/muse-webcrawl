Divergent thinking

Divergent thinking is a thought process or method used to generate creative ideas by exploring many possible solutions. The term was coined by psychologist J. P. Guilford in the 1950s. It typically occurs in a spontaneous, free-flowing, "non-linear" manner, such that many ideas are generated in an emergent cognitive fashion. Many possible solutions are explored in a short amount of time, and unexpected connections are drawn. Following divergent thinking, ideas and information are organized and structured using convergent thinking, which follows a particular set of logical steps to arrive at one solution, which in some cases is a "correct" solution.

Characteristics

Divergent thinking is characterized by fluency (the ability to produce many ideas), flexibility (the ability to produce ideas in different categories), originality (the ability to produce unusual ideas), and elaboration (the ability to develop ideas in detail). It is often measured by tasks such as the Unusual Uses Task, in which participants list as many uses as possible for a common object such as a brick.

Relation to creativity

Divergent thinking is frequently studied in the psychology of creativity. Researchers have found correlations between divergent thinking scores and creative achievement, though divergent thinking is generally considered a component of creativity rather than its equivalent. Personality traits associated with divergent thinking include openness to experience, curiosity, and willingness to take risks.
