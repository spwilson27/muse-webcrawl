Ralph Waldo Ellison (March 1, 1913 – April 16, 1994) was an American writer, literary critic and scholar best known for his novel Invisible Man, which won the National Book Award in 1953. He also wrote Shadow and Act (1964) and Going to the Territory (1986), collections of essays on literature, music and American culture.

Early life

Ellison was born in Oklahoma City, named after Ralph Waldo Emerson. His father died when he was three. A talented trumpeter, Ellison won a scholarship to study music at the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama (1933–1936), an experience echoed in Invisible Man. Moving to New York in 1936 hoping to earn money for his studies, he met Langston Hughes and Richard Wright, who encouraged him to write; he published reviews and stories and worked for the Federal Writers' Project.

Invisible Man

Published in 1952, Invisible Man tells of an unnamed Black narrator driven underground — literally, into a coal cellar lit by 1,369 light bulbs — after a series of betrayals by college, factory, political movement and society. Departing from the protest naturalism of Wright, Ellison drew on modernism, folklore, blues and jazz. The novel was an immediate triumph and in polls has been ranked among the greatest American novels of the 20th century.

Later life

Ellison spent four decades working on a second novel, published posthumously in edited form as Juneteenth (1999). He taught at Bard College, Rutgers and New York University, received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1969, and became the first African American admitted to the Century Association. He died of pancreatic cancer in Harlem in 1994.
