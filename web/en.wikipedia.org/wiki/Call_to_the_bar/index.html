Call to the bar

The call to the bar is the ceremony at which a person is formally admitted as a barrister. In England and Wales, students are called by one of the four Inns of Court — Lincoln's Inn, Inner Temple, Middle Temple, and Gray's Inn — which alone retain this power; the phrase "the Bar" itself refers to the bar of the court, the barrier separating the court from the public, which qualified advocates are entitled to pass.

[Image: newly called barristers in wigs and gowns outside their Inn's hall]

England and Wales

To be called, a student must join an inn, complete the academic and vocational stages of training (including the Bar training course), and attend the required qualifying sessions — historically, dining terms in hall. Call ceremonies are held several times a year in each inn's hall, where the Treasurer of the inn formally calls the graduands to the bar. Call alone does not entitle a person to practise: a new barrister must normally complete twelve months of pupillage in chambers and obtain a practising certificate from the Bar Standards Board before exercising full rights of audience.

Other jurisdictions

Comparable ceremonies and enrolments exist across the common-law world: in Ireland, barristers are called by the Chief Justice after study at the King's Inns; in Canada, provincial law societies hold call-to-the-bar ceremonies at which new lawyers take the oath; in many U.S. states, admission to the bar is marked by a swearing-in ceremony before a court. The idiom "called to the bar" is also used historically for judges "called to the Bench".
