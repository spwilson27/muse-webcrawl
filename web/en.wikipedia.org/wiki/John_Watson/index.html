John Broadus Watson (January 9, 1878 – September 25, 1958) was an American psychologist who founded behaviourism (behaviorism), the school that dominated experimental psychology for much of the early-to-mid twentieth century. In his 1913 manifesto lecture and paper, "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", Watson rejected the introspective study of consciousness and argued that psychology should be a purely objective natural science concerned with the prediction and control of observable behaviour.

Life and career

Watson was born in Travelers Rest, South Carolina, and raised in poverty in a religious household. He studied at Furman University and then the University of Chicago, where he earned his PhD in 1903 with research on animal learning. In 1908 he became professor of psychology at Johns Hopkins University, where he remained until 1920, editing the Psychological Review and serving as president of the American Psychological Association in 1915.

At Johns Hopkins, Watson extended Ivan Pavlov's classical conditioning to human emotion. With his graduate student Rosalie Rayner, he conducted the famous — and now notorious — "Little Albert" experiment (1920), conditioning an 11-month-old infant to fear a white rat by pairing its presentation with a loud noise; the fear generalised to other furry objects. The study demonstrated that emotional responses could be learned, but it is condemned today for its ethics and for never deconditioning the child.

In 1920 Watson was forced to resign from Johns Hopkins after his affair with Rayner became public; he married her and moved into advertising in New York, rising to a vice-presidency at the J. Walter Thompson agency, where he applied behavioural techniques — including market research and emotional appeals — to consumer products. He continued to popularise behaviourism in books such as Behaviorism (1924/1930) and Psychological Care of Infant and Child (1928), the latter advising a detached, scheduled approach to child-rearing that he later partly regretted.

Views

Watson championed the primacy of environment over heredity in shaping behaviour, most quotably in his boast: "Give me a dozen healthy infants, well-formed, and my own specified world to bring them up in and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist I might select — doctor, lawyer, artist, merchant-chief and, yes, even beggar-man and thief, regardless of his talents, penchants, tendencies, abilities, vocations, and race of his ancestors." He immediately added, in the sentence often omitted, "I am going beyond my facts and I admit it."

Legacy

Watson's methodological behaviourism set the agenda for decades of American psychology and paved the way for B. F. Skinner's radical behaviourism and operant conditioning, though cognitive psychologists from the 1960s onward reinstated the study of mental processes. In 1957, shortly before his death, the American Psychological Association honoured Watson for his foundational contributions to psychology.
