Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: माध्यमक, "Middle Way"), also known as Śūnyavāda ("the emptiness doctrine"), is a tradition of Mahāyāna Buddhist philosophy founded by the Indian monk-philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE). Madhyamaka thought had a decisive influence on the development of Mahāyāna Buddhism in India and became the dominant philosophical framework of Tibetan Buddhism, while also shaping East Asian schools.

Central ideas

The school's root text, Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, argues through relentless dialectical analysis (prasaṅga) that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of svabhāva — intrinsic, independent existence. Emptiness is identified with dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): because things arise only in dependence on causes, parts, and conceptual designation, they cannot exist by their own nature. Crucially, emptiness is not nihilism: Nāgārjuna calls emptiness itself empty, and frames his teaching as a "middle way" between eternalism and annihilationism. The doctrine of the two truths — conventional truth, within which the world, karma, and the Buddhist path function, and ultimate truth, the emptiness of all things — reconciles philosophical analysis with practice.

Development

Commentators such as Buddhapālita and Bhāviveka (6th century) split the tradition into styles later Tibetan doxographers labelled Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika; Candrakīrti (7th century), whose Madhyamakāvatāra and commentary on Nāgārjuna defended the consequentialist method, was largely forgotten in India but became the supreme authority in Tibet through Tsongkhapa's synthesis. Other key figures include Āryadeva, Nāgārjuna's chief disciple, and Śāntideva. The later tradition also debated the relation of Madhyamaka to Yogācāra and Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha) teachings, giving rise in Tibet to the rangtong/shentong distinction.

Legacy

Madhyamaka spread to China (as the Sanlun or "Three Treatises" school), Korea, Japan, and Tibet, where it anchors the scholastic curriculum of the Gelug and other orders and the logic of the great debate courtyards. Its arguments about identity, causation, and language continue to attract comparative philosophers.
