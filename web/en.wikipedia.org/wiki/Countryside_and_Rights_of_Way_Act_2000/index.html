The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 (c. 37), usually called the CRoW Act, is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. Its best-known provision created a statutory right of access on foot — often called the "right to roam" — to mapped areas of mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land in England and Wales, subject to certain restrictions and exclusions.

The Act also reformed the law of rights of way, strengthened protection for Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), introduced measures for the conservation of Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs), and amended wildlife protection law, including increased penalties and greater protection for wild birds and their nesting sites.

Background

Access campaigners had sought a general right to roam since the mass trespasses of the 1930s, most famously the 1932 trespass on Kinder Scout in the Peak District. Scotland legislated its own, broader access rights separately in the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003. The 1997 Labour government under Tony Blair committed to legislation, and the bill passed after lengthy debate between landowners' and walkers' organisations such as the Ramblers' Association.

Part I: Access to the countryside

Part I of the Act grants the public a right of access on foot for open-air recreation to "access land": land shown on statutory maps as mountain (land above 600 metres), moor, heath or down, all registered common land, and land dedicated voluntarily by its owner. The right is subject to restrictions in Schedule 2 — it does not extend to riding horses or bicycles, camping, or taking dogs off a short lead near livestock, among other limitations — and to land excepted under Schedule 1, such as gardens, parks, quarries in active use and military land.

About 865,000 hectares — roughly 8 per cent of England — was eventually mapped as access land, together with some 180,000 hectares in Wales. Landowners may close access land for up to 28 days a year (with notice), and longer closures require the consent of the access authority.

Other parts

  • Part II reformed public rights of way law: highway authorities must prepare rights of way improvement plans, and a cut-off date was set for recording certain historic rights of way on the definitive map (later postponed and, in England, slated for repeal by subsequent legislation).
  • Part III strengthened SSSI protection, imposing duties on public bodies and giving English Nature (now Natural England) greater powers, and provided for management agreements and payments.
  • Part IV required conservation boards for AONBs and management plans.
  • Part V amended the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, creating an offence of recklessly disturbing protected birds at the nest and increasing maximum penalties.

Implementation

The access provisions were rolled out region by region between 2004 and 2005, completing with the whole of England opened in October 2005. Later reforms built on the framework: the Marine and Coastal Access Act 2009 created the England Coast Path, and the government consulted on extending access to woodlands, riversides and other landscapes.
