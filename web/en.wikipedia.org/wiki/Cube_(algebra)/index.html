In arithmetic and algebra, the cube of a number n is its third power, the result of multiplying the number by itself twice: n3 = n × n × n. It is also the number multiplied by its square: n3 = n × n2. The term derives from geometry: the volume of a cube with side length n is n3.

Properties

Unlike squares, cubes can be negative: (−5)3 = −125. Every real number has exactly one real cube root. The sum of the first n cubes equals the square of the n-th triangular number: 13 + 23 + … + n3 = (1 + 2 + … + n)2.

A perfect cube is an integer that is the cube of an integer: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, …. Waring's problem asks how many cubes are needed to sum to any natural number; it is known that nine cubes always suffice (g(3) = 9), and that 19 suffices for all but finitely many (G(3) = 19).

In number theory

The last digit of a cube in base 10 determines the last digit of its cube root uniquely — a property not shared by squares. Fermat's Last Theorem states that no three positive integers satisfy a3 + b3 = c3; more generally the sum of two rational cubes can fail to be a rational cube, a fact tied to elliptic curves and the taxicab number 1729, the smallest number expressible as the sum of two positive cubes in two different ways.
