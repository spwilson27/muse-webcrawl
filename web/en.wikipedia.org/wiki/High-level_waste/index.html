High-level waste

High-level waste (HLW) is a type of nuclear waste created by the reprocessing of spent nuclear fuel, and in some jurisdictions spent fuel itself is classified as high-level waste. It contains highly radioactive fission products and transuranic elements, generates considerable decay heat, and remains hazardous for thousands to hundreds of thousands of years.

Characteristics

Fresh spent fuel is dominated by short-lived fission products such as caesium-137 and strontium-90 (half-lives of about 30 years), so its heat and radioactivity fall by roughly 99% in the first century. The long-term hazard comes from actinides such as neptunium-237 and plutonium isotopes. In reprocessing countries (France, Russia, Japan, India), separated HLW is vitrified — melted into borosilicate glass — and stored in steel canisters.

Disposal

The international consensus is that HLW should ultimately be emplaced in deep geological repositories. The United States' Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (for defence transuranic waste) opened in 1999; Finland's Onkalo repository is the world's first licensed repository for spent fuel, and Sweden and France have selected repository sites. The US civilian repository program at Yucca Mountain was defunded in 2011, leaving American spent fuel stored at reactor sites in pools and dry casks.
