Alaska Native corporation

Alaska divided into the twelve ANCSA regional corporation areas

Alaska Native corporations are for-profit corporations created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) of 1971 to settle the aboriginal land claims of Alaska Natives. In exchange for the extinguishment of most Native land claims, the Act transferred about 44 million acres of land and a cash settlement of $962.5 million to newly created Native-owned corporations rather than to tribal governments or individuals.

Structure

ANCSA established twelve geographic regions, each with a regional corporation, and more than 200 village corporations. A thirteenth regional corporation was later created for Alaska Natives living outside the state, receiving cash but no land. Eligible Alaska Natives received shares of stock in their regional and village corporations; shares initially could not be sold, traded or inherited outside Native ownership except by inheritance, a restriction Congress later allowed corporations to modify by shareholder vote.

Operations and significance

The corporations invest in natural resources, tourism, government contracting, construction and real estate, and several — such as Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, NANA Regional Corporation and Sealaska — rank among Alaska's largest businesses. They pay dividends to shareholders, run scholarship and cultural foundations, and are major political and economic actors in the state. The corporate model has been debated for decades, praised for generating Native-owned capital and criticised for exposing communal land and culture to commercial pressures; ANCSA village and regional corporations meanwhile remain central to subsistence management and land stewardship in rural Alaska.
