Boundary layer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Development of a boundary layer over a flat plate (image placeholder)

In fluid mechanics, a boundary layer is the thin layer of fluid in the immediate vicinity of a bounding surface, in which the effects of viscosity are significant. Within the boundary layer the flow velocity varies from zero at the surface (the no-slip condition) to the free-stream value at its outer edge.

History

The concept was introduced by Ludwig Prandtl in a celebrated 1904 paper, which showed that at high Reynolds numbers viscous effects are confined to a thin layer near the wall, while the outer flow can be treated as inviscid. This insight resolved longstanding paradoxes such as d'Alembert's paradox and founded modern aerodynamics.

Thickness and behaviour

For laminar flow over a flat plate of length x, the boundary-layer thickness grows approximately as δ ≈ 5x/√Rex. Boundary layers may be laminar or turbulent; transition typically occurs around Rex ≈ 5×105. Turbulent boundary layers are thicker, mix momentum more effectively, and produce higher skin-friction drag but resist separation better.

Separation

In an adverse pressure gradient the slow-moving fluid near the wall can be brought to a halt and the flow detaches, or separates, forming a wake. Separation governs the stall of airfoils, the drag of bluff bodies, and much of practical aerodynamic design; devices such as vortex generators and boundary-layer suction are used to delay it. The boundary-layer equations, derived by Prandtl from the Navier–Stokes equations, and the Blasius solution for the flat plate, are foundational results.

Other boundary layers

Analogous thermal and concentration boundary layers arise in heat and mass transfer, and the planetary boundary layer is a central concept in meteorology.
