Church Committee

The Church Committee (formally the United States Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities) was a U.S. Senate select committee in 1975 that investigated abuses by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), National Security Agency (NSA), Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), and the Internal Revenue Service.

Background

It was chaired by Senator Frank Church of Idaho, from whom it took its popular name. Revelations in 1974, including the New York Times reporting by Seymour Hersh on CIA domestic spying and the aftermath of Watergate, prompted Congress to examine intelligence activities that included assassination plots against foreign leaders, COINTELPRO surveillance of civil rights and antiwar groups, Operation SHAMROCK, and the opening of mail.

Investigations

The committee held public and closed hearings, reviewed classified documents, and interviewed officials. It documented plots against Fidel Castro, Patrice Lumumba and others, the FBI's campaign against Martin Luther King Jr., and widespread warrantless surveillance of Americans.

Reports and legacy

Its final reports in 1976 recommended permanent oversight. It led to creation of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, and passage of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978. Its disclosures remain a landmark in debates over secrecy and civil liberties, alongside the Pike Committee in the House.
