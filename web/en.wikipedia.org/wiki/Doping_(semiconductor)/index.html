Doping (semiconductor)

Donor and acceptor dopants create n-type and p-type semiconductors.

In semiconductor production, doping is the intentional introduction of impurities into an intrinsic (undoped) semiconductor for the purpose of modulating its electrical, optical, and structural properties. The doped material is referred to as an extrinsic semiconductor. Without doping, most semiconductors behave as isolators, rather than conductors, at room temperature; by carefully controlling the amount and kind of doping, conductivity can be varied over many orders of magnitude.

Carrier concentration

Even a very small amount of doping, such as 1 part in 100,000, can convert an otherwise poor conductor into a good one. A semiconductor doped to such high levels that it acts more like a conductor than a semiconductor is referred to as a degenerate semiconductor.

n-type

Doping with an element having one more valence electron than the host (for example, phosphorus in silicon) produces n-type material, in which electrons are the majority charge carriers; the dopant atoms are called donors.

p-type

Doping with an element having one fewer valence electron (for example, boron in silicon) produces p-type material, in which electron vacancies — holes — are the majority carriers; the dopant atoms are called acceptors.

Methods

Dopants are introduced during crystal growth, by thermal diffusion from a gas or deposited layer at high temperature, or by ion implantation, which fires energetic dopant ions into the wafer and offers precise control of dose and depth, followed by annealing to repair lattice damage and activate the dopants. The junction between n-type and p-type regions forms the p–n junction at the heart of diodes and transistors.

History

The effects of impurities on conductivity were observed in the earliest studies of selenium and cuprous oxide rectifiers. Systematic doping was developed during the Second World War for radar detectors, and was perfected by John Robert Woodyard, whose wartime work on doped germanium led to a fundamental patent on the process. Doping made possible the controllable semiconductor devices that displaced vacuum tubes.
