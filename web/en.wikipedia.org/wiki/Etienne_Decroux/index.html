Étienne Decroux (July 19, 1898 – March 12, 1991) was a French actor and mime artist regarded as the father of modern mime. He developed corporeal mime (mime corporel), a rigorous physical theatre technique that treats the actor's body — trunk, articulation and weight — as the primary instrument of expression, distinct from the illustrative gesture-language of traditional pantomime.

Career

Of working-class background, Decroux studied at Jacques Copeau's École du Vieux-Colombier in the 1920s, where he began his decades-long research into movement. He acted with companies of Charles Dullin and Louis Jouvet and appeared in films including Marcel Carné's Children of Paradise (1945), in which he played the deaf-mute Anselme Deburau alongside his former student Jean-Louis Barrault. Another student, Marcel Marceau, carried mime to worldwide popularity, though Marceau's illusionistic style diverged from Decroux's abstract, sculptural approach.

Corporeal mime

Decroux built a codified repertoire of walks, inclinations, rotations and "dynamo-rhythms", analysing how the body shows effort, resistance and thought; signature pieces such as The Factory, The Washerwoman and The Carpenter distilled labour into pure movement. After a period teaching in the United States at New York's Erwin Piscator Dramatic Workshop, he returned to Paris and taught at his school in Boulogne-Billancourt until late in life. His writings and the work of disciples such as his son Maximilien Decroux and the Théâtre de l'Ange Fou have kept corporeal mime central to physical theatre training.
