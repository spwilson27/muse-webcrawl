Newgate Prison

Newgate Prison was a prison at the corner of Newgate Street and the Old Bailey in the City of London, on the site of a gate in the Roman London Wall. It operated for over 700 years, from 1188 until its closure in 1902, and became notorious for its squalor and for the public executions held outside its walls.

History

A prison existed at Newgate from the 12th century. The gatehouse was rebuilt in the 15th century with funds from the will of Richard Whittington. The prison was repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt, notably after the Great Fire of London (1666) and during the Gordon Riots (1780). Public executions were moved from Tyburn to Newgate in 1783 and held outside the prison until public hanging was abolished in 1868; executions continued inside until 1902. The Ordinary of Newgate's published accounts of condemned prisoners were popular early examples of criminal biography.

Legacy

Conditions at Newgate, exposed by reformers such as Elizabeth Fry in the early 19th century, helped drive British prison reform. The prison was demolished in 1904 and the Central Criminal Court — the Old Bailey — was built on the site, opening in 1907. Famous inmates included Daniel Defoe, William Kidd and Casanova.
