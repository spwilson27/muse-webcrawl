Gnaeus Domitius Annius Ulpianus (c. 170 – 223/228), commonly known as Ulpian, was a Roman jurist born in Tyre in Roman Syria. He was a prolific and systematic legal writer, and he served as praetorian prefect — effectively chief minister — under the emperor Severus Alexander. His writings supplied roughly a third of Justinian's Digest, through which they profoundly shaped the civil-law tradition.

Career

Ulpian was a pupil of the jurist Papinian, under whom he served in the imperial administration. Exiled by the emperor Elagabalus, he was recalled by Severus Alexander in 222, becoming the emperor's chief adviser and praetorian prefect. His attempts to curb the power and privileges of the Praetorian Guard made him hated by them, and he was murdered by mutinous guardsmen — according to the sources, in the emperor's own palace and in his mother's presence, or shortly afterwards.

Works and ideas

Ulpian wrote commentaries on the praetorian edict (Ad edictum, 83 books) and on the civil law, monographs on individual topics, and works on the duties of officials. His famous definitions became canonical: justice is "the constant and perpetual will to give each his due"; the precepts of law are "to live honestly, not to injure another, and to give each his own"; and jurisprudence is "the knowledge of things divine and human, the science of the just and the unjust".

Ulpian distinguished public law from private law and articulated an influential account of natural law, ius naturale, which he — unusually — extended even to animals. He is also known for a life table (Ulpian's table) used for valuing annuities and usufructs, an early exercise in actuarial computation. The Law of Citations (426) ranked him among the five authoritative jurists.
