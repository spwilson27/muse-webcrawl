John Webster (c. 1578 – c. 1634) was an English Jacobean dramatist best known for his tragedies The White Devil and The Duchess of Malfi, which are often regarded as masterpieces of early 17th-century English theatre. Along with William Shakespeare, he is considered one of the finest dramatists of his age, famed for his dark, densely poetic vision of human corruption.

Life

Little is known of Webster's life. He was the son of a prosperous London coachmaker and tailor, and was probably educated at Merchant Taylors' School. By 1602 he was writing for Philip Henslowe's theatrical syndicate, collaborating with Thomas Dekker, Thomas Heywood, John Ford, and others on comedies and city plays, including Westward Ho and Northward Ho. He also wrote the induction to Marston's The Malcontent and contributed to Middleton and Rowley's works.

Tragedies

Webster's reputation rests on his two great solo tragedies, both drawn from Italian sources. The White Devil (first performed 1612) dramatizes the intrigues surrounding Vittoria Accoramboni, murdered in Padua in 1585. The Duchess of Malfi (1613–14), based on the story of Giovanna d'Aragona, Duchess of Amalfi, depicts a widowed duchess who secretly marries her steward and is persecuted and murdered by her brothers, the Cardinal and Ferdinand. The plays are celebrated for their macabre imagery, memorable villains and heroines, and lines such as "Cover her face; mine eyes dazzle: she died young" and "I know death hath ten thousand several doors / For men to take their exit."

Later work and death

Webster also wrote the tragicomedy The Devil's Law-Case (c. 1617), the lost tragedy The Guise, and a Lord Mayor's pageant, Monuments of Honour (1624). He died around 1634. After a period of neglect, his tragedies were revived by the Romantics — Charles Lamb praised them in 1808 — and they are now staples of the classical repertory, frequently performed by companies such as the Royal Shakespeare Company.
