François Mitterrand

François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (26 October 1916 – 8 January 1996) was a French statesman who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest-serving president in French history and the first of the Socialist Party. His two terms saw the abolition of the death penalty, major decentralisation reforms, a period of "cohabitation" with a right-wing government, and a central French role in the Maastricht Treaty creating the European Union.

Early life and career

Born in Jarnac in southwestern France, Mitterrand studied law and political science in Paris. Mobilised in 1939, he was wounded and captured in 1940, escaped from German captivity, and worked in the Vichy administration's prisoners' service while moving into the Resistance. After the war he was elected a deputy and served as a minister in eleven Fourth Republic governments, including as Minister of the Interior and Minister of Justice.

Road to the Élysée

An opponent of de Gaulle's Fifth Republic, Mitterrand narrowly lost the 1974 presidential election to Valéry Giscard d'Estaing. As First Secretary of the Socialist Party from 1971, he rebuilt the French left and in May 1981 defeated Giscard to become president.

Presidency

His first government, which included Communist ministers, nationalised banks and major industries, raised the minimum wage and abolished the death penalty under Justice Minister Robert Badinter. Economic difficulties forced a turn to austerity (the tournant de la rigueur) in 1983. Re-elected in 1988, Mitterrand served through a second term marked by the fall of the Berlin Wall, German reunification, the Gulf War and the Maastricht Treaty; from 1993 he governed in "cohabitation" with the right-wing premier Édouard Balladur. He also commissioned the "Grands Projets" that reshaped Paris, including the Louvre Pyramid, the Bastille Opera and the National Library that now bears his name.

Death and legacy

Mitterrand had been diagnosed with prostate cancer shortly after his election, a fact concealed until 1992. He died in Paris on 8 January 1996, eight months after leaving office. He remains a defining figure of the French Fifth Republic.
