L-system

An L-system or Lindenmayer system is a parallel rewriting system and a type of formal grammar. It consists of an alphabet of symbols, a collection of production rules that expand each symbol into a larger string, an initial "axiom" string, and a mechanism for translating the generated strings into geometric structures. L-systems were introduced in 1968 by the Hungarian theoretical biologist and botanist Aristid Lindenmayer as a mathematical model of plant development.

Structure

An L-system is a tuple G = (V, ω, P), where V is an alphabet of symbols (variables and constants), ω is the axiom (starting string), and P is a set of production rules. Unlike a Chomsky grammar, productions are applied in parallel to all symbols simultaneously, reflecting the simultaneous growth of cells in an organism.

A classic example models algae growth: variables A, B; axiom A; rules A → AB and B → A. Successive generations produce A, AB, ABA, ABAAB, ABAABABA… — strings whose lengths follow the Fibonacci numbers.

Variants and interpretation

L-systems may be context-free (D0L systems when deterministic) or context-sensitive; stochastic L-systems choose rules probabilistically, and parametric L-systems attach numerical parameters to symbols. For graphics, strings are interpreted as turtle graphics commands: F draws forward, + and − turn, and brackets [ ] push and pop the turtle state, enabling branching structures such as trees and ferns. Combined with the fractal-like self-similarity of the rewriting process, L-systems generate realistic models of plants, trees and other branching organisms, and are used in computer graphics and procedural generation.

History

Lindenmayer developed the formalism to model the development of multicellular organisms such as the filamentous alga Anabaena. The 1990 book The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants by Przemysław Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer popularised L-systems for plant modelling and computer graphics.
