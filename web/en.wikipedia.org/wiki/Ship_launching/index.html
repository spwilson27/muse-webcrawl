Ship launching

Ship launching is the process of transferring a vessel to the water. It is a naval tradition in many cultures, dating back thousands of years, that has been observed for countless vessels, from canoes and merchantmen to the largest battleships and aircraft carriers. The term also describes the process of placing a new vessel into the water for the first time upon completion of its hull, having been built ashore on slipways or in a dry dock.

Methods

There are three principal methods of launching. In a longitudinal (end) launch, the ship slides stern-first down inclined ways into the water; this was the standard method for vessels built on slipways and gives the spectacle familiar from traditional ceremonies. In a sideways launch, the ship is dropped or rolled sideways into the water — used where the waterway is too narrow for an end launch, as on the Great Lakes and in many river yards, producing a dramatic splash and rolling motion as the vessel rights itself. In an air-bag launch, now common in smaller yards, the hull rolls into the water on deflating rubber airbags. Vessels built in dry docks are simply floated out (or floated up) by flooding the dock when construction is complete.

Launch ceremony

Launchings have traditionally been public ceremonial occasions. Customs include a sponsor — frequently a woman of prominence — naming the vessel and breaking a bottle of champagne (or, historically, wine) across its bow, a practice believed to bring luck; a bottle that fails to break is traditionally viewed as an ill omen. Religious blessings, national anthems, flags and bunting, and crowds of shipyard workers accompany the event. The launch is a milestone distinct from later stages: after launching, the vessel is fitted out at a pier, undergoes trials, and only then is commissioned or delivered.

Risks

Launching is one of the most perilous moments in a ship's life: the hull experiences stresses it may never meet again, and failures have been catastrophic. The 1858 launch of Brunel's SS Great Eastern sideways down her ways took months of hydraulic pushing. Calculation errors in weight and sliding friction, or in the buoyancy and stability of the partly immersed hull, have caused vessels to stick on the ways, strike the opposite bank, or in rare cases capsize.
