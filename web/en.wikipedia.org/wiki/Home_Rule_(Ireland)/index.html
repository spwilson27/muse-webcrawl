Home Rule (Ireland)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Home Rule was the name given to the political movement in Ireland from the 1870s until the early 1920s that sought self-government for Ireland within the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. Supporters of Home Rule, known as Home Rulers, generally sought the creation of an Irish parliament responsible for domestic affairs, while Ireland would remain part of the United Kingdom and the British monarch would remain head of state.

Background

Ireland had been united with Great Britain under the Acts of Union 1800, which abolished the Parliament of Ireland and brought Irish MPs to Westminster. Many Irish nationalists resented rule from London and sought first the repeal of the Union and later a measure of devolved government.

The Home Rule movement

In 1870 Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association. Butt was succeeded in 1879 by Charles Stewart Parnell, who turned the Irish Parliamentary Party into a disciplined political force at Westminster. Under Parnell, and later John Redmond, the party held the balance of power in the House of Commons and pressed successive Liberal governments to introduce Home Rule legislation.

The Home Rule bills

Three bills were introduced. The First Home Rule Bill of 1886, championed by Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone, was defeated in the Commons and split the Liberal Party. The Second Home Rule Bill of 1893 passed the Commons but was rejected by the House of Lords. The Third Home Rule Bill, introduced in 1912 under H. H. Asquith, became law as the Government of Ireland Act 1914 after the Parliament Act 1911 removed the Lords' veto, but its implementation was suspended for the duration of the First World War.

Opposition and the Ulster crisis

Unionists, particularly Protestants in Ulster, fiercely opposed Home Rule, fearing domination by a Catholic-majority parliament in Dublin; their slogan was "Home Rule is Rome Rule". In 1912 unionists signed the Ulster Covenant, and the Ulster Volunteer Force was formed. Nationalists responded by forming the Irish Volunteers. The crisis brought Ireland to the brink of civil war in 1914.

Aftermath

The Easter Rising of 1916 and the subsequent rise of Sinn Féin, which demanded an independent republic rather than devolution, eclipsed the Home Rule movement. After the Irish War of Independence, the Government of Ireland Act 1920 created separate home rule parliaments for Northern Ireland and Southern Ireland; the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921 led to the Irish Free State in 1922.
