Moist heat sterilization

Moist heat sterilization is a sterilization method that uses hot water vapour (steam) under pressure to kill microorganisms, including bacterial endospores. It is the most widely used method for sterilizing heat-stable medical instruments, surgical dressings, laboratory media and pharmaceutical preparations, and is most commonly performed in an autoclave.

Principle

Moist heat kills microorganisms by denaturing and coagulating their proteins. Saturated steam transfers heat far more efficiently than dry air because the latent heat released as steam condenses on a cooler surface raises its temperature rapidly; consequently, moist heat sterilizes at lower temperatures and in shorter times than dry heat. Standard conditions are 121 °C at approximately 15 psi (103 kPa) above atmospheric pressure for 15–20 minutes, or 134 °C for 3–4 minutes in pre-vacuum sterilizers.

Process

A typical autoclave cycle comprises conditioning (air removal and steam penetration), the exposure or holding period at the sterilizing temperature, and exhaust and drying. Complete air removal is essential, since trapped air prevents steam contact and creates cold spots. Cycles are validated using physical monitoring (temperature and pressure records), chemical indicators, and biological indicators — typically spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus, whose destruction demonstrates sterility.

Applications and limitations

Moist heat is used for surgical instruments, glassware, culture media, aqueous pharmaceutical solutions and biohazardous waste. It is unsuitable for heat-labile materials, powders and oils that steam cannot penetrate, and anhydrous substances; such items require dry heat, filtration, irradiation or chemical sterilants instead. Sterility assurance is expressed as a sterility assurance level (SAL), conventionally a probability of a surviving microorganism of 10−6 or less.
