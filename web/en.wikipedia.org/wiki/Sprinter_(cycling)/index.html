Sprinter (cycling)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sprinter is a road bicycle racer or track cyclist who can finish a race very explosively by accelerating quickly to high speed, typically using the slipstream of other cyclists to conserve energy until the final metres. Sprinters are specialists in flat finishes and are among the most celebrated riders in the sport, contesting stage victories in Grand Tours and the points classifications that reward consistent high finishes.

Road sprinting

In a bunch finish, a sprinter's team organises a lead-out train: teammates ride at the front in turn at maximum effort, sheltering their sprinter from the wind until the final 200–300 metres, when the last lead-out rider swings aside and the sprinter launches. Top sprinters reach speeds above 70 km/h (43 mph) in the final metres. Because they usually carry more muscle mass than climbers, sprinters struggle in the mountains, where their aim is merely to finish within the time limit (gruppetto), yet they dominate flat stages and one-day classics such as Milan–San Remo when the race stays together.

Renowned road sprinters include Mario Cipollini, Erik Zabel, Mark Cavendish — who holds the record for Tour de France stage wins — André Greipel, Marcel Kittel, Peter Sagan (a versatile sprinter-classics rider), and Jasper Philipsen. In Grand Tours, sprinters compete for the points jerseys: the green jersey of the Tour de France and the ciclamino jersey of the Giro d'Italia.

Track sprinting

On the velodrome, "sprinter" also denotes specialists in the match sprint, team sprint, and keirin. Track sprinters are heavily muscled power athletes; tactical cat-and-mouse riding at low speed precedes a final explosive lap. The keirin, originating in Japanese gambling racing, uses a motorised pacer (derny) to bring riders up to speed before a sprint finish.

Physiology

Sprinters rely on a high proportion of fast-twitch muscle fibres and can produce peak power outputs exceeding 2,000 watts for a few seconds. Their training emphasises maximal strength, short accelerations, and repeated sprint endurance, in contrast to the sustained aerobic output of climbers and time trialists.
