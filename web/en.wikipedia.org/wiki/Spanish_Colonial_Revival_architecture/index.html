Spanish Colonial Revival architecture

A Spanish Colonial Revival residence in California

Spanish Colonial Revival architecture (also known simply as Spanish Eclectic) was a dominant US architectural stylistic movement of the early 20th century, arising in Florida and California and spreading nationwide. It drew inspiration from the architecture of the Spanish colonies of the Americas — and from Spain itself — including elements of Moorish, Byzantine, Gothic, and Renaissance precedents filtered through colonial building traditions. Hallmarks include smooth stucco walls, low-pitched red clay-tile roofs, arched windows and doorways, wrought-iron ornament, decorative tilework, and asymmetrical facades, often arranged around courtyards and patios.

Origins

The style's formal debut came at the 1915 Panama–California Exposition in San Diego, where Bertram Goodhue and Carleton Winslow designed the buildings of Balboa Park in an opulent Spanish Baroque (Churrigueresque) idiom, drawing on their study of colonial churches in Mexico. The exposition ignited popular taste: after the 1925 earthquake, Santa Barbara rebuilt itself largely in the Spanish Colonial Revival manner under strong design review, becoming a showcase of the style.

Spread and characteristics

In Florida, architects such as Addison Mizner built romantic Spanish-inspired resorts and houses in Palm Beach and Boca Raton during the 1920s land boom. In California and the Southwest, developers and architects including George Washington Smith, Wallace Neff, and Paul Williams made the style the default for suburban houses, apartment courts, railway stations, theatres, and schools between the wars.

Typical features include:

  • Stucco wall surfaces, often white or pastel
  • Low-pitched roofs of red barrel tile, with little or no eaves overhang
  • Arches over doors, windows, and porches
  • Wrought-iron window grilles, balconies, and light fixtures
  • Glazed decorative tiles, courtyards, fountains, and arcaded loggias
  • Occasional towers, carved stone or cast-stone ornament, and painted ceilings

Legacy

Falling from fashion after the Second World War, the style was revived in the late 20th century and remains ubiquitous in the American Sun Belt, where stucco-and-tile subdivisions continue its vocabulary in simplified form. It is related to, but distinct from, the earlier Mission Revival style, which was inspired more narrowly by the Spanish missions of the Southwest.
