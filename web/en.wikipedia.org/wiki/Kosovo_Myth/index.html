Kosovo Myth

The Kosovo Myth, also known as the Kosovo cult or Kosovo legend, is a Serbian national myth concerning the Battle of Kosovo in 1389 and its hero, Prince Lazar Hrebeljanović. The myth interprets the defeat of the Serbian army by the Ottoman Empire not as a simple military loss but as a moral and spiritual victory: Lazar is said to have been offered a choice between an earthly kingdom and a heavenly kingdom, and to have chosen the latter, consecrating the Serbian nation through sacrifice.

Origins and development

The earliest texts mourning Lazar, composed in the decades after the battle, were liturgical works produced within the Serbian Orthodox Church, which canonised him as a saint and martyr. Over centuries of Ottoman rule the story was preserved and elaborated in oral epic poetry, sung to the gusle by folk singers. In the 19th century the folklorist Vuk Stefanović Karadžić collected and published the Kosovo cycle of epic poems, and the myth became a cornerstone of Serbian national identity and state-building.

Central motifs

Key figures beside Lazar include Miloš Obilić, the hero who assassinated Sultan Murad I; the tragic heroine, the Maiden of Kosovo, who tends the wounded on the battlefield; and Vuk Branković, cast in legend (against historical evidence) as the traitor whose desertion caused the defeat. The feast of Vidovdan (St Vitus Day, 28 June) became the sacred anniversary of the battle.

Modern political use

The myth was invoked at major junctures, including the Balkan Wars, the First World War, and most notoriously at the 600th anniversary in 1989, when Slobodan Milošević addressed a huge crowd at Gazimestan, the battle site, shortly before the Yugoslav wars. Historians debate how the legend's themes of sacrifice and betrayal have shaped Serbian politics toward Kosovo.
