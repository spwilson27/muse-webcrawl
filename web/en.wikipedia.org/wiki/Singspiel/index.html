Singspiel

Singspiel
Stylistic originsGerman theatre, ballad opera, opéra comique
Cultural origins18th century, German-speaking Europe
Typical featuresSpoken dialogue alternating with songs and ensembles

A Singspiel (German: [ˈzɪŋˌʃpiːl]; plural: Singspiele; literally "sing-play") is a form of German-language music drama, now regarded as a genre of opera. It is characterised by the alternation of spoken dialogue with musical numbers — songs, ballads, arias and ensembles — and often includes comic or folk-like elements, magical or exotic settings, and a rescue plot.

Origins

The Singspiel developed in the mid-18th century from translations and imitations of English ballad operas and French opéras comiques. Early examples by composers such as Johann Adam Hiller, including Die Jagd (1770), established the genre's mix of simple, tuneful songs and spoken scenes. Emperor Joseph II encouraged German-language works and founded the Nationalsingspiel in Vienna in 1778.

Notable works

  • Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — Die Entführung aus dem Serail (1782) and Die Zauberflöte (The Magic Flute, 1791), the most famous Singspiel
  • Carl Maria von Weber — Der Freischütz (1821)
  • Ludwig van Beethoven — Fidelio (1805, rev. 1814) is in Singspiel form
  • Heinrich Marschner and Albert Lortzing continued the tradition in the 19th century

Characteristics

Unlike Italian opera of the period, which used sung recitative, the Singspiel relied on ordinary speech between numbers, making it accessible to broad audiences. Its songs were often strophic and folk-like. The genre was an important precursor of German Romantic opera and of modern musical theatre.
