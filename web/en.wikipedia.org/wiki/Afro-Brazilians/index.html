Afro-Brazilians (Portuguese: afro-brasileiros) are Brazilians who have predominantly African ancestry. The term generally refers to people classified as pretos (black) and sometimes pardos (brown/mixed) in Brazilian census terminology. Most Afro-Brazilians are descendants of Africans brought to Brazil during the transatlantic slave trade, which lasted from the 16th century until slavery was abolished in 1888.

History

Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other country in the Americas — an estimated 4.8 million people, roughly 40 percent of all Africans transported across the Atlantic. Enslaved laborers worked on sugar plantations in the Northeast, in gold and diamond mines in Minas Gerais, and on coffee estates in the Southeast. Brazil was the last country in the Western Hemisphere to abolish slavery, doing so with the Golden Law (Lei Áurea) of 13 May 1888.

Culture

African heritage profoundly shaped Brazilian culture, including music genres such as samba and maracatu, the martial art capoeira, the Afro-Brazilian religions Candomblé and Umbanda, and the cuisine of Bahia, exemplified by dishes such as acarajé and moqueca. The state of Bahia, whose capital Salvador was the first colonial capital, has the highest proportion of Afro-Brazilian residents.

Demographics

In the 2022 census, about 10.2 percent of Brazilians self-identified as preto and about 45.3 percent as pardo, together forming a majority of the population. Afro-Brazilians remain disproportionately affected by poverty and violence, and affirmative-action policies, including racial quotas in federal universities introduced in 2012, have been adopted to address inequality.
