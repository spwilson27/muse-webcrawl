Miniskirt

A miniskirt (sometimes hyphenated as mini-skirt or simply mini) is a skirt with its hemline well above the knees, generally at mid-thigh level, normally no longer than 10 centimetres (4 inches) below the buttocks. It became an iconic fashion garment of the 1960s and a symbol of the decade's youth culture and changing social attitudes.

Origins

Short skirts had appeared earlier in the 20th century in sportswear, dance costumes and science fiction, but the miniskirt as everyday fashion emerged in London in the mid-1960s. British designer Mary Quant, whose King's Road boutique Bazaar opened in 1955, is most often credited with popularizing it; she named the skirt after her favourite car, the Mini. French couturier André Courrèges independently showed short skirts in his 1964 collections, and the question of priority between Quant and Courrèges has been debated ever since. Model Jean Shrimpton caused a sensation wearing a short white dress to the Melbourne Cup in 1965.

Cultural significance

Rising hemlines tracked the availability of tights (pantyhose), which replaced stockings and suspenders and made very short skirts practical. The miniskirt became associated with the "Swinging London" scene, women's liberation and generational rebellion, and provoked bans and protests in some countries and institutions. The even shorter micro-skirt appeared in the late 1960s.

Later history

Miniskirts have repeatedly returned to fashion, notably in the 1980s and in the "Y2K" revival of the early 2020s, and remain a staple of popular dress. The garment's cultural meaning continues to be discussed in debates about modesty, objectification and personal expression.
