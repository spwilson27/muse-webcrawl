Custom car

A custom car is a passenger vehicle that has been substantially modified to improve its performance, change its aesthetics, or both. Custom cars are distinct from hot rods in emphasis: while hot rodding centers on performance, customizing traditionally centers on styling — reshaping bodywork for a sleeker, lower, more flowing appearance.

History

Customizing began in the United States in the 1930s and flourished after the Second World War, particularly in Southern California. Shops run by builders such as George and Sam Barris, the Alexander Brothers and Ed "Big Daddy" Roth turned late-1940s cars — especially the 1949–1951 Mercury — into "lead sleds," named for the lead body filler used before plastic fillers. Signature techniques include chopping (lowering the roof), channeling (lowering the body over the frame), sectioning, shaving trim and door handles, and fitting elaborate multi-layer candy and metalflake paint jobs.

Styles

Later movements include lowriders with hydraulic suspension, restomods combining classic styling with modern drivetrains, and the Japanese and European tuning scenes. Famous customs include the Hirohata Merc (1952) and Roth's bubble-topped show cars of the 1960s.
