The contact angle (symbol θ) is the angle, conventionally measured through the liquid, where a liquid–vapour interface meets a solid surface. It quantifies the wettability of a solid surface by a liquid via the Young equation. A given system of solid, liquid and vapour at a given temperature and pressure has a unique equilibrium contact angle. Low contact angles indicate that the liquid spreads over the surface (good wetting, hydrophilic for water), while high contact angles indicate poor wetting and beading of the liquid (hydrophobic for water).

The contact angle is determined by the balance of adhesive forces between the liquid and solid and cohesive forces within the liquid itself. It is a central concept in surface science, with applications in coatings, printing, adhesion, oil recovery, and microfluidics.

Young's equation

In 1805 Thomas Young described the equilibrium of forces at the three-phase contact line. For an ideal (smooth, homogeneous, rigid) surface, the equilibrium contact angle θY is related to the interfacial tensions by Young's equation:

γSG = γSL + γLG cos θY

where γSG, γSL and γLG are the solid–gas, solid–liquid and liquid–gas interfacial tensions (surface energies) respectively. Rearranged, cos θY = (γSG − γSL) / γLG.

Interpretation

For water on a solid surface, conventional ranges are:

  • θ < 90° — hydrophilic; the liquid wets the surface and spreads.
  • θ > 90° — hydrophobic; the liquid beads up and minimises contact.
  • θ > 150° — superhydrophobic; droplets are nearly spherical and roll off readily, as on the lotus leaf.
  • θ ≈ 0° — complete (perfect) wetting; the liquid spreads as a thin film.

A surface with a water contact angle near zero is sometimes called superhydrophilic. Classic examples of hydrophobic surfaces include waxes and fluoropolymers such as PTFE (Teflon), with water contact angles around 110–120°.

Contact angle hysteresis

Real surfaces rarely give a single contact angle. The advancing contact angle is measured as liquid is added to a drop, and the receding contact angle as liquid is withdrawn; their difference is the contact angle hysteresis. Hysteresis arises from surface roughness, chemical heterogeneity, and reconfiguration of the surface. The angle at which a droplet begins to slide down an inclined plane is related to the hysteresis and governs droplet mobility.

Measurement

The most common technique is the sessile drop method: a droplet is deposited on the surface, imaged in profile with a camera, and the angle fitted from the drop shape — the instrument is called a contact angle goniometer. For complete shape analysis, the axisymmetric drop shape analysis (ADSA) fits the Laplace–Young equation to the whole profile. Other methods include the Wilhelmy plate method for fibres and plates, the captive bubble method (useful for surfaces immersed in liquid), and the Washburn method for powders.

Roughness and heterogeneity

Two classical models modify Young's angle for non-ideal surfaces. The Wenzel model treats a chemically homogeneous but rough surface: roughness amplifies the intrinsic wetting behaviour, making hydrophilic surfaces more hydrophilic and hydrophobic ones more hydrophobic. The Cassie–Baxter model treats a composite surface — for example a droplet sitting on a mixture of solid and trapped air pockets — and underlies the design of superhydrophobic surfaces inspired by the lotus effect.

Applications

Contact angle measurements are used to characterise surface treatments and contamination in semiconductor manufacturing, to design waterproof and self-cleaning coatings, to predict print quality and adhesive bond strength, in enhanced oil recovery where wettability of reservoir rock controls production, and in the study of lung surfactants and biomedical implants.
