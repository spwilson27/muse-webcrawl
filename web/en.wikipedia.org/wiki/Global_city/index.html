A global city, also called a world city or alpha city, is a city that serves as a primary node in the global economic network. The concept originates in geography and urban studies and rests on the idea that globalization creates strategic geographic locations — cities — that can be ranked according to their importance in linking spatially distinct regions into a single global economy.

Characteristics

Global cities are characterized by a high concentration of advanced producer services — banking, finance, law, accounting, advertising and management consultancy — along with corporate headquarters, stock exchanges, major universities, cultural institutions and international transport hubs. They attract a highly skilled transnational workforce and often host large immigrant communities, making them cosmopolitan centers of exchange.

Rankings

The best-known ranking is produced by the Globalization and World Cities Research Network (GaWC) at Loughborough University, which classifies cities into Alpha, Beta and Gamma tiers based on the office networks of major service firms. London and New York consistently occupy the top "Alpha++" category. Other indices, such as the Global Power City Index and the A.T. Kearney Global Cities Index, weigh economic activity, human capital, information exchange, cultural experience and political engagement.

Criticism

Critics argue that rankings overemphasize corporate connectivity at the expense of livability, inequality, environmental sustainability and the experience of ordinary residents. Global cities frequently display sharp internal contrasts between wealthy financial districts and impoverished peripheral neighborhoods.
