Amicus curiae

An amicus curiae (Latin for "friend of the court"; plural amici curiae) is a person or organisation that is not a party to a legal case but offers the court information, expertise or insight relevant to the issues before it, usually by filing a written submission known as an amicus brief. The court decides whether to accept the brief.

Role and practice

Amicus participation is especially common in appellate litigation, including before the Supreme Court of the United States, where interest groups, professional associations, academics, industry bodies and governments file briefs supporting one side or addressing the wider consequences of a ruling. Briefs may present legal argument, empirical research or technical knowledge that the parties have not supplied. Under Supreme Court rules, amici generally need the consent of the parties or leave of the Court, and must disclose who funded the brief.

History

The device derives from Roman law and developed in English common law as a way for bystanders to alert the court to errors or relevant authority. In the United States it grew dramatically in the twentieth century, as organisations such as the NAACP and the ACLU used amicus briefs in landmark civil rights litigation, and it has since become a standard feature of major constitutional cases, sometimes attracting scores of briefs in a single case.

International practice

Amicus submissions are also accepted, with variations, by international courts and tribunals such as the European Court of Human Rights and the International Court of Justice, and by courts in many common law and some civil law jurisdictions, where they allow civil-society and expert voices to be heard without becoming parties.
