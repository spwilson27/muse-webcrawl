Tyranny

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tyranny is a form of government in which one person — a tyrant — holds absolute power, typically seized or maintained by force or manipulation rather than by lawful succession. In modern usage the term is strongly pejorative, implying cruel and oppressive rule.

Ancient Greece

In archaic and classical Greece, a tyrannos was a ruler who had seized power unconstitutionally; the word did not originally carry its later moral charge. Tyrants such as Pisistratus of Athens, Polycrates of Samos and Dionysius I of Syracuse often rose by championing the common people against aristocracies, and many were capable administrators and patrons of the arts.

Philosophical analysis

Plato and Aristotle classified tyranny as the worst form of government — the perversion of monarchy, in which the ruler governs in his own interest rather than the common good. Aristotle analysed the methods by which tyrants preserve power, including sowing distrust among citizens and keeping them poor and occupied.

Modern usage

The concept shaped Enlightenment and revolutionary thought: the American Declaration of Independence justified rebellion by cataloguing the "tyranny" of George III. Today "tyrant" is applied to authoritarian rulers generally, and phrases such as "tyranny of the majority" (Tocqueville, Mill) extend the term to democratic abuses.
