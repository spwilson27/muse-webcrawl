Thermal efficiency

DefinitionUseful energy output divided by heat input
Symbolηth
Upper boundCarnot efficiency for a heat engine between two reservoirs
Applies toHeat engines, power plants, heating and cooling devices (with related coefficients)

In thermodynamics, thermal efficiency is the fraction of heat supplied to a device that is converted into the desired output. For a heat engine the output is net work, so ηth = Wnet/Qin. Because energy is conserved, the remainder leaves as rejected heat or is lost through friction, exhaust, radiation and auxiliary loads. Thermal efficiency is dimensionless and is often expressed as a percentage.

Heat engines and the Carnot limit

The second law of thermodynamics imposes an upper limit independent of mechanical ingenuity. A reversible Carnot engine operating between a hot reservoir at absolute temperature TH and a cold reservoir at TC has efficiency 1 − TC/TH. Real engines fall below this because combustion, heat transfer across finite temperature differences, throttling, friction and unsteady flow generate entropy. Raising peak temperature and pressure, reducing exhaust losses and recovering waste heat can improve performance but cannot cross the Carnot bound for the chosen reservoirs.

Different efficiencies

Engineers qualify the term carefully. Indicated efficiency uses work developed inside an engine cylinder; brake efficiency uses shaft output after friction. A power plant may report gross or net efficiency depending on whether station auxiliary power is subtracted, and fuel-based values differ according to whether the higher or lower heating value of the fuel is used. For heat pumps and refrigerators the analogous figure is a coefficient of performance, which can exceed 1 because the device moves heat rather than converting all input work into heat flow.

Typical values

Small petrol engines often convert roughly a fifth to a third of fuel energy into shaft work; modern automotive diesels and hybrid-assisted engines can do better. Large steam plants reach around the high thirties to mid-forties percent depending on conditions, while combined-cycle gas turbines can exceed 60% on a lower-heating-value basis in favourable utility service. These numbers are sensitive to load, ambient temperature, maintenance and accounting convention, so comparisons require care.

Losses and improvement

Major losses include heat rejected to cooling water or air, hot exhaust gases, incomplete combustion, pumping work, mechanical friction and generator or gearbox losses. Combined heat and power changes the question by treating some “waste” heat as useful output for buildings or industry. Bottoming cycles, turbo-compounding, waste-heat boilers, regenerative feed heating and better materials all try to extract work before energy degrades to ambient temperature.

Relation to exergy

Thermal efficiency counts energy quantities but not energy quality. Exergy analysis measures how much of an input could theoretically become work relative to the environment. A process can have respectable first-law efficiency while destroying much exergy through large temperature drops or mixing. For that reason advanced plant diagnosis often reports both thermal and exergetic efficiencies.
