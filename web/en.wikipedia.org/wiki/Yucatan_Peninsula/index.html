The Yucatán Peninsula (Spanish: Península de Yucatán) is a large peninsula in southeastern Mexico and adjacent portions of Belize and Guatemala that separates the Caribbean Sea from the Gulf of Mexico, with the Yucatán Channel between the peninsula and Cuba connecting the two. The Mexican part comprises the states of Yucatán, Campeche and Quintana Roo, and the peninsula also includes the Petén Department of Guatemala and northern Belize.

Geography and geology

The peninsula is a great slab of limestone — part of the Yucatán Platform — lying generally low and flat, with only the modest Puuc hills rising in the south. Its northern shore forms the southern edge of the Bay of Campeche, and its eastern Caribbean coast is fringed by the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef, the largest barrier reef in the Western Hemisphere.

Some 66 million years ago the peninsula's northwest coast was struck by the asteroid that formed the Chicxulub crater, an impact widely held responsible for the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs. The buried crater is marked at the surface by a ring of sinkholes, the Ring of Cenotes.

Hydrology

Because the limestone is porous karst, the peninsula has virtually no surface rivers in the north. Rainwater percolates down to a freshwater lens above the water table, accessible through natural sinkholes called cenotes, which were sacred to the Maya and served as their principal water source. The longest known underwater cave systems in the world run beneath Quintana Roo.

History

The peninsula was a heartland of the Maya civilization, with cities such as Chichen Itza, Uxmal, Calakmul and Tikal. After the Classic-period collapse, Postclassic polities such as Mayapan flourished until the Spanish conquest in the 16th century, led in Yucatán by the Montejo family. Under colonial rule the region produced henequen (sisal) in the 19th and early 20th centuries, and the long Caste War of Yucatán (1847–1901) saw Maya rebels hold much of the east. Quintana Roo's Caribbean coast was developed for tourism from the 1970s, with Cancún built as a planned resort.

Economy and tourism

Tourism dominates the modern economy of the Caribbean coast — Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum and the island of Cozumel form the "Riviera Maya" — while offshore oil extraction in the Bay of Campeche is nationally significant. Agriculture includes citrus, cattle and traditional Maya milpa farming. In 2023–2024 Mexico opened the Tren Maya railway circuit around the peninsula.

Ecology

The peninsula's dry and moist tropical forests shelter jaguars, ocelots, howler monkeys and hundreds of bird species; the northern coast's estuaries host American flamingos, notably at Celestún and Ría Lagartos. The calamity of landfalling hurricanes shapes both ecology and settlement.
