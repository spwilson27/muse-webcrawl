Mental health law

Mental health law balances treatment, rights, and public safety

Mental health law is the area of law that governs the rights of people with mental disorders and the powers of the state and professionals to detain and treat them, in some circumstances without their consent. It includes legislation on involuntary commitment, capacity and guardianship, consent to treatment, confidentiality, and the insanity defence in criminal law.

Involuntary commitment

Most jurisdictions permit the compulsory detention of a person with a mental disorder when specified criteria are met, typically that the person poses a danger to themselves or others, or is gravely disabled and unable to care for themselves. Procedures usually involve certification by physicians, time limits on detention, and review by a tribunal or court. Landmark cases such as O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975) in the United States limited the confinement of non-dangerous individuals.

Capacity and rights

Mental health law intersects with capacity law, which determines when a person can make legally effective decisions about treatment, finances, and personal welfare, and when substitute decision-makers may act. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) has prompted debate and reform, emphasizing supported decision-making and the equal legal capacity of persons with disabilities, and pressing states to review systems of substituted decision-making and involuntary treatment.

Examples of legislation

Significant statutes include the Mental Health Act 1983 (as amended in 2007) in England and Wales, the Mental Capacity Act 2005, various state laws in the United States, the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 in India, and mental health acts in Australia, Canada, and other Commonwealth countries. Reforms commonly address review procedures, community treatment orders, and safeguards around electroconvulsive therapy and other treatments given without consent.
