Smoking ban

Smoking bans, or smoke-free laws, are public policies, including criminal laws and occupational safety and health regulations, that prohibit tobacco smoking in certain spaces, commonly in indoor work-places, and in other designated public places. Such policies are usually justified on the grounds that secondhand smoke imposes significant health risks on non-smokers and workers, and they are a central measure recommended by the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control.

History

Early restrictions on smoking in public arose for fire safety and moral reasons, but the modern smoke-free movement followed scientific evidence in the 1970s–1980s about the dangers of environmental tobacco smoke. Ireland became, in March 2004, the first country in the world to implement a comprehensive national ban on smoking in all enclosed workplaces, including bars and restaurants. New Zealand followed the same year, and Italy and Norway introduced similar laws soon after. Much of the European Union, Canada, Australia, and many US states and cities adopted workplace and hospitality bans during the 2000s and 2010s.

Scope

Bans vary in scope: some cover all enclosed public places and workplaces; others exempt private clubs, cigar bars, or specially ventilated smoking rooms. Increasingly, bans extend to outdoor settings such as parks, beaches, hospital grounds, school campuses, public transport stops and outdoor dining areas, and to cars carrying children. India's 2008 rules, Bhutan's ban on tobacco sales (in force 2005–2021), and Mexico's 2023 prohibition covering most public spaces are among the broadest measures adopted.

Effects

Studies consistently find that comprehensive smoke-free laws sharply reduce exposure to secondhand smoke, are followed by declines in hospital admissions for heart attacks and respiratory illness, and are associated with reduced smoking prevalence and increased quit attempts. Contrary to predictions from parts of the hospitality industry, most economic studies find no net negative effect on bar and restaurant revenues. Opponents raise arguments about personal freedom, property rights and the effect on small venues; enforcement and compliance are nevertheless generally high.
