Liberal arts colleges in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberal arts colleges in the United States are undergraduate institutions that emphasise a broad curriculum in the liberal arts — the humanities, natural sciences, social sciences and fine arts — rather than vocational or professional training. They are typically small, residential, privately funded, and characterised by small class sizes, close faculty–student contact and a predominantly full-time undergraduate student body.

Characteristics

Most liberal arts colleges enrol between a few hundred and about 3,000 students and award mainly bachelor's degrees, with distribution requirements or core curricula spanning disciplines. Well-known examples include Williams, Amherst and Swarthmore Colleges; the historically women's "Seven Sisters"; Oberlin; and the Claremont Colleges consortium. Faculty are generally expected to prioritise teaching while remaining research-active.

History and challenges

Many of the oldest American colleges were founded by religious denominations in the colonial and early republic periods. In the twentieth century the model distinguished American undergraduate education from the more specialised European pattern. Since the 2000s, smaller colleges have faced financial pressures from demographic decline in traditional college-age populations, discounting of tuition, and competition from larger universities, leading to a number of mergers and closures.
