Carillion

Carillion plc was a British multinational construction and facilities-management company headquartered in Wolverhampton. Formed in 1999 by a demerger from Tarmac, it grew by acquisition into one of the UK government's largest contractors, delivering hospitals, schools, prisons, rail projects and defence services. On 15 January 2018 Carillion went into compulsory liquidation with debts of around £7 billion — the largest corporate failure in Britain for a decade — leaving some 3,000 public-sector contracts, 43,000 employees worldwide and a large pension deficit in limbo.

Carillion was a major builder of hospitals, schools and rail infrastructure.

Growth

Carillion expanded from Tarmac's former construction arm into support services, buying Mowlem and Alfred McAlpine and winning large PFI and outsourcing contracts: hospital building and maintenance, school catering and cleaning, prison facilities, Network Rail works, and military housing. It worked on projects including the Tate Modern extension, the Library of Birmingham, the Royal Liverpool University Hospital and HS2 enabling works (in joint venture). Overseas, it operated in Canada and the Middle East, notably in Qatar — where its treatment of migrant workers was criticised in the run-up to the 2022 World Cup.

Collapse

In July 2017 Carillion issued a profit warning and an £845 million write-down on contracts, its share price collapsed, and chief executive Richard Howson resigned. Loss-making projects (including the Royal Liverpool and Midland Metropolitan hospitals and the Aberdeen bypass), a pension deficit of roughly £600 million and aggressive accounting — including reverse factoring ("supply-chain finance") that masked borrowing — left banks unwilling to lend further without government support. The government refused a bailout, and the company entered liquidation rather than administration because it had almost no assets to sell.

Aftermath

The Official Receiver kept public services running while contracts were transferred to other providers; thousands of jobs were saved though around 3,000 UK redundancies followed. Parliamentary inquiries by the Work and Pensions and BEIS committees condemned directors, auditors (KPMG), regulators and the company's "recklessness, hubris and greed", and criticised the government for continuing to award contracts after the profit warning. The affair prompted reforms to audit, insolvency rules on "phoenix" arrangements, and debate about the outsourcing of public services. In 2023 the former finance director was fined and banned; proceedings against other directors were largely dropped.
