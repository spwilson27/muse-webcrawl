Validity (statistics)

Accuracy versus precision as an analogy for validity and reliability

Validity is the extent to which a concept, conclusion or measurement is well-founded and likely corresponds accurately to the real world. The word "valid" is derived from the Latin validus, meaning strong. In measurement, validity concerns whether an instrument measures what it purports to measure; in research design, it concerns whether conclusions drawn from a study are justified.

Types in measurement

  • Content validity — the extent to which a measure represents all facets of a given construct
  • Criterion validity — the extent to which a measure predicts or agrees with an external criterion, including concurrent and predictive forms
  • Construct validity — the degree to which a measure reflects the theoretical construct it targets, supported by convergent and discriminant evidence
  • Face validity — the extent to which a test appears, on its surface, to measure what it claims

Validity in research design

Donald Campbell and collaborators distinguished internal validity — whether an observed effect is genuinely caused by the treatment rather than confounds — from external validity, the extent to which results generalise to other settings, people, and times. Statistical conclusion validity concerns whether inferences about covariation are sound.

Relation to reliability

A measure can be reliable (consistent) without being valid, but validity places an upper bound linked to reliability: a measure that does not consistently measure anything cannot measure the right thing. Both properties are central concerns of psychometrics.
