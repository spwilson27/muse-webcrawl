Discriminant validity

A multitrait-multimethod matrix

Discriminant validity (also called divergent validity) is the degree to which a test or measure does not correlate with measures of constructs from which it is supposed to differ. It is the counterpart of convergent validity, and together the two were introduced by Donald Campbell and Donald Fiske in their 1959 paper on the multitrait-multimethod matrix.

Assessment

In the multitrait-multimethod approach, several traits are each measured by several methods; discriminant validity is supported when correlations between measures of different traits are weaker than correlations between different methods measuring the same trait. Modern structural equation modelling uses criteria such as the Fornell–Larcker criterion, which compares the average variance extracted by a construct with its squared correlations with other constructs, and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT), with values above roughly 0.85 to 0.90 signalling a lack of discriminant validity.

Example

A new test of mathematical ability should correlate strongly with established mathematics tests (convergent validity) but only weakly with tests of verbal creativity or measures of height; a high correlation with an unrelated construct would suggest the test is measuring something other than its intended target.
