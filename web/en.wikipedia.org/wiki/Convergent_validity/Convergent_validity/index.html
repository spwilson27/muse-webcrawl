Convergent validity

FieldPsychometrics, social science methodology
Related conceptsDiscriminant validity, construct validity, multitrait-multimethod matrix
IntroducedCampbell and Fiske (1959)

Convergent validity is the degree to which two measures of constructs that theoretically should be related are in fact related. It is a subtype of construct validity used in psychometrics and social science research to assess whether a test or scale measures the underlying construct it purports to measure.

Definition and assessment

Convergent validity is demonstrated when scores from different measures of the same construct correlate strongly with one another. For example, a new depression inventory should correlate highly with established depression scales. In structural equation modelling, convergent validity is often evaluated using the average variance extracted (AVE), with a value of 0.5 or greater commonly taken as evidence that the construct explains at least half of the variance of its indicators; composite reliability is also examined.

History

The concept was formalised by Donald T. Campbell and Donald W. Fiske in their influential 1959 paper, which introduced the multitrait-multimethod matrix (MTMM). In the MTMM, convergent validity is shown by high correlations between measures of the same trait obtained by different methods (the "validity diagonal"), while discriminant validity requires that these correlations exceed correlations between different traits measured by the same method.

Relation to discriminant validity

Convergent validity is normally assessed alongside discriminant validity: measures should converge on the intended construct (converge) yet diverge from measures of unrelated constructs. Together, the two provide evidence for construct validity.
