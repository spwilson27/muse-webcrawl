Convergent validity

Convergent validity is a subtype of construct validity in psychometrics: it is the degree to which two measures that are theoretically supposed to assess the same construct produce similar results — that is, the measures "converge" on the same underlying trait or concept.

Assessment

Convergent validity is usually assessed by the correlation between scores on a test and scores on other, established measures of the same or a closely related construct. High correlations support convergent validity. Campbell and Fiske's multitrait–multimethod matrix (1959) formalized the comparison of convergent correlations with discriminant validity correlations, which compare measures of different constructs.

In structural equation modeling and factor analysis, convergent validity is often quantified using the average variance extracted (AVE), with values above 0.5 commonly taken as evidence that a latent factor explains a majority of the variance of its indicators.

Example

A new questionnaire for depression should correlate strongly with existing validated depression inventories and with clinical ratings of depression severity; such correlations would support its convergent validity, whereas equally strong correlations with, say, an intelligence test would undermine it.
