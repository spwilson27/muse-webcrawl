Foehn wind

TypeKatabatic / downslope wind
RegionOriginally the European Alps (Föhn)
EffectWarm, dry air on the lee side of a mountain range

A foehn or föhn (from German Föhn, originally from the Alps) is a type of dry, warm, down-slope wind that occurs in the lee (downwind side) of a mountain range. Foehn winds can raise temperatures by as much as 14 °C (25 °F) in just a matter of hours, and are credited with rapidly melting snow — hence names such as "snow eater" for the North American Chinook.

Mechanism

Several mechanisms contribute, the classic one being adiabatic compression: as air descends the lee slope it is compressed and warms at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (about 9.8 °C per kilometre), faster than it cooled while rising on the windward side, especially if moisture condensed and fell as precipitation during the ascent, removing latent heat from the air mass. Other mechanisms involve the drawing-down of warm air from aloft and turbulent mixing.

Local names

Similar winds have regional names worldwide, including the Chinook of the Rocky Mountains, the Santa Ana winds of Southern California, the Zonda of the Argentine Andes, the Bergwind of South Africa, and the Nor'wester of New Zealand's Canterbury Plains.

Effects

Foehn winds dry vegetation and raise wildfire risk, melt snowpack (sometimes triggering floods or avalanches), and are anecdotally linked in Alpine folklore to headaches and changes in mood, a phenomenon called Föhnkrankheit ("foehn sickness").
