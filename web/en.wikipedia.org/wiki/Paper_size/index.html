Paper size

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Paper size standards govern the size of sheets of paper used as writing material and in printing. The international standard is ISO 216, based on the German DIN 476 standard, which defines the A, B, and C series of sizes. It is used in most countries of the world. North America uses a separate, inch-based system whose best-known size is Letter (8.5 × 11 in).

ISO 216: A, B and C series

The ISO system is based on an aspect ratio of 1:√2, so that folding a sheet in half parallel to its shorter side produces the next smaller size with the same proportions. The A series begins with A0, defined as having an area of one square metre (841 × 1189 mm). Each successive size is half the area of the previous.

A4 is the most common size for letters, documents, and printers worldwide. The B series provides intermediate sizes, and the C series is used mainly for envelopes designed to hold A-series sheets.

North American sizes

The United States, Canada, and parts of Latin America use sizes standardized by ANSI/ASME Y14.1 and related standards, including Letter (8.5 × 11 in), Legal (8.5 × 14 in), Tabloid/Ledger (11 × 17 in), and Executive (7.25 × 10.5 in). These sizes do not share a uniform aspect ratio.

Other standards

Japan uses JIS P 0138, which includes a B series slightly different from ISO B. Traditional British Imperial sizes (such as Foolscap, Demy, and Royal) remain in limited use. Book and newspaper formats, photograph sizes, and index-card sizes follow their own conventions.
