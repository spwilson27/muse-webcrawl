Western riding

Western riding is a style of horseback riding that evolved from the ranching and warfare traditions that were brought to the Americas by the Spanish conquistadors, and from the working practices of cattle ranchers in the western United States and northern Mexico. It is distinguished by its distinctive saddle, tack, and riding techniques, in which horses are trained to respond to light rein aids, often neck reining with one hand.

Equipment

The Western saddle is large and heavy compared with the English saddle, with a prominent pommel horn used for dallying a rope when working cattle, a deep seat, and wide stirrups; it is designed to distribute weight over a larger area of the horse's back for long days of work. Riders traditionally wear a wide-brimmed cowboy hat, high-heeled cowboy boots, and often chaps. The bridle commonly uses a curb bit with long shanks, though younger horses may be started in a snaffle bit or a bosal hackamore.

Techniques and disciplines

Western horses are trained to be ridden with a loose rein and to change speed and direction in response to subtle shifts of the rider's weight, legs, and one-handed neck reining. Competitive disciplines derived from ranch work include reining, cutting, working cow horse, team penning, barrel racing, and roping events, while show-ring classes include Western pleasure, trail, and Western horsemanship. Trail riding and ranch work remain the everyday contexts of the style.

Comparison with English riding

Whereas English riding typically uses two hands and direct rein contact, Western riding emphasises self-carriage on a slack rein; gaits are also named differently — the jog corresponds roughly to the trot and the lope to a slow, relaxed canter.
