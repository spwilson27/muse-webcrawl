Lignin

Lignin
TypeComplex organic polymer (phenylpropanoid)
Building blocksp-Coumaryl, coniferyl and sinapyl alcohols (monolignols)
OccurrenceSecondary cell walls of vascular plants; 15–40% of wood by dry weight

Lignin is a class of complex organic polymers that form key structural materials in the support tissues of most plants. Lignins are particularly important in the formation of cell walls, especially in wood and bark, because they lend rigidity and do not rot easily. Chemically, lignins are polymers made by oxidative coupling of the monolignols — p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol and sinapyl alcohol, giving the H, G and S units respectively.

Lignin is the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth after cellulose; the total production by plants is estimated in the tens of billions of tonnes per year. It fills the spaces in the cell wall between cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin components, conducts water in xylem tracheids, and gives land plants the strength to grow tall.

Structure

Unlike cellulose, lignin has an irregular, cross-linked structure with no repeating unit, formed by enzyme-initiated radical polymerisation of monolignols. The proportion of H, G and S units differs between softwoods (mostly G), hardwoods (G and S) and grasses (all three). The dominant inter-unit linkage is the β-O-4 ether bond.

Biological function

Lignification waterproofs the cell wall, enabling long-distance water transport; stiffens stems; and forms a barrier against pathogens and herbivores. Its appearance in the fossil record coincides with the evolution of vascular plants, and fallen lignified wood that resisted decay in the Carboniferous is a major source of coal.

Economic significance

In papermaking, lignin is largely removed from wood pulp — by the kraft or sulfite processes — because it yellows paper; the removed lignin is mostly burned for energy, though it is also used to make vanillin, dispersants, adhesives and carbon fibre precursors. Lignin removal is also the key obstacle in producing cellulosic biofuels, driving research into low-lignin engineered plants and better deconstruction methods.

Degradation

Only white-rot fungi can fully mineralise lignin, using extracellular enzymes such as lignin peroxidases and laccases; this slow recycling returns the carbon locked in wood to the atmosphere as CO2.
