Wald test

The Wald test (named after Abraham Wald) is a parametric statistical test named after the statistician Abraham Wald. Whenever a relationship within or between data items can be expressed as a statistical model with parameters to be estimated from a sample, the Wald test can be used to test the true value of the parameter based on the sample estimate.

For example, suppose an economist, who works with a sample of data, wishes to test the hypothesis that the mean of a certain variable equals some hypothesised value. The Wald test compares the difference between the sample estimate and the hypothesised value, scaled by the estimated standard error of the estimate.

The test statistic

Under the null hypothesis, the Wald statistic approximately follows a chi-squared distribution when the sample size is large. In the univariate case, the Wald statistic is the square of the ratio of the difference between the estimate and the hypothesised value to the standard error of the estimate; its signed square root is often reported as a z-statistic.

The test is one of the three classical approaches to hypothesis testing, alongside the likelihood ratio test and the Lagrange multiplier (score) test. Under regularity conditions, the three tests are asymptotically equivalent, though they can differ in finite samples.

Advantages and limitations

An important advantage of the Wald test is that it only requires estimation of the unrestricted model; the likelihood ratio test, by contrast, requires estimation of both the restricted and unrestricted models. However, the Wald test is not invariant to reparameterisation: testing a nonlinear function of a parameter can give different results depending on how the hypothesis is written. It can also perform poorly in small samples or when the parameter lies near the boundary of the parameter space, a problem highlighted by the Hauck–Donner effect, in which the Wald statistic can decrease toward zero as the true effect becomes very large.
