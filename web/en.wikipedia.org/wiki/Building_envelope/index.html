Building envelope

The building envelope (also called the building enclosure) is the physical separator between the conditioned and unconditioned environment of a building, including everything that makes up its exterior shell: walls, roof, foundation, windows and doors. It resists the passage of air, water, heat, light and noise, and must also handle structural loads and be aesthetically appropriate.

Functions

The envelope performs three principal control functions, traditionally considered in this order of importance:

  • Water control — shedding rain and preventing water penetration through drainage planes, flashing and rainscreen cladding;
  • Air control — limiting uncontrolled air leakage, which carries heat and moisture; achieved with a continuous air barrier system;
  • Thermal control — providing insulation and limiting thermal bridging to reduce heat loss and gain.

Vapour control is also managed through the placement of vapour retarders appropriate to the climate. Poorly designed envelopes are a common source of building defects, including condensation, mould growth, water damage and excessive energy use.

Design considerations

Envelope design varies strongly with climate: cold climates emphasise insulation and airtightness (exemplified by the Passivhaus standard, which requires very low air leakage and high insulation), while hot-humid climates emphasise shading, ventilation and rain management. Windows and glazing are typically the thermally weakest elements of the envelope, and their performance is described by U-values and solar heat gain coefficients. Building envelope commissioning (BECx) has emerged as a quality-assurance process for verifying that the enclosure performs as designed.
