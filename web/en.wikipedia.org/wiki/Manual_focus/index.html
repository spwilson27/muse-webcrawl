Manual focus (MF) is the method of focusing a camera lens by hand, with the operator turning a focusing ring (or moving the camera) until the subject appears sharp in the viewfinder or on the focusing screen. Before the introduction of practical autofocus systems in the late 1970s and 1980s, manual focus was the only focusing method on interchangeable-lens cameras, and it remains standard on view cameras, cinema lenses, and many specialist lenses.

Focusing aids

Classic SLR focusing screens combine a matte ground-glass field with a split-image rangefinder spot and a microprism collar: the split image aligns, and the microprisms stop shimmering, when focus is exact. Rangefinder cameras use a coupled superimposed-image mechanism. Mirrorless digital cameras add electronic aids — magnified live view, and focus peaking, which highlights in-focus edges in a chosen colour.

Techniques

Photographers may "zone focus," pre-setting the aperture and focus distance so the depth of field covers the subject area — a mainstay of street photography — or use the distance and depth-of-field scales engraved on the lens barrel. In macro and astrophotography, focusing rails and fine helicoids allow micrometre adjustments, and astrophotographers often prefer manual focus because autofocus hunts in darkness.

Advantages and disadvantages

Manual focus gives the photographer precise control of the focus point (useful in low light, through glass or foliage, and in video, where AF hunting is distracting) and works silently with any adapted lens. Its disadvantages are speed — fast-moving subjects are hard to track — and its dependence on the user's eyesight and viewfinder quality. Most autofocus lenses offer a manual override mode (MF/MA) combining both approaches.
