The Essex class is a retired class of aircraft carriers of the United States Navy. The 20th century's most numerous class of capital ship, the class consisted of 24 vessels, which came in "short-hull" and "long-hull" versions. Thirty-two ships were originally ordered; however as World War II wound down, six were canceled before construction, and two were canceled during construction. No Essex-class ships were lost to enemy action, despite several sustaining severe damage.

Service history

The Essex-class carriers formed the core of the United States Navy's combat strength during World War II from mid-1943 onward, and, together with the three Midway-class carriers added just after the war, they continued to provide the core of U.S. naval power through the 1960s and 1970s. Ships of the class participated in the Korean War and the Vietnam War, and the class also supported the space programme: Lake Champlain recovered Alan Shepard's capsule in 1961, and Hornet recovered Apollo 11 in 1969.

Modernisation

Most ships of the class were extensively modernised under the SCB-27 and SCB-125 programmes, receiving angled flight decks, enclosed hurricane bows, steam catapults and mirror landing systems so they could operate jet aircraft. A number were reclassified as antisubmarine carriers (CVS) or amphibious assault ships (LPH).

Surviving ships

Four Essex-class carriers survive as museum ships: Yorktown at Patriot's Point, South Carolina; Intrepid in New York City; Hornet in Alameda, California; and Lexington in Corpus Christi, Texas. The last ship of the class in service, Lexington, was decommissioned in 1991.
