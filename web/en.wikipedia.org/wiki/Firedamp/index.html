Firedamp

Main componentMethane (CH4)
Found inCoal mines
Explosive rangeRoughly 5–15% in air
Related termsBlackdamp, afterdamp, whitedamp, stinkdamp

Firedamp is a flammable gas found in coal mines, consisting principally of methane (CH4) mixed with other gases. The name comes from the German word Dampf, meaning vapour. Firedamp is released from coal seams as they are worked, and because methane is lighter than air it can accumulate in pockets near the roofs of mine workings.

Explosions

Methane forms an explosive mixture with air at concentrations of roughly 5 to 15 percent. Ignition by a naked flame, a spark, or hot surfaces can cause a violent explosion, which may in turn raise coal dust into the air and trigger a far more destructive secondary coal-dust explosion. Firedamp explosions were responsible for many of the worst mining disasters of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including the Senghenydd disaster of 1913 in Wales.

Detection

Miners traditionally used the flame safety lamp, developed in rival forms by Humphry Davy and George Stephenson in 1815, to detect firedamp: the flame inside the lamp elongates and develops a pale blue "cap" in the presence of methane, while the wire gauze of the lamp prevents the flame from igniting the surrounding gas. Canaries were also carried underground as sensitive indicators of toxic gases. Modern mines use electronic methanometers and continuous monitoring systems, together with powerful ventilation to dilute the gas below dangerous concentrations.

Other mine gases

Miners named several related hazards: blackdamp (a suffocating mixture of carbon dioxide and nitrogen), afterdamp (the toxic mixture of gases left after an explosion, rich in carbon monoxide), whitedamp (carbon monoxide), and stinkdamp (hydrogen sulfide).
