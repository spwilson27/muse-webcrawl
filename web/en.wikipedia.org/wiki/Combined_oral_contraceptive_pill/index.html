The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP), often referred to as the birth control pill or colloquially as "the pill", is a type of birth control that is designed to be taken orally by women. The pill contains both estrogen and a progestin (a synthetic form of the hormone progesterone). When taken correctly, it alters the menstrual cycle to eliminate ovulation and prevent pregnancy. It is one of the most widely used contraceptive methods worldwide, used by more than 150 million women.

Mechanism of action

The hormones suppress the release of gonadotropins (FSH and LH), preventing development and release of an egg; the progestin also thickens cervical mucus, impeding sperm passage, and thins the endometrium. Pills are taken daily, conventionally for 21 days followed by a 7-day hormone-free interval during which a withdrawal bleed occurs, though extended and continuous regimens are also used.

History

The pill grew out of research by Gregory Pincus, John Rock and others, funded by Margaret Sanger and Katharine McCormick. Enovid, containing mestranol and norethynodrel, was approved by the US FDA for menstrual disorders in 1957 and for contraception in 1960. Its availability is widely credited with major social effects, separating contraception from the sexual act for the first time and expanding women's educational and professional opportunities. Early high-dose formulations were replaced by lower-dose pills with better safety.

Benefits and risks

Beyond contraception, COCPs are used to treat acne, dysmenorrhea, endometriosis symptoms and polycystic ovary syndrome, and long-term use reduces the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancers. The principal serious risk is venous thromboembolism; risk varies with the progestin type and is increased by smoking and age, and the estrogen component slightly increases risks of stroke and myocardial infarction in susceptible users. Common side effects include nausea, breast tenderness and breakthrough bleeding.
