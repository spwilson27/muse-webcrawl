Yamagata Aritomo

Prince Yamagata Aritomo (山縣 有朋; 14 June 1838 – 1 February 1922) was a Japanese field marshal and statesman, twice Prime Minister of Japan (1889–1891 and 1898–1900), who is regarded as the founder of the modern Imperial Japanese Army and a dominant genrō (elder statesman) of the Meiji and Taishō eras. He directed Japan's military modernisation on Prussian lines, created the conscription system and the Imperial General Staff, and steered much of Japan's policy toward Korea and continental expansion.

Early career and the army

Yamagata was born into a low-ranking samurai family of Chōshū Domain and fought in the Boshin War in the Kiheitai militia of the Chōshū reformers. After the Meiji Restoration he became a leader of the new military; in 1872–1873 he toured Europe to study military organisation, favouring the Prussian general staff model. As army minister he imposed universal conscription (1873) despite peasant resistance, suppressed the Satsuma Rebellion (1877), and in 1878 founded the Army General Staff, establishing the institutional separation of command from civilian government — the Army's right of direct access to the emperor (gunji no dokuri) that later enabled military insubordination.

Statesman

As Home Minister (1883–1890) he created the modern prefectural system and the independent civil service, and he drafted the Meiji Constitution's election laws. As the third Prime Minister (1889–1891), he oversaw the opening of the Imperial Diet; in 1900 he promulgated the requirement that army and navy ministers be active-duty generals and admirals — the "military ministers must be on active duty" rule that gave the services a veto over cabinets. He advocated the "sovereign line of interest" doctrine toward Korea and China, commanded troops in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), and was made field marshal during the Russo-Japanese War. As the last surviving genrō he remained influential until his death, blocking liberal reforms and backing the Siberian intervention of 1918.

Legacy

Yamagata is both celebrated as an architect of modern Japan and criticised as the originator of the army's autonomy from civilian control, a structural flaw many historians trace to Japan's militarism of the 1930s.
