Interval (music)

Interval
DefinitionDifference in pitch between two sounds
TypesMelodic / harmonic; simple / compound; consonant / dissonant
MeasurementSemitones, cents, frequency ratio

In music theory, an interval is the difference in pitch between two sounds. An interval may be described as harmonic if the two tones sound simultaneously, or melodic if they sound in succession; melodic intervals may be ascending or descending. Intervals are the building blocks of scales, chords and melody, and are measured by their size (how many letter names they span) and their quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented or diminished).

Naming

The number of an interval counts the staff positions (letter names) it includes: C to G is a fifth (C-D-E-F-G). The quality is determined by the number of semitones. Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves take the quality perfect; seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths are major or minor. Widening a perfect or major interval by a chromatic semitone makes it augmented; narrowing a perfect or minor interval makes it diminished.

Consonance, compound intervals and inversion

Intervals larger than an octave are compound (a ninth is a compound second). Inverting an interval — moving the lower note up an octave — pairs major with minor, augmented with diminished, and numbers summing to nine (a third inverts to a sixth). Perfect intervals and the octave are traditionally classified as consonances, as are major and minor thirds and sixths; seconds, sevenths and the tritone are dissonances, with the perfect fourth treated contextually. Interval size can be measured fine-grained in cents (1/100 of an equal-tempered semitone) and in just intonation is expressed as frequency ratios: octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, major third 5:4.
