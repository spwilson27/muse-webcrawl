Daisetsu Teitarō Suzuki (鈴木 大拙 貞太郎, 18 October 1870 – 12 July 1966), commonly known as D. T. Suzuki, was a Japanese scholar and author who was instrumental in introducing Zen Buddhism to the West. His books and lectures made Zen a major influence on Western spirituality, art and psychology in the mid-20th century.

Life

Suzuki was born in Kanazawa into a samurai family that had fallen on hard times. He studied at Tokyo Imperial University and practised Zen at Engaku-ji in Kamakura under the master Soyen Shaku, experiencing a breakthrough in his koan study. In 1897 he travelled to the United States to work with the philosopher and publisher Paul Carus in Illinois, translating Buddhist texts into English; he remained abroad for over a decade, also travelling in Europe.

Career

Returning to Japan, Suzuki taught at Ōtani University in Kyoto and co-founded the Eastern Buddhist Society and its journal The Eastern Buddhist. His three series of Essays in Zen Buddhism (1927–1934) and An Introduction to Zen Buddhism (1934, with a foreword by Carl Jung in later editions) presented Zen to English readers. After the Second World War he lectured widely in America, including at Columbia University, influencing figures such as Erich Fromm, Thomas Merton, John Cage and the Beat writers, notably Jack Kerouac and Alan Watts's popular audience.

Thought and legacy

Suzuki emphasised the experiential, non-dual character of Zen enlightenment (satori) and the limits of logical, conceptual thought. Critics have noted that his presentation reflected his Rinzai training and a modernist, somewhat romanticised reading of Zen shaped by his time in the West, but his historical role in spreading Buddhism outside Asia is widely acknowledged.
