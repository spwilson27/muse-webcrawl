Legality of cannabis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legality of cannabis
Cannabis law varies widely around the world

The legality of cannabis for medical and recreational use varies by country and, in federal systems, by subnational jurisdiction, in terms of possession, distribution, cultivation and medical use. Cannabis has been restricted as a drug for most of the 20th century and remains controlled under the international drug conventions, but since the 2010s a growing number of countries and US states have legalised or decriminalised it.

History

Criminalisation spread internationally in the early 20th century: cannabis was added to the international opium conventions in 1925, banned federally in the United States by the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, and placed in the strictest schedules of the UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961). The Netherlands adopted its policy of tolerance (gedoogbeleid) in 1976, permitting retail sale in licensed "coffeeshops" while production remained illegal. Portugal decriminalised possession of all drugs for personal use in 2001.

Recreational legalisation

Uruguay became the first country to fully legalise recreational cannabis in 2013. In the United States, Colorado and Washington legalised by ballot initiative in 2012, and by the mid-2020s around half of US states permitted recreational use even while cannabis remained a federal controlled substance (with a proposed federal rescheduling to Schedule III announced in 2024). Canada legalised nationally under the Cannabis Act in October 2018. Malta (2021), Luxembourg (2023) and Germany (2024, via cannabis social clubs and home cultivation) were the first European Union states to legalise possession, while South Africa and Thailand liberalised in part (Thailand later moved to re-restrict non-medical use). Most other countries continue to prohibit recreational use, with penalties ranging from fines and administrative sanctions after decriminalisation to imprisonment — and in some states, capital punishment for trafficking.

Medical cannabis

Medical use is authorised in several dozen countries, including much of Europe, Australia, Israel, Canada and most US states. Products range from herbal cannabis to standardised extracts (such as nabiximols) and synthetic cannabinoids, prescribed for indications such as chemotherapy-induced nausea, chronic pain, spasticity in multiple sclerosis and certain severe childhood epilepsies (cannabidiol/Epidiolex).

Decriminalisation and enforcement

Decriminalisation — removing criminal penalties for small-scale possession while retaining civil sanctions — has been adopted by jurisdictions including Portugal, Spain (private use), the Czech Republic and numerous US cities and states before full legalisation. Reform debates centre on public health, racial and social disparities in enforcement, organised crime, tax revenue and road safety.
