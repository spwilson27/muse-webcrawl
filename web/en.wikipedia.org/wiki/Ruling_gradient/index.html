Ruling gradient

FieldRailway engineering
Also calledRuling grade
DefinitionSteepest sustained gradient on a line or section

The ruling gradient (or ruling grade) is the steepest sustained gradient on a railway line, or on a defined section of it. It is the gradient that determines the maximum load a locomotive of a given power can haul over that section, and therefore governs the line's capacity and economics.

Significance

Train resistance rises sharply with gradient: a locomotive that can pull a heavy freight train on the level may manage only a fraction of that load on a steep bank. Railway engineers therefore expend great effort — through tunnels, cuttings, viaducts and spirals — to ease the ruling gradient, balancing construction cost against operating cost. Famous examples include the 1 in 37 (2.7%) Lickey Incline in England, long worked with banking engines, and the 1 in 40 grades of many mountain crossings.

Related concepts

The ruling gradient differs from a short, steep "pusher grade" or momentum grade, where a train's kinetic energy helps it surmount a brief rise. Curve resistance and altitude also enter the practical calculation of permissible loads.
