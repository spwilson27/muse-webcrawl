Hodgkin–Huxley model

[Image: Voltage-clamp records and the modelled action potential of the squid giant axon]

Overview

The Hodgkin–Huxley model is a mathematical model that describes how action potentials in neurons are initiated and propagated. Developed by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley from experiments on the giant axon of the Atlantic squid (Doryteuthis pealeii) and published in 1952 in the Journal of Physiology, it treats the cell membrane as an electrical circuit: a capacitor in parallel with voltage- and time-dependent sodium and potassium conductances and a leak conductance.

Equations and legacy

Four coupled nonlinear differential equations govern membrane voltage and the gating variables m, h and n for sodium activation, sodium inactivation and potassium activation. The model quantitatively reproduced the shape and conduction velocity of the action potential and predicted ion-channel gating twelve years before channels were directly observed — later confirmed by patch-clamp work and by the discovery of gating currents. It remains the founding model of computational neuroscience, simplified in later work (FitzHugh–Nagumo, Morris–Lecar) and honoured by the 1963 Nobel Prize.
