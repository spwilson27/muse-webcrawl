Nocturnal enuresis

Nocturnal enuresis, commonly known as bedwetting or nighttime incontinence, is involuntary urination during sleep in a person old enough to reasonably be expected to stay dry through the night. It is the most common urinary complaint among children referred to paediatric care.

Classification

Primary nocturnal enuresis describes a child who has never achieved a sustained period of nighttime dryness, while secondary nocturnal enuresis describes wetting that begins after at least six months of dryness. Enuresis is also divided into monosymptomatic (no daytime lower urinary tract symptoms) and non-monosymptomatic forms.

Epidemiology

Bedwetting is common in early childhood: roughly 15–20% of five-year-olds wet the bed at least occasionally, and the rate falls with age, to about 5% at age ten and 1–2% among teenagers and adults. It is more common in boys than in girls. Each year, about 15% of affected children stop wetting spontaneously.

Causes

The condition is generally attributed to a combination of three factors: excessive urine production at night (nocturnal polyuria, sometimes linked to low nighttime secretion of antidiuretic hormone), reduced functional bladder capacity or detrusor overactivity, and an inability to wake in response to a full bladder. Genetics plays a strong role — a child with two parents who wet the bed has a much higher likelihood of bedwetting. Secondary enuresis may follow urinary tract infection, constipation, diabetes, or psychological stress.

Treatment

Management begins with reassurance, regular daytime voiding, treating constipation, and limiting evening fluids. The most effective long-term treatment for monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis is the enuresis alarm, which conditions the child to wake to bladder signals. The synthetic hormone desmopressin reduces nighttime urine production and is widely used for short-term dryness, for example during sleepovers or camps. Punishment is counterproductive and discouraged.
