Hulegu Khan, also known as Hülegü or Hulagu (c. 1215 – 8 February 1265), was a Mongol ruler who conquered much of Western Asia. Son of Tolui, grandson of Genghis Khan, and brother of both Möngke Khan and Kublai Khan, Hulegu became the first Ilkhan — ruler of the Ilkhanate in Persia — after his conquests of the Abbasid Caliphate and Ayyubid Syria.

Conquests

Commissioned by his brother, the Great Khan Möngke, Hulegu marched west in 1253 with one of the largest Mongol armies ever assembled, including Chinese siege engineers. His forces first destroyed the Nizari Ismaili state in Persia — the "Assassins" — taking the fortress of Alamut in 1256. He then advanced on Baghdad, the seat of the Abbasid caliphs. After a short siege the city fell in February 1258 and was sacked with enormous loss of life; the last Abbasid caliph of Baghdad, al-Musta'sim, was executed, ending the Baghdad caliphate.

In 1260 Hulegu took Aleppo and Damascus, but after Möngke's death took him back east with much of his army, his remaining force under Kitbuqa was defeated by the Mamluks of Egypt at the Battle of Ain Jalut (September 1260) — the permanent western limit of Mongol expansion in the Levant.

The Ilkhanate

Hulegu ruled Persia from his capitals at Maragheh and Tabriz. He was notably favourable to Buddhism and to Nestorian Christianity — his chief wife, Doquz Khatun, was a Christian — while pursuing war against the Muslim Mamluks and, from 1262, against his cousin Berke of the Golden Horde in the first civil war between Mongol khanates. He patronised learning: the Persian astronomer Nasir al-Din al-Tusi built the great observatory at Maragheh under his sponsorship, and the historian Rashid al-Din served his successors.

Death and legacy

Hulegu died at Maragheh in February 1265 and was buried on Shahi Island in Lake Urmia. He was succeeded by his son Abaqa. His conquests permanently reshaped the Middle East: Persia passed under the Ilkhanate for some seventy years, and the sack of Baghdad became one of the most infamous catastrophes in Islamic historiography, though recent scholarship debates the extent of long-term damage to irrigation and population.
