Inulin

TypeFructan (polysaccharide)
CompositionChains of fructose units, usually terminated by a glucose unit
SourcesChicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, dahlia tubers, garlic, onion, agave
UsesDietary fiber, prebiotic, food ingredient, renal function testing (inulin clearance)

Inulins are a group of naturally occurring polysaccharides produced by many types of plants, industrially most often extracted from chicory. They belong to a class of dietary fibers known as fructans. Inulin is used by some plants as a means of storing energy and is typically found in roots or rhizomes. Most plants that synthesize and store inulin do not store other forms of carbohydrate such as starch.

Chemistry and properties

Inulin is composed of chains of fructose molecules linked β(2→1), usually with a terminal glucose unit, giving molecules of the form GFn or Fm with degrees of polymerization ranging from about 2 to 60. Because human digestive enzymes cannot break the β(2→1) bonds, inulin passes largely undigested to the colon, where it is fermented by gut bacteria — the basis of its classification as a soluble dietary fiber and prebiotic. Inulin is mildly sweet (roughly a tenth the sweetness of sugar, varying with chain length), forms a creamy gel in water, and imparts about 1–1.5 kcal per gram.

Uses

Food manufacturers use inulin to replace fat or sugar, increase fiber content, and improve texture in products such as yogurts, baked goods, and ice cream. As a fructooligosaccharide-rich prebiotic it is marketed for digestive health, though excessive intake can cause flatulence and bloating, and it counts as a FODMAP, restricted in diets for irritable bowel syndrome. In medicine, inulin clearance is the gold-standard measurement of the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), because inulin is freely filtered by the kidney and neither secreted nor reabsorbed; however, the test's complexity has largely replaced it with creatinine- and cystatin C–based estimates in routine practice.

Natural sources

Rich sources include chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, dahlia tubers, yacón, garlic, onions, leeks, bananas, and agave. Historically, inulin-rich plants formed a significant part of some Indigenous diets in North America, notably camas bulbs and Jerusalem artichokes.
