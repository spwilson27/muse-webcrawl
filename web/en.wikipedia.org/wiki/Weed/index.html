Weed

DefinitionA plant considered undesirable in a particular human-controlled setting
ExamplesDandelion, crabgrass, bindweed, kudzu, Japanese knotweed
ControlCultivation, mulching, mowing, herbicides, biological control
Related conceptsInvasive species; noxious weed; integrated pest management

A weed is a plant considered undesirable in a particular situation, growing where it conflicts with human preferences or activities — "a plant in the wrong place". The term has no botanical significance: a plant that is a weed in one context (a lawn, crop field or garden) may be valued in another, and many weeds are also wildflowers, herbs or pioneer species of ecological importance. Common characteristics of weeds include abundant seed production, rapid growth, persistent seed banks, and effective dispersal.

Types and impacts

Agricultural weeds compete with crops for light, water and nutrients and are a principal cause of yield loss worldwide; notable examples include black-grass in European cereals, Palmer amaranth in North American row crops and striga (witchweed) parasitising African cereals. Environmental weeds invade natural ecosystems — kudzu in the southeastern United States and Japanese knotweed and rhododendron in Britain are classic examples — and some of the world's worst "noxious weeds" are subject to statutory control. Other categories include aquatic weeds such as water hyacinth, which chokes waterways, and lawn and pavement weeds such as dandelion and crabgrass.

Control

Weed management strategies include cultural methods (crop rotation, stale seedbeds, competitive planting, mulching), mechanical methods (hoeing, tillage, mowing, flame weeding), biological control using introduced insects or pathogens, and chemical control with herbicides. Dependence on herbicides has driven the evolution of herbicide-resistant weeds — notably glyphosate-resistant species — a major agronomic problem. Integrated weed management combines methods to reduce reliance on any single one.

Benefits

Many weeds provide ecosystem services: they protect bare soil from erosion, feed pollinators and birds, accumulate nutrients, and serve as indicators of soil conditions. Some are edible or medicinal, including dandelion, nettle and purslane. The ecological view associated with writers such as Emerson — who called a weed "a plant whose virtues have not yet been discovered" — underlies modern re-evaluations of "messy" urban greenery and rewilding.
