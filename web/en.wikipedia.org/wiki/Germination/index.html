Germination

Germination is the process by which an organism grows from a seed or spore. The term is applied to the sprouting of a seedling from a seed of an angiosperm or gymnosperm, the growth of a sporeling from a spore, such as the spores of fungi, ferns, and bacteria, and the growth of the pollen tube from the pollen grain of a seed plant.

Seed germination

Germination of a seed typically begins with the uptake of water (imbibition), which swells and ruptures the seed coat, activates enzymes and restarts metabolic activity. Food reserves stored in the endosperm or in the cotyledons are mobilised — in cereals, the hormone gibberellin signals the aleurone layer to produce amylases that digest starch. The embryonic root (radicle) usually emerges first, anchoring the seed and absorbing water, followed by the shoot (plumule).

Types

In epigeal germination (e.g. beans, castor, sunflower), the hypocotyl elongates and lifts the cotyledons above the soil, where they may become photosynthetic. In hypogeal germination (e.g. peas, maize, coconut), the epicotyl elongates and the cotyledons remain below ground in the seed.

Requirements and dormancy

Germination requires water, oxygen and a suitable temperature; some seeds also require light or darkness, or cold stratification. Many seeds pass through a period of dormancy imposed by the seed coat or by chemical inhibitors such as abscisic acid, which prevents sprouting under unfavourable conditions. Fire cues such as smoke karrikins, passage through an animal gut, or scarification of the coat may be needed to break dormancy in some species.

Rate and testing

Agriculturists measure germination as the percentage of seeds in a lot that produce normal seedlings under defined conditions. Germination rate and uniformity are critical to crop establishment; seed companies routinely test lots and print germination percentages on seed packets.
