Etchplanation

An etchplanation is a landform, a plain formed and shaped by deep chemical weathering — etching — of bedrock, followed by stripping of the weathered material (regolith). The concept is associated especially with the geomorphologist E. J. Wayland and later work on tropical and subtropical landscapes, notably in Africa, Australia and South America.

FieldGeomorphology
Key processDeep weathering then stripping
RelatedPeneplain, pediplain, inselberg, bornhardt

Process

Under warm, humid conditions water penetrates joints and fractures, rotting rock to depths of tens of metres and creating an irregular weathering front. If climate changes, uplift occurs, or base level falls, loose saprolite is removed by erosion, exposing the etched bedrock surface as a plain studded with resistant residuals such as inselbergs and bornhardts. Double planation — repeated cycles of etching and stripping — can lower landscapes over very long timescales.

Debate

Etchplanation was proposed as an alternative or complement to W. M. Davis's peneplanation and Lester King's pediplanation models, emphasizing subsurface chemical processes in the tropics.
