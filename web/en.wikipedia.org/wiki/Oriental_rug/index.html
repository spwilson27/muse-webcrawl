Oriental rug

An Oriental rug is a heavy textile made for a wide variety of utilitarian and symbolic purposes and produced in "Oriental countries" — the region often called the "Rug Belt", stretching from Morocco across North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia to northern India and China. Oriental rugs are traditionally hand-knotted or hand-woven from wool, cotton or silk, and are distinguished from machine-made carpets by their construction, materials and regional designs. The term largely describes rugs exported to the West from the 19th century onwards.

History

The oldest surviving pile carpet, the Pazyryk carpet (c. 5th century BC), was preserved in a frozen Scythian burial mound in Siberia and already shows a sophisticated knotted-pile technique. Carpet weaving flourished in Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus through the Islamic era; "classical" carpets of the Safavid period (1501–1736) — including the Ardabil Carpet of 1539–40, now in the Victoria and Albert Museum — represent the art's most famous peak. Large quantities were exported to Europe from the Ottoman Empire from the 16th century, appearing in Renaissance paintings by artists such as Hans Holbein the Younger, whose names became attached to pattern types ("Holbein" and "Lotto" carpets).

Construction

Hand-knotted rugs are built on a loom: vertical warp threads form the foundation, horizontal wefts hold the rows of knots, and the pile is made by tying short lengths of yarn around pairs of warps. The two principal knots are the symmetrical ("Turkish" or Ghiordes) knot and the asymmetrical ("Persian" or Senneh) knot. Knot density, pile material, dyes and the precision of the weave largely determine quality and value. Flat-woven rugs without pile are known as kilims.

Regional types

Major categories include Persian carpets (named for cities and regions such as Tabriz, Kashan, Isfahan, Heriz and Qom), Turkish or Anatolian rugs, Caucasian rugs, Turkmen rugs, and Chinese and Indian carpets. Designs combine geometric or floral motifs, medallions, prayer arches (mihrab), stylised animals and tribal emblems (guls), whose exact meanings are often debated.

Materials and dyes

Wool — especially from highland sheep — is the most common pile material, with cotton typical for the foundation and silk used for the finest pieces. Traditional dyes came from plants and insects such as madder (red), indigo (blue) and weld (yellow), as well as cochineal and iron mordants; synthetic dyes spread from the late 19th century.
