The Ranger program was a series of unmanned space missions by the United States in the 1960s whose purpose was to obtain the first close-up images of the surface of the Moon. The Ranger spacecraft were designed to take images of the lunar surface and transmit them to Earth until the spacecraft were destroyed upon impact.

History

The program was managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, California. It was divided into three "blocks" of spacecraft with progressively greater capability. The first six missions (1961–1964) all failed in some way — launch vehicle malfunctions, spacecraft failures, or misses of the Moon. These early failures were a public embarrassment but generated crucial engineering reforms at JPL.

The final three missions succeeded: Ranger 7 (July 1964) returned more than 4,300 photographs of Mare Nubium before impact; Ranger 8 (February 1965) photographed the Sea of Tranquility; and Ranger 9 (March 1965) sent back live television images of the crater Alphonsus as it descended. The photographs revealed the Moon's surface at resolutions up to a thousand times better than Earth-based telescopes.

Significance

Ranger's images proved that the lunar maria were smooth enough for crewed landings and provided essential data for selecting Apollo landing sites, complementing the Lunar Orbiter and Surveyor programs. The Ranger spacecraft design also established the template for later JPL planetary missions such as Mariner.

Missions

  • Rangers 1–2 (1961): test flights, stranded in low Earth orbit.
  • Rangers 3–5 (1962): first lunar attempts, all failed.
  • Ranger 6 (1964): impacted the Moon but cameras failed.
  • Ranger 7 (1964): first fully successful mission.
  • Ranger 8 (1965): Sea of Tranquility photography.
  • Ranger 9 (1965): live televised impact approach to Alphonsus.
