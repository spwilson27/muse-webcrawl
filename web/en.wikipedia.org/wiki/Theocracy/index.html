Theocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Theocracy is a form of government in which one or more deities are recognised as the supreme ruling authority, whose laws are interpreted and administered by religious authorities — priests or clerics ruling in the deity's name. The term derives from Greek theokratia, coined by Josephus for the constitution of ancient Israel.

Examples and varieties

Clear modern examples are few: Iran's system since 1979 combines elected institutions with clerical supremacy (the Supreme Leader and Guardian Council vetting candidates and legislation against Islamic criteria); Vatican City is an elective absolute monarchy under the Pope as head of the Catholic Church; and the Taliban's Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan grounds rule in its interpretation of sharia. Historical theocracies include pharaonic Egypt, Tibet under the Dalai Lamas, Calvin's Geneva, the Papal States and Puritan Massachusetts Bay. Many states fall short of theocracy while maintaining established churches or religious law in personal matters — a spectrum scholars map between full theocracy and strict secularism.
