New Sculpture

The New Sculpture was a movement in late-19th-century British sculpture (roughly 1879–1914) that reacted against the dry classicism and routine idealism of mid-Victorian monumental statuary. Its sculptors sought greater naturalism, movement, and surface vitality, often working in bronze, embracing literary and symbolist themes, and modelling the figure with a painterly sensitivity influenced by French sculpture — especially the work of Jules Dalou, who taught in London in the 1870s — and by the Arts and Crafts movement's respect for craftsmanship and materials.

Characteristics

  • Naturalistic modelling — figures observed from life, with animated surfaces that catch light, rather than smooth idealised forms.
  • Dynamic poses and small-scale bronzes — statuettes for domestic display alongside public monuments.
  • Literary and allegorical subjects — drawn from poetry, myth, and symbolism rather than only portraiture and state commemoration.
  • Truth to materials and craft — renewed interest in bronze casting (including lost-wax casting) and in the sculptor's own hand, reflecting Arts and Crafts ideals.

Artists

The leading figure was Frederic Leighton, whose Athlete Wrestling with a Python (1877) is often taken as the movement's starting point. Other key sculptors include Alfred Gilbert (the Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain — "Eros" — in Piccadilly Circus, 1893), Hamo Thornycroft, Edward Onslow Ford, Thomas Brock, George Frampton, and William Goscombe John. The critic Edmund Gosse championed the movement in a series of articles in The Art Journal in 1894, coining and popularising the label "the New Sculpture".

Context and legacy

The movement coincided with the foundation of the Art Workers' Guild (1884) and renewed national confidence in British art before the First World War. New Sculptors produced many of the era's defining public works, from the Albert Memorial's later additions to memorials across the British Empire. After 1918 taste shifted toward the direct carving and modernism of Jacob Epstein, Eric Gill, and Henry Moore, and the New Sculpture was long dismissed as merely decorative; scholarship since the 1970s has reassessed it as a genuinely innovative reinvigoration of the British sculptural tradition.
