Bias–variance tradeoff

Bias–variance tradeoff
Field: statistics, machine learning
Components: bias, variance, irreducible error
Related concepts: overfitting, underfitting, regularization, model selection

In statistics and machine learning, the bias–variance tradeoff describes the balance between two sources of prediction error. A model with high bias makes strong simplifying assumptions and tends to underfit the data, while a model with high variance is overly sensitive to the training sample and tends to overfit. The expected squared error of a prediction can be decomposed into the square of the bias, the variance, and an irreducible noise term.

Motivation

Simple models such as linear regression have high bias but low variance: their predictions are stable but may systematically miss nonlinear structure. Complex models such as deep decision trees or high-degree polynomials have low bias but high variance: they can fit the training data closely yet generalize poorly to new data. Good model selection seeks the complexity that minimizes the sum of squared bias and variance.

Methods for managing the tradeoff

Techniques include cross-validation for estimating out-of-sample error, regularization methods such as ridge and lasso regression that deliberately introduce bias to reduce variance, ensemble methods such as bagging and random forests that average many high-variance models, and increasing the size of the training set, which typically reduces variance. The tradeoff is a central explanation of why more flexible models are not automatically better.
