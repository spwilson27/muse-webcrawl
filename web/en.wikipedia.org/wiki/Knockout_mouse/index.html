Knockout mouse

A knockout mouse has a specific gene inactivated to reveal its function.

A knockout mouse, or knock-out mouse, is a genetically modified mouse (Mus musculus) in which researchers have inactivated, or "knocked out", an existing gene by replacing it or disrupting it with an artificial piece of DNA. They are important animal models for studying the role of genes which have been sequenced but whose functions have not been determined. By causing a specific gene to be inactive in the mouse, and observing any differences from normal behaviour or physiology, researchers can infer its probable function. Large collaborative programmes, such as the International Knockout Mouse Consortium and the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium, aim to knock out every protein-coding gene in the mouse genome.

Method

Traditionally, a targeting construct containing the disrupted gene is introduced into embryonic stem (ES) cells, where homologous recombination replaces the normal gene in a small fraction of cells. Selected ES cells are injected into blastocysts, producing chimeric mice; breeding chimeras whose germline carries the modification yields heterozygous and then homozygous knockout offspring. Mario Capecchi, Martin Evans, and Oliver Smithies received the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this gene-targeting technology.

Conditional knockouts

Knocking out a gene that is essential in development can kill the embryo, hiding the gene's adult functions. Conditional knockouts circumvent this: using the Cre–loxP system, the gene is flanked by loxP sites ("floxed") and inactivated only in chosen tissues or at chosen times when Cre recombinase is expressed. Inducible systems (e.g. CreER, activated by tamoxifen) add temporal control.

Uses and limitations

Knockout mice model human diseases involving single genes — cancers, obesity, diabetes, cystic fibrosis, and neurological disorders among them — and are used to test candidate drugs. Limitations include differences between mouse and human physiology, genetic redundancy (paralogues compensating for the lost gene), and embryonic lethality. Newer genome-editing tools such as CRISPR–Cas9 have made generating knockouts faster and cheaper, supplementing classical ES-cell targeting.
