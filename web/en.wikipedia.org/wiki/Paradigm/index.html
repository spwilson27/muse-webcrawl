Paradigm

A paradigm is a distinct set of concepts, patterns, or frameworks that shapes how a field understands and investigates its subject. The word derives from the Greek paradeigma, meaning "pattern" or "example." Its modern prominence comes from the philosopher of science Thomas S. Kuhn, whose 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions made "paradigm" and "paradigm shift" central to debates about how science changes.

Kuhn's account

For Kuhn, a paradigm is the constellation of beliefs, values, exemplars, and techniques shared by a scientific community — including symbolic generalizations, metaphysical commitments, and the model problems and solutions ("exemplars") that students learn. Within a paradigm, researchers practise "normal science," puzzle-solving guided by the framework. When anomalies accumulate and resist solution, a crisis may occur, ending in a scientific revolution in which a new paradigm replaces the old; Kuhn argued successive paradigms can be "incommensurable," lacking a common measure for direct comparison.

Other uses

In linguistics, a paradigm is the full set of inflected forms of a word (a declension or conjugation table). In computer science, programming paradigms — imperative, functional, object-oriented, declarative — describe fundamental styles of structuring programs. More loosely, the word is used in business and everyday speech for any dominant model or worldview.

Criticism

Kuhn's concept was criticized for ambiguity — Margaret Masterman counted over twenty senses of "paradigm" in his book — and later Kuhn refined the term, speaking of "disciplinary matrices" and exemplars. Philosophers such as Imre Lakatos and Karl Popper offered rival accounts of scientific change.
