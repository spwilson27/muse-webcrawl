Star cluster

A star cluster is a group of stars held together by mutual gravitational attraction. Clusters are born from the same giant molecular cloud at roughly the same time, so their member stars share an age and initial chemical composition while differing in mass — making clusters natural laboratories for testing theories of stellar evolution. Two main types are recognised: globular clusters and open clusters.

Globular clusters

Globular clusters are dense, roughly spherical collections of hundreds of thousands to millions of old stars, typically 10–13 billion years old, residing in the halo of a galaxy. The Milky Way hosts about 150 known globular clusters, such as Omega Centauri and 47 Tucanae. Their stars are metal-poor, formed early in the galaxy's history, and their ages provide a lower bound on the age of the universe. Many contain exotic objects — blue stragglers, millisecond pulsars and dense concentrations of stellar remnants.

Open clusters

Open clusters are loose groupings of a few dozen to a few thousand stars found in the galactic disk. They are young — typically tens to hundreds of millions of years old — and gradually disperse as they are disrupted by gravitational interactions. Famous examples include the Pleiades and the Hyades in Taurus, and the Double Cluster in Perseus. Because their stars are coeval, the position of the main-sequence turnoff in a cluster's Hertzsprung–Russell diagram yields its age.

Other groupings

Looser, unbound associations of young stars (stellar associations, such as OB associations) mark recent star-forming regions, while dense "super star clusters" in starburst galaxies may be young globular clusters in formation. Embedded clusters still within their parent nebulae, such as the Trapezium in the Orion Nebula, show star formation in progress.

Importance

Star clusters underpin the cosmic distance ladder's calibration in the local universe, trace the formation history and chemical evolution of galaxies, and provide crucial tests of stellar structure, dynamics and the formation of compact objects.
