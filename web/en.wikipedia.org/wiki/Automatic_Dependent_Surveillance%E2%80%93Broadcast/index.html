Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) is a surveillance technology in which an aircraft determines its position via satellite navigation or other sensors and periodically broadcasts it, enabling it to be tracked. The information can be received by air traffic control ground stations as a replacement for secondary surveillance radar, since no interrogation signal is needed from the ground.

ADS-B is "automatic" in that no pilot or controller input is required for the data to be transmitted. It is "dependent" in that it depends on data from the aircraft's navigation system in order to provide surveillance data to air traffic control. It is a key element of the worldwide move towards more satellite-based air traffic management, and underpins initiatives such as the United States' NextGen programme and the Single European Sky ATM Research project (SESAR).

How it works

An ADS-B equipped aircraft uses a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver, typically GPS, to determine its precise position, altitude, velocity and other data. This information, together with the aircraft's identity, is broadcast automatically about once per second over a radio link, most commonly the 1090 MHz extended squitter (1090ES) used by Mode S transponders. In the United States, general aviation aircraft may instead use the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) link.

Ground stations receive these transmissions and feed them into air traffic control automation systems, giving controllers a radar-like picture of traffic. Aircraft equipped with ADS-B In can also receive the broadcasts of nearby aircraft (ADS-B Out) and display them in the cockpit, improving pilots' situational awareness.

The United States Federal Aviation Administration required ADS-B Out in most controlled airspace from 1 January 2020. Australia mandated ADS-B for instrument flight rules aircraft earlier, and the European Union has required it for larger aircraft under its surveillance performance and interoperability rules. Because ADS-B broadcasts are unencrypted and unauthenticated, networks of hobbyist receivers can track aircraft worldwide, a capability that has raised both safety-research interest and privacy concerns.
