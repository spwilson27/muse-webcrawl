John Keats

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of John Keats (1821–22) by Joseph Severn: the poet in his final Roman days, reading, drawn by his travelling companion]

John Keats (31 October 1795 – 23 February 1821) was an English Romantic poet who died of tuberculosis at 25 with his work barely known — and is now ranked among the greatest poets in the language. In five years of writing he produced the odes of 1819 ('Ode to a Nightingale', 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', 'To Autumn'), the narrative poems Endymion (1818), 'Lamia', 'The Eve of St. Agnes' and the unfinished Hyperion, and letters that are themselves classics of poetic thought.

His letters give criticism its enduring phrases — 'negative capability', the willingness to dwell in uncertainties, and the 'vale of soul-making' — summarised here rather than quoted. The hostile reviews of his Cockney origins ('back to the shop, Mr John', sneered Blackwood's, in the usual paraphrase) and Shelley's elegy Adonais fixed the myth of the poet killed by critics; Keats himself, nursing his dying brother and haemorrhaging in 1820, knew his enemy was consumption.

The odes and the last year

The annus mirabilis of 1819 — the odes, The Eve of St Agnes, Lamia — was written while Keats fell in love with Fanny Brawne, whose engagement his illness froze. Sailing for Italy in hope of the climate's cure, he died in Rome in the house by the Spanish Steps now his museum, asking that his gravestone bear no name but the words — here Englished in his own request — that his name was written in water.

Reputation

The Victorian recovery (Tennyson, the Pre-Raphaelites) made Keats the poet's poet; the odes' sensuous density and the letters' intelligence sustain that verdict. 'Beauty is truth' closes the Grecian Urn — the most argued-over two words in English lyric criticism.
