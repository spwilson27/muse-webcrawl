Orpheus and Eurydice

The myth of Orpheus and Eurydice is a story from Greek mythology about the legendary musician Orpheus and his descent into the underworld to recover his dead wife, Eurydice. It is one of the most retold myths of antiquity, treated by Virgil in the Georgics, by Ovid in the Metamorphoses, and in Plato's Symposium, and it has been reinterpreted continuously in poetry, painting, opera and film ever since.

Orpheus looks back at Eurydice during the ascent from Hades.

The myth

Orpheus, son of the Muse Calliope (in some traditions of Apollo), possessed a lyre playing of supernatural power: wild beasts followed him, and trees and stones were said to move at his music. He married the nymph Eurydice, but on their wedding day she was bitten by a viper while fleeing the advances of Aristaeus (in Virgil's version) and died.

Grief-stricken, Orpheus descended alive into Hades. His song moved the gods of the dead: the Furies wept, Tantalus forgot his thirst, and Hades and Persephone agreed to release Eurydice — on one condition: Orpheus must walk ahead of her and not look back until both had reached the upper world. Near the exit, fearing he was being deceived and longing to see her, Orpheus turned; Eurydice, still in shadow, was lost a second time, uttering only a farewell. Orpheus was refused a second descent.

In the aftermath Orpheus wandered, singing of his loss and (in Ovid) rejecting the love of women; he was eventually torn apart by Maenads. His head and lyre floated down the river Hebrus, the head still singing, to the island of Lesbos.

Interpretations and legacy

Ancient authors diverged: Plato presents a moralising variant in which the gods showed Orpheus only a phantom because he lacked the courage to die for love. The myth became a foundational story about art's power and limits in the face of death. It launched the operatic genre — Peri's Euridice (1600) is the earliest surviving opera, followed by Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607) — and includes Gluck's Orfeo ed Euridice (1762) and Offenbach's satirical Orpheus in the Underworld (1858). Modern retellings include Cocteau's film Orphée (1950), the Brazilian film Black Orpheus (1959), and the stage musical Hadestown.
