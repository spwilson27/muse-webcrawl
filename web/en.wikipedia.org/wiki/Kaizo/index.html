Kaizo (Japanese: 改造, "remodel" or "reconstruction") is a philosophy and genre of video game level design, originally applied to ROM hacks of the 1990 platform game Super Mario World, in which levels are made exceptionally difficult — often unfairly — demanding precise execution, deep knowledge of game mechanics, and repeated attempts. The genre takes its name from Kaizo Mario World (2006/2007), an infamous hack that popularised extreme-precision platforming. Kaizo design later migrated into Super Mario Maker and its sequel, which hosted large communities of kaizo creators and players. History

The ROM-hacking community around SMW Central developed editing tools such as Lunar Magic beginning in 2000, enabling custom levels with the game's physics. T. Takemoto created Kaizo Mario World (from Jikū Mario World) around 2006–2007 as a joke challenge for a friend; videos of its brutal levels spread widely online, giving the style its name. Successive works — including the Item Abuse hacks, which required frame-perfect tricks such as juggling shells against walls — refined the genre's vocabulary. The release of Super Mario Maker (2015) brought kaizo design to a mass audience, albeit with quality-control disputes over levels that exploited hidden blocks and "troll" elements.

Design conventions

Classic kaizo design includes invisible coin blocks placed over jumps ("kaizo blocks"), near-pixel-perfect leaps between spikes or Munchers, forced reliance on advanced techniques (shell jumps, cape flight, spin jumps on enemies) often within seconds, and mid-air item manipulations. A distinction is drawn between "kaizo" proper — difficult but designed to be beaten through skill and readable setups, ideally without save states — and "pit" hacks, which demand frame-perfect inputs throughout and are typically completed only with tool assistance. Modern kaizo hacks frequently include instant-restart systems, indicators for intended tricks, and graded difficulty from "kaizo: beginner" to "kaizo: expert".

Community and streaming culture

Kaizo became a prominent streaming genre on Twitch and YouTube in the 2010s, with speedrunners and creators such as GrandPooBear and Barbarian playing and designing hacks, and annual showcases presenting community creations. Events like the Kaizo Level competitions at SMW Central and the "Blind Kaizo Race" format — players racing unseen hacks — are staples of the scene.

Influence

The kaizo approach influenced commercial precision platformers such as Celeste and Super Meat Boy, and the term is used informally for punishing challenge hacks of other games, from Pokémon difficulty hacks to The Legend of Zelda randomiser variants. Nintendo's own Super Mario Maker "Super Expert" difficulty and endless-challenge leaderboards institutionalised parts of the culture.
