Homological algebra is the branch of mathematics that studies homology in a general algebraic setting. It extracts the algebraic machinery originally developed for algebraic topology—where homology groups measure the "holes" in a topological space—and applies it across algebra, algebraic geometry and representation theory. Its fundamental idea is to study an object by resolving it with simpler objects, and to measure the failure of functors to be exact by means of derived functors.

Chain complexes and homology

A chain complex is a sequence of abelian groups or modules connected by homomorphisms (differentials) whose composition is zero, so that the image of each map lies in the kernel of the next. The homology groups of the complex are the quotients of cycles (kernel elements) by boundaries (image elements); they vanish exactly where the sequence is exact. Short exact sequences induce long exact sequences in homology, the basic computational tool of the subject.

Derived functors

Many important functors, such as taking invariants or tensor products, preserve exactness on one side only. By applying the functor to a projective or injective resolution of an object and taking homology, one obtains its derived functors: Ext, the derived functor of Hom, measures extensions of modules; Tor, derived from the tensor product, detects torsion and flatness; sheaf cohomology and group cohomology arise in the same framework. Spectral sequences, introduced by Leray and systematised by Grothendieck, organise complicated computations of these invariants.

History

The subject crystallised in the 1940s and 1950s from work in topology (Eilenberg, Steenrod), group cohomology, and commutative algebra (notably the homological characterisation of regular local rings by Serre, and Auslander–Buchsbaum). The textbook Homological Algebra (1956) by Henri Cartan and Samuel Eilenberg codified the field, and Alexander Grothendieck's Tôhoku paper (1957) recast it in terms of abelian categories. In recent decades, derived categories and triangulated categories have become the standard language, and homological methods pervade modern algebraic geometry, number theory and mathematical physics.
