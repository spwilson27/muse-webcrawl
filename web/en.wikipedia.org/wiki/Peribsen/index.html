Peribsen (also known by his Greek name or by the name form Seth-Peribsen) was an early Egyptian king (pharaoh) of the 2nd Dynasty who ruled during the Early Dynastic Period. He is famous for a remarkable break with tradition: his royal serekh — the palace-façade emblem containing the king's name — is surmounted not by the falcon of the god Horus, as for all other early kings, but by the Seth animal, the creature of the god Seth. He is the only king of Pharaonic Egypt known to have placed Seth alone above his serekh.

Name and the Seth question

Peribsen's choice of Seth has been interpreted in several ways. An influential older theory held that Egypt was split between rival Horus and Seth cults, with Peribsen a Seth-worshipping ruler of Upper Egypt and a religious schism dividing the kingdom. Later scholars have argued instead that Seth was a legitimate royal god associated with kingship in the south, that the change may mark a reorganisation of royal ideology, or that Peribsen's rule was confined to Upper Egypt for political rather than theological reasons. Under his successor Khasekhemwy, both Horus and Seth appear together above the serekh, suggesting a reconciliation, and Khasekhemwy's name ("the two powers arise") is often read as celebrating reunification.

Scholars also debate Peribsen's relationship to king Sekhemib, whose serekh bears the Horus falcon and who added the epithet Perenmaat; many now think Sekhemib and Peribsen may be the same king who changed his name, while others treat them as separate rulers.

Reign and tomb

Few events of Peribsen's reign are recorded. Inscriptions and seal impressions bearing his name were found at Abydos, Elephantine and elsewhere in Upper Egypt; his apparent absence from Lower Egyptian sites has fuelled the divided-kingdom debate. His tomb is Tomb P at Umm el-Qa'ab near Abydos, a modest mudbrick structure smaller than the tombs of the 1st Dynasty kings. A famous diorite stela from the tomb, now in the British Museum, shows his Seth-topped serekh; notably, the Seth animal on some monuments was later defaced, apparently in a subsequent reaction against his choice.
