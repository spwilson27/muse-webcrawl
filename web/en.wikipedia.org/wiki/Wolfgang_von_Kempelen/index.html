Wolfgang von Kempelen

Wolfgang von Kempelen (Hungarian: Kempelen Farkas; 23 January 1734 – 26 March 1804) was a Hungarian author and inventor, best known for his chess-playing automaton known as the Mechanical Turk and for constructing one of the earliest practical speech synthesisers.

Life

Kempelen was born in Pressburg (now Bratislava) in the Kingdom of Hungary, into a family of noble rank. He studied law and philosophy in Vienna and entered the service of the Habsburg court, where he supervised salt mining in Transylvania, worked on waterworks — he designed the fountains of Schönbrunn Palace and a steam engine — and served the Empress Maria Theresa as a court councillor.

The Mechanical Turk

In 1770 Kempelen unveiled his most famous creation: a life-size figure in Ottoman dress seated at a cabinet, which appeared to play chess against human opponents — and usually won. The "Turk" toured Europe and America for decades, defeating players including Napoleon and Benjamin Franklin. It was in fact an elaborate illusion: a skilled human chess player hid inside the cabinet, operating the figure through a system of levers. The secret was debated throughout the machine's life and only fully explained after it was destroyed by fire in Philadelphia in 1854.

Speaking machine

Kempelen's more genuinely scientific work was his mechanical speech synthesiser, described in his book Mechanismus der menschlichen Sprache (1791). The bellows-and-reed device, fitted with a hand-shaped "mouth", could produce vowels, consonants, and short phrases, and is regarded as a pioneering achievement in phonetics and speech science.
