Intertextuality

Intertextuality is the shaping of a text's meaning by another text, including the interconnections between similar or related works of literature. The term was coined by the philosopher and literary theorist Julia Kristeva in the 1960s, drawing on Mikhail Bakhtin's ideas of dialogism and Ferdinand de Saussure's semiotics.

Concepts

Kristeva argued that a text is "a mosaic of quotations" — a permutation and transformation of other texts — so that meaning is never produced in isolation. Later theorists distinguished deliberate intertextuality (quotation, allusion, parody, adaptation) from the broader, unavoidable intertextuality by which any text participates in the discourse of its culture. Gérard Genette systematised the relationships under the term transtextuality, including hypertextuality (a text derived from an earlier one) and paratextuality (titles, prefaces, and other framing material).

Examples

James Joyce's Ulysses reworks Homer's Odyssey; T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land is dense with quotations and allusions; modern retellings, sequels by other hands, fan fiction, and sampling in music all exhibit intertextual structures.

Criticism

Critics have charged that in its broadest form the concept becomes trivially true of all texts and loses analytical power, and some scholars prefer to restrict the term to traceable, intentional relationships between specific works.
