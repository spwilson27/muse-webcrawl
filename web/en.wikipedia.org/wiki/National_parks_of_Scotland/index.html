The national parks of Scotland are managed areas of outstanding landscape where habitat and scenery are protected, sustainable development is promoted, and public access is encouraged. Scotland has two national parks, created under the National Parks (Scotland) Act 2000: Loch Lomond and The Trossachs National Park (2002) and the Cairngorms National Park (2003), the latter the largest national park in the United Kingdom at about 4,528 km².

Aims and governance

Scottish national parks have four statutory aims: to conserve and enhance natural and cultural heritage; to promote sustainable use of natural resources; to promote public understanding and enjoyment; and to promote sustainable economic and social development of the area's communities. Unlike the wilderness model used in some countries, Scottish parks are living, working landscapes with farms, villages, and towns; each is run by a national park authority combining appointed and locally elected members.

The parks

Loch Lomond and The Trossachs covers the great freshwater loch, the Trossachs glens popularised by Sir Walter Scott, and the mountains around Ben Lomond, drawing millions of visitors from nearby Glasgow. The Cairngorms encompasses Britain's largest area of high Arctic-alpine ground, ancient Caledonian pine forest, and a quarter of the UK's threatened species, as well as ski centres and towns such as Aviemore. Many parts of both parks are also designated National Scenic Areas. Proposals for additional parks (for example in Galloway) have been under consideration by the Scottish Government.
