Textile manufacture during the British Industrial Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Textile manufacture during the British Industrial Revolution was centered in south Lancashire and the towns on both sides of the Pennines. In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, mechanization transformed cotton spinning and weaving from a domestic, hand-based craft into a factory industry powered first by water and then by steam, making Britain the world's leading producer of cotton cloth.

Before mechanization

Early textile production operated under the putting-out (domestic) system, in which merchant clothiers distributed raw cotton to families who carded, spun, and wove it at home. Weaving was speeded by John Kay's flying shuttle (1733), which created a bottleneck in spinning that a series of inventions set out to relieve.

Key inventions

James Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764) let one worker spin many threads at once. Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) produced stronger thread and required powered factories — his Cromford Mill in Derbyshire is a landmark of the factory system. Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) combined the jenny and water frame. Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785), gradually improved, mechanized weaving and shifted weavers into mills. Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793), though American, vastly increased supplies of raw cotton grown in the American South by enslaved labor.

The factory system

Production concentrated in mill towns such as Manchester ("Cottonopolis"), Bolton, Oldham, and Preston. Working conditions were harsh — long hours, child labor, and dangerous machinery — prompting factory legislation (the Factory Acts) and working-class movements including Luddism and Chartism. Output grew enormously: British cotton imports rose from a few million pounds of raw cotton annually in the 1760s to hundreds of millions of pounds by the mid-19th century, and cotton goods became Britain's largest export.
