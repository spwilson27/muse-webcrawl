Amalthea (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Amalthea or Amaltheia (Ancient Greek: Ἀμάλθεια, "tender goddess") was the figure who nursed the infant Zeus in hiding on Crete, protecting him from his father Cronus, who had swallowed his other children. In the best-known version she is a goat whose milk fed the god; in other traditions she is a nymph — daughter of Oceanus, of Haemonius, or of King Melissus of Crete — who fed Zeus on goat's milk.


The infant Zeus with the goat Amalthea (after Nicolas Poussin)

Myth

Rhea hid her newborn son Zeus in a cave on Mount Ida (or Mount Dicte) in Crete and entrusted him to Amalthea and the Curetes, who drowned out the baby's cries by clashing their weapons. Amalthea's milk nourished the future king of the gods.

One of the goat's horns was accidentally broken off; Zeus, in gratitude, filled it with fruits and flowers, making it the cornucopia, the horn of plenty that provides whatever its owner wishes. In another account, Zeus used Amalthea's unbreakable horn as the first cornucopia and placed the goat among the stars as the constellation Capella's surroundings — the star Capella being associated with Amalthea, and her hide later becoming the aegis, the shield of Zeus and Athena.

Interpretation

Amalthea belongs to a group of Cretan nurse-figures of Zeus (including Adrasteia and Ida) connected with the island's ancient cult of a dying-and-rising vegetation and storm god. The cornucopia originating from her horn became one of the most widespread symbols of abundance in classical and later Western art.

In art

The nursing of Zeus was a popular subject in Renaissance and Baroque art: Jacob Jordaens painted The Infant Jupiter Nurtured by the Goat Amalthea, and Nicolas Poussin treated the subject in The Nurture of Jupiter. A celebrated marble group by Gian Lorenzo Bernini (c. 1615), The Goat Amalthea with the Infant Jupiter and a Faun, is among his earliest surviving works.
