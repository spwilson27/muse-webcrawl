Teleology

Teleology
FieldPhilosophy, metaphysics, ethics, biology
EtymologyGreek telos ("end, purpose") + logos
Key figuresPlato, Aristotle, Aquinas, Kant, Hegel
RelatedFinal cause, teleological argument, teleonomy

Teleology is the study or doctrine of ends, purposes and goals — the explanation of phenomena by the purpose they serve rather than by their causes alone. A teleological explanation attributes the existence of something to the end toward which it is directed, as when the heart is said to exist in order to pump blood.

Ancient and medieval

Plato's demiurge orders the cosmos toward the good, and Aristotle made the "final cause" one of his four causes: for Aristotle, natural things have intrinsic ends, as the acorn's end is the oak. Medieval thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas integrated final causality into natural theology — his Fifth Way argues from the directedness of unintelligent things to a governing intelligence.

Modern critiques

The scientific revolution of the seventeenth century, with Bacon, Descartes and Spinoza, expelled final causes from physics in favour of efficient causation. Kant retained teleology as a "regulative" principle indispensable for judging organisms but not constitutive of nature. Darwin's theory of natural selection is often seen as explaining apparent purpose in biology without design; philosophers of biology coined "teleonomy" for such goal-directedness produced by natural processes.

Contemporary uses

Teleological language remains pervasive in biology (functions of organs), in ethics (consequentialism evaluates acts by ends), and in theories of action, where behaviour is explained by intentions and goals.
