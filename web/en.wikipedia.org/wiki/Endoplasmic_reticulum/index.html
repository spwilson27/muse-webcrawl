Endoplasmic reticulum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is the largest membrane system inside eukaryotic cells: a network of flattened sacs and tubules continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It synthesises proteins and lipids, folds and checks proteins, stores calcium, and detoxifies some compounds.

Rough and smooth ER

The rough ER is studded with ribosomes on its cytoplasmic surface and is the entry point of the secretory pathway: proteins destined for membranes, secretion or lysosomes are threaded into its lumen as they are made, folded with the help of chaperones, and sent on to the Golgi apparatus. The smooth ER lacks ribosomes and makes lipids and steroid hormones, breaks down glycogen, detoxifies drugs (notably in liver cells) and, in muscle, stores the calcium that triggers contraction — there called the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Quality control and stress

Misfolded proteins are normally refolded or extracted and degraded. When they accumulate, the unfolded protein response slows production and expands the ER; persistent ER stress contributes to diseases including diabetes and some neurodegenerations.
