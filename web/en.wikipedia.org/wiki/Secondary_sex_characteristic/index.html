Secondary sex characteristic

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals, distinguishing the two sexes of a species but not directly involved in reproduction — unlike the primary sex characteristics (the gonads and reproductive organs). They develop in response to rising levels of sex hormones: androgens such as testosterone in males, and estrogens in females.

In humans

Males

Under testosterone, males develop facial, chest and pubic hair; an enlarged larynx (Adam's apple) and deepened voice; increased muscle mass and stature; broadened shoulders; and increased activity of sebaceous and apocrine glands.

Females

Under estrogen, females develop breasts (thelarche is usually the first sign of puberty), widened hips and a higher proportion of body fat, and begin menstruation (menarche). Both sexes develop pubic and axillary hair, partly under adrenal androgens (adrenarche).

In other animals

Secondary sex characteristics across the animal kingdom include the mane of the male lion, the bright breeding plumage of many male birds, the antlers of deer, the enlarged claws of male fiddler crabs and the tusks of narwhals. Many such traits are products of sexual selection, as proposed by Charles Darwin in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871): they may signal fitness to mates or serve in competition between males, even at some cost to survival.
