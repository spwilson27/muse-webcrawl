Uruk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Reconstruction of the White Temple on its ziggurat at Uruk]

Uruk was an ancient city of Sumer in southern Mesopotamia (modern Warka, Iraq), one of the first true cities in the world and, for much of the 4th millennium BC, likely the largest settlement on Earth, with perhaps 40,000–80,000 inhabitants. In the Uruk period (c. 4000–3100 BC) urban life, monumental temple architecture, the potter's wheel, cylinder seals and — above all — writing (the earliest cuneiform tablets) appeared here. In Sumerian tradition its king was Gilgamesh, hero of the Epic of Gilgamesh, credited with building its great walls.

The first city

Uruk's two temple districts, Eanna (dedicated to Inanna) and Anu, raised ziggurats and mosaic-cone temples of unprecedented scale. The city's influence spread across Mesopotamia and beyond in the 'Uruk expansion' — colonies and outposts in Syria, Turkey and Iran trading for timber, stone and metal. Writing emerged around 3400–3300 BC in the Eanna precinct as pictographic accounting for temple estates, the administrative technology of history's first urban bureaucracy.

Later history

Uruk remained a great city under Akkad, Ur III and Babylon, famous for scholarship and the cult of Ishtar; it was still inhabited in Seleucid and Parthian times (its scholars produced some of the last cuneiform astronomical texts), before silting canals and shifting rivers ended occupation in the early Islamic centuries. German excavations from 1912 onward made Uruk central to understanding the birth of cities.
