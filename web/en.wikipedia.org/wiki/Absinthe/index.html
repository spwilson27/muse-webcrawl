Absinthe

Absinthe is a distilled, highly alcoholic spirit flavoured with botanicals including the flowers and leaves of Artemisia absinthium (grand wormwood), green anise, Florence fennel and other herbs. It is traditionally green in colour, hence its nickname la fée verte ("the green fairy"). Absinthe originated in the canton of Neuchâtel in Switzerland in the late 18th century and became enormously popular in late 19th- and early 20th-century France, particularly among Parisian artists and writers such as Charles Baudelaire, Paul Verlaine and Oscar Wilde.

History

A medicinal elixir of wormwood developed by Dr Pierre Ordinaire in Couvet, Switzerland, was commercialised by the Pernod family, who built a distillery at Pontarlier, France, in 1805. By the 1860s the daily ritual of l'heure verte ("the green hour") was entrenched in French café life, aided by cheap production that made absinthe affordable to all classes.

Controversy and bans

Absinthe was blamed for "absinthism", a syndrome of hallucinations and madness attributed to the compound thujone in wormwood, and temperance campaigners linked it to crime and social decay. Most countries banned it by 1915, including France, Switzerland and the United States. Modern research indicates that historic absinthe contained little thujone and that its harms were essentially those of its very high alcohol content. Bans were progressively lifted from the 1980s onward under EU rules, and the spirit enjoyed a revival; the United States effectively legalised it in 2007.

Preparation

Absinthe is traditionally diluted with iced water poured over a sugar cube resting on a slotted absinthe spoon; the water turns the spirit milky (the "louche") as herbal oils come out of solution. It is rarely drunk neat and forms the basis of classic cocktails such as the Sazerac.
