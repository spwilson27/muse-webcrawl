Lenapehoking (Unami: Lënapehòkink) is the traditional homeland of the Lenape people, an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands. At the time of European contact in the early seventeenth century, Lenapehoking covered the Delaware River valley and the lands around it — present-day eastern Pennsylvania, nearly all of New Jersey, northern Delaware, the lower Hudson Valley and New York City area, and western Long Island. The name combines Lënape, the people's name for themselves, with a locative element meaning "place" or "land".

Geography and peoples

The Lenape comprised many semi-autonomous communities speaking two closely related Eastern Algonquian languages: Unami in the south and Munsee in the north. The related Munsee-speaking peoples of the lower Hudson are sometimes distinguished as a separate group. Lenape settlements stood along the Delaware River (called Lënapei Sipu) and its tributaries and along the coasts; communities farmed maize, beans, and squash, fished the rivers, and hunted in the interior forests. Political organization was by village and kin group under sachems rather than any central state.

Colonization and dispossession

Dutch traders arrived in the early 1600s, followed by Swedish, Finnish, and then English settlers. William Penn's treaties with Lenape leaders in the 1680s became a byword for fair dealing, but his sons perpetrated the fraudulent Walking Purchase of 1737, which seized a vast tract along the upper Delaware. Through the eighteenth century, disease, land loss, and violence drove most Lenape westward — the process remembered in their own oral history and in Moravian mission records — into Pennsylvania's interior, then Ohio, Indiana, and beyond. The Treaty of Easton (1758) and later agreements formalized much of the dispossession.

Diaspora and present day

Today Lenape descendants live principally in Oklahoma (the Delaware Nation and the Delaware Tribe of Indians), in Wisconsin (the Stockbridge-Munsee Community), and in Ontario, Canada (at Moraviantown and Munsee-Delaware Nation). A small number of communities remained in or returned to the homeland, including the Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape Tribal Nation and the Ramapough Lenape Nation in New Jersey, which hold state but not federal recognition. In recent years Lenapehoking has become a familiar name in the homeland itself: land acknowledgments, museum exhibitions, and public art in New York and Philadelphia now routinely invoke it, and Lenape language and cultural revitalization efforts are under way in both the diaspora and the homeland.
