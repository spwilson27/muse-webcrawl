Soundproofing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Soundproofing is any means of impeding sound propagation — reducing the sound pressure transmitted between a source and a receiver. Soundproofing addresses both airborne sound (voices, music) and structure-borne sound (footfalls, machinery vibration), and is distinct from sound absorption, which reduces echoes within a space rather than blocking transmission between spaces.

Principles

Effective sound insulation generally combines four strategies:

Mass. Heavy, dense materials resist being set into vibration by sound waves, so thick masonry or multiple layers of plasterboard transmit less sound (the mass law predicts roughly 6 dB improvement per doubling of mass).

Decoupling. Separating the two faces of a wall — with staggered studs, resilient channels, or floating floors — interrupts the path of vibration through the structure.

Absorption. Filling wall cavities with mineral wool or fibreglass dissipates sound energy within the cavity and improves the performance of the assembly.

Damping and sealing. Viscoelastic damping compounds reduce panel resonance, and airtight sealing of gaps, doors, and penetrations is essential because small openings can dominate overall leakage.

Measurement

Performance is rated with the Sound Transmission Class (STC) in North America and the weighted sound reduction index (Rw) internationally. Impact insulation is measured by the Impact Insulation Class (IIC). Building codes commonly require party walls and floors in multi-unit housing to meet minimum STC/IIC ratings.

Applications

Soundproofing is used in recording studios, cinemas, hospitals, hotels, offices, and homes, and industrially to protect workers from noise and to comply with environmental noise regulations. Approaches range from simple door seals to "room within a room" construction for studios.
