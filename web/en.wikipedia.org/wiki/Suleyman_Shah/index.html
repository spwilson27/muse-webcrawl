Suleyman Shah

Died1227 (traditional date), near the Euphrates River
BurialTomb of Suleyman Shah (relocated several times)
HouseKayı tribe (Oghuz Turks)
IssueTraditionally including Ertuğrul (father of Osman I)

Suleyman Shah (Ottoman Turkish: Süleyman Şah; died c. 1227) is a semi-legendary figure of early Ottoman tradition, presented as the leader of the Kayı tribe of Oghuz Turks and, in Ottoman dynastic genealogy, the grandfather of Osman I, founder of the Ottoman Empire. According to tradition, Suleyman Shah led his tribe westward from Central Asia fleeing the Mongol invasions, and drowned while crossing the Euphrates River in present-day Syria. His tomb became a revered site and, remarkably, a tiny Turkish exclave within Syria maintained for centuries.

Traditional account

Ottoman chronicles relate that Suleyman Shah, son of Kaya Alp, migrated with his people from Merv in Khorasan as the Mongols advanced, reaching the region of the Euphrates near the castle of Qal'at Ja'bar. In 1227, while attempting to cross the river, he drowned (some accounts say his horse stumbled and both were swept away). His body was buried near the castle, and the site became known as Türk Mezarı, "the Tomb of the Turk". His sons divided the tribe: two returned east, while Ertuğrul and Dündar continued west into Anatolia, where Ertuğrul's son Osman would found the Ottoman beylik. Historians caution that the earliest Ottoman sources are late and the genealogy may be a legitimating construction, but the tomb's historical significance in Turkish national memory is undisputed.

The tomb as Turkish exclave

Under Article 9 of the 1921 Treaty of Ankara between France and Turkey, the tomb and a small plot around it remained Turkish property even though it lay in the French Mandate of Syria (later the Syrian Arab Republic), with Turkey permitted to station guards and fly its flag. Flooding from the Tabqa Dam forced a first relocation in 1973 to a site near Karakozak. In February 2015, as the Syrian civil war and the advance of ISIS threatened the enclave, Turkey carried out Operation Shah Euphrates, a cross-border military incursion that evacuated the guard detachment and exhumed the remains, temporarily moving them to a site just inside Turkey; the tomb was subsequently re-established at Eşme, Syria, near the Turkish border. The episode underscored the tomb's unique status as one of the world's most unusual pieces of sovereign territory.
