Soft gamma repeater

Soft gamma repeaters (SGRs) are astronomical sources that emit large bursts of gamma rays and X-rays at irregular intervals. They are a type of magnetar, a neutron star with an extremely powerful magnetic field, or possibly neutron stars with fossil disks around them.

On March 5, 1979, a powerful gamma-ray burst was detected from the source later designated SGR 0525−66 in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Unlike ordinary gamma-ray bursts, soft gamma repeaters emit repeated, softer bursts, and a small number of "giant flares" of extraordinary luminosity. The giant flare from SGR 1806−20 on December 27, 2004, was the brightest extrasolar event ever recorded, briefly outshining the full Moon in gamma rays and ionizing Earth's upper atmosphere despite being some 50,000 light-years away.

History

The first soft gamma repeater was identified after the March 5, 1979 event, when repeated bursts were traced to the supernova remnant N49 in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Further repeaters were discovered through the 1980s and 1990s, including SGR 1806−20 and SGR 1900+14, whose giant flare on August 27, 1998 also noticeably affected Earth's ionosphere.

Magnetar model

In the magnetar model, bursts are powered by the decay and sudden reconfiguration of a neutron star's magnetic field, which can exceed 1014–1015 gauss. Stress in the star's crust, or magnetic reconnection in the magnetosphere, releases energy as bursts; giant flares are attributed to large-scale magnetic rearrangements. The slowly rotating periods of SGRs (typically 5–12 seconds) and their rapid spin-down are consistent with magnetic dipole braking by such enormous fields.

Known sources

Only about a dozen soft gamma repeaters are known, mostly in the Milky Way and the Large Magellanic Cloud. They overlap in properties with anomalous X-ray pulsars, and both classes are now understood as magnetars at different activity levels.
