Taupo Volcano

Location: North Island, New Zealand
Type: Caldera / supervolcano (rhyolitic)
Last major eruption: Hatepe eruption, c. 232 CE
Caldera: partly filled by Lake Taupo

The Taupo Volcano is a large rhyolitic supervolcano in the centre of New Zealand's North Island, within the Taupo Volcanic Zone. The volcano's caldera is partly filled by Lake Taupo, the country's largest lake. Taupo has produced two of the world's most violent eruptions of the last 50,000 years.

Eruptive history

Taupo began erupting about 300,000 years ago. The Oruanui eruption of about 26,500 years ago is the world's largest known eruption of the past 70,000 years, ejecting roughly 1,170 cubic kilometres of material and forming much of the present caldera structure; it is rated VEI 8, the "supervolcanic" class.

The Hatepe eruption, dated to about 232 CE (with an older tradition placing it around 186 CE), was the most violent eruption of the last 5,000 years (VEI 7). It ejected some 120 cubic kilometres of material, devastated the central North Island with pyroclastic flows covering about 20,000 square kilometres, and produced a pumice raft and atmospheric effects recorded as red skies in Roman and Chinese chronicles. The eruption also expanded the lake basin and dammed its outlet, raising lake levels before the outlet was re-established.

Present state

Taupo is considered dormant rather than extinct. It is monitored by GeoNet; episodes of ground deformation and earthquake swarms beneath the lake — as in 1983, 2019 and 2022–23, when the volcanic alert level was briefly raised — are attributed to magma movement or hydrothermal activity at depth and are considered a normal part of the volcano's restlessness.
