Manetho (Egyptian: originally possibly "gift of Thoth"; Greek: Μανέθων, Manethōn) was an Egyptian priest of the early Ptolemaic period, probably from Sebennytos in the Nile Delta, who wrote the Aegyptiaca, a history of Egypt in Greek, in the early 3rd century BC, likely under Ptolemy I Soter or Ptolemy II Philadelphus.

The Aegyptiaca

Manetho had access to temple records, king lists and annals unavailable to Greek writers, and he arranged Egypt's rulers into thirty dynasties (a thirty-first is sometimes appended) from the legendary King Menes to the eve of Alexander's conquest — a dynastic framework that Egyptologists still use today, alongside the conventional divisions into Old, Middle and New Kingdoms that later scholars built upon it. His three volumes also covered the mythical rule of gods and demigods preceding the human kings.

Transmission

The original work is lost. What survives are epitomes and quotations preserved by later authors: the Jewish historian Josephus (who quoted Manetho, sometimes tendentiously, on the Hyksos and the Exodus), and the Christian chronographers Julius Africanus and Eusebius, whose versions were transmitted through George Syncellus. The two epitome traditions differ in many names and reign lengths, and scribal corruption makes exact reconstruction difficult, but comparison with native sources such as the Turin King List confirms the essential reliability of Manetho's scheme.

Other works and influence

Other writings were attributed to Manetho in antiquity, including works on religion and ritual and the astrological poem Apotelesmatica (probably pseudepigraphal). He is also associated in ancient sources with the introduction of the cult of Serapis under Ptolemy I. His dynastic system remains the backbone of Egyptian chronology.
