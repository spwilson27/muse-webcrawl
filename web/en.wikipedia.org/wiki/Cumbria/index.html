Cumbria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Derwentwater and the fells of the Lake District under a clearing sky]
Derwentwater and the fells of the Lake District under a clearing sky

Cumbria is a ceremonial county in North West England, formed in 1974 from Cumberland, Westmorland and parts of Lancashire and Yorkshire. Sparsely populated (about 500,000) and dominated by the Lake District National Park — a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2017 for its agro-pastoral landscape — it contains England's highest mountain (Scafell Pike, 978 m) and deepest and longest lakes. Its administrative centre is Carlisle.

Geography and history

Cumbria's fells and lakes were carved by ice; its valleys bred the Herdwick sheep and the fell pony. The region was the Brittonic kingdom of Rheged, then contested among Scots, Norse, Normans and the English crown (it changed hands until 1092 and was border-reiver country for centuries); Hadrian's Wall crosses its north. Industrial west Cumbria (coal, iron, steel at Workington and Barrow's shipyards) contrasts with the pastoral interior Wordsworth made famous.

Modern county

Tourism, farming, and the nuclear complex at Sellafield — site of the 1957 Windscale fire and now Europe's largest decommissioning project — dominate the economy; the county council was replaced in 2023 by two unitary authorities (Cumberland, and Westmorland and Furness). Flooding (Carlisle 2005 and 2015, Cockermouth 2009) is its recurring emergency, and its remoteness shapes debates over services and second homes.
