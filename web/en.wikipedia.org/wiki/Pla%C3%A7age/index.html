Plaçage

Plaçage was a system of formalized concubinage practised in French and Spanish colonial Louisiana, and elsewhere in the Caribbean, from the eighteenth into the nineteenth century. Under it, a man of European descent — barred by law and custom from marrying across the colour line — would "place" a woman of colour in a long-term, publicly recognized union, providing a house, an allowance, and support for their children.

Origins and practice

The practice grew from the shortage of European women in the colonies and the constraints of codes such as Louisiana's Code Noir. Arrangements were often negotiated by the woman's mother and formalized with arrangements for property; the women, known as placées, commonly belonged to the free people of colour (gens de couleur libres) of New Orleans. Their children were frequently educated, sometimes in France, and formed much of the Creole of colour community.

Quadroon balls

Popular tradition associates plaçage with the so-called "quadroon balls" at which matches were supposedly made, though historians regard the elaborate version of this story as partly romantic legend.

Decline

After the Louisiana Purchase, hardening American racial law — including bans on interracial marriage and on the inheritance rights of such unions — undermined the system, which largely disappeared by the mid-nineteenth century. Its legacy persisted in the distinct Creole communities of New Orleans.
