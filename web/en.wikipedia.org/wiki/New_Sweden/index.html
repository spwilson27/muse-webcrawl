New Sweden (Swedish: Nya Sverige) was a Swedish colony on the Delaware River in North America from 1638 to 1655, in present-day Delaware, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Maryland. It was Sweden's only North American colonial venture, established under the New Sweden Company, half-owned by the Swedish crown and Dutch investors, with many settlers drawn from Finland, then part of the Swedish realm.

Founding

The first expedition, led by Peter Minuit (formerly director of Dutch New Netherland), sailed on the Kalmar Nyckel and Fogel Grip and arrived in March 1638. The Swedes purchased land from the Lenape along the west bank of the Delaware and built Fort Christina, named for Queen Christina of Sweden, at the site of present-day Wilmington. The colony traded for beaver pelts with the Lenape and Susquehannock and planted tobacco.

Growth under Printz

Governor Johan Printz (1643–1653), a large and energetic former cavalry officer, expanded the settlements, building Fort Elfsborg in New Jersey and Fort Nya Gothenborg and the Printzhof residence on Tinicum Island, where he also established New Gothenburg. Swedish and Finnish settlers introduced log-cabin construction to America, cleared farms, and maintained generally good relations with the Lenape. At its height the colony numbered only a few hundred settlers, and supplies from Sweden arrived irregularly.

Dutch conquest

The Dutch West India Company never accepted the Swedish presence. In 1651 director-general Peter Stuyvesant built Fort Casimir near Fort Christina. Swedish governor Johan Risingh seized Fort Casimir in 1654 (renaming it Fort Trinity), prompting Stuyvesant to return in 1655 with a large force; the Swedes capitulated in September 1655, ending New Sweden. Most settlers remained on their farms under Dutch, then (from 1664) English rule.

Legacy

Swedish-Finnish cultural traces persist in the Delaware Valley: place names (Swedesboro, Finns Point), Lutheran churches such as Gloria Dei ("Old Swedes'") in Philadelphia (1700) and Holy Trinity in Wilmington (1698), the log cabin tradition, and the American Swedish Historical Museum in Philadelphia. In 1938 the tercentenary was celebrated with the dedication of Fort Christina Park by Sweden's crown prince.
