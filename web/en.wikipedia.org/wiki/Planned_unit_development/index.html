A planned unit development (PUD) is a type of real estate development and regulatory process in which a tract of land is developed as a single, integrated project under a unified plan, rather than lot-by-lot under conventional zoning rules. In exchange for flexibility in lot sizes, setbacks, housing types, and the mixing of uses, the developer negotiates an overall site plan with the local government, typically providing amenities such as preserved open space, recreation facilities, and landscaping beyond what standard subdivision rules would require.

Characteristics

PUDs commonly cluster housing at higher density on part of the site in order to permanently preserve the remainder as common open space, woodland, or farmland. They may combine single-family houses, townhouses, and apartments, and sometimes shops and offices, within one community. Common areas are usually owned and maintained by a homeowners association (HOA) funded by residents' dues. In the United States, PUD is also a classification used in mortgage lending — Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac apply specific project standards to units in PUDs — referring to developments with HOAs that own the common amenities.

History and purpose

Planned unit development emerged in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s as a response to rigid Euclidean zoning and monotonous suburban subdivisions. Writers such as William H. Whyte advocated clustered development to save open land, and municipalities adopted PUD ordinances to gain design quality and public benefits through negotiation rather than fixed rules. The concept influenced later movements such as New Urbanism and conservation subdivisions, though critics note that negotiation can be unpredictable, that HOA governance is private and sometimes unaccountable, and that PUDs have often been built as car-dependent enclaves.

Approval process

Because a PUD departs from the base zoning, approval typically requires a legislative rezoning or special permit with public hearings, review of a master plan, and conditions recorded against the land. Once approved, the plan itself becomes the governing regulation for the site.
