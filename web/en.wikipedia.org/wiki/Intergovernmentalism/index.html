Intergovernmentalism

Intergovernmentalism is a theory of regional integration in political science which holds that nation-states, acting in their own interests, remain the primary actors in international organisations, and that integration proceeds only as far as governments permit. It is the leading theoretical rival to neofunctionalism in the study of the European Union.

Core claims

Classical intergovernmentalism, associated with Stanley Hoffmann's reading of Charles de Gaulle's Europe, stresses the distinction between "high politics" (security, constitutional questions), where states jealously guard sovereignty, and "low politics" (trade, welfare), where cooperation is easier. Integration outcomes reflect bargaining among governments rather than any automatic spillover.

Liberal intergovernmentalism

Andrew Moravcsik's liberal intergovernmentalism (developed in The Choice for Europe, 1998) explains EU bargains in three steps: national preferences are formed by domestic economic interests; governments bargain asymmetrically according to their stake in an agreement; and states delegate to supranational institutions only to make commitments credible.

Intergovernmental and supranational methods

In EU practice, the "intergovernmental method" — unanimous decision-making in the Council with a limited role for the Commission and Parliament — contrasts with the "Community method" of qualified-majority voting and co-decision. Foreign policy, defence and treaty change remain predominantly intergovernmental.

Criticism

Critics argue the theory understates the autonomous influence of the European Commission, the Court of Justice and transnational actors, and struggles to explain episodes such as the creation of the euro or the dynamics of crises.
