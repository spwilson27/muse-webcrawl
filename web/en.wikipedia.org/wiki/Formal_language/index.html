Formal language

In logic, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics, a formal language consists of words whose letters are taken from an alphabet and are well-formed according to a specific set of rules. The alphabet of a formal language consists of symbols, letters, or tokens that concatenate into strings of the language. Each string concatenated from symbols of this alphabet is called a word, and the words that belong to a particular formal language are sometimes called well-formed words or well-formed formulas.

A formal language is often defined by means of a formal grammar such as a regular grammar or context-free grammar, which consists of its formation rules. Unlike natural languages, formal languages have precise, unambiguous syntax, making them suitable for programming languages, mathematical notation, and the theory of computation.

Definitions

An alphabet is a finite set of symbols. A string (or word) is a finite sequence of symbols from the alphabet, including possibly the empty string. A formal language is a set of strings over an alphabet — finite or infinite. Standard operations on languages include union, intersection, complement, concatenation, and the Kleene star (repetition).

The Chomsky hierarchy

In 1956 Noam Chomsky classified formal grammars into a hierarchy of increasing power: regular languages (recognised by finite automata), context-free languages (recognised by pushdown automata), context-sensitive languages (recognised by linear-bounded automata), and recursively enumerable languages (recognised by Turing machines). This hierarchy is foundational in theoretical computer science and in the analysis of programming language syntax, which is typically specified with context-free grammars such as those written in Backus–Naur form.

Applications

Formal languages underpin compiler design and parsing, regular expressions and text processing, the specification of communication protocols and data formats, and mathematical logic, where formulas of a formal system are strings of a formal language. They are also used in linguistics and cognitive science to model the syntax of natural languages.
