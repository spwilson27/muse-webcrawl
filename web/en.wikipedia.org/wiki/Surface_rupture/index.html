Surface rupture

Surface rupture is the visible breaking and displacement of the Earth's surface along the trace of an active fault during an earthquake. It occurs when slip on a fault at depth propagates all the way to the ground surface, offsetting soils, roads, fences and other features.

Characteristics

Ruptures may be a few metres to several hundred kilometres long and can involve horizontal (strike-slip), vertical (dip-slip) or mixed offsets ranging from centimetres to several metres. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake produced a surface rupture roughly 430 km long on the San Andreas Fault, while the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake in Taiwan created a prominent fault scarp up to several metres high.

Hazards and land use

Structures built directly across an active fault trace can be torn apart by surface rupture even if they survive the shaking. For this reason, jurisdictions such as California (under the Alquist-Priolo Earthquake Fault Zoning Act) restrict construction within mapped fault zones, and fault-rupture hazard studies are a standard part of siting for critical facilities such as pipelines, dams and hospitals.

Not all earthquakes rupture the surface

Many earthquakes, especially deep or moderate ones, produce no surface rupture; the fault slip remains buried. Paleoseismologists use trenching across fault scarps to identify past surface-rupturing events and estimate recurrence intervals.
