Ben-Hadad I (Aramaic: Bar-Hadad, "son of Hadad") was a king of Aram-Damascus in modern Syria who reigned in the early 9th century BC, roughly 885–865 BC. He was the son of Tabrimmon and grandson of Hezion, according to the Hebrew Bible (1 Kings 15). His name invokes Hadad, the storm god of the Arameans.

Reign

The biblical account in 1 Kings 15:16–22 relates that Baasha, king of Israel, built the fortress of Ramah to blockade the kingdom of Judah. Asa of Judah responded by sending the treasures of the Temple and palace to Ben-Hadad in Damascus, with a request to break his alliance with Baasha. Ben-Hadad accepted the bribe and attacked Israel's northern cities — Ijon, Dan, Abel-beth-maacah and the region of Naphtali — forcing Baasha to abandon Ramah. The campaign marked the beginning of Aramean expansion at Israel's expense and the rise of Damascus as the dominant power in the southern Levant.

Ben-Hadad is also commonly credited by historians with extending Aramean influence over the northern Israelite territories and with the fortification of Damascus, though extra-biblical sources for his reign are sparse; the later Melqart stele, erected by a Bar-Hadad, has been attributed variously to him or to a later namesake. He was succeeded by Ben-Hadad II, who fought a series of wars against King Ahab of Israel.
