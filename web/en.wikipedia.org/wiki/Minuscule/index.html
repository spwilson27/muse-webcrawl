Minuscule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Minuscule refers to lowercase letters in a writing system, as opposed to majuscule (uppercase or capital) letters. The terms derive from scribal practice with medieval manuscripts: minuscule (from Latin minuscula littera, "somewhat smaller letter") described the small, cursive-derived book hands used for the main text, while the larger formal capitals were reserved for headings and display. In modern typography the pair is expressed as "lowercase" and "uppercase", terms inherited from the compositor's type cases, in which minuscule sorts were stored in the lower case.

Carolingian minuscule, the clear book hand from which modern lowercase letterforms descend.

History

Classical Latin was written in capitals; distinct minuscule letterforms developed from Roman cursive in late antiquity, giving rise to half-uncial and regional hands such as Insular minuscule in Ireland and Britain and Visigothic and Beneventan scripts. The decisive development was Carolingian minuscule, promoted in the late eighth and ninth centuries under Charlemagne and scholars such as Alcuin of York: a round, legible hand with clear word separation and consistent letterforms that became the standard book script of Europe. Renaissance humanists, mistaking Carolingian manuscripts for ancient Roman ones, adapted the hand into humanist minuscule, which — combined with Roman capitals — became the basis of the first printed typefaces and hence of modern lowercase letters.

Greek and other scripts

Greek minuscule developed in the ninth century from cursive hands and is the ancestor of modern lowercase Greek type. Bicameral scripts — those with a case distinction — include Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Armenian, Georgian (modern usage), Coptic and Cherokee; most of the world's scripts, such as Arabic, Hebrew, Devanagari and Chinese, are unicameral and have no minuscule/majuscule distinction.

Terminology in textual criticism

In New Testament textual criticism, "minuscules" are the roughly 2,900 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament written in the minuscule hand (as opposed to the earlier uncials), catalogued with plain numbers in the Gregory–Aland system and dating mostly from the ninth to the fifteenth century.
