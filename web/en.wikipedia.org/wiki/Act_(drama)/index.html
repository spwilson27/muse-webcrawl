Act (drama)

An act is a major division of a theatre work, including a play, film, opera, or musical theatre, consisting of one or more scenes. The term can either refer to a conscious division placed within a work by a playwright (usually itself subdivided into scenes) or a unit of analysis for dividing a dramatic work into sequences.

[Image: Theatre curtain at the end of an act]

History

The structuring of plays into acts derives from ancient Greek drama, in which episodes were separated by choral odes. Roman dramatists such as Seneca formalised the five-act structure, which was revived during the Renaissance and became the classical norm for playwrights including Shakespeare, whose plays were divided into five acts by later editors. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the three-act structure became dominant, and many modern works use two acts separated by a single intermission.

Structure

An act may be divided into scenes, which change when the setting changes or characters enter or exit. In performance, acts are commonly separated by an intermission, during which the audience leaves the auditorium briefly and scene changes can be made. The number and length of acts varies: one-act plays run without a break, while epic works may have many acts.

Operas and musicals

Operas and musical theatre works are also divided into acts, often two or three, with musical numbers grouped within them. A finale typically closes an act with a climactic ensemble.
