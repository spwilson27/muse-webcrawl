Duchy of Brittany

The Duchy of Brittany (Breton: Dugelezh Breizh; French: Duché de Bretagne) was a medieval feudal state covering the north-western peninsula of present-day France, roughly the modern region of Brittany together with the Loire-Atlantique area around Nantes. It emerged in the 10th century from the earlier Breton kingdom, which had resisted Frankish domination, and persisted as a largely self-governing polity until its union with the French crown in 1532.

History

After Viking invasions shattered the Breton kingdom, Alan II "Wrybeard" rebuilt Breton power in the 930s–950s, and his successors ruled as dukes, nominally vassals of the French king but often effectively independent. The duchy was contested between local dynasties and the houses of Rennes, Cornouaille, and Penthièvre, drew in Norman, English, and French intervention, and passed by marriage to the Plantagenets in the 12th century and later to the Capetian cadet line of Dreux.

The War of the Breton Succession (1341–1365), a theatre of the Hundred Years' War, ended with the victory of the Montfort line, under whom the duchy reached its greatest autonomy, with its own estates, chancery, and court centred on Nantes and Rennes. Duchess Anne of Brittany's marriages to Charles VIII (1491) and then Louis XII brought Brittany into the French orbit; her daughter Claude married Francis I, and in 1532 the Estates of Brittany voted perpetual union with France, while retaining Breton fiscal and legal privileges until the Revolution.
