Suede

Suede is a type of leather with a fuzzy, napped finish, made from the underside of an animal skin. Because it is made from the inner split of a hide rather than the tough outer grain layer, suede is softer, thinner, and more pliable than standard full-grain leather, but also less durable and more easily stained. The name derives from the French gants de Suède — "gloves of Sweden" — after the soft Swedish gloves imported into France in the nineteenth century.

Production

Suede is produced by splitting a hide and buffing or sanding the flesh side (the corium) to raise a velvety nap of short protein fibers. Lambskin is the most common source for high-quality suede used in garments, although goatskin (often called "doeskin"), calfskin, and deerskin are also used; pigskin suede is tougher and widely used for shoes and gloves. Because the fibers are exposed, suede readily absorbs water and dirt, so finished goods are usually treated with protective sprays.

Uses

Suede is used for jackets, shoes (famously blue suede shoes), gloves, handbags, upholstery, and linings for jewelry boxes and instrument cases. Its soft texture makes it suitable for polishing cloths — a suede cloth is traditionally used to clean lenses, silver, and automobile finishes. In the automotive industry, synthetic suede substitutes such as Alcantara and Ultrasuede are common because they offer similar appearance with better wear resistance.

Care

Suede should be kept dry and cleaned with a stiff brush or a special suede eraser to lift dirt and restore the nap. It should not be treated with the creams and polishes used on smooth leather, which would mat the fibers. Water stains can often be evened out by lightly dampening the whole surface and allowing it to dry slowly.

Alternatives

Nubuck resembles suede but is made from the outside (grain side) of the hide, sanded to a fine nap; it is generally more durable. Pat­ent imitations and microfiber synthetic suedes are used where cost or water resistance matters.
