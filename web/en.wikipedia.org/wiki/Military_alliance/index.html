Military alliance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A military alliance is a formal agreement between nations concerning national security, in which the contracting parties agree to mutual protection and support in case of a crisis that has not been identified in advance. Military alliances typically contain provisions for collective defence — an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all — although obligations vary from binding commitments of armed assistance to promises of consultation.

History

Alliances are as old as organised warfare: the city-states of ancient Greece formed leagues such as the Delian and Peloponnesian Leagues, and the balance-of-power politics of early modern Europe produced shifting coalitions against aspiring hegemons. The nineteenth century saw both rigid alliance systems, culminating in the blocs of 1914, and the looser diplomacy of the Concert of Europe. After 1945, permanent peacetime alliances institutionalised on an unprecedented scale, above all NATO (founded 1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955–1991).

Functions and problems

Alliances serve to deter aggression by aggregating the capabilities of their members, to reassure partners, and to restrain allies from adventurism. International-relations theorists note the alliance security dilemma: each member fears both abandonment by its partners and entrapment in a conflict provoked by them. Alliances may also encourage risk-taking by protected states (moral hazard) and can chain members into wars none sought directly.

Modern alliances

Contemporary examples include NATO, whose Article 5 collective-defence clause was invoked for the first and only time after the September 11 attacks; the United States' bilateral alliances with Japan, South Korea, and Australia; the Collective Security Treaty Organization; and the Five Power Defence Arrangements. Scholars dispute whether alliances without a plausible adversary persist through institutional inertia or adapt to new purposes such as crisis management and partnership-building.
