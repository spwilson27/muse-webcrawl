Cytokine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cytokine
TypeSmall signalling proteins
Size~5–20 kDa
FunctionsCell signalling in immunity, inflammation, haematopoiesis
ClassesInterleukins, interferons, TNFs, chemokines, colony-stimulating factors

Cytokines are a broad and loose category of small proteins (roughly 5–20 kDa) important in cell signalling. Cytokines are peptides and cannot cross the lipid bilayer of cells to enter the cytoplasm; instead they act through specific receptors on cell surfaces. They are released by many cell types — especially immune cells — and are crucial in modulating the balance between humoral and cell-based immune responses, inflammation, and the production of blood cells.

Classification

  • Interleukins (IL-1 to IL-38 and counting) — originally named for signalling between leukocytes, now known to act much more widely.
  • Interferons — interfere with viral replication and activate immune cells.
  • Tumor necrosis factors (TNFs) — regulate immune cells and inflammation.
  • Chemokines — direct the migration of cells (chemotaxis) to sites of infection or injury.
  • Colony-stimulating factors — drive production of blood cells in the bone marrow.

Clinical significance

Cytokines act in cascades, often with redundancy and pleiotropy. Excessive release can be dangerous: a "cytokine storm" — an uncontrolled positive-feedback release — occurs in severe infections such as sepsis and in some cases of influenza and COVID-19, and can be lethal. Recombinant cytokines and cytokine-blocking drugs (such as anti-TNF antibodies) are mainstays of modern therapy for autoimmune diseases, cancers, and viral hepatitis.
