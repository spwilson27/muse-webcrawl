National Revival

A national revival (also called a national awakening) is a period of national cultural, social, and political awakening in the history of a nation, typically a stateless or subordinated people recovering its language, culture, and historical consciousness. The concept applies especially to the peoples of Central and Eastern Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries, whose revivals unfolded within the Habsburg, Ottoman, and Russian empires.

Pattern

Scholars such as Miroslav Hroch have described a recurring pattern: an initial phase of scholarly interest in the national language and folklore, followed by patriotic agitation through newspapers, schools, and cultural societies, and finally by a mass political movement seeking autonomy or independence. Standardization of the literary language was usually central: figures such as Ľudovít Štúr for the Slovaks, Vuk Karadžić for the Serbs, and the collectors of folklore and epics (for example Elias Lönnrot's Kalevala in Finland) gave their nations durable cultural foundations.

Examples

Prominent revivals include the Czech National Revival, the Bulgarian National Revival, the Greek national awakening culminating in the war of independence, the Estonian and Latvian national awakenings, the Finnish Fennoman movement, the Lithuanian National Revival, and parallel movements among Ukrainians, Croats, and Slovenes. Outside Europe, analogous movements include the Gaelic Revival in Ireland and the Hebrew language revival.

Legacy

The institutions founded during revivals — national theatres, museums, academies, and newspapers — became the framework of later nation-states, and revival-era writers and activists remain national heroes in many countries.
