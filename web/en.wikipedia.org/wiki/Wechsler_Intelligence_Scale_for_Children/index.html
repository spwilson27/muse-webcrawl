The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) is an individually administered intelligence test for children between the ages of 6 and 16. It is one of the most widely used measures of cognitive ability in educational and clinical psychology, descended from the scales developed by psychologist David Wechsler, who also created the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI).

History

The original WISC was published in 1949, adapted downward from the Wechsler-Bellevue scales, and produced separate Verbal and Performance IQ scores along with a Full Scale IQ. Successive revisions — the WISC-R (1974), WISC-III (1991), WISC-IV (2003) and WISC-V (2014) — updated norms, removed outdated items, and reorganised the test around index scores reflecting contemporary theories of cognitive abilities, including the Cattell–Horn–Carroll framework. Like all intelligence tests the WISC is periodically re-normed, in part because of the Flynn effect (population gains in test scores over time).

Structure of the WISC-V

The fifth edition comprises 16 subtests (10 primary and 6 secondary), including Block Design, Similarities, Matrix Reasoning, Vocabulary, Figure Weights, Visual Puzzles, Digit Span, Picture Span, Coding and Symbol Search. Results are reported as a Full Scale IQ (standardised to a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15) and five primary index scores: Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory and Processing Speed. Administration takes roughly 45–65 minutes for the core subtests and must be performed by a trained examiner.

Uses and interpretation

The WISC is used in psychoeducational evaluations to identify intellectual disability and giftedness, to support diagnosis of learning disorders and ADHD, and in neuropsychological assessment. Interpretation emphasises the profile of index scores rather than the Full Scale IQ alone when significant scatter exists. Critics have long debated the cultural fairness of intelligence tests; modern editions are developed with bias-review panels, differential item functioning analyses and large representative standardisation samples.
