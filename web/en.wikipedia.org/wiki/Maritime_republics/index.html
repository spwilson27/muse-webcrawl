Maritime republics

The maritime republics (Italian: repubbliche marinare) were thalassocratic city-states of the Mediterranean that flourished from the Middle Ages through the early modern period. The best known are Venice, Genoa, Pisa and Amalfi, whose coats of arms appear on the flags of the Italian Navy and merchant marine. Other states often counted among them include Ragusa (Dubrovnik), Gaeta, Ancona and Noli.

Characteristics

These republics owed their wealth to overseas trade, shipping and naval power rather than to agricultural territory. They established networks of colonies, trading posts and consulates around the Mediterranean and Black Sea, negotiated commercial privileges with the Byzantine Empire and Muslim states, and played a decisive role in transporting crusaders to the Holy Land. Their governments were typically oligarchies dominated by merchant families, headed in Venice and Genoa by a doge elected for life.

Rivalry and decline

Venice and Genoa fought a series of wars for commercial supremacy, culminating in the War of Chioggia (1378–1381), after which Venice emerged as the dominant Italian sea power. The shifting of trade routes to the Atlantic after the Portuguese rounding of Africa and the advance of the Ottoman Empire gradually reduced the republics' importance, though Venice survived as an independent state until 1797 and Ragusa until 1808.

Legacy

The maritime republics pioneered maritime law, marine insurance, bills of exchange and double-entry bookkeeping's spread through the Mediterranean world. Their galleys and later sailing ships were crewed in part by soldiers comparable to modern marines, and their trading networks remain a classic subject in the study of market towns and commercial institutions.
