Actigraphy is a non-invasive method of monitoring human rest and activity cycles. A small unit called an actigraph is worn, usually on the wrist like a wristwatch, for days to weeks; its accelerometer records gross motor activity, from which sleep–wake patterns are estimated using validated algorithms.

A double-plotted actogram showing rest–activity rhythms across successive days.

Uses

Actigraphy is widely used in sleep medicine and research to characterise sleep duration, timing and fragmentation in a person's natural environment, complementing — but not replacing — polysomnography, which measures sleep stages directly. It is particularly useful in evaluating insomnia, circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders (such as delayed sleep phase and shift-work disorder), and in studies of sleep in infants, older adults and occupational settings.

Limitations

Because actigraphy infers sleep from movement, it can overestimate sleep when a person lies awake motionless, and it cannot distinguish sleep stages. Accuracy varies by device and scoring algorithm, and data are usually combined with a sleep diary.
