MOX fuel

Mixed oxide fuel, commonly referred to as MOX fuel, is nuclear fuel that contains more than one oxide of fissile material, usually consisting of plutonium blended with natural uranium, reprocessed uranium, or depleted uranium. MOX fuel is an alternative to the low-enriched uranium (LEU) fuel used in most light-water reactors.

Composition and manufacture

MOX fuel typically contains around 7% plutonium mixed with depleted uranium, giving it behaviour broadly similar to the low-enriched uranium oxide fuel for which most reactors were designed.[1] The plutonium can come from civilian reprocessing of spent fuel or from dismantled nuclear weapons. Fresh MOX is manufactured by blending plutonium dioxide and uranium dioxide powders, milling them, and pressing and sintering the mixture into ceramic pellets, which are loaded into fuel rods and assemblies.

Use in reactors

MOX fuel has been used commercially since the 1980s, chiefly in France, where a substantial fraction of the pressurised water reactor fleet is licensed to load MOX assemblies as part of the core. It is also used in Germany, Switzerland, Belgium and Japan, and in fast reactors in Russia. Because plutonium isotopes such as Pu-240 and Pu-242 build up with each irradiation cycle, plutonium recycled more than once becomes progressively less attractive as thermal-reactor fuel, though it remains usable in fast neutron reactors.

Advantages and concerns

Advocates argue that MOX fuel extracts additional energy from uranium resources and reduces the stockpile of separated plutonium and the volume of high-level waste requiring disposal. Critics raise proliferation concerns, since the plutonium in fresh MOX, while not directly weapons-usable without further chemical processing, is a safeguard-sensitive material, and point to the higher cost of MOX fabrication compared with fresh LEU fuel.
