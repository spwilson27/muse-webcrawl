The realisation that dinosaurs are closely related to birds raised the obvious possibility of feathered dinosaurs. Fossils of Archaeopteryx include well-preserved feathers, but it was not until the early 1990s that clearly non-avialan dinosaur fossils were discovered with preserved feathers. Today more than 50 genera of non-avialan dinosaurs are known to have been feathered, and many more are inferred from phylogenetic bracketing.

History of research

Thomas Henry Huxley argued in the 1860s–70s that birds evolved from dinosaurs, comparing Archaeopteryx and Compsognathus. John Ostrom's work on Deinonychus from 1969 revived the theory. The decisive evidence came from the Liaoning deposits of northeastern China: Sinosauropteryx (1996) was the first non-avialan dinosaur found with filamentous feather-like structures, followed by Caudipteryx and Protarchaeopteryx (1998) with true pennaceous feathers, and the four-winged Microraptor (2003).

Distribution and types

Feather types range from simple filaments ("dino-fuzz") to downy tufts, symmetrical vaned feathers, and asymmetrical flight feathers. Feathered taxa include small dromaeosaurids, troodontids, oviraptorosaurs, therizinosaurs, tyrannosauroids (Dilong, the huge Yutyrannus of 2012), and compsognathids. Filamentous integument in ornithischians such as Tianyulong and Kulindadromeus, and pycnofibres in pterosaurs, has prompted debate on whether feathers are ancestral to dinosaurs or even to all ornithodirans. Studies of melanosomes in fossils have reconstructed actual feather colours, e.g. the iridescent black plumage of Microraptor and the ginger-and-white banded tail of Sinosauropteryx.

Function

Feathers most likely evolved for insulation and/or display rather than flight; flight feathers appeared later in small maniraptorans, with powered flight evolving in avialans (and perhaps independently in some dromaeosaurids and scansoriopterygids with membranous wings). Feathered dinosaurs are now a staple of modern palaeoart, transforming the popular image of dinosaurs from scaly reptiles to active, often brightly plumaged animals.
