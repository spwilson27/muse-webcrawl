Kamehameha I

Kamehameha I (c. 1758 – 8 May 1819), also known as Kamehameha the Great, was the founder and first ruler of the Kingdom of Hawaii. Through conquest and diplomacy he unified the Hawaiian Islands — previously divided among warring chiefdoms — into a single kingdom by 1810.

Rise to power

Born at Kohala on Hawaiʻi island, Kamehameha was a nephew of Kalaniʻōpuʻu, the island's ruler, and distinguished himself in the wars following the king's death in 1782, notably at the Battle of Mokuʻōhai. He built a fleet armed with Western cannon and muskets advised by the seamen John Young and Isaac Davis. In 1790 he invaded Maui, and in 1795 his army defeated Kahekili's successor Kalanikūpule at the Battle of Nuʻuanu on Oʻahu, where hundreds of warriors were driven over the Nuʻuanu Pali cliff. Kauaʻi and Niʻihau joined peacefully by agreement with Kaumualiʻi in 1810.

Reign

Kamehameha established his capital on Oʻahu, promulgated the Kānāwai Māmalahoe — the "Law of the Splintered Paddle", guaranteeing the safety of non-combatants in war, a landmark of indigenous human-rights law — and channelled foreign trade, including the valuable sandalwood trade, through the crown. He maintained the traditional kapu religion while engaging steadily with European and American traders. He died in 1819 at Kailua-Kona and was succeeded by his son Liholiho (Kamehameha II); his burial place remains secret.

Legacy

Kamehameha is honoured throughout Hawaii, notably on 11 June, Kamehameha Day, with floral parades and draping of his statues in lei.
