Fugger

Jakob Fugger "the Rich" (1459–1525), portrait by Albrecht Dürer.

The House of Fugger is a German family that was a historically prominent group of European bankers, members of the fifteenth- and sixteenth-century patriciate of Augsburg, international mercantile bankers, and venture capitalists. Alongside the Welser family, the Fugger family controlled much of the European economy in the 16th century, accumulating enormous wealth. The Fuggers largely replaced the Medicis as the dominant bankers to the papacy and the Habsburgs.

Rise

The family began as weavers and textile merchants in Augsburg; Hans Fugger settled there in 1367. Under Jakob Fugger the Elder the firm moved into trading silk, spices and metals. His son Jakob Fugger "the Rich" (1459–1525) transformed the business into Europe's greatest finance house: he took control of Tyrolean silver and Hungarian copper mining, lent massively to the Habsburg emperors Maximilian I and Charles V, financed Charles's election as Holy Roman Emperor in 1519, and secured repayment through mining concessions and Spanish revenues. His fortune at death is estimated in the millions of gulden — by some measures comparable to the richest individuals of the modern era, adjusted for the size of the contemporary economy.

Height and decline

Jakob's nephew Anton Fugger (1493–1560) doubled the firm's assets again, to some 5 million gulden; the Fuggers financed the Habsburg wars, the Spanish crown and the Counter-Reformation. It was a Fugger-linked agent's sale of indulgences in Germany (to repay Albert of Mainz's loan for his archbishopric) that provoked Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses in 1517. Repeated Spanish state bankruptcies (from 1557 onwards) eroded the firm's capital; the family withdrew gradually from banking in the 17th century, though branches survived as landed nobility. The family still exists, with seats at Babenhausen and Kirchheim in Bavaria.

Legacy

Jakob Fugger founded the Fuggerei in Augsburg (1516–1521), the world's oldest social housing complex still in use, where needy Catholic citizens pay a nominal rent of one Rhenish guilder per year (about €0.88) plus daily prayers for the founder. The Fugger chapel in St. Anna's church is a masterpiece of the German Renaissance. The Fugger newsletter (Fuggerzeitungen), a network of commercial intelligence reports, is a precursor of modern journalism.
