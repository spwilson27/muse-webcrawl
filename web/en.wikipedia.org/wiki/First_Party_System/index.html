First Party System

The First Party System is the model of American politics used in history and political science to periodize the political party system that existed in the United States between roughly 1792 and 1824. It featured two national parties competing for control of the presidency, Congress and the states: the Federalist Party, created chiefly by Alexander Hamilton, and the rival Democratic-Republican Party, formed by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison.

Origins

The parties crystallised in the 1790s over Hamilton's financial programme — assumption of state debts, a national bank and pro-British trade policy — which Jeffersonians attacked as consolidating and aristocratic. Foreign policy deepened the split: the French Revolution, the Citizen Genêt affair, the XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War pushed Federalists toward Britain and Democratic-Republicans toward France, while the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 provoked the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions.

Course

The election of 1800 — the "Revolution of 1800" — brought Jefferson to the presidency and began two decades of Democratic-Republican dominance (Jefferson, Madison, Monroe). The Federalists, discredited by the Hartford Convention during the War of 1812, collapsed after 1815, leaving the one-party "Era of Good Feelings". Factional struggles within the Democratic-Republicans in the 1820s then produced the Second Party System of Democrats and Whigs.

Characteristics

Although the founding generation distrusted parties, the First Party System developed recognisably modern features: partisan newspapers, congressional caucuses, state organisations and high voter turnout driven by intense ideological conflict over the scope of federal power, finance and foreign policy.
