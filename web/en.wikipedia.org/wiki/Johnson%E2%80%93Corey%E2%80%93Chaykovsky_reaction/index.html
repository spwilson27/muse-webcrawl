The Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky reaction (also called the Corey–Chaykovsky reaction) is a chemical reaction used in organic synthesis for the synthesis of epoxides, aziridines, and cyclopropanes. It involves the reaction of a sulfonium or sulfoxonium ylide with a carbonyl compound, imine, or electron-deficient alkene.

Mechanism

In the epoxidation variant, a sulfur ylide — generated from a sulfonium salt and a strong base — adds to the carbon of a carbonyl group. The resulting betaine intermediate then closes to form the three-membered epoxide ring, expelling a sulfide. With α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds, the reaction instead gives cyclopropanes.

History

The reaction was originally described by A. William Johnson in 1961 and was developed independently by E. J. Corey and Michael Chaykovsky, who extended its scope and introduced the widely used dimethylsulfoxonium methylide reagent.
