Inner product space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The inner product generalises the dot product and defines angles between vectors.

In mathematics, an inner product space (or, rarely, pre-Hilbert space) is a real or complex vector space equipped with an operation called an inner product, which associates each pair of vectors with a scalar. The inner product generalises the Euclidean dot product and allows geometric notions such as length, distance, and angle to be defined in abstract vector spaces. A complete inner product space is called a Hilbert space.

Definition

An inner product on a vector space V over the field of real or complex numbers is a map ⟨·, ·⟩ : V × V → F satisfying, for all vectors x, y, z and scalars a:

  • Conjugate symmetry: ⟨x, y⟩ = conjugate of ⟨y, x⟩.
  • Linearity in the first argument: ⟨ax + y, z⟩ = a⟨x, z⟩ + ⟨y, z⟩ (in physics conventions, linearity is often placed in the second argument).
  • Positive-definiteness: ⟨x, x⟩ ≥ 0, with equality only for x = 0.

Norms and geometry

The inner product induces a norm ‖x‖ = √⟨x, x⟩, and hence a metric. Fundamental consequences include the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality |⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖, the triangle inequality, the parallelogram law, and the Pythagorean theorem for orthogonal vectors. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero. Every finite-dimensional inner product space admits an orthonormal basis, constructed by the Gram–Schmidt process.

Examples

The Euclidean space ℝn with the dot product is the prototypical example. The space of square-integrable functions L² has inner product given by the integral of the product of two functions (with conjugation in the complex case); Fourier series are expansions of functions in an orthonormal basis of such a space. Sequence spaces ℓ² and matrix spaces with the Frobenius inner product are further standard examples.

Applications

Inner product spaces underpin functional analysis, quantum mechanics (where states are vectors in a Hilbert space and observables are operators), signal processing and Fourier analysis, least-squares approximation, and machine learning, where kernel methods implicitly compute inner products in high-dimensional feature spaces.
