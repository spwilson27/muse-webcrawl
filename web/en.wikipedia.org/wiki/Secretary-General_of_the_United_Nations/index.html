Secretary-General of the United Nations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Secretary-General of the United Nations is the chief administrative officer of the United Nations and head of the UN Secretariat, described in the Charter's practice as the organisation's chief diplomat and global civil servant. Appointed by the General Assembly on the recommendation of the Security Council (where the permanent members' vetoes govern) for a five-year term, customarily renewable once, the Secretary-General is selected with attention to regional rotation — and, after a century of men, against a long-running campaign for the first woman to hold the post.

Role

The office blends functions its holders balance differently: administrator of a vast bureaucracy; political actor empowered by Article 99 to bring threats to peace to the Security Council's attention; mediator and appointor of envoys; and a pulpit ("the world's secular pope" is the flattery and the trap). The line runs from Trygve Lie (1946–52) through Dag Hammarskjöld (whose activist reading of the office and death in a 1961 plane crash during the Congo crisis remain its myth and mystery), U Thant, Waldheim, Pérez de Cuéllar, Boutros-Ghali (denied a second term by US veto), Kofi Annan (Iraq-war-era friction with Washington; Oil-for-Food scandal), Ban Ki-moon, and António Guterres (from 2017, reappointed 2021) — whose tenure through the Ukraine and Gaza wars has dramatised the office's dependence on the very great powers it must sometimes condemn.
