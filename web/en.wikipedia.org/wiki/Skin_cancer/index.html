Skin cancer

Skin cancers are cancers that arise from the skin. They result from the development of abnormal cells that have the ability to invade or spread to other parts of the body. The three main types are basal-cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC), and melanoma. BCC and SCC together are often called nonmelanoma skin cancer. Skin cancer is the most common form of cancer globally, accounting for at least 40% of cancer cases.

The primary cause is ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun and tanning devices. Risk is greatest in fair-skinned people, those with many moles, a history of sunburn, immunosuppression, or exposure to certain chemicals such as arsenic.

Types

Basal-cell carcinoma

BCC grows slowly, rarely metastasizes, and often appears as a painless raised, pearly area of skin with small blood vessels, or as a flat lesion. It is the most common skin cancer.

Squamous-cell carcinoma

SCC usually presents as a red, scaly patch or a growing lump; it can ulcerate and has a small but significant risk of metastasis, especially in immunosuppressed patients.

Melanoma

Melanoma arises from melanocytes. A changing mole — asymmetry, irregular border, varied color, diameter over 6 mm, evolution (the "ABCDE" signs) — is a warning sign. Though less common, melanoma causes the majority of skin-cancer deaths; immunotherapy and targeted drugs (BRAF/MEK inhibitors) have greatly improved survival in advanced disease.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention includes limiting UV exposure, sunscreen, and protective clothing. Most skin cancers are treated by surgical excision, including Mohs surgery for high-risk facial lesions; radiation, topical agents, and systemic therapy are used in selected cases. Early detection gives cure rates above 95% for most types.
