CQD

[Image placeholder: A ship's radio room of the Marconi era, c. 1910]

CQD, transmitted in Morse code as – · – ·   – – · –   – · ·, is one of the first distress signals adopted for radio use. Marconi radio operators used the "CQ" prefix, a general call meaning "calling all stations"; appending a "D" for "distress" produced the CQD signal. It was adopted by the Marconi Company on 7 January 1904 and remained in use until it was superseded by the SOS signal.

History

Land telegraph stations in the United Kingdom had long used "CQ" (from the French sécurité, or from the English "seek you") as a prefix for messages of general interest to all stations. When wireless telegraphy went to sea, Marconi operators carried the convention with them. In 1904 the Marconi International Marine Communication Company issued Circular 57, specifying that "ships in distress shall use the following signal: CQD, repeated at brief intervals."

Supersession by SOS

At the International Radiotelegraphic Convention in Berlin in 1906, the German Notzeichen proposal, the SOS signal (· · · – – – · · ·), was adopted as the international standard, largely because of its distinctive and unmistakable rhythm. British and Marconi operators were reluctant to abandon CQD, and it remained in partial use for several years. CQD was notably sent by RMS Republic in 1909, whose passengers were all rescued — the first large-scale demonstration of wireless saving lives at sea — and was also transmitted by RMS Titanic in 1912, interleaved with SOS.
