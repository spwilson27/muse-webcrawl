Displaced persons camps in post–World War II Europe

Displaced persons (DP) camps were temporary settlements established across Germany, Austria, and Italy after the Second World War to house the millions of people uprooted by the conflict — former forced labourers, prisoners of war, concentration camp survivors, and refugees fleeing Eastern Europe. At the war's end there were an estimated 11 million displaced persons in Europe.

Repatriation and the "last million"

Most DPs were repatriated within months of the war's end, but roughly a million people — including Jews who had survived the Holocaust, Balts, Poles, Ukrainians, and Yugoslavs unwilling to return to Soviet-dominated homelands — remained in camps for years. They were administered first by the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA) and from 1947 by the International Refugee Organization (IRO).

Life in the camps and resettlement

The camps developed rich internal lives, with schools, newspapers, theatres, political organisations, and sports clubs. Jewish DP camps in the American zone, such as Landsberg and Feldafing, became centres of Zionist organisation and of the She'erit ha-Pletah ("surviving remnant") community. Resettlement accelerated after the US Displaced Persons Act of 1948 and similar schemes in Britain, Canada, Australia, and the new state of Israel. Most camps closed by 1952; the last, Föhrenwald in Bavaria, closed in 1957.
