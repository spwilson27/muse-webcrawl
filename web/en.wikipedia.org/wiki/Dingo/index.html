Dingo

Scientific name: Canis lupus dingo (taxonomy debated)
Range: Australia
Height: 52–60 cm at the shoulder
Weight: 13–20 kg
Diet: Carnivore

The dingo (Canis familiaris, Canis familiaris dingo, Canis dingo, or Canis lupus dingo) is an ancient (basal) lineage of dog found in Australia. Its taxonomic classification is debated as indicated by the variety of scientific names presently applied in different publications. It is considered a feral dog, a semi-domesticated dog, or a subspecies of the wolf, depending on the authority.

Origins

Dingoes descend from domestic dogs brought to Australia by seafarers, probably from Southeast Asia, at least 3,500–5,000 years ago; the oldest dingo fossil dates to about 3,450 years ago. Once established, they spread across the continent and became Australia's largest terrestrial predator. Dingoes hold deep cultural significance for Aboriginal Australians, featuring in Dreaming stories, and were kept as companions and hunting aids in some groups.

Characteristics and behaviour

Dingoes stand 52–60 cm at the shoulder and weigh 13–20 kg, with a typically ginger coat (though black, tan, and white variants occur), erect ears, and a bushy tail. Unlike domestic dogs they breed only once a year, howl rather than bark extensively, and have distinctive skull and wrist features. They live in stable family packs with defined territories, hunting kangaroos, wallabies, rabbits, and livestock, and also scavenge. By suppressing foxes and feral cats, dingoes are thought to benefit small native mammals — an ecological role argued to deserve protection.

Conflict and management

Dingoes prey on sheep and calves, prompting the construction of the Dingo Fence — over 5,600 km long, among the longest structures in the world — and widespread poison-baiting and shooting, even as the dingo is protected in some jurisdictions. Hybridisation with domestic dogs threatens the genetic distinctness of wild populations. Public attention to dingoes was heightened by the 1980 disappearance of Azaria Chamberlain at Uluru, in which her mother Lindy Chamberlain was wrongfully convicted before a dingo-attack account was accepted.
