Joseph Harold Greenberg (28 May 1915 – 7 May 2001) was an American linguist, principally known for his work in linguistic typology and the genetic classification of languages. His classifications of the languages of Africa — grouping them into four families — remain broadly accepted, and his work on language universals with Charles Ferguson and others founded modern typology. His later proposal uniting most indigenous languages of the Americas into an "Amerind" family proved far more controversial.

Early life and education

Greenberg was born in Brooklyn, New York, and studied anthropology and linguistics at Columbia University under Franz Boas and Ruth Benedict, completing his doctorate at Northwestern University in 1940 with a study of Hausa. Fieldwork on the Jos Plateau of Nigeria in the 1940s grounded his African classification.

Classification of African languages

In a series of studies published from 1949, culminating in The Languages of Africa (1963), Greenberg classified the continent's roughly 1,500 languages into four stocks: Niger–Congo, Nilo-Saharan, Afroasiatic and Khoisan. Using his method of "mass comparison" — inspecting basic vocabulary across many languages at once — he replaced a tangle of racially inflected schemes with a durable framework that subsequent scholarship has largely retained.

Language universals

Greenberg's 1963 paper "Some Universals of Grammar with Particular Reference to the Order of Meaningful Elements" set out 45 proposed universals of word order and morphology, launching the modern study of linguistic typology. He later worked on universals in numeral systems, kinship terms and phonology.

Amerind and later work

In Language in the Americas (1987) Greenberg proposed that nearly all Native American languages belong to a single Amerind family, distinct from Na-Dene and Eskimo–Aleut, and proposed similar macro-families for Eurasia. Most historical linguists rejected these groupings as unsupported by the comparative method, and they remain a subject of sharp debate, though his African and typological work is unchallenged in influence.
