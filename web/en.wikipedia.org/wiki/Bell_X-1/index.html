The Bell X-1 (originally designated XS-1) was a rocket engine–powered aircraft, a joint National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA)–U.S. Army/USAF supersonic research project built by Bell Aircraft. Conceived during 1944 and designed and built in 1945, it achieved a speed of nearly 1,000 miles per hour (1,600 km/h) in 1948.

Breaking the sound barrier

On 14 October 1947, the first XS-1, serial 46-062 — nicknamed Glamorous Glennis by its pilot, Captain Charles "Chuck" Yeager, in honour of his wife — became the first crewed airplane to exceed the speed of sound in level flight. Dropped from the bomb bay of a modified B-29 bomber at about 45,000 feet, Yeager fired the Reaction Motors XLR11 rocket engine and reached Mach 1.06 (about 700 mph). Yeager flew with two broken ribs, injured falling from a horse two nights earlier, a fact he concealed from his superiors.

Design

The aircraft's fuselage was shaped like a .50-caliber machine-gun bullet, a form known to be stable at supersonic speeds. It used a very thin, straight wing and an adjustable-incidence horizontal tail, which proved crucial: early flights had suffered loss of elevator effectiveness near Mach 1 due to shock-wave formation, and trimming the whole stabilizer restored control. The X-1 was painted bright orange for visibility.

Legacy

The X-1 program demonstrated that the "sound barrier" was a solvable engineering problem and launched the X-plane research series. The original Glamorous Glennis is displayed at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C. Later variants (X-1A through X-1E) explored higher Mach numbers and contributed data on inertial coupling and transonic aerodynamics.
