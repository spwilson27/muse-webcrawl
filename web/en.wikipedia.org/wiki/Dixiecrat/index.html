Dixiecrat

The States' Rights Democratic Party, whose members were often called Dixiecrats, was a short-lived segregationist political party in the United States, active primarily in the South. It arose in 1948 due to a Southern regional split within the Democratic Party over civil rights. The term "Dixiecrat" is a portmanteau of "Dixie", the nickname for the Southern United States, and the ending of "Democrat".

Strom Thurmond, the Dixiecrat presidential nominee in 1948.

1948 election

After President Harry S. Truman proposed civil rights legislation and issued executive orders desegregating the armed forces, dissatisfied Southern delegates walked out of the 1948 Democratic National Convention and formed their own party. Meeting in Birmingham, Alabama, they nominated South Carolina governor Strom Thurmond for president and Mississippi governor Fielding L. Wright for vice president.

The Thurmond–Wright ticket carried four states — South Carolina, Mississippi, Alabama, and Louisiana — winning 39 electoral votes and about 2.4 percent of the national popular vote. Despite the split, Truman won the election.

Legacy

The party dissolved after the 1948 election, but the Dixiecrat revolt foreshadowed the later realignment of Southern politics, as white Southern voters gradually shifted from the Democratic Party to the Republican Party in the following decades.
