Folsom point

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Folsom points are stone projectile points made by the Paleo-Indian Folsom tradition of North America, dating to roughly 10,900–10,200 years ago. They are distinguished by a long, shallow groove — a flute — running most of the length of each face, among the most technically demanding stone-tool forms ever produced.

Discovery

The type was named after Folsom, New Mexico, where in 1926–1927 archaeologists excavating at Wild Horse Arroyo found a fluted point embedded among the ribs of an extinct giant bison (Bison antiquus). Cowboy George McJunkin had first noticed the bones after a flood in 1908. The Folsom find, verified in place by visiting scientists, was decisive evidence that humans had lived in the Americas during the late Ice Age, thousands of years earlier than most archaeologists then believed.

Manufacture and form

Folsom points are typically lanceolate, 3–8 cm long, of fine chert or similar stone. After shaping a preform, the knapper removed a long channel flake from each face using a prepared striking platform — a high-risk step that often snapped the point, so sites are full of broken "preforms" and fluting failures. The finished point was hafted onto a spear or dart, probably thrown with an atlatl. The function of the flute is debated: it may have lightened the point, improved hafting, or helped it seat in a split foreshaft.

Distribution and lifeway

Folsom sites spread across the Great Plains and Rocky Mountain West, from Canada to Mexico. Their makers were highly mobile hunters who specialised in bison, including large communal kills such as the Lindenmeier site in Colorado, one of the largest Folsom camps known.
