Researchers study links between heavy digital media use, displaced sleep and adolescent well-being.

The relationships between digital media use and mental health have been investigated by various researchers — predominantly psychologists, sociologists, anthropologists and medical experts — especially since the mid-1990s, after the growth of the World Wide Web and the advent of smartphones and social media. A significant body of research has explored "overuse" phenomena, commonly known as digital addictions, and their effects on psychological development and well-being.

Findings and debate

Studies frequently report small but statistically significant associations between heavy social media use or screen time and symptoms of depression and anxiety, particularly in adolescents, alongside more consistent findings that late-night device use displaces and degrades sleep. However, the field is marked by debate over effect sizes and causality: large-scale analyses by researchers such as Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski have found that digital technology use explains only a tiny fraction of variation in adolescent well-being, while others, such as Jean Twenge and Jonathan Haidt, argue that smartphone-based childhood has driven the sharp rise in teen mental-health problems seen since the early 2010s. Reverse causation — distressed people using media more — and self-report measurement problems complicate interpretation.

Problematic use and policy

The World Health Organization included gaming disorder in ICD-11, and "internet addiction" remains a debated proposed diagnosis. Some clinical and educational guidance recommends balanced, supervised use rather than blanket bans. Governments have responded with measures ranging from school phone restrictions to age-verification laws for social media, even as researchers caution that the evidence base is still developing.
