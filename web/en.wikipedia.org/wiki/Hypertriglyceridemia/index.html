Hypertriglyceridemia

SpecialtyEndocrinology, cardiology
DefinitionElevated blood triglycerides, typically fasting level above 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L)
RisksAtherosclerosis, acute pancreatitis (at very high levels)

Hypertriglyceridemia is the presence of high amounts of triglycerides in the blood. Triglycerides are the most abundant fat molecules in the body; elevated levels are associated with atherosclerosis and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, and very high levels (above roughly 500–1000 mg/dL) can precipitate acute pancreatitis.

Causes

Most cases are multifactorial, arising from genetic predisposition combined with lifestyle and secondary factors, including:

  • Obesity, metabolic syndrome, and poorly controlled type 2 diabetes.
  • Excess alcohol consumption and diets high in refined carbohydrates.
  • Hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and nephrotic syndrome.
  • Medications such as corticosteroids, some beta blockers, thiazides, estrogens, and certain antiretrovirals.

Rare monogenic disorders, such as familial chylomicronemia syndrome caused by lipoprotein lipase deficiency, produce extremely high triglyceride levels from childhood.

Signs and symptoms

Moderate hypertriglyceridemia is usually asymptomatic and is detected on a lipid panel. Severe elevations may cause eruptive xanthomas, lipemia retinalis, hepatosplenomegaly, and recurrent abdominal pain from pancreatitis.

Treatment

Management begins with lifestyle measures — weight loss, reduced alcohol and sugar intake, aerobic exercise, and improved glycemic control. For very high levels or elevated cardiovascular risk, medications are used: fibrates, prescription omega-3 fatty acids, niacin, and statins (which primarily lower LDL cholesterol but also reduce triglycerides moderately). Newer agents targeting apolipoprotein C-III and ANGPTL3 have been developed for severe genetic forms.
