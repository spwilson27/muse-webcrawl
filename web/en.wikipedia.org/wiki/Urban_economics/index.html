Urban economics

The bid-rent curve: land values fall with distance from the centre

Urban economics is broadly the economic study of urban areas, using the tools of economics — especially microeconomics — to analyse urban issues such as land use, housing, transportation, local public finance, crime, and education. At its core it studies the location choices of households and firms and the spatial structure of cities: why cities exist, why they take the shapes they do, and how land is allocated between competing uses.

Foundations

The field builds on the location theory of Johann Heinrich von Thünen (1826) and the monocentric city model developed by William Alonso, Richard Muth, and Edwin Mills in the 1960s. In that model, households trade off commuting cost against land cost, producing a downward-sloping bid-rent curve: land values and densities decline with distance from the central business district. Modern research relaxes the monocentric assumption to allow multiple employment centres and complex transport networks.

Key topics

  • Agglomeration economies: the productivity and innovation benefits firms and workers gain from clustering — knowledge spillovers, labour-market pooling, and shared inputs — which explain why cities exist and why wages and rents are higher in large cities (the "urban wage premium" and "superstar cities").
  • Housing markets and land use: filtering of housing stock, zoning and land-use regulation, gentrification, and the determinants of house prices and rents.
  • Transportation: congestion pricing, the economics of transit, and induced demand (the Downs–Thomson paradox and the fundamental law of road congestion).
  • Local public finance: property taxation, the Tiebout model of households "voting with their feet" among jurisdictions, and the financing of schools and services.
  • Urban problems: poverty concentration, segregation, crime, and homelessness, and policies addressing them.

Relation to other fields

Urban economics overlaps regional science, economic geography, and real estate economics. The "new economic geography" of Paul Krugman and others formalized how increasing returns and transport costs generate core–periphery patterns at regional and international scales.
