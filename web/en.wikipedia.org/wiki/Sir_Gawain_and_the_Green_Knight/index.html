Sir Gawain and the Green Knight

Sir Gawain and the Green Knight is a late-14th-century chivalric romance in Middle English alliterative verse. It is one of the best-known Arthurian stories, in which Sir Gawain, a knight of King Arthur's Round Table, accepts a challenge from the mysterious Green Knight, who allows himself to be beheaded on condition that Gawain seek him out a year and a day later to receive a return blow. The poem survives in a single manuscript, Cotton Nero A.x, alongside three religious poems — Pearl, Purity (or Cleanness) and Patience — all believed to be by the same anonymous author, known as the "Pearl Poet" or "Gawain Poet". Written in the North West Midlands dialect, the poem consists of 2,530 lines arranged in stanzas of alliterative long lines concluding with a rhyming "bob and wheel".

Plot

At a New Year feast at Camelot, a huge green knight rides in and proposes a "beheading game": any knight may strike him with his axe if, in a year and a day, he will submit to a return blow. Gawain accepts and beheads him, whereupon the Green Knight picks up his head and reminds Gawain of the appointment at the Green Chapel. As the deadline approaches, Gawain travels north through a wintry landscape to a castle where he is entertained by Lord Bertilak and his wife. Bertilak proposes an exchange-of-winnings bargain: each day he will give Gawain what he catches hunting, in return for whatever Gawain gains in the castle. On three mornings the lady visits Gawain's chamber and tempts him; he resists all but accepts a green girdle she says will protect him from death — and conceals it from Bertilak, breaking the bargain. At the Green Chapel, the Green Knight feints twice and on the third stroke nicks Gawain's neck, then reveals himself as Bertilak, transformed by the sorceress Morgan le Fay to test Arthur's court. Gawain returns to Camelot wearing the girdle as a badge of shame, but the court adopts it as an emblem of honour.

Themes

The poem explores the conflict between the codes of chivalry and Christianity, testing Gawain's virtues of courtesy, honesty and fidelity (the "pentangle" virtues blazoned on his shield). Its intricate structure interweaves three hunts with three bedroom temptations, and critics have debated the girdle's meaning — as a sign of failure, of survival instinct, or of the limits of human perfection — as well as the poem's treatments of nature versus civilisation, games, and confession.

Manuscript and authorship

The unique manuscript, held by the British Library, also contains Pearl, Cleanness and Patience and is illustrated with twelve miniatures. The anonymous poet is generally placed in Cheshire or Staffordshire in the later 14th century, a contemporary of Chaucer and a leading figure of the so-called Alliterative Revival. The poem was first published in 1839 and has been translated by, among others, J. R. R. Tolkien and Simon Armitage.

Adaptations

The poem has inspired operas (notably Harrison Birtwistle's Gawain, 1991), novels, and films, including David Lowery's The Green Knight (2021) starring Dev Patel.
