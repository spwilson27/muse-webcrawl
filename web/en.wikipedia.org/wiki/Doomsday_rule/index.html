Doomsday rule

Doomsday rule
John Horton Conway (1937–2020)
Devised byJohn Horton Conway, 1973
PurposeMental calculation of the day of the week for any date

The Doomsday rule, also known as the Doomsday algorithm, is an algorithm of determination of the day of the week for a given date. It provides a perpetual calendar because the Gregorian calendar moves in cycles of 400 years. The algorithm for mental computation was devised by John Conway in 1973, drawing inspiration from Lewis Carroll's perpetual calendar algorithm. It takes advantage of the fact that each year has a certain day of the week, called the doomsday, upon which certain easy-to-remember dates all fall.

How it works

In any year, the dates 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, and 12/12 all fall on the year's doomsday, as do 5/9 and 9/5, 7/11 and 11/7, the last day of February, and (in the usual mnemonics) 3/14 (Pi Day). To find the doomsday for a year, one combines a century anchor day — for the 2000s, Tuesday — with an offset computed from the last two digits of the year (dividing by 12, taking the remainder, adding the number of leap years, and so on in Conway's "odd + 11" or dozenal methods). Any other date's weekday then follows by counting from the nearest memorised doomsday date.

Example

The doomsday for 2024 is Thursday: 4 April, 6 June and 12 December 2024 were all Thursdays, as was 29 February. From these anchors, the weekday of any other date in 2024 can be computed mentally in seconds.

History

Conway reportedly could compute the weekday for any date in under two seconds and devised the algorithm after Lewis Carroll published a similar method in Nature in 1887. The rule is popular among mental calculators and calendar enthusiasts, and Conway used it as a demonstration exercise with his students, rewarding those who mastered it.
