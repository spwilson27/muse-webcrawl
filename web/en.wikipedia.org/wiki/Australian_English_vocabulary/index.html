Australian English vocabulary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Australian English is a major variety of the English language spoken in Australia, with a distinctive vocabulary drawing on British and Irish English, on Aboriginal languages, and on the country's gold-rush, convict and pastoral history. The Macquarie Dictionary, first published in 1981, is the standard reference for Australian spelling and usage.

Origins

Early Australian English incorporated words from the convict "flash language" and from Aboriginal languages, particularly for flora, fauna and place names: kangaroo, koala, wombat, billabong, boomerang and didgeridoo entered general English from languages including Guugu Yimithirr and Dharug. The gold rushes of the 1850s produced digger and influenced mate, while pastoral life gave outback, bush, swagman and jumbuck.

Characteristic features

Australian vocabulary is famous for diminutives and abbreviations formed with -o and -ie suffixes: arvo (afternoon), servo (service station), bottle-o, barbie (barbecue), Aussie, mozzie (mosquito) and brekkie (breakfast). Everyday terms that differ from other varieties include thongs (flip-flops), esky (cooler), ute (pickup truck), footy (football), fair dinkum (genuine) and no worries. Spelling follows British conventions (colour, organise) with some exceptions such as the Australian Labor Party's American-style spelling.

Rhyming slang and idiom

Rhyming slang, inherited from Cockney usage, survives in expressions such as septic tank for "Yank". Military service in two world wars enriched the lexicon — digger, furphy (rumour) and Anzac itself. Modern usage continues to absorb American media vocabulary while retaining a recognisable local flavour documented in works such as the Australian National Dictionary.
