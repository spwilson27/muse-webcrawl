Population ethics

Population ethics is the philosophical study of the ethics of creating, or failing to create, new people, and of how to evaluate states of affairs that involve different numbers of people or different people existing. It is a central topic in moral philosophy because many of our choices — about reproduction, climate policy, public health, and resource use — affect not only how well people's lives go but also who, and how many people, will exist. The field was given its modern shape largely by Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984).

The nonidentity problem

The nonidentity problem arises because policies that seem harmful — for example, depleting resources — often change the timing and identity of future people, so that the people who eventually exist would not have existed at all under a different policy. If those people's lives are still worth living, it is hard to say they were harmed by the policy, even though the outcome seems worse. Parfit argued this pushes ethics toward impersonal principles that compare outcomes by their overall value rather than by harms to specific individuals.

Total and average views

Two leading families of views evaluate populations by total or average welfare. The total view holds that the value of an outcome increases with the sum of welfare across all people. It implies Parfit's repugnant conclusion: for any population of people with very high welfare, there is a better population consisting of a vastly larger number of people whose lives are only barely worth living. The average view instead ranks outcomes by average welfare, but it implies difficulties of its own — for example, that adding happy people can make an outcome worse if they are less happy than average, and that a tiny population of ecstatic people could be best of all.

Other approaches

Numerous alternatives have been developed to navigate these paradoxes: critical-level utilitarianism, which counts added lives only above a positive threshold; person-affecting views, which restrict moral claims to people who exist or will exist independently of the choice; variable-value views, on which welfare's marginal value declines as population grows; and prioritarian and sufficientarian variants. Formal work has shown that no population axiology satisfies all of a set of initially plausible adequacy conditions, a result sometimes called the impossibility theorems of population ethics. The field bears directly on debates about procreative ethics, extinction risk, longtermism, and the ethics of climate change.
