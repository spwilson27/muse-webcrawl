Ejection seat

An ejection seat or ejector seat is a system designed to rescue the pilot or other crew of an aircraft (usually military) in an emergency. In most designs, the seat is propelled out of the aircraft by an explosive charge or rocket motor, carrying the pilot with it. The concept of an ejectable escape capsule has also been tried.

Once clear of the aircraft, the ejection seat deploys a parachute and forces the pilot's legs to separate, facilitating a safe landing. Ejection seats are common on military aircraft.

History

In 1910, the first recorded use of a parachute from an airplane took place. Early aircraft used simple escape methods, but as speeds increased, safely bailing out became difficult. The first ejection seats were developed independently during World War II. In 1939, the Heinkel He 176 and He 178 used compressed air systems, and the He 280 prototype featured an ejection seat operated by compressed air. The first pilot to use an ejection seat in an emergency was Helmut Schenk in the He 280 on 13 January 1942 after its control surfaces iced up.

Swedish manufacturer Saab developed the first production ejection seat for the Saab 21, first tested in 1941 and used operationally from 1945. The British company Martin-Baker became a leading manufacturer; its first live test was performed by Bernard Lynch on 24 July 1946 from a Gloster Meteor.

Modern systems

Modern seats such as the ACES II (used in many United States Air Force aircraft) and Martin-Baker seats can operate at zero altitude and zero airspeed ("zero-zero" capability), allowing escape from an aircraft on the ground. The seat is fired by an explosive cartridge, rocket motors lift it clear, drogue parachutes stabilize it, and the main parachute deploys automatically based on altitude and airspeed sensors.

The Soviet/Russian K-36 seat, used in aircraft such as the Su-27 and MiG-29, is widely regarded as one of the most capable ejection seats.

Safety

The chance of surviving an ejection is roughly 80-90%, depending on circumstances. Ejection can cause serious injuries, particularly spinal compression from the acceleration forces, and pilots are typically limited in how many ejections they can safely undergo during their career.
