The Purkinje fibers, or Purkinje fibres (pronounced /pʊərˈkɪndʒiː/), are located in the inner ventricular walls of the heart, just beneath the endocardium in a space called the subendocardium. They are specialised cardiomyocytes forming the terminal part of the heart's electrical conduction system, larger than ordinary heart muscle cells with comparatively few myofibrils and many mitochondria.

Function

Purkinje fibers receive the action potential from the bundle of His via the right and left bundle branches and conduct it very rapidly — around 2–4 m/s, much faster than ordinary myocardium — throughout the ventricular walls. This near-simultaneous delivery causes the ventricular muscle to contract in a coordinated wave from the apex upward, efficiently ejecting blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery with each heartbeat.

History and clinical significance

The fibers are named for the Czech physiologist Jan Evangelista Purkyně, who described them in 1839. Purkinje cells can act as subsidiary pacemakers at 20–40 beats per minute if higher pacemakers fail, and abnormal automaticity or conduction in the network can contribute to ventricular arrhythmias.
