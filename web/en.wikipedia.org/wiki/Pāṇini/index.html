Pāṇini (Sanskrit: पाणिनि) was an ancient Indian grammarian and linguist, traditionally dated to between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE, probably living in Śalātura in Gandhara, in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent. His Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters") is the earliest surviving comprehensive grammar of Sanskrit and one of the most remarkable intellectual achievements of the ancient world.

The Aṣṭādhyāyī

The work consists of about 3,959 terse rules (sūtras) arranged in eight chapters of four sections each. Using an ingenious metalanguage—marker sounds (anubandhas), abbreviations formed by the Śiva-sūtras, and rules about the rules themselves—Pāṇini constructed a complete generative system describing Sanskrit phonology, morphology, and syntax. The grammar generates correct forms by ordered application of rules and is routinely compared by modern linguists to a formal system or even a computer program.

Influence

Pāṇini's grammar fixed Classical Sanskrit and anchored the Indian grammatical tradition for over two millennia, generating great commentaries by Kātyāyana and Patañjali. Rediscovered by European scholars in the 19th century, it helped found modern comparative linguistics; Franz Bopp, Ferdinand de Saussure, and Leonard Bloomfield all acknowledged its influence, and its rule formalism anticipated aspects of Backus–Naur form used to describe programming languages.
