Pre-ignition

Pre-ignition (or preignition) in a spark-ignition internal combustion engine is a technically different phenomenon from engine knocking (detonation). It occurs when the air-fuel mixture in a cylinder ignites before the spark plug fires, typically because of a localised hot spot in the combustion chamber — such as an overheated spark plug electrode, a glowing carbon deposit, or a sharp edge — rather than by the timed spark.

Mechanism and effects

Because ignition begins too early, pressure rises while the piston is still ascending on the compression stroke, so the engine works against its own combustion pressure. The result is a loss of power, roughness, and rapidly rising component temperatures, which can make the hot spot hotter still and produce runaway pre-ignition severe enough to melt or hole a piston in minutes. Pre-ignition can also trigger detonation, and detonation can in turn create the hot spots that cause pre-ignition, which is why the two are often confused.

Causes and prevention

Common causes include spark plugs of too hot a heat range, excessive carbon deposits, overheated exhaust valves, lean mixtures, and overly advanced ignition timing. Modern engines use knock sensors and electronic control to retard timing and enrich the mixture when abnormal combustion is detected, and low-speed pre-ignition (LSPI) in turbocharged direct-injection engines has prompted the development of new engine-oil specifications designed to suppress it.
