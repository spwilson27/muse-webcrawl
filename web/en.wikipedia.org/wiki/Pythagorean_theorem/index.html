Pythagorean theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A right triangle with squares drawn on each of its three sides, the two smaller shaded like the largest]

In mathematics, the Pythagorean theorem states that the area of the square whose side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides: a² + b² = c². The theorem is named after the Greek mathematician Pythagoras (traditionally credited, c. 570-495 BC), though knowledge of the relationship is older — Babylonian, Indian and Chinese sources all show familiarity with it.

Famous integer solutions include the triple (3, 4, 5). The theorem is fundamental to geometry, trigonometry and distance measurement, and hundreds of distinct proofs of it are known — more than for any other theorem.

Uses

Surveying, construction, navigation and computer graphics all rely on it whenever right triangles and distances are involved; in coordinates it becomes the distance formula.

Generalisations

The law of cosines extends the result to non-right triangles, and the idea generalises to inner-product spaces and higher dimensions.
