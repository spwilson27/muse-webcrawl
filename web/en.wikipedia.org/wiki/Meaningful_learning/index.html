Meaningful learning

Meaningful learning is the concept, developed by educational psychologist David Ausubel, that learned knowledge (such as a fact) becomes fully understood when it can be related to prior knowledge held by the learner. In contrast to rote learning—memorization of isolated facts—meaningful learning anchors new information to existing cognitive structure, making it retained longer and transferable to new problems.

Ausubel's theory

In his influential works, including The Psychology of Meaningful Verbal Learning (1963) and Educational Psychology: A Cognitive View (1968), Ausubel argued that "the most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows. Ascertain this and teach him accordingly." Meaningful learning occurs when three conditions are met:

  1. The material itself is potentially meaningful—logically organized and relatable to human knowledge.
  2. The learner possesses relevant prior knowledge ("anchoring ideas") to which the new material can be connected.
  3. The learner actively chooses to integrate the new material into that structure.

Advance organizers

Ausubel proposed advance organizers—introductory material presented before the main lesson at a higher level of abstraction—to bridge what learners know and what they need to know. He also described learning as proceeding through progressive differentiation (ideas becoming more detailed) and integrative reconciliation (connections among ideas becoming explicit).

Concept mapping

Joseph D. Novak, a student of Ausubel's ideas at Cornell, developed concept mapping in the 1970s as a way to represent and assess learners' knowledge structures, making meaningful learning visible and measurable. Novak's later work extended Ausubel's theory into a broader theory of education integrating thinking, feeling, and acting.

Distinctions

Ausubel insisted that reception learning (being presented with material) can be fully meaningful, and that discovery learning can be rote; the meaningful/rote dimension is independent of the reception/discovery dimension—a corrective to assumptions that hands-on discovery is always superior.
