Scurvy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scurvy is a disease resulting from a deficiency of vitamin C (ascorbic acid), required for collagen synthesis. Its signs follow collagen failure: swollen, bleeding gums and tooth loss; wounds that fail to heal and old scars reopening; bleeding into skin, joints and muscles; anaemia, exhaustion and, untreated, death. Humans, unlike most mammals, cannot synthesise vitamin C — a shared primate mutation in the GULO gene — and depend entirely on diet.

Scurvy haunted long sea voyages for centuries, killing more sailors than storms and battles combined on some expeditions (of da Gama's and Magellan's crews, large fractions died). James Lind's 1753 shipboard trial — among the first controlled clinical trials — showed citrus fruit cured it; the Royal Navy's eventual adoption of juice rations ('limeys') preceded, by decades, the 1932 identification of vitamin C itself.

Modern occurrence

Scurvy persists wherever diets are extremely restricted — severe poverty, food insecurity, restrictive eating disorders, and infants fed only boiled milk historically. It presents within months of a vitamin-C-free diet; recovery on repletion is rapid and dramatic, among the most satisfying reversals in medicine.

Science

Lind's Treatise of the Scurvy (1753) and the slow Admiralty response are the classic case study in evidence-versus-practice delay. The antiscorbutic factor's isolation by Szent-Györgyi and Haworth (1932) closed the story biochemically; ascorbic acid means 'no-scurvy acid'.
