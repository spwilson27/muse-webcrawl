Ichetucknee River

The Ichetucknee River is a spring-fed river in north-central Florida, United States. Only about 6 miles (9.7 km) long, it flows from Ichetucknee Springs through Ichetucknee Springs State Park before joining the Santa Fe River. Fed by a group of first-magnitude springs, the river is renowned for its exceptionally clear water, constant temperature of about 72 °F (22 °C) year-round, and its popularity for tubing, swimming, kayaking, and wildlife viewing.

Description

Nine named springs, including the Ichetucknee Headspring and Blue Hole Spring, discharge a combined average of roughly 230 million gallons of water per day into the river. The name "Ichetucknee" is generally said to derive from a Creek (Muscogee) word meaning "pond of the beaver." The river runs through hardwood hammock and floodplain forest within the state park, and its clear water supports lush beds of submerged vegetation, turtles, otters, manatees in winter, wading birds, and many fish species.

Human history

Indigenous peoples lived along the river for thousands of years; a Spanish mission, San Martín de Timucua, stood near the springs in the seventeenth century. In the early twentieth century the area was mined for phosphate and logged for cypress and longleaf pine. The State of Florida purchased the springs and surrounding land in 1970 and opened Ichetucknee Springs State Park in 1972. The river and springs were designated a National Natural Landmark in 1972.

Recreation

Tubing the Ichetucknee is a signature Florida summer activity: visitors float the river's run from the mid-point or south entrance launches to downstream take-outs, with tram service in season. To protect the river's ecology, tubing is seasonal on the upper run, visitor numbers are limited, and food, drink, and disposable items are prohibited on the river. Scuba divers explore Blue Hole Spring's cavern system, which requires cave-diving certification.

Conservation

Like many Florida springs, the Ichetucknee has experienced declining flows and rising nitrate levels from groundwater withdrawals and fertilizer use in its springshed, prompting state-funded restoration and water-quality monitoring programs.
