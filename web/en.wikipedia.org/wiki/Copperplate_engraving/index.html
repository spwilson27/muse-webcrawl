Copperplate engraving

Copperplate engraving is an intaglio printmaking technique in which an image is incised into the surface of a copper plate with a sharp tool called a burin. The plate is inked, the surface wiped clean so that ink remains only in the incised lines, and dampened paper is pressed against the plate in a rolling press to draw out the image.

Technique

The engraver pushes the burin through the copper, throwing up a clean furrow; the characteristic swelling and tapering line results from varying pressure and rotating the plate. Tones are built up with hatching and cross-hatching. Copper is soft enough to cut precisely but wears under the press, limiting editions unless the plate is steel-faced.

History

Engraving developed from goldsmiths' practice of decorating metalwork; the earliest dated engravings come from the 1440s. Masters such as Martin Schongauer and Albrecht Dürer raised the medium to independent art, and reproductive engraving spread images of paintings across Europe for centuries. It was also the standard technique for map-making and book illustration before photomechanical processes took over in the 19th century.

Script

The term "copperplate" also names a style of formal handwriting taught from engraved copybooks, characterized by shaded, looped letterforms written with a flexible pointed pen.
