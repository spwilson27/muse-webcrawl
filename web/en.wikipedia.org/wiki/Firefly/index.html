Firefly

Fireflies
Family: Lampyridae
Order: Coleoptera (beetles)
Species: more than 2,000 described
Distribution: worldwide except Antarctica; most diverse in the tropics

Fireflies are winged beetles in the family Lampyridae, commonly called lightning bugs in North America. They are known for their conspicuous use of bioluminescence during twilight to attract mates or prey. More than 2,000 species are known, mostly in temperate and tropical regions. Despite the name, fireflies are beetles, not flies, and not all species glow as adults — some are active by day and rely on pheromones instead.

Light production

Firefly light is generated in special organs (lanterns) on the underside of the abdomen. The enzyme luciferase acts on the substrate luciferin in the presence of oxygen, ATP, and magnesium, producing oxyluciferin and light with very little heat — a "cold light" that can approach 100% efficiency. The colour ranges from yellow-green to pale red depending on species. Flashing is controlled by regulating oxygen supply to the lantern cells. Each species has a characteristic flash pattern — a code of pulse duration, interval, and flight path — by which males advertise and females reply. In some Southeast Asian species (Pteroptyx), thousands of males on riverside trees flash in near-perfect synchrony. Females of the genus Photuris mimic the replies of other species to attract and eat their males, acquiring defensive chemicals from the prey.

Life cycle

Fireflies undergo complete metamorphosis. Eggs are laid in soil or leaf litter; the larvae ("glowworms") live for one to two years as predators of snails, slugs, and earthworms, injecting digestive fluids into their prey. Many larvae and even eggs also glow. After pupation, adults live only a few weeks, feeding little or not at all, and devote their energy to mating.

Defence and decline

Fireflies are distasteful to many predators because of defensive steroids called lucibufagins, and their light likely also serves as a warning signal. Populations of many species are declining owing to habitat loss (wetlands, meadows, and rotting wood for larvae), light pollution that interferes with flash communication, and pesticide use; several species are assessed as endangered. Firefly watching is a popular ecotourism activity in Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, and the Great Smoky Mountains of the United States.
