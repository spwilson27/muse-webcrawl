The Chilean flamingo (Phoenicopterus chilensis) is a species of large flamingo native to South America. Closely related to the American flamingo and the greater flamingo, it was formerly often considered a subspecies of the latter. It breeds in shallow saline and alkaline lakes and lagoons across temperate South America, despite its name occurring widely beyond Chile.

Description

The Chilean flamingo stands about 110–130 cm (43–51 in) tall and weighs roughly 2.5 kg. Its plumage is pale pink, deeper pink on the wing coverts with black flight feathers. It is distinguished from other flamingos by its greyish legs with conspicuous pink "knees" (actually ankle joints) and pink feet, and by a bill that is more than half black, with a whitish base. Juveniles are greyish-white and acquire adult colouration — derived from carotenoids in their food — over several years.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from Peru and Brazil south through Bolivia, Paraguay, Chile, and Uruguay to Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego in Argentina. It inhabits salt lakes, estuaries, and coastal lagoons from sea level up to high Andean wetlands at about 4,500 m. Important sites include the Salar de Atacama, Laguna de los Flamencos National Reserve in Chile, and Mar Chiquita in Argentina. Populations are estimated at around 200,000–300,000 birds.

Behaviour

Chilean flamingos are highly social filter feeders, straining algae, diatoms, and small crustaceans such as brine shrimp from the water with their specialised bills, often stamping their feet to stir up food. Breeding colonies build cone-shaped mud nests in shallow water; both parents incubate the single egg and feed the chick with crop milk. Chicks gather in crèches guarded by a few adults.

Conservation

The species is listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN, threatened by habitat disturbance, water extraction from high-altitude aquifers for mining and agriculture, egg collection, and drought. It is commonly kept in zoos worldwide.
