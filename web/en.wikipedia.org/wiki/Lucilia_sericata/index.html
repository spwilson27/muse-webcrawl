Lucilia sericata is a species of blow fly in the family Calliphoridae, commonly known as the common green bottle fly (or bottlefly). It is found in most areas of the world and is one of the best-known species of the genus Lucilia. The adult flies are characterised by a brilliant, metallic, blue-green or golden colouring with black markings. They have short, sparse black bristles (setae) and three cross-grooves on the thorax. The wings are clear with light brown veins, and the legs and antennae are black.

The species is of great importance in forensic entomology, because the timing of the development of its larvae in a corpse can be used to estimate the post-mortem interval. Its larvae are also used in maggot therapy, in which sterile larvae are introduced into wounds to remove necrotic tissue.

Description

The adult L. sericata is between 10 and 14 millimetres in length, somewhat larger than a housefly. Its most distinctive feature is the metallic green or blue-green sheen of the thorax and abdomen. Like other calliphorids, it has large red compound eyes and clear wings with a characteristic closed cell near the wing margin. The species closely resembles its congener Lucilia cuprina, and the two are distinguished by microscopic features such as the colour of the frontoclypeal membrane and the number of paravertical bristles.

Distribution and habitat

L. sericata is distributed worldwide in temperate and warm climates. The adult flies prefer warmer and drier conditions than many other blow flies, and are frequently found in sunlit areas near carrion, faeces and refuse, on which the females lay their eggs.

Life cycle

Females lay masses of up to 200 eggs on carrion or other protein-rich substrates. The eggs hatch within about a day into first-instar larvae (maggots), which feed on the substrate and pass through three larval instars before leaving the food source to pupate in soil. Development time is strongly temperature-dependent, a property exploited in forensic investigations to estimate the minimum time since death.

Medical and forensic importance

In maggot therapy (biosurgery), medical-grade sterile larvae of L. sericata are applied to chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers and pressure sores, where they debride dead tissue, disinfect the wound and promote healing. The species can also cause myiasis in sheep, known as blow fly strike, a significant problem in animal husbandry in countries such as Australia and New Zealand.
