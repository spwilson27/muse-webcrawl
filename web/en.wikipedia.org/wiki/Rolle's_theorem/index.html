Rolle's theorem

Rolle's theorem
FieldReal analysis, calculus
Named afterMichel Rolle
First stated1691 (for polynomials); 12th-century anticipation by Bhāskara II
GeneralisationMean value theorem

In calculus, Rolle's theorem states that any real-valued differentiable function that attains equal values at two distinct points must have at least one point between them where the derivative is zero — geometrically, a point where the tangent to the graph is horizontal.

Statement

Let f be a real-valued function continuous on the closed interval [a, b], differentiable on the open interval (a, b), and such that f(a) = f(b). Then there exists at least one c in (a, b) such that

f′(c) = 0.

Proof idea

By the extreme value theorem, f attains a maximum and a minimum on [a, b]. If both occur at the endpoints, then since f(a) = f(b) the function is constant and every point works. Otherwise, f has an extremum at an interior point c, and by Fermat's theorem on stationary points, f′(c) = 0.

History

The result was known in essence to the 12th-century Indian mathematician Bhāskara II. Michel Rolle gave a proof for polynomial functions in 1691, ironically while remaining a critic of the new calculus. The modern formulation with differentiability hypotheses was developed in the 19th century; the name "Rolle's theorem" was in use by the 1830s–40s.

Necessity of the hypotheses

Each condition matters: a function with a jump discontinuity may return to its starting value with no horizontal tangent; the function f(x) = |x| on [−1, 1] is continuous with f(−1) = f(1) but is not differentiable at 0, and its derivative is never zero elsewhere; and if f(a) ≠ f(b) the conclusion need not hold — that more general case is handled by the mean value theorem, of which Rolle's theorem is the special case and the usual stepping stone in the proof.
