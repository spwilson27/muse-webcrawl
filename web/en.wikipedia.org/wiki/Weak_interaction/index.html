Weak interaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Feynman diagram of beta decay, the classic weak process mediated by a W boson]
Beta decay, mediated by the weak force.

The weak interaction (also called the weak force or weak nuclear force) is one of the four known fundamental interactions, alongside the strong interaction, electromagnetism and gravity. It is responsible for radioactive beta decay and for nuclear fusion reactions in stars, and it is the only interaction that can change the flavour of quarks and leptons — metamorphosing particles into one another.

The weak interaction is mediated by the massive W and Z bosons, whose mass (80–91 GeV) restricts its range to about 10⁻¹⁸ m — hence its apparent feebleness at low energies, where its effective coupling (Fermi's constant) is small. Within the Standard Model it is unified with electromagnetism as the Electroweak interaction. It is unique among forces in violating parity (mirror symmetry) maximally — it acts only on left-handed particles — and in violating CP symmetry, features central to the matter–antimatter asymmetry problem.

Role in the cosmos

Without the weak force, the Sun could not burn (its first fusion step, two protons → deuterium, is a weak process), heavy elements' radioactive decays would not power Earth's internal heat, and supernovae would not radiate their energy in neutrinos. Its strength sets the pace of stellar life and of the early universe's freeze-outs — weak interaction rates determined the neutron-to-proton ratio that fixed the primordial helium abundance in Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
