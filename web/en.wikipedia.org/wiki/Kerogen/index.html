Kerogen is solid, insoluble organic matter embedded in sedimentary rocks such as shales. Consisting of polymeric material formed from the buried remains of algae, plankton, spores and plant matter, kerogen is the precursor from which petroleum and natural gas are generated when the rock is buried and heated over geological time. It is the most abundant form of organic carbon on Earth, exceeding the carbon in all living biomass and in fossil-fuel reserves.

Formation and maturation

When organisms die in oxygen-poor waters, their organic remains accumulate in fine sediments. During diagenesis, microbial action and mild heating convert this material first to kerogen and release biogenic methane. With deeper burial and temperatures roughly between 60 and 150 °C (the "oil window"), kerogen cracks — a process called catagenesis — into liquid petroleum and gas; the residual kerogen becomes progressively richer in carbon and poorer in hydrogen, eventually forming inert graphite-like material.

Types

Geochemists classify kerogen by its hydrogen-to-carbon and oxygen-to-carbon ratios, conventionally plotted on a van Krevelen diagram. Type I kerogen, mainly from lake algae, is hydrogen-rich and highly oil-prone. Type II, from marine plankton and algae, is the source of most conventional oil and gas. Type III, derived from woody land plants, is gas-prone and associated with coals. Type IV is oxidised, inert material with essentially no hydrocarbon potential.

Exploitation

In oil shale, kerogen has not been naturally matured, and oil can be produced by retorting — heating the rock to about 500 °C to pyrolyse the kerogen into shale oil. In unconventional "tight" reservoirs, kerogen within mature source rock has already generated oil and gas retained in place, which is produced by horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing.
