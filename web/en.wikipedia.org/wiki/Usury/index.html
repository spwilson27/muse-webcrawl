Usury


A moneylender in a medieval manuscript illumination

Usury (/ˈjuːʒəri/) is the practice of making loans that are considered to carry unfairly high or immoral interest. Historically, the term referred to the charging of any interest on loans, and it was condemned by numerous religious traditions and moral philosophers; in modern usage it typically denotes interest rates deemed excessive or exploitative, and many jurisdictions cap lawful interest rates through usury laws.

Religious and historical attitudes

In Judaism, the Torah prohibits charging interest to fellow Israelites in need while permitting it in dealings with foreigners, a distinction that shaped centuries of interpretation. In Christianity, usury was condemned by church councils and scholastic theologians — Thomas Aquinas argued that charging for the use of money was charging for something that does not exist, since money's use is its consumption. Medieval canon law banned usury among Christians altogether, pushing much lending toward Jewish communities, which in turn fed antisemitic persecution. In Islam, riba (usury/interest) is prohibited by the Qur'an, a prohibition that underlies modern Islamic finance, which structures transactions as trade, leasing or profit-sharing instead of interest-bearing loans.

Reformation and modern development

From the late Middle Ages, distinctions between usurious and legitimate interest eroded in practice: compensation for risk, delay and forgone profit (lucrum cessans) came to be accepted. John Calvin accepted moderate interest on commercial loans, and by the 18th–19th centuries most Western legal systems had replaced bans with statutory maximum rates. Jeremy Bentham's Defence of Usury (1787) argued against rate caps on economic-liberal grounds.

Usury laws today

Most countries retain some combination of licensing rules, disclosure requirements, and maximum-rate legislation, although the rules often vary by type of lender and borrower; many jurisdictions exempt business and corporate borrowing. Debates continue over payday lending, credit-card rates and microfinance interest, where the line between legitimate compensation for risk and exploitative usury remains contested.
