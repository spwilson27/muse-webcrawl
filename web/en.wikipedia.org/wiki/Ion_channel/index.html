Ion channel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A cross-section diagram of a potassium ion channel embedded in a cell membrane]
A cross-section diagram of a potassium ion channel embedded in a cell membrane

Ion channels are pore-forming membrane proteins that conduct ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) across cell membranes, down their electrochemical gradients. They underlie the electrical excitability of nerves and muscles — the Action potential is a choreography of voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels — as well as secretion, volume regulation and sensory transduction. Their study is a triumph of modern biophysics, crowned by the 2003 Nobel Prize for Roderick MacKinnon's channel structures.

Types

Channels are classified by gating: voltage-gated (nerve impulses, heartbeat), ligand-gated (neurotransmitter receptors at synapses — nicotinic, GABA_A, NMDA), mechanically gated (touch, hearing — the Piezo channels, Nobel 2021), and leak channels. Selectivity filters pass one ion species at rates up to 100 million ions per second while excluding others a fraction of an ångström different in size.

History

The Hodgkin–Huxley model (1952) predicted voltage-gated channels from squid-axon currents before anyone could see them; patch-clamp recording (Neher and Sakmann, Nobel 1991) measured single channels; MacKinnon's 1998 potassium-channel structure revealed the selectivity filter's atomic logic.

Disease and drugs

Channel defects ('channelopathies') cause epilepsy, cystic fibrosis (the CFTR chloride channel), periodic paralysis, long-QT syndrome and chronic pain. Channels are among the richest drug targets in medicine — local anaesthetics, calcium-channel blockers, antiepileptics and the CFTR modulators that transformed cystic fibrosis care.
