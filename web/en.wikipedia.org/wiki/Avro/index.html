Avro

Avro (initially A.V. Roe and Company) was a British aircraft manufacturer founded in 1910 by Alliott Verdon Roe in Manchester, England. One of the world's first specialist aircraft companies, Avro produced some of the most famous British aircraft of both world wars, including the Avro 504 trainer, the Lancaster heavy bomber of the Second World War, and, after the war, the Vulcan delta-wing nuclear bomber. The company was absorbed into Hawker Siddeley Aviation in 1963.

Early years

Alliott Verdon Roe had flown Britain's first successful powered aeroplane of his own design in 1908–1909 before founding A.V. Roe and Company with his brother Humphrey in 1910. The Avro 504, first flown in 1913, became the company's early mainstay: more than 11,000 were built, and it served as a trainer with the Royal Flying Corps and RAF for two decades. Between the wars Avro produced trainers, transports and the Tutor basic trainer under the technical direction of Roy Chadwick.

Second World War

Chadwick's designs defined Avro's war: the Anson (1935) served in maritime patrol and training; the twin-engined Manchester's shortcomings were redeemed by the four-engined Lancaster (1941), which became the RAF's principal night heavy bomber — 7,377 were built — and carried out the Dambusters raid of May 1943 with 617 Squadron. The Lincoln and the transport York followed.

Post-war

Post-war Avro produced the Shackleton maritime patrol aircraft, the Tudor and Ashton airliners, and the Vulcan (first flown 1952), the delta-winged strategic bomber that formed part of Britain's V-force nuclear deterrent and, late in life, flew the "Black Buck" raids in the 1982 Falklands War. The Avro Canada subsidiary built the CF-105 Arrow interceptor, cancelled in 1959. Avro's last major design, the 748 twin-turboprop airliner, was a success continued by Hawker Siddeley and British Aerospace as the HS 748. In 1963 Avro was merged into Hawker Siddeley Aviation, and the name disappeared as a separate manufacturer.
