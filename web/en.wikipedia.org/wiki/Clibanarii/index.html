Clibanarii

The clibanarii (singular clibanarius; from Greek klibanarios, derived from klibanos, "oven" or "furnace") were a type of super-heavy cavalry used by the Roman and later Byzantine armies, as well as by their Persian rivals. Both rider and horse were covered in full armour, and the rider's enclosed metal helmet, with only small slits for the eyes, gave the impression of a man baked in an oven — hence the name.

Description

The historian Ammianus Marcellinus described Persian clibanarii as "all clad in iron, so that the forms of their bodies were covered with plate armour fitted to the limbs... statues polished by the hand of Praxiteles, rather than men." They fought as shock cavalry, charging with the long contus lance, and are closely related to — and often used interchangeably with — the cataphracts, though clibanarii are sometimes distinguished by even heavier barding for the horse.

History

Rome adopted units of clibanarii from the 3rd–4th centuries AD, largely for service in the east against Parthian and Sasanian cavalry. Units such as the Equites clibanarii appear in the Notitia Dignitatum, and the type survived in Byzantine armies for centuries as the klibanophoroi.
