The Port of London comprises the tidal River Thames and its wharves, terminals, and facilities from Teddington Lock to the North Sea — about 95 miles of river administered by the Port of London Authority (PLA). For centuries it was the largest port in the world, the commercial heart of the British Empire. Although the great enclosed docks of central London closed in the later 20th century as container traffic moved downriver, the port remains the United Kingdom's largest inland waterway freight operation and typically ranks among the country's top two or three ports by tonnage.

History

Roman Londinium was founded as a port on the Thames, and trade has defined the city ever since. In the 18th and early 19th centuries, congestion of ships moored in the river led to the building of the enclosed dock systems — West India Docks (1802), London Docks, East India Docks, the Surrey Commercial Docks, and later the Royal Docks (the Royal Victoria, Royal Albert, and King George V) — forming the largest complex of wet docks in the world. During the Second World War the port was a prime target of the Blitz. The PLA was established in 1909 to unify control of the river and docks.

Decline of the upriver docks and regeneration

Containerisation from the 1960s required deep water, large back-up land, and mechanised handling that the upriver docks could not provide. Traffic concentrated downriver at Tilbury (and at Felixstowe and Southampton outside the port), and between 1967 and 1981 the London docks closed in succession, with the loss of tens of thousands of docklands jobs. The derelict dockland areas were redeveloped from the 1980s by the London Docklands Development Corporation: the Isle of Dogs became Canary Wharf, a second financial district, and the Royal Docks acquired the ExCeL exhibition centre and London City Airport.

The port today

Modern cargo handling takes place at more than 70 terminals along the tidal Thames, most privately operated. Tilbury, including the Tilbury2 expansion, handles containers, forest products, aggregates, and ro-ro traffic; the London Gateway terminal at Stanford-le-Hope, opened by DP World in 2013 on the former Shell Haven refinery site, is a major deep-sea container port. The river also carries the bulk of London's aggregates, construction materials, and municipal waste by barge, and is used by passenger services and the Thames Barrier navigation. The PLA acts as harbour authority — responsible for navigation, pilotage, conservancy, and licensing — rather than as a cargo operator.
