Prostaglandin

Prostaglandin E2, one of the best-known prostaglandins.

Prostaglandins are a group of physiologically active lipid compounds called eicosanoids, having diverse hormone-like effects in animals. Prostaglandins have been found in almost every tissue in humans and other animals. They are derived enzymatically from the fatty acid arachidonic acid and each contains 20 carbon atoms, including a 5-carbon ring.

History

The name prostaglandin derives from the prostate gland, because the compounds were first isolated from seminal fluid in 1935 by the Swedish physiologist Ulf von Euler, who believed they came from the prostate. They were later shown to be produced by the seminal vesicles and by many other tissues.

Biosynthesis

When arachidonic acid is released from cell membranes by phospholipase A2, the enzyme cyclooxygenase (COX) converts it into the unstable intermediates PGG2 and PGH2, which tissue-specific synthases then transform into the various prostaglandins (PGE2, PGD2, PGF2α, PGI2 — prostacyclin — and thromboxane A2). Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen act by inhibiting COX enzymes.

Functions

Prostaglandins act locally as autocrine and paracrine mediators. Depending on the type and the receptor involved, they can constrict or dilate vascular smooth muscle, cause platelet aggregation or disaggregation, sensitize spinal neurons to pain, induce labour, decrease intraocular pressure, regulate inflammation, and protect the stomach lining by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate secretion while reducing acid output.

Clinical uses

Synthetic prostaglandins are used as drugs: misoprostol (a PGE1 analogue) prevents NSAID-induced gastric ulcers and is used in obstetrics; dinoprostone (PGE2) induces labour; latanoprost (a PGF2α analogue) treats glaucoma; and alprostadil maintains patency of the ductus arteriosus in newborns.
