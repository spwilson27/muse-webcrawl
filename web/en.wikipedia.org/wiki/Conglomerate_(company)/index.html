Conglomerate (company)

A diversified conglomerate owns businesses across many unrelated industries

A conglomerate is a type of multi-industry company that consists of several different and unrelated business entities that operate in various industries under one corporate group. A conglomerate usually has a parent company that owns controlling stakes in smaller companies, which conduct business separately. Conglomerates are often large and multinational and are common in industries such as media, food processing, manufacturing and finance. Well-known examples include Berkshire Hathaway, which owns insurers, railroads, manufacturers and retailers; the Tata group and Reliance Industries in India; Samsung and chaebol groups in South Korea; and historically ITT Corporation, Gulf+Western and General Electric in the United States.

History

Conglomerates became a dominant corporate form during the merger wave of the 1960s in the United States, when relaxed antitrust enforcement against unrelated acquisitions, high stock valuations and "financial engineering" techniques such as the pooling-of-interests accounting method allowed acquisitive firms — ITT under Harold Geneen, Ling-Temco-Vought under James Ling, and Litton Industries among them — to grow rapidly by buying companies in unrelated fields. The market collapse of 1969–1970 and evidence that conglomerates traded at a "conglomerate discount" relative to the sum of their parts led to a wave of break-ups and spin-offs in the 1970s and 1980s, when corporate raiders such as Carl Icahn targeted diversified firms. Activist investors and private equity continue to press diversified companies, such as General Electric — which announced in 2021 that it would split into three focused companies — to simplify their structures.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue that conglomerates diversify risk across industries, allocate capital internally to its best uses without relying on external capital markets, exploit tax and borrowing advantages, and apply professional management across businesses. Critics respond that internal capital markets often subsidise weak divisions, that diversification destroys shareholder value compared with investors diversifying their own portfolios, that size and complexity foster bureaucracy and opacity, and that empirical studies find diversified firms trading at an average discount of roughly 10–15 per cent to comparable focused firms. In emerging markets, business groups remain common because they substitute for underdeveloped capital markets, legal systems and managerial labour markets.

Conglomerates by region

In South Korea, family-controlled chaebol such as Samsung, Hyundai, SK and LG dominate the economy; in Japan, the pre-war zaibatsu were dissolved after 1945 and partly re-formed as bank-centred keiretsu. Indian business houses such as Tata, Birla and Reliance, Latin American grupos económicos, and diversified groups in Southeast Asia and Africa play similar roles.
