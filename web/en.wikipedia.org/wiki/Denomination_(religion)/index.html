Religious denomination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A religious denomination is a subgroup within a religion that operates under a common name, tradition, and identity. The term refers to the various Christian denominations (for example, Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic, and the many Protestant traditions), but is also used for other religions: Judaism has movements such as Orthodox, Conservative and Reform; Islam has branches including Sunni and Shia; Hinduism includes traditions such as Shaivism and Vaishnavism.

Christianity

In Christianity, a denomination is a distinct religious body under a common administrative and doctrinal umbrella. Christian denominations differ in matters such as the nature of Jesus, authority of apostolic succession, eschatology and papal primacy. Major families include Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, Protestantism (including Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist and Pentecostal traditions), and Restorationist movements. There are estimated to be tens of thousands of distinct Christian denominations worldwide, depending on how they are counted.

Multi-denominational

The term "multi-denominational" may describe events involving several denominations, such as a citywide good-Friday service. "Interdenominational" organisations cooperate across denominational boundaries, while "non-denominational" churches and organisations decline to identify with any particular denomination.

Other religions

Within Judaism, movements (sometimes called denominations) include Hasidic, Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism. In Islam, the principal branches are Sunni and Shia, with further schools of law (madhhab) and theology within them. Hindu denominations centre on devotion to particular deities or philosophies, notably Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism and the Smarta tradition. Buddhism is divided into Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana vehicles, each with many schools.
