Heirloom tomato

An heirloom tomato is an open-pollinated, non-hybrid tomato cultivar whose seeds have been saved and passed down, often for generations, because of valued characteristics such as flavour, colour or adaptation to a locality. Unlike the uniform red hybrids bred for shipping and shelf life that dominate commercial trade, heirloom varieties display enormous diversity in size, shape and colour—green, yellow, purple, striped and bicoloured fruit among them—and are widely prized by gardeners and chefs for taste, though they are typically more fragile, irregular in yield and prone to cracking and disease.

Definition and history

The term "heirloom" in this sense was popularised by seed-saver organisations from the 1970s, notably the Seed Savers Exchange (founded 1975), which defines heirlooms as varieties maintained by gardeners rather than by commercial seed houses. Well-known varieties include 'Brandywine' (a large pink potato-leaf type dating to the 1880s), 'Mortgage Lifter', 'Cherokee Purple', 'Green Zebra' (a modern open-pollinated introduction from 1983) and 'Amish Paste'. Because they are open-pollinated, heirlooms grow true from saved seed, allowing local selection.

Cultivation

Heirloom tomatoes require the same care as other garden tomatoes—full sun, fertile soil, staking or caging—but their thin skins and irregular ripening make them unsuitable for mechanical harvesting and long transport. They are therefore sold mainly at farmers' markets and grown in home gardens. Seed-saving movements credit the continued existence of hundreds of old varieties to hobbyist growers; commercial breeders have also begun crossing heirloom types for flavour while retaining heirloom names for marketing.
