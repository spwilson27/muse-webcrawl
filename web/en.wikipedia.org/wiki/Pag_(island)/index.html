Pag (Croatian: Pag; Italian: Pago) is a Croatian island in the northern Adriatic Sea, the fifth-largest island of the Croatian coast and the one with the longest coastline. It is administratively divided between Lika-Senj County in the north and Zadar County in the south. The island is known for its stark, moonlike limestone landscape, its salt pans, its lace-making tradition and Pag cheese (Paški sir).

Geography

Pag is about 60 kilometres long and narrow, running parallel to the mainland beneath the Velebit mountains. Much of its eastern side, exposed to the fierce bora wind that carries salt spray from the sea, is bare rock, while the western side is greener. The island is connected to the mainland by the Pag Bridge (opened 1968) in the south and by a ferry from Prizna to Žigljen in the north.

Economy and culture

Salt has been produced in the pans near the town of Pag for centuries. Paški sir, a hard sheep's-milk cheese flavoured by the salt-laden herbs the sheep graze, is internationally awarded and protected by geographical indication. Pag lace (Paška čipka) is inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage. In summer, the beach at Zrće near Novalja hosts large open-air electronic music festivals and clubs, earning it a reputation as a party destination.

Settlements

The principal towns are Pag, the historic centre founded in the 15th century under Venetian rule to a plan associated with the architect Juraj Dalmatinac, and Novalja in the northwest, which originated as the Roman port of Navalia.
