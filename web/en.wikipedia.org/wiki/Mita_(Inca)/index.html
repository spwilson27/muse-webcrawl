Mita (Inca)

The mita (Quechua: mit'a, "turn") was a system of mandatory public service in the society of the Inca Empire. It was, in effect, a form of labour tax: communities were required to provide a quota of workers for a set period each year to build and maintain roads, bridges, temples, terraces, fortresses, and other imperial infrastructure, and to serve in the army and in mines.

Operation under the Inca

Because the Inca state had no currency or markets of the European kind, labour was its principal means of taxation. Households organised through the ayllu (kin group) owed a rotating turn of service; in return the state provided food, drink, and clothing during the work period and maintained storehouses (qullqa) that supplied communities in times of dearth. Large projects attributed to mita labour include the great road network of the Qhapaq Ñan and the fortress of Sacsayhuamán.

Colonial mita

After the Spanish conquest, Viceroy Francisco de Toledo reorganised the mita in the 1570s to supply forced labour to the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica. Under the colonial system, one-seventh of the adult male tributary population of designated provinces was drafted each year. Conditions were brutal, mortality was high, and the system became a byword for exploitation, though elements of it also supplied labour for public works and textile workshops (obrajes). The colonial mita was formally abolished in the early 19th century, though informal drafts persisted.

Legacy

The contrast between the reciprocal Inca mita and its coercive colonial adaptation is a central theme in Andean historiography. Related Andean traditions of communal labour, such as minka and ayni, continue in some rural communities today.
