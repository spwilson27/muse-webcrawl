Reindeer herding

Reindeer herding is the practice of herding reindeer (known as caribou in North America) by the indigenous peoples of the Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Eurasia and, more recently, North America. Herders follow their animals on seasonal migrations across vast territories of tundra and taiga, and the reindeer provide meat, hides, antlers, milk and transportation.

Peoples and regions

Reindeer herding is practised by the Sámi of Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula; the Nenets, Khanty, Evenki, Chukchi and other peoples of Siberia; and Inuit and Alaska Native communities, where reindeer were introduced from Siberia in the 1890s. The largest herds are those of the Nenets on the Yamal Peninsula.

Practice

Traditional herding is nomadic or semi-nomadic: herds are moved between summer pastures on the tundra and winter pastures in the forest, where reindeer dig through snow for lichen. Herders traditionally used sleds drawn by reindeer and skis; modern practice often uses snowmobiles, all-terrain vehicles and helicopters. Animals are marked with ear-notches identifying their owners, gathered for calf-marking in summer and for slaughter and separation in autumn.

Challenges

Herding is threatened by climate change, oil, gas and mining development on pasture lands, predation, and competition for grazing land.
