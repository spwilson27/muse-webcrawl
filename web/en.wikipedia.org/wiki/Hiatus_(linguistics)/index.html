Hiatus (linguistics)

In phonology, hiatus (from Latin hiatus, "opening") is the occurrence of two vowel sounds in adjacent syllables without an intervening consonant, as in English cooperate [koʊˈɒpəreɪt], naïve, or boa. The two vowels are pronounced in separate syllables rather than fused into a diphthong or monophthong.

Resolution strategies

Languages vary in whether they tolerate hiatus. Common strategies for avoiding it include:

  • Glide formation — a high vowel becomes a glide [j] or [w] before the following vowel.
  • Glide insertion — an epenthetic glide or glottal stop is inserted between the vowels, as in non-rhotic English "law[r] and order".
  • Vowel deletion or coalescence — one vowel is dropped or the two merge, as in the sandhi rules of Sanskrit.
  • Diphthongization — the sequence is compressed into a single syllable.

In English

English tolerates hiatus in words such as create, poem, and geometry, though speakers often smooth the transition with a transitional glide. The indefinite article alternation a/an also serves partly to break up hiatus before vowel-initial words. Historically, the diaeresis (as in coöperate) was used in print to mark a hiatus.
