Hyperboloid structure

Hyperboloid structures are architectural structures designed with hyperboloid geometry — surfaces of a hyperboloid of one sheet, which is a doubly ruled surface. Often these are tall structures, such as towers, where the hyperboloid geometry provides structural strength using straight beams arranged in a lattice (gridshell) pattern. The first hyperboloid structures were built by the Russian engineer Vladimir Shukhov in the 1890s.

Geometry and properties

A hyperboloid of one sheet can be generated by rotating a hyperbola about its axis or by sweeping a straight line around a skew axis; consequently the curved surface can be constructed entirely from straight elements connecting two rings, twisted relative to each other. The resulting lattice distributes loads efficiently, resists wind with low drag, and encloses maximum volume with minimum material. Hyperboloid geometry is also used in cooling towers, where the shape both strengthens the shell and promotes the chimney effect for airflow.

History

Vladimir Shukhov patented the hyperboloid gridshell in 1899 and built the first such tower for the All-Russia Exhibition in Nizhny Novgorod in 1896. His most famous work, the Shukhov Tower in Moscow (1922), rises 160 m and originally supported radio broadcasting antennas. Later architects and engineers — including Antoni Gaudí, who used hyperboloid elements in the Sagrada Família, Le Corbusier, Oscar Niemeyer (Brasília Cathedral), and Frei Otto — adopted the form. Natural-draft cooling towers for power stations, first built in the Netherlands and Britain in the early twentieth century, became the most widespread hyperboloid structures in the world.

Examples

  • Shukhov Tower, Moscow, Russia
  • Adziogol Lighthouse, Ukraine (also by Shukhov)
  • Canton Tower, Guangzhou, China (610 m)
  • Brasília Cathedral, Brazil
  • Kobe Port Tower, Japan
  • Cooling towers of thermal and nuclear power stations worldwide
