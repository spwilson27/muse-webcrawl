Emil Wilhelm Georg Magnus Kraepelin (15 February 1856 – 7 October 1926) was a German psychiatrist, regarded as the founder of modern scientific psychiatry, psychopharmacology and psychiatric genetics. His textbook of psychiatry, published in successive editions from 1883, established a classification of mental disorders based on longitudinal course and outcome that shaped the field for a century.

Career

Kraepelin studied medicine at Leipzig and Würzburg, working with Wilhelm Wundt, whose experimental psychology influenced his methods. He held professorships at Dorpat (Tartu), Heidelberg and, from 1903, Munich, where he founded the German Institute for Psychiatric Research (later the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry). His collaborators and students included Alois Alzheimer and Franz Nissl.

The Kraepelinian dichotomy

In the sixth edition of his textbook (1899), Kraepelin divided the major psychoses into two groups: dementia praecox — later renamed schizophrenia by Eugen Bleuler — characterised by a deteriorating course, and manic-depressive insanity, characterised by episodic illness with recovery between episodes and including what is now called bipolar disorder and recurrent depression. This "Kraepelinian dichotomy" underlies the structure of modern diagnostic manuals such as the DSM and ICD.

Other work

Kraepelin also conducted pioneering studies of the effects of alcohol, fatigue and drugs on psychological performance, campaigned for temperance, and promoted systematic record-keeping of patients' courses of illness using his famous diagnosis cards (Zählkarten).
