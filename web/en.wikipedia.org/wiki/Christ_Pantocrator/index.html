Christ Pantocrator

Christ Pantocrator (Greek: Χριστὸς Παντοκράτωρ, "Christ Almighty" or "Christ the Ruler of All") is a specific depiction of Christ common in Eastern Orthodox iconography. Christ is shown half-length, holding the New Testament in his left hand and making the gesture of teaching or blessing with his right. The facial expression is typically stern, emphasizing Christ's role as judge and ruler of the world.

History

Among the earliest known examples is the sixth-century encaustic icon at Saint Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai, in which Christ's face is subtly asymmetrical — one side merciful, the other stern — expressing both divine grace and judgment. Monumental examples appear in the apse mosaics of Byzantine churches, most famously at Hagia Sophia in Constantinople and at Monreale Cathedral in Sicily, and in the dome mosaic of Daphni Monastery near Athens (c. 1100).

Theology

The title derives from the Greek translation of the Hebrew divine name El Shaddai used in the Septuagint, where "Pantocrator" renders "Almighty." In Revelation and the Pauline epistles the term underscores God's sovereignty over creation. The icon is usually accompanied by the letters IC XC, a Christogram formed from the first and last letters of the Greek words for Jesus and Christ.

Depictions

The Pantocrator became the standard image for the central dome of Orthodox churches, looking down upon the congregation. In the West, the related image of Christ in Majesty (Maiestas Domini) served a similar function in Romanesque art.
