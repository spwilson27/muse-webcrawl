Stephen I, also known as King Saint Stephen (Hungarian: Szent István király; born Vajk; c. 975 – 15 August 1038), was the last Grand Prince of the Hungarians and the first King of Hungary, reigning from 1000 or 1001 until his death. He is credited with founding the Hungarian state, converting the Hungarians to Christianity, and organising the kingdom's church and administration. He is Hungary's patron saint, and his feast day, 20 August, is Hungary's national day.

Reign

Stephen succeeded his father Géza as Grand Prince in 997 and defeated his pagan rival Koppány, who claimed the throne. According to tradition he was crowned with a crown sent by Pope Sylvester II, with the assent of Emperor Otto III. He crushed the remaining pagan chieftains, established at least eight bishoprics and the archbishopric of Esztergom, founded monasteries such as Pannonhalma, issued two law codes, and organised the kingdom into royal counties. His Admonitions to his son Emeric are a classic mirror-for-princes text. Emeric died in a hunting accident in 1031, leaving Stephen without a direct heir; the succession was contested after his death.

Legacy

Stephen was canonised in 1083 together with Emeric and Bishop Gerard. The Holy Crown of Hungary, the Holy Right (his preserved right hand), and St Stephen's Basilica in Budapest are central symbols of Hungarian national identity.
