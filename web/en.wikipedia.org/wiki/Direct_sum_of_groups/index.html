Direct sum of groups

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The direct sum of groups is a construction in group theory that produces a new group from two or more existing groups. For abelian groups written additively, and for finite families of groups generally, the direct sum coincides with the direct product. The distinction appears for infinite families: the direct sum of infinitely many groups consists of those tuples in the direct product that have only finitely many non-identity components.

Definition

Given groups G and H, their direct sum (equivalently, direct product) G ⊕ H has as its underlying set the Cartesian product G × H, with componentwise operation: (g1, h1) · (g2, h2) = (g1g2, h1h2). Each factor embeds as a normal subgroup, the two factors intersect trivially, commute with each other, and together generate the whole group.

A group is said to decompose as an internal direct sum of subgroups if it is isomorphic to their external direct sum in this way. The familiar statement that the Klein four-group is Z2 ⊕ Z2, and that Z6 ≅ Z2 ⊕ Z3, are standard examples.

Properties and theorems

The fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups states that every finitely generated abelian group is a direct sum of cyclic groups — a free part Zr and a torsion part that is a direct sum of cyclic groups of prime-power order. The Krull–Schmidt theorem gives conditions under which decompositions into indecomposable summands are unique. In category-theoretic terms, the direct sum of abelian groups is their coproduct, whereas the direct product is the product; the two constructions agree for finite families, which is why the distinction is often glossed over for finitely many summands.
