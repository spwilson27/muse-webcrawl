The black-footed ferret (Mustela nigripes), also known as the American polecat or prairie dog hunter, is a species of mustelid native to central North America. It is listed as endangered by the IUCN, because of its dwindling populations — it was twice thought to be extinct.

Description and behaviour

The black-footed ferret has a long, slender body with black feet, ears, parts of the tail, and a distinctive black "bandit mask" across the eyes; the rest of the pelage is buffy-yellow. It is a nocturnal, solitary predator specialised for life in prairie dog colonies: prairie dogs comprise about 90 percent of its diet, and the ferret shelters in their burrows. A single ferret may eat more than 100 prairie dogs in a year.

Decline and reintroduction

Ferret populations collapsed as prairie dogs were eradicated as agricultural pests and as sylvatic plague and canine distemper spread. The species was declared extinct in 1979, but in 1981 a ranch dog near Meeteetse, Wyoming, killed a ferret, revealing a remnant population. After disease struck that colony, the last 18 wild ferrets were taken into captivity (1985–1987) as the foundation of a captive-breeding programme. Their descendants have been reintroduced at sites in the United States (including Arizona, South Dakota, Montana, Wyoming and Kansas), Canada and Mexico, and the population now numbers several hundred breeding adults in the wild. In 2020 a black-footed ferret named Elizabeth Ann became the first cloned endangered native species in North America, cloned from cells frozen in 1988 to boost genetic diversity.
