Biosafety

Biosafety is the prevention of large-scale loss of biological integrity, focusing both on ecology and human health. These prevention mechanisms include conduction of regular reviews of the biosafety in laboratory settings, as well as strict guidelines to follow. Biosafety is used to protect from harmful incidents. Many laboratories handling biohazards employ an ongoing risk management assessment and enforcement process for biosafety. A related concept is biosecurity, which concerns protecting biological materials from theft, loss, or deliberate misuse.

Biosafety levels

Laboratories are classified by biosafety level (BSL), from BSL-1 (lowest risk) to BSL-4 (highest risk), based on the agents handled:

LevelAgentsExamples
BSL-1Well-characterised agents not known to cause disease in healthy adultsNon-pathogenic E. coli
BSL-2Agents of moderate potential hazardStaphylococcus aureus, hepatitis viruses
BSL-3Indigenous or exotic agents that can cause serious disease via inhalationMycobacterium tuberculosis, SARS-CoV-2
BSL-4Dangerous and exotic agents posing high individual risk, often fatal, with no available vaccines or treatmentsEbola virus, Marburg virus

Containment combines laboratory practice and technique, safety equipment (primary barriers such as biosafety cabinets and personal protective equipment), and facility design (secondary barriers).

History and governance

The modern biosafety regime grew out of mid-20th-century biological weapons programmes' defensive research and the 1975 Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA, at which scientists voluntarily adopted containment guidelines. In the United States, the CDC and NIH publish the manual Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories (BMBL). The Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (2000, in force 2003) to the Convention on Biological Diversity governs the transboundary movement of living modified organisms.
