Type II supernova

A Type II supernova (plural: supernovae) is a category of supernova characterised by the presence of hydrogen lines in its spectrum, distinguishing it from Type I supernovae. Type II supernovae result from the rapid collapse and violent explosion of the core of a massive star — a core-collapse supernova. The progenitor star must have at least eight times the mass of the Sun at the end of its life, though the explosion leaves behind a neutron star or, for the most massive cases, a black hole.

A red supergiant collapses once its core can no longer support itself, blasting its hydrogen-rich envelope into space.

Formation and mechanism

A massive star fuses progressively heavier elements in its core, building up an inert core of iron-peak elements. Iron fusion absorbs rather than releases energy, so once the iron core exceeds roughly the Chandrasekhar limit (~1.4 solar masses), thermal pressure can no longer support it and the core collapses in less than a second to form a proto-neutron star. The infalling outer core rebounds off the dense inner core, launching a shock wave. The shock stalls, but is believed to be revived by heating from the enormous flux of neutrinos emitted as the neutron star forms — the delayed neutrino-heating mechanism. The revived shock expels the star's outer layers at thousands of kilometres per second. The total energy released is on the order of 1046 joules, of which about 99% is carried away by neutrinos and only about 1% appears as kinetic energy and light.

Classification by light curve

Type II supernovae are divided by the shape of their light curves:

  • Type II-P ("plateau") — the most common kind; the light curve shows a broad plateau of roughly constant brightness lasting about 100 days, produced when the expanding hydrogen envelope recombines. The progenitor is typically a red supergiant with a massive hydrogen envelope.
  • Type II-L ("linear") — the brightness declines steadily with no clear plateau, suggesting a smaller hydrogen envelope.
  • Type IIn — shows narrow hydrogen emission lines, indicating the ejecta are colliding with dense circumstellar material shed by the star before exploding.
  • Type IIb — an intermediate case: hydrogen is present early but fades, leaving a spectrum resembling a Type Ib; the star had been mostly stripped of its hydrogen envelope.

Examples

The brightest and best-studied is SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud, whose unusual blue-supergiant progenitor and detected neutrino burst confirmed core-collapse theory. Other well-observed events include SN 1993J (a Type IIb) and the Type II-P SN 1999em.

Role in the universe

Core-collapse supernovae disperse heavy elements forged in the progenitor star and during the explosion itself, enriching the interstellar medium. The expanding remnants compress nearby gas and can trigger new star formation, while the compact remnant may be observed as a pulsar.
