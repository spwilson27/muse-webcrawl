The Sámi people (also Saami or Sami; Northern Sámi: Sámit) are an indigenous Finno-Ugric people inhabiting Sápmi, a region spanning northern Norway, Sweden and Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula. They are the only indigenous people recognised and protected under international conventions by the Nordic states, with their own parliaments in Norway, Sweden and Finland.

Culture and livelihood

Sámi culture is closely associated with semi-nomadic reindeer herding, though only a minority of Sámi herd reindeer today; fishing, hunting, sheep farming and handicrafts (duodji) are also traditional pursuits. Distinctive features include the colourful costume (gákti), the joik vocal tradition, and the tent-like lavvu and turf hut. The Sámi languages — most widely Northern Sámi — belong to the Uralic family.

History and politics

From the Middle Ages the Sámi were taxed by neighbouring kingdoms and progressively Christianised; in the 19th and 20th centuries assimilation policies such as Norway's fornorsking suppressed their language and culture, and boarding schools separated children from their families. Political mobilisation grew after the Alta conflict (1979–1981) over a hydroelectric dam; Norway ratified ILO Convention 169 in 1990, and the Sámi parliaments and Norway's Finnmark Act (2005) advanced indigenous land and cultural rights.
