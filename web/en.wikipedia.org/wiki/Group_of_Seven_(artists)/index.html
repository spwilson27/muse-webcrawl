Group of Seven (artists)

The Group of Seven, also known as the Algonquin School, was a group of Canadian landscape painters from 1920 to 1933, originally consisting of Franklin Carmichael (1890–1945), Lawren Harris (1885–1970), A. Y. Jackson (1882–1974), Frank Johnston (1888–1949), Arthur Lismer (1885–1969), J. E. H. MacDonald (1873–1932), and Frederick Varley (1881–1969). A. J. Casson later joined, and the group expanded into the Canadian Group of Painters in 1933.

History

The artists first came together in Toronto in the 1910s, several of them working as commercial designers and making sketching trips to Algonquin Provincial Park and other northern Ontario locations. Influenced by the earlier work of Tom Thomson, who died in 1917 and is closely associated with the group though never a formal member, they sought to create a distinctly Canadian art rooted in direct experience of the rugged northern landscape. Their first exhibition as the Group of Seven was held at the Art Gallery of Toronto in May 1920.

Style and reception

The group's paintings are characterised by bold colour, simplified forms, and dramatic depictions of windswept pines, rocky shorelines, and northern lakes, often in Algoma, Georgian Bay, and the north shore of Lake Superior. Early critics attacked their work as garish and unskilled, but the group gained official support, including from the National Gallery of Canada under Eric Brown, and by the later 1920s they were celebrated as the founders of a national school of painting. Their reputations have since been re-examined, including criticism that their vision of an empty wilderness erased Indigenous presence.

Legacy

The Group of Seven remains the best-known school of Canadian art, and their works command the highest prices in the Canadian art market. The McMichael Canadian Art Collection in Kleinburg, Ontario, holds a major collection of their work and is the burial site of several members.
