Tuoba

The Tuoba (Chinese: 拓跋; pinyin: Tuòbá), also known by the later Chinese surname Yuan (元), were a prominent clan of the Xianbei peoples of the eastern Eurasian steppe. They rose to power in northern China during the period of disunion following the fall of the Han dynasty, founding the state of Dai and, most importantly, the Northern Wei dynasty (386–534), which unified northern China and began the eventual reunification of the country completed under the Sui dynasty.

Origins and rise

The Tuoba emerged in the region of modern Inner Mongolia and northern Shanxi. Under Tuoba Yilu in the early 4th century they were recognised by the Jin dynasty as rulers of Dai. After Dai's destruction by the Former Qin in 376, Tuoba Shiyijian's grandson Tuoba Gui restored the state in 386, adopted the dynastic name Wei, and as Emperor Daowu conquered much of northern China. His successors completed the unification of the north by 439, ending the era of the Sixteen Kingdoms.

Northern Wei and sinicisation

The Tuoba-led Northern Wei became a major patron of Buddhism — the cave temples of Yungang and Longmen date from this era — and its rulers gradually adopted Chinese institutions. The culmination came under Emperor Xiaowen (reigned 471–499), whose reforms moved the capital to Luoyang, promoted Chinese language, dress and surnames at court, and changed the imperial clan's own surname from Tuoba to Yuan in 496. The dynasty split in 534 into Eastern and Western Wei, and the Tuoba/Yuan line faded from power, though descendants remained prominent: the imperial house of the later Tang dynasty claimed Tuoba ancestry through its maternal lines and the Longxi Li lineage traditions.
