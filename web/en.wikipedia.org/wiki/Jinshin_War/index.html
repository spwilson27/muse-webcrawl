Jinshin War

The Jinshin War (壬申の乱, Jinshin no Ran) was a war of imperial succession fought in Japan in the summer of 672, named after the year designation jinshin in the sexagenary cycle. It pitted Prince Ōtomo, the son and designated heir of Emperor Tenji, against Tenji's younger brother Prince Ōama, and ended with Ōama's victory and enthronement as Emperor Tenmu. It was the largest armed conflict of ancient Japan.

Background and course

Emperor Tenji, architect of the centralising Taika Reforms, had appointed his brother Ōama crown prince, but later raised his son Ōtomo to high office, creating a disputed succession. When Tenji died in late 671, Ōama — ostensibly retired to the mountains of Yoshino as a monk — received word that the court at Ōmi (near Lake Biwa) was plotting against him. In June 672 he fled east, gathering support in the provinces, sealed the mountain passes, and raised an army from the eastern lands. Fighting spread along several fronts toward the capital at Ōtsu; Ōama's generals won a series of engagements, and in August Prince Ōtomo, defeated, took his own life, barely eight months after succeeding his father. He is recognised in the official list of emperors as Emperor Kōbun.

Consequences

Emperor Tenmu's reign accelerated the building of a centralised, Chinese-style imperial state: court ranks and cap systems were overhauled, the capital was fixed at Fujiwara-kyō under his successor, land and census reforms advanced, and the official histories culminating in the Nihon Shoki were compiled partly to legitimise the victors' version of events. The war also deepened the militarisation of the eastern provinces from which Ōama had drawn his strength.
