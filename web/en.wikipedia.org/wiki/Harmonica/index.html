The harmonica, also known as a French harp, mouth organ or blues harp, is a free reed wind instrument used worldwide in many musical genres, notably in blues, American folk music, classical music, jazz, country, and rock. The harmonica is played by using the mouth (lips and tongue) to direct air into or out of one or more holes along a mouthpiece. Behind each hole is a chamber containing at least one reed; a reed is a flat, elongated spring typically made of brass, stainless steel, or bronze, secured at one end over a slot that serves as an airway. When the free end of the reed vibrates, it periodically alternately blocks and opens the airway, producing sound.

Types

The two most common types are the diatonic harmonica, tuned to a single major scale and central to folk and blues — where players use "cross harp" (second position) and note bending for expressive effect — and the chromatic harmonica, which has a spring-loaded button that redirects air to a second set of reeds a semitone higher, giving the full chromatic scale. Tremolo, octave, and orchestral bass and chord harmonicas are also made.

History

Free-reed instruments such as the Chinese sheng are ancient; the modern harmonica was developed in Europe in the early 19th century, with Christian Friedrich Ludwig Buschmann of Berlin often credited with an early form (the "Aura", c. 1821). Mass production began in Trossingen, Germany, where Matthias Hohner's company, founded in 1857, made harmonicas a global folk instrument. In the United States the harmonica became central to blues, with players such as Sonny Boy Williamson II, Little Walter — who pioneered amplified harmonica through a microphone and amplifier — and Bob Dylan popularising it further.

Techniques

Players shape pitch and timbre by bending (lowering pitch by altering embouchure), overblowing, tongue-blocking, and hand effects (wah). Harmonica ensembles and orchestras exist, particularly in East Asia, where the instrument is widely taught in schools.
