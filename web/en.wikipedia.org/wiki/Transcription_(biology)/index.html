Transcription (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A diagram of RNA polymerase transcribing a DNA strand into messenger RNA
A diagram of RNA polymerase transcribing a DNA strand into messenger RNA

In genetics, transcription is the process of copying a segment of DNA into RNA — the first step of gene expression. The enzyme RNA polymerase binds a promoter, unwinds the helix and synthesises a complementary RNA strand (messenger RNA for protein-coding genes). In eukaryotes the primary transcript is then processed (capping, splicing, polyadenylation) and exported for translation into protein; regulation of transcription is the cell's principal means of controlling which proteins it makes.

Contents1. Mechanism
2. Reverse and viral variants

Mechanism

Initiation assembles polymerase and general transcription factors at the promoter (aided or blocked by enhancer-bound regulators); elongation proceeds at some 20–70 nucleotides per second with proofreading; termination follows polyadenylation signals in eukaryotes, or hairpin and Rho mechanisms in bacteria. Alternative splicing lets one gene yield many proteins — most human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced.

Reverse and viral variants

Retroviruses invert the flow: reverse transcriptase copies RNA into DNA (the discovery that revised the 'central dogma' and won the 1975 Nobel). Transcriptional dysregulation — oncogene activation, transcription-factor fusions — is a core cancer mechanism, and transcription is the target of drugs from rifampicin to CDK inhibitors.
