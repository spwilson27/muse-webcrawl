Periodic trends

Periodic trends are the patterns in the properties of chemical elements that recur as atomic number increases across the periodic table. They arise from the structure of electron shells and the varying effective nuclear charge felt by valence electrons, and they allow prediction of an element's behaviour from its position.

Principal trends

Atomic radius generally decreases across a period (as nuclear charge rises and pulls electrons inward) and increases down a group (as new shells are added). Ionisation energy and electronegativity generally increase across a period and decrease down a group, reaching maxima near fluorine. Electron affinity becomes more negative across a period, with notable irregularities. Metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period.

Explanations and exceptions

The trends are explained by the competition between increasing nuclear charge, electron shielding by inner shells, and the filling of subshells. Exceptions — for example the ionisation energies of nitrogen versus oxygen, reflecting the stability of half-filled subshells — illustrate the details of electronic configuration. Understanding periodic trends is a foundation of inorganic chemistry and of the periodic law first formulated by Dmitri Mendeleev and others in the 1860s.
