The Mace of the United States House of Representatives, also called the Mace of the Republic, is a ceremonial mace and one of the oldest symbols of the authority of the United States government. It has been used in the House of Representatives since 1789, and the present mace, made in 1842, is carried by the Sergeant at Arms and placed on a pedestal in the House chamber whenever the House is in session.

Description

The current mace is 46 inches (117 cm) long and consists of thirteen ebony rods — representing the original thirteen states — bound together with silver strands twisted into a design evoking the Roman fasces. It is topped by a silver globe surmounted by a silver eagle with outspread wings. It was made by New York silversmith William Adams in 1842, replacing the original mace, which had been destroyed when British troops burned the Capitol in 1814 during the War of 1812. Between 1814 and 1842 a simple wooden mace painted silver was used.

Use

At the beginning of each daily session, the Sergeant at Arms carries the mace into the chamber ahead of the Speaker in a procession, and sets it on its pedestal to the Speaker's right. When the House resolves into the Committee of the Whole, the mace is lowered to a lower position on the pedestal, symbolising that the House is not formally in session.

The mace also serves as a disciplinary symbol: in cases of disorder on the floor, the Sergeant at Arms, on the Speaker's direction, lifts the mace from its pedestal and presents it before the offending members to restore order. This has occasionally been done in modern times, and the image of the mace appears on the seal of the House and the flag of the Sergeant at Arms.
