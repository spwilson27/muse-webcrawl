Romano Guardini (17 February 1885 – 1 October 1968) was an Italian-born German Catholic priest, philosopher and theologian, one of the most important figures in Catholic intellectual life in the 20th century. His writings on liturgy, religious philosophy and the critique of modernity influenced the Second Vatican Council and the thought of later popes, including Benedict XVI and Francis.

Life

Guardini was born in Verona and raised in Mainz, Germany. After abandoning studies in chemistry and economics, he studied theology at Freiburg and Tübingen and was ordained a priest in 1910. His book Vom Geist der Liturgie (The Spirit of the Liturgy, 1918) became a foundational text of the Liturgical Movement. In 1923 he was appointed to a chair of religious philosophy at the University of Berlin, which the Nazi regime forced him to relinquish in 1939; he was also closely involved with the Catholic youth movement at Burg Rothenfels. After the Second World War he taught at Tübingen and then at Munich (1948–1962), drawing large audiences. Pope Paul VI offered to make him a cardinal in 1965, an honour he declined.

Thought

Guardini's work is characterised by the method of "polar opposition" (Gegensatz): reality is held in living tension between poles such as form and life, authority and freedom. His meditations on Jesus, Der Herr (The Lord, 1937), became a classic of modern Christology. In cultural criticism — The End of the Modern World (1950) and Power and Responsibility — he analysed the modern age's technological mastery of nature and warned of power exercised without ethical formation. He also wrote influential studies of Dante, Pascal, Dostoyevsky, Hölderlin and Rilke.

Legacy

Guardini's cause for beatification was opened by the Archdiocese of Munich and Freising in 2017. Pope Francis cited him extensively in the encyclical Laudato si', particularly Guardini's analysis of the "technocratic paradigm".
