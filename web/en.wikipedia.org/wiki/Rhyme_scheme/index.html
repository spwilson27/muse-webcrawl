Rhyme scheme


Illustration placeholder: letter notation for a rhyming stanza

A rhyme scheme is the pattern of rhymes at the end of each line of a poem or song. It is usually referred to by using letters to indicate which lines rhyme; lines designated with the same letter all rhyme with each other.

An example of the ABAB rhyming scheme, from "To Anthea, who may command him Anything", by Robert Herrick:

Bid me to weep, and I will weep        A
While I have eyes to see;              B
And having none, and yet I will keep   A
A heart to weep for thee.              B

Common schemes

  • Alternate rhyme (ABAB) — used in quatrains and in the ballad stanza.
  • Coupled rhyme (AA BB CC…) — consecutive rhyming couplets, as in heroic couplets.
  • Enclosed rhyme (ABBA) — the outer lines rhyme, enclosing a rhymed pair.
  • Monorhyme (AAAA) — all lines share one rhyme, common in Arabic, Persian, and Welsh verse.
  • Chain rhyme (ABA BCB CDC…) — as in terza rima, invented by Dante for the Divine Comedy.
  • Limerick (AABBA) — five anapaestic lines.

Schemes in fixed forms

Sonnet

The Petrarchan sonnet rhymes ABBA ABBA in the octave, followed by a sestet (CDECDE or variants). The Shakespearean sonnet uses three ABAB quatrains and a closing couplet: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. The Spenserian sonnet interlocks its rhymes: ABAB BCBC CDCD EE.

Other forms

The villanelle (as in Dylan Thomas's "Do not go gentle into that good night") repeats two rhymes and two refrains across five tercets and a quatrain (ABA … ABAA). The French ballade uses ABABBCBC stanzas with a refrain. The ottava rima stanza of Byron's Don Juan rhymes ABABABCC; rhyme royal, used by Chaucer, is ABABBCC; the Burns stanza is AAABAB.

Notation variations

Some analysts use lowercase letters or primes (a, a′, b) to distinguish exact from slant rhyme, and "x" to mark unrhymed lines. Rhyme schemes interact with metre and stanza structure to create a poem's formal music; systematic disruption of an expected scheme is itself an expressive device.
