Collocation

In corpus linguistics, a collocation is a series of words or terms that co-occur more often than would be expected by chance. In phraseology, a collocation is a type of compositional phraseme — a predictable pairing such as strong tea or make a decision, where one word (the base) freely selects the meaning and the other (the collocate) is chosen in a restricted way: English speakers "make" a decision rather than "do" one, and tea is "strong", not "powerful".

Types of collocation

  • Adjective + noun: heavy rain, bitter wind, fast food
  • Verb + noun: make progress, take a risk, commit a crime
  • Noun + verb: dogs bark, alarms go off
  • Adverb + adjective: deeply concerned, bitterly cold
  • Verb + adverb: speak fluently, apologise profusely

History of the concept

The term was popularised by the British linguist J. R. Firth in the 1950s, who famously asserted "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." Collocation became a central topic in lexicography — notably in the work of John Sinclair and the COBUILD project — and in corpus linguistics, where statistical measures such as mutual information and t-score are used to identify significant co-occurrence in large text corpora.

In language teaching

Lexical approaches to second-language teaching emphasise that fluency depends heavily on knowing collocations: learners may produce grammatically correct sentences that sound unnatural because the word pairings violate collocational norms. Dictionaries of collocations (e.g. the Oxford Collocations Dictionary) are a standard learner's reference.

Collocation vs. idiom

Unlike idioms, whose meaning cannot be deduced from their parts, collocations are mostly transparent in meaning but restricted in form; they occupy a middle ground on the spectrum between free word combinations and fixed expressions.
