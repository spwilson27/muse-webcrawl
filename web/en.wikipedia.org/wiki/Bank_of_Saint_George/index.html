Bank of Saint George

The Bank of Saint George (Italian: Banco di San Giorgio) was a financial institution of the Republic of Genoa. Founded in 1407, it is often regarded as one of the first modern banks and was among the most powerful institutions in Europe; it managed much of Genoa's public debt, administered colonies including Corsica, and at times effectively governed territories in the Republic's name. It was dissolved during the Napoleonic era, in 1805.

History

The Bank was created by consolidating Genoa's various funded debts (compere) into a single association of creditors, the Casa di San Giorgio. In exchange for loans to the Republic, the Casa received the right to collect specified taxes. Its shares (luoghi) were transferable and traded, making the institution an early example of a corporation with tradable securities.

Because the Republic repeatedly assigned it revenues and territories as security, the Bank came to administer Crimea's Genoese colonies for a period, and ruled Corsica from 1453 to 1562. Niccolò Machiavelli praised the Casa as a "state within the state". Christopher Columbus was among its account-holders; letters of his are preserved in association with the Bank.

Dissolution and legacy

The institution declined with Genoa's fortunes and was suppressed in 1805 under French rule. Historians of finance cite it as a precursor of central banks and of the modern chartered company.
