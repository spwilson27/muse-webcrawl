Martin Buber

Martin Buber (Hebrew: מרטין בובר; February 8, 1878 – June 13, 1965) was an Austrian-born Israeli Jewish philosopher best known for his philosophy of dialogue, a form of existentialism centred on the distinction between the I–Thou relationship and the I–It relationship. In his 1923 work I and Thou (Ich und Du), Buber described this distinction and argued that genuine encounter between persons is the basis of authentic human existence.

Life

Buber was born in Vienna into an Orthodox Jewish family and spent much of his childhood with his grandparents in Lemberg (now Lviv). He studied philosophy at the universities of Vienna, Berlin, Zurich and Leipzig. He became involved in the Zionist movement, editing journals and promoting cultural Zionism and Jewish renewal, and he developed a deep interest in Hasidism, whose tales he retold and interpreted for a Western audience.

Buber taught at the University of Frankfurt from 1924 until the Nazi government forced him from his post in 1933. In 1938 he emigrated to Mandatory Palestine, where he taught at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. He advocated a binational Jewish-Arab state and remained an influential, sometimes controversial, voice in Israeli intellectual life until his death in Jerusalem in 1965.

Philosophy of dialogue

In Buber's account, human beings address the world in two ways. The I–It attitude treats other beings as objects to be used, classified and experienced; it is necessary for practical life but cannot yield fulfilment. The I–Thou relation, by contrast, is a direct, mutual encounter in which the other is met as a subject in their own right. Buber held that the divine can be encountered through such genuine relations, a theme he also developed in his retellings of Hasidic tales and in his Bible translation work with Franz Rosenzweig.
