Bel canto

Bel canto (Italian for "beautiful singing") is a term used both for a style of operatic singing and for the Italian operatic repertoire of the early 19th century associated with it. As a technique it prizes beauty of tone, seamless legato, agility in coloratura passage-work, and expressive phrasing; as a repertoire label it centres on the operas of Gioachino Rossini, Vincenzo Bellini and Gaetano Donizetti.

The technique

Bel canto singing grew out of Italian vocal pedagogy of the 17th and 18th centuries, when castrati and prima donnas displayed feats of breath control, messa di voce (the swelling and diminishing of a sustained note), trills and improvised ornamentation. Training emphasised evenness of registers, a flexible, unforced tone and long-breathed lines. Manuals by teachers such as Manuel García II codified the method in the 19th century.

The repertoire

Rossini's comic and serious operas, Bellini's long-arching melodies in Norma and La sonnambula, and Donizetti's prolific output — including Lucia di Lammermoor with its famous mad scene — define the bel canto canon. Verdi inherited and transformed the tradition, and heavier late-Romantic styles later pushed it aside, until a mid-20th-century revival led by singers such as Maria Callas, Joan Sutherland and Montserrat Caballé restored the operas to the stage.

Meaning and debate

Because the term was applied retrospectively, its precise meaning is contested: some writers use it narrowly for the Rossini–Bellini–Donizetti style, others for Italian art singing generally from the late Baroque onward. All usages, however, contrast bel canto's emphasis on vocal beauty and agility with more declamatory, orchestra-driven styles.
