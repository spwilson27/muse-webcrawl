Third nerve palsy

SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SymptomsDrooping eyelid, "down and out" eye position, double vision, dilated pupil
CausesAneurysm, diabetes, tumor, trauma, stroke, infection

Third nerve palsy, also known as oculomotor nerve palsy, is a disorder of the third cranial nerve (the oculomotor nerve) that impairs eye movement and eyelid control. Because the nerve supplies most of the muscles that move the eye, its paralysis produces a characteristic picture in which the affected eye rests in a "down and out" position.

Signs and symptoms

A complete third nerve palsy causes ptosis (drooping of the upper eyelid, due to paralysis of the levator palpebrae superioris), deviation of the eye downward and outward (because the lateral rectus and superior oblique muscles remain functional), diplopia (double vision), and, when the parasympathetic fibers are involved, a dilated pupil that reacts poorly to light and difficulty with accommodation. Partial palsies may affect only some of these functions.

Causes

Causes include compression by an aneurysm of the posterior communicating artery (a surgical emergency, typically painful and involving the pupil), microvascular ischemia associated with diabetes or hypertension (which classically spares the pupil), tumors, trauma, infection, inflammation, and midbrain stroke. In children, causes include congenital palsy, trauma, and infection.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on the clinical examination, followed by imaging such as MRI or CT angiography to identify aneurysms or other structural lesions, particularly when the pupil is involved. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause; aneurysms require urgent neurosurgical or endovascular intervention. Diplopia may be managed with prisms or patching, and strabismus or ptosis surgery may be considered after spontaneous recovery has plateaued, often after six months or more.
