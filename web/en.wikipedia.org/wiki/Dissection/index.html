Dissection

Dissection
Used inAnatomy, biology education, pathology, forensics
Key figuresGalen; Andreas Vesalius; Herophilos
RelatedAutopsy, vivisection, prosection

Dissection (from Latin dissecare, "to cut to pieces"), also called anatomization, is the dismembering of the body of a deceased animal or plant to study its anatomical structure. In humans, dissection of cadavers has been central to anatomical education since the Renaissance, and dissection remains a core practical exercise in biology and medical training.

History

Systematic human dissection was practised in Hellenistic Alexandria by Herophilos and Erasistratus in the 3rd century BC, then largely abandoned; Galen's anatomy, based mainly on animal dissection, dominated for over a millennium. Public dissections resumed in medieval Italian universities, and Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543), based on careful human dissection, corrected many Galenic errors and founded modern anatomy. Demand for cadavers in the 18th and 19th centuries led to grave-robbing and, in the notorious Burke and Hare murders of 1828, killing for the anatomy trade — prompting legislation such as Britain's Anatomy Act 1832 regulating body supply.

Modern practice

Medical students typically dissect donated bodies under supervision, or study prosections prepared by demonstrators; bodies are obtained through regulated donation programmes. Dissection is also used in schools for studying animals such as frogs and rats, a practice increasingly supplemented or replaced by virtual dissection software, and in pathology and forensic medicine, post-mortem dissection (autopsy) establishes cause of death.
