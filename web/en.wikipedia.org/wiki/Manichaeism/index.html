Manichaeism was a major religion founded in the 3rd century CE in the Sasanian Empire by the Persian prophet Mani (216–274 or 277 CE). It combined elements of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Buddhism into a strongly dualistic cosmology: an eternal struggle between a good, spiritual world of light and an evil, material world of darkness. At its height, Manichaeism spread from Roman North Africa and Spain in the west to China in the east, making it one of the most widespread religions of the ancient world.

Beliefs

Manichaean teaching described a primordial invasion of the realm of light by the Prince of Darkness, as a result of which particles of divine light became trapped in matter. Salvation consisted in liberating that light through knowledge (gnosis), prayer, fasting, and ethical conduct. The community was divided between the Elect, celibate ascetics who avoided harming the light in plants and animals, and the Hearers, lay supporters who provided alms food and could marry and hold property. Jesus, Zoroaster, and the Buddha were honoured as earlier messengers; Mani presented himself as the final "Seal of the Prophets."

History

Mani preached under Shapur I but was imprisoned and died under Bahram I amid Zoroastrian clerical hostility. Missionaries carried the faith along the Silk Road; it became the state religion of the Uyghur Khaganate in 763 CE. In the Roman Empire it was persecuted by Diocletian and the Christian emperors — Augustine of Hippo was a Hearer for nine years before converting and becoming its most famous critic. In China, Manichaeism (as Móníjiào) survived persecutions, influenced secret societies, and left the Cao'an temple in Fujian, the only intact Manichaean site of worship.

Sources

Knowledge of the faith was transformed by 20th-century discoveries of original Manichaean manuscripts at Turpan (Xinjiang), in Egypt (Medinet Madi), and the Cologne Mani Codex, which preserves a biography of the prophet.
