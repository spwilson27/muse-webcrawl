Miscarriage, also known in medical terms as a spontaneous abortion and pregnancy loss, is the death of an embryo or fetus before it is able to survive independently. Miscarriage before 6 weeks of gestation is sometimes termed chemical pregnancy. The most common symptom of a miscarriage is vaginal bleeding with or without pain.

Causes and risk factors

Most miscarriages occur because the fetus is not developing properly. Problems with the baby's chromosomes are responsible for about 50 percent of early pregnancy losses; most chromosome problems occur by chance as the embryo divides and grows. Other risk factors include maternal age, obesity, smoking, alcohol use, chronic conditions such as diabetes and thyroid disease, infections, and structural abnormalities of the uterus.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically involves a pelvic examination, an ultrasound scan, and blood tests for the pregnancy hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). Miscarriage is classified by stage, including threatened, inevitable, incomplete, complete, and missed miscarriage.

Management

Management depends on the type of miscarriage and the woman's condition, and may include expectant management (watchful waiting), medication such as misoprostol to help expel the pregnancy tissue, or a surgical procedure such as dilation and curettage or vacuum aspiration. Emotional support and counselling are important aspects of care, as miscarriage can cause significant psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and grief.

Epidemiology

Miscarriage is the most common complication of early pregnancy. Among women who know they are pregnant, the miscarriage rate is roughly 10 to 20 percent, though the true rate including unrecognised pregnancies is higher. Risk increases with maternal age, being considerably greater for women over 35 and especially over 40.
