Hypervitaminosis A

Hypervitaminosis A
Specialty: Toxicology, endocrinology
Symptoms: Nausea, dizziness, blurred vision, skin peeling, liver damage
Causes: Excess intake of preformed vitamin A (retinol)
Treatment: Discontinuation of vitamin A intake

Hypervitaminosis A refers to the toxic effects of ingesting too much preformed vitamin A (retinoids). Because vitamin A is fat-soluble, it is stored in the liver and can accumulate to toxic levels, unlike water-soluble vitamins which are readily excreted. The condition can be acute (from a single very large dose) or chronic (from prolonged excessive intake).

Signs and symptoms

Acute toxicity produces nausea, vomiting, headache, dizziness, blurred vision, and increased intracranial pressure. Chronic toxicity causes dry skin, hair loss, cracked lips, bone and joint pain, fatigue, liver enlargement, and in severe cases hepatotoxicity and cirrhosis. Excess vitamin A during pregnancy is teratogenic and can cause birth defects of the central nervous system and heart. In children it may cause premature epiphyseal closure and impaired growth.

Causes

Most cases arise from overuse of vitamin A supplements or retinoid medications such as isotretinoin. Dietary causes include eating the liver of certain animals: polar bear and seal liver are so rich in vitamin A that small portions can be toxic — a hazard documented among Arctic explorers. Plant-derived beta-carotene (provitamin A) does not cause hypervitaminosis A because its conversion to retinol is regulated, though excess can cause harmless orange skin discoloration (carotenemia).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on history of excessive intake, clinical features, and elevated serum retinyl esters. Treatment is simply to stop vitamin A intake; symptoms generally resolve over weeks, though liver damage may persist in chronic cases.

History

Vitamin A poisoning was described in accounts of Arctic exploration; the ill-fated Mawson expedition of 1912–13 is often cited, in which Xavier Mertz died after eating sled-dog livers. Similar toxicity affected explorers who consumed polar bear liver.
