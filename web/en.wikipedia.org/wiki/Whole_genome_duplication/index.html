Whole genome duplication

Whole genome duplication (WGD) is an event in which an organism's entire genome is duplicated, producing a polyploid descendant with extra complete sets of chromosomes. Whole genome duplications are considered major events in evolution because the surplus genetic material provides raw material for the evolution of new gene functions.

Schematic of whole genome duplication producing a tetraploid from a diploid ancestor.

Mechanisms

Whole genome duplication can arise through autopolyploidy, duplication within a single species (for example through failure of meiosis producing unreduced gametes), or allopolyploidy, in which genomes from two different species are combined through hybridization followed by chromosome doubling. After duplication, the genome typically undergoes extensive loss of duplicated genes, chromosomal rearrangements, and diploidisation, gradually returning to a functionally diploid state over evolutionary time.

Consequences

Duplicated genes (ohnologs, named after Susumu Ohno, who proposed the importance of gene duplication in evolution in 1970) may be lost, retained unchanged, subfunctionalised so that each copy performs part of the ancestral function, or neofunctionalised so that one copy evolves a new function. Retention is often biased toward genes involved in regulation and development, which may help explain the association between ancient duplications and evolutionary innovation.

Examples

Well-studied whole genome duplications include the two rounds of duplication early in vertebrate evolution (the 2R hypothesis), a further duplication in the ancestor of teleost fish, the ancient duplication in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and numerous events in flowering plants, whose genomes often carry traces of several successive duplications. Many crop plants, including wheat, cotton, and canola, are recent polyploids.
