Friction stir welding

Friction stir welding (FSW) is a solid-state joining process that uses a non-consumable tool to join two facing workpieces without melting the workpiece material. Heat is generated by friction between the rotating tool and the workpiece material, which leads to a softened region near the tool. As the tool is traversed along the joint line, it mechanically intermixes the two pieces of metal and forges the hot, softened metal by the mechanical pressure applied by the tool, much like joining clay or dough.

The process was invented and experimentally proven at The Welding Institute (TWI) in the United Kingdom in December 1991, originally for welding aluminium alloys that are difficult to join by conventional fusion techniques.

Principles of operation

The rotating tool consists of a cylindrical shoulder and a profiled pin (or probe). The pin is inserted into the joint between the two clamped plates until the shoulder contacts the surface. Frictional heating softens the material around the pin without reaching the melting point, and the rotation of the tool stirs this plasticised material from the front to the back of the pin, producing a solid-phase bond as the tool moves forward. On cooling, the weld is left with a characteristic microstructure containing a central stir zone (or nugget), a thermomechanically affected zone and a heat-affected zone.

Advantages and limitations

Because the material never melts, FSW avoids the solidification cracking, porosity and distortion associated with fusion welding, produces joints with good mechanical properties and low residual stress, and requires no filler material, shielding gas or surface cleaning. Its disadvantages include the exit hole left when the tool is withdrawn, the need for rigid clamping and backing support, and slower travel speeds than some arc processes. Welding of high-strength steels and titanium is possible but tool wear is much greater.

Applications

FSW is used extensively in shipbuilding for aluminium deck panels, in the aerospace industry for fuel tanks and fuselage panels — including the Space Shuttle external tank and later SpaceX and NASA vehicles — in railway rolling stock, and in the automotive industry for wheels, suspension components and battery trays for electric vehicles. A related process, friction stir spot welding, is used for lap joints in car body construction.
