St James's Park is a 23-hectare (57-acre) park in the City of Westminster, central London. It is the oldest of the Royal Parks of London. The park lies at the southern end of the St James's area, which was named after a leper hospital dedicated to St James the Less. It is bounded by Buckingham Palace to the west, the Mall to the north, Horse Guards to the east, and Birdcage Walk to the south.

History

The marshy land was acquired by Henry VIII in 1532 and used as a deer park. James I drained and landscaped it and kept exotic animals there, including camels, crocodiles, and an elephant. Charles II had the park redesigned in a more formal French style, opened it to the public, and famously socialised there; the pelicans that still inhabit the park descend from birds presented to him by the Russian ambassador in 1664.

In the 1820s John Nash remodelled the park in a romantic style for George IV, converting the straight canal into a naturalistic lake and creating the layout that survives today. The Blue Bridge across the lake offers a celebrated view between Buckingham Palace and Whitehall.

Features

The park contains St James's Park Lake with two islands, Duck Island and West Island, a playground, and the Tiffany Fountain. It is famous for its waterfowl and its resident colony of pelicans, which are fed daily. The Inn the Park café and the nearby Guards Memorial and Queen Victoria Memorial stand in or beside the park. The park gives its name to St James's Park tube station on the District and Circle lines.
