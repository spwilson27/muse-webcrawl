Regional theater in the United States

Type: Nonprofit professional theatre
Movement began: 1940s–1960s
Key figures: Margo Jones, Zelda Fichandler, Tyrone Guthrie
Peak institutions: Arena Stage, Guthrie Theater, Actors Theatre of Louisville

Regional theater in the United States refers to professional, nonprofit theatre companies located outside New York City that produce their own seasons of plays with resident or regularly engaged artists. The regional theatre movement arose in the mid-twentieth century as an alternative to the commercial Broadway model, aiming to build permanent companies and loyal local audiences across the country.

Origins

Pioneers included Margo Jones, whose Theatre '47 in Dallas (founded 1947) was among the first nonprofit resident theatres; Nina Vance's Alley Theatre in Houston; and Zelda Fichandler, who co-founded Arena Stage in Washington, D.C., in 1950. The movement gained momentum in the 1960s with support from the Ford Foundation and the founding of the Guthrie Theater in Minneapolis in 1963 under Tyrone Guthrie.

Characteristics

Regional theatres typically operate as nonprofits with boards of trustees, subscription seasons, education programmes and a mix of classics, contemporary plays and new work. Many premieres that later transfer to Broadway or win the Pulitzer Prize begin at regional theatres; the Tony Awards recognise their work with the Regional Theatre Tony Award.

Role in American theatre

The movement decentralised American theatre, employed thousands of actors, directors and designers outside New York, and became the principal home for new play development in the United States.
