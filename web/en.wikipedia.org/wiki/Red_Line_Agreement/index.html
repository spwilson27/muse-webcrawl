Red Line Agreement

The Red Line Agreement was an agreement signed by partners in the Turkish Petroleum Company on 31 July 1928. It marked out, with a red line drawn on a map, the boundaries of the former Ottoman Empire in the Middle East, within which the partners agreed not to seek oil concessions independently of one another.

Background

The Turkish Petroleum Company (renamed the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1929) was owned by British, Dutch, French, and American oil interests, together with a 5 percent share held by Calouste Gulbenkian, the Armenian businessman known as "Mr. Five Percent" who had brokered the arrangement. To prevent competition among the partners for the region's oil, the agreement imposed a "self-denying ordinance": no partner could pursue oil interests within the red-line area except through the consortium.

Consequences

The agreement shaped the development of Middle Eastern oil for two decades, restricting independent American ventures in Bahrain and Saudi Arabia, where the line did not fully apply in practice. After the Second World War, American partners sought to escape its restrictions to join the Saudi Aramco partnership, leading to litigation by Gulbenkian and the French; the agreement was effectively abandoned by 1948.
