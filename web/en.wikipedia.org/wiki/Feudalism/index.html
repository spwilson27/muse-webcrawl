Feudalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A medieval manuscript illumination of a vassal kneeling in homage before his lord]

Feudalism is the term historians use — controversially — for the political, military and social system of medieval Europe in which land (the fief) was held by vassals in exchange for service, above all military service, to a lord, in a hierarchy of personal oaths reaching from peasants to kings. Emerging from the fragmentation of Carolingian authority in the 9th and 10th centuries, the system of lords, vassals, manors and serfs structured much of European society until the later Middle Ages. Modern historians debate whether 'feudalism' is a coherent system at all or a later construct (notably since Elizabeth A. R. Brown's and Susan Reynolds' critiques).

Lords, vassals and manors

At the system's core was the commendation ceremony: a vassal knelt, placed his hands in his lord's, and swore fealty, receiving in return a fief — land, a castle, an office or revenues — from which to support himself as a mounted knight. Below the free tenantry, the manor organised peasant labour: serfs, bound to the land, owed labour services and dues to the lord while holding customary plots for their own subsistence. Castles, private war and local justice by lords filled the vacuum left by weak kings.

Decline

From the 12th century, monetary economies, growing towns, professional armies paid in cash and stronger royal administrations eroded feudal tenures; the Black Death's labour scarcity further weakened serfdom in the west (while it hardened in the east). Feudal dues and legal survivals nonetheless persisted in places until the French Revolution abolished 'the feudal regime' in August 1789.
