Chicha

Chicha is a fermented (alcoholic) or non-fermented beverage of Latin America, emerging from the Inca and earlier Andean cultures. It is usually made from a specific kind of yellow maize (jora), but can also be prepared from quinoa, kañiwa, peanut, cassava, palm fruit, potato, oca, chañar or various other materials, depending on the region.

History

Chicha has been consumed in the Andes for millennia; residues resembling chicha have been found at pre-Inca sites. In the Inca Empire, chicha de jora was central to ritual and feasting: it was offered to the gods and ancestors, poured onto the earth for Pachamama, and produced in state facilities by the aclla, chosen women who brewed it for the court and religious ceremonies. Spanish chroniclers described its importance in taxation, reciprocity and celebration.

Preparation

In the traditional method for chicha de jora, yellow maize is germinated to convert its starches to sugars (producing jora), then ground, boiled, and strained. Traditionally the mash was chewed so that enzymes in saliva could break down the starch — a practice largely replaced by malting — and the liquid is fermented in large earthenware vessels for several days to a mildly alcoholic drink of about 1–3% alcohol by volume.

Regional varieties

  • Chicha de jora (Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador) — the classic fermented maize beer.
  • Chicha morada (Peru) — an unfermented, sweet purple drink made from purple maize, pineapple rind, cinnamon and cloves, served cold.
  • Chicha de yuca (Amazonian regions) — made from cassava, traditionally chewed and fermented.
  • Chicha venezolana — a thick, sweet, non-alcoholic rice-based drink flavoured with cinnamon.

The word "chicha" has also lent its name to Peruvian cumbia music (música chicha) and, in Spain, the phrase ni chicha ni limonada ("neither chicha nor lemonade") describes something that is neither one thing nor the other.
