The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (originally The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere) is the longest major poem by the English poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge, written in 1797–98 and published in 1798 in the first edition of Lyrical Ballads, the joint volume with William Wordsworth that launched English Romanticism. A revised version with a marginal gloss appeared in Sibylline Leaves (1817).

Synopsis

An aged mariner detains a wedding guest and tells his tale. His ship, driven south by a storm, became icebound in Antarctic waters until an albatross appeared and led it free — whereupon the Mariner shot the bird with his crossbow. Drought and the deaths of the whole crew followed; the dead sailors' souls passed by, and the Mariner was saved only when, watching water snakes in the moonlight, he blessed them "unaware". The albatross fell from his neck, rain came, and the ship was supernaturally borne home. Condemned to wander the earth retelling his story as penance, he concludes: "He prayeth best, who loveth best / All things both great and small."

Legacy

The poem gave English such expressions as "an albatross around one's neck", "water, water, every where, nor any drop to drink", and "a sadder and a wiser man". Gustave Doré's engraved illustrations (1876) are among the most famous for any English poem. Its themes of guilt, nature and redemption have made it a staple of literary study and a frequent subject of adaptation and allusion in popular culture.
