John Duns Scotus (c. 1266 – 8 November 1308) was a Scottish Catholic priest and Franciscan friar, university professor, philosopher and theologian, one of the three most important philosopher-theologians of Western Europe in the High Middle Ages, together with Thomas Aquinas and William of Ockham. His sophisticated arguments earned him the nickname Doctor Subtilis, the "Subtle Doctor".

Life

Scotus was born at Duns in Berwickshire and joined the Franciscans, studying and later teaching at Oxford and Paris, before moving to Cologne, where he died in 1308. His works include the Ordinatio (his commentary on the Sentences of Peter Lombard), the Tractatus de Primo Principio, and the Quodlibeta.

Philosophy

Scotus argued for the univocity of being: "being" is said in the same sense of God and creatures, against the doctrine of analogy. He developed the concept of haecceity (haecceitas, "thisness"), the property that makes an individual the particular individual it is, and the formal distinction, a distinction midway between real distinction and a mere distinction of reason. In theology he vigorously defended the Immaculate Conception of Mary — a position eventually defined as dogma in 1854 — and held that the will, not the intellect, is primary in God and in human freedom.

Legacy

The school of "Scotism" influenced later Catholic thought for centuries. Ironically, his followers' later perceived pedantry led humanist critics to turn his name into the word "dunce". Pope John Paul II beatified him in 1993.
