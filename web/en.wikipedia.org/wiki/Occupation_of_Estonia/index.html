Occupation of Estonia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The occupation of Estonia refers to the successive occupations of the Republic of Estonia by the Soviet Union (1940–1941), Nazi Germany (1941–1944), and again by the Soviet Union (1944–1991), which annexed the country as the Estonian Soviet Socialist Republic. Estonia, together with Latvia and Lithuania, regards the Soviet annexation as illegal under international law, and many Western states never recognised it de jure.

Background

Estonia declared independence in 1918 and confirmed it by the Treaty of Tartu (1920) with Soviet Russia. The secret protocols of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939 assigned Estonia to the Soviet sphere of influence.

Soviet occupation, 1940–1941

Following an ultimatum and military blockade in June 1940, Soviet forces occupied Estonia; a staged election produced a communist-dominated parliament that proclaimed the Estonian SSR and requested admission to the USSR, which followed in August 1940. Mass repression ensued, culminating in the June deportation of 1941, in which more than 10,000 Estonians were deported to Siberia.

German occupation, 1941–1944

After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, German forces drove the Red Army from Estonia. Estonia was incorporated into Reichskommissariat Ostland. The occupation brought the Holocaust to Estonia's small Jewish community and the conscription of Estonians into German units, while others fought in Finnish and Soviet formations.

Second Soviet occupation, 1944–1991

The Red Army retook Estonia in 1944, including the Battle of Narva and the capture of Tallinn in September. A renewed campaign of repression followed, including the mass deportation of March 1949 (Operation Priboi), in which over 20,000 Estonians were deported. Armed resistance by the Forest Brothers continued into the 1950s. Soviet rule lasted until Estonia restored its independence in August 1991 during the collapse of the USSR; the last Russian troops withdrew in 1994.

Legal status and memory

Estonia's official position, supported by the European Court of Human Rights and many states, is that the country was illegally occupied and that the restored republic of 1991 is legally continuous with the republic of 1918–1940. Russia disputes this characterisation. The Museum of Occupations in Tallinn (now part of the Vabamu Museum) documents the period.
