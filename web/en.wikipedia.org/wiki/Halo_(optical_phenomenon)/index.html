Halo (optical phenomenon)

A halo is an optical phenomenon produced by sunlight (or moonlight) interacting with ice crystals suspended high in the atmosphere, typically in cirrostratus clouds. Halos take many forms — rings, arcs, pillars and bright spots — depending on the shape and orientation of the crystals and the path light takes through them.

Common types

The most frequently seen halo is the 22° halo, a ring of angular radius about 22° around the Sun or Moon, produced by refraction through randomly oriented hexagonal prism crystals; its inner edge is distinctly reddish and no light appears just inside the ring. Rarer is the 46° halo, formed by rays passing through a side face and the base of the crystal. Other phenomena of the halo family include sun dogs (parhelia), the circumzenithal arc, tangent arcs, light pillars and the parhelic circle.

Formation

Hexagonal ice crystals act as tiny prisms. For a prism angle of 60°, minimum deviation is about 22°, which fixes the radius of the common halo; for the 90° angle between a prism face and the basal plane, minimum deviation is about 46°. Whether a given display appears depends on crystal shape (plates, columns, or capped columns), their orientation as they fall, and the altitude of the light source.

In weather lore and history

Because cirrostratus often precedes a warm front, the saying "ring around the Moon means rain soon" has some statistical basis. Halo displays have been recorded since antiquity and were catalogued by chroniclers; complex displays, with multiple arcs, occasionally became famous omens. Aristotle discussed halos in the Meteorology.
