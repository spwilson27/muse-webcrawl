Polyhedron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The five Platonic solids drawn as translucent geometric models]

A polyhedron is a three-dimensional solid with flat polygonal faces, straight edges and vertices — cubes, pyramids, prisms and the regular Platonic solids are examples. The word is Greek for 'many-seated'.

For any convex polyhedron, the numbers of vertices V, edges E and faces F obey Euler's formula V − E + F = 2, discovered by Leonhard Euler around 1750 and one of the first topological theorems. The formula survives generalisation to surfaces of every shape, where the value 2 changes with the genus.

Regular polyhedra

There are exactly five convex regular polyhedra — tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron — known since antiquity and associated with Plato, who used them in his cosmology. Their duals pair up faces and vertices.

Polyhedra appear in architecture (geodesic domes), chemistry (molecular cages) and computer graphics as polygon meshes.
