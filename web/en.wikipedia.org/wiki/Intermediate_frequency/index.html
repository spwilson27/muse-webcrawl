Intermediate frequency

In a superheterodyne receiver, the incoming signal is converted to a fixed intermediate frequency for filtering and amplification.

In communications and electronic engineering, an intermediate frequency (IF) is a frequency to which a carrier wave is shifted as an intermediate step in transmission or reception. The intermediate frequency is created by mixing the carrier signal with a local oscillator signal in a process called heterodyning, resulting in a signal at the difference or beat frequency. Intermediate frequencies are used in superheterodyne radio receivers, in which an incoming signal is shifted to an IF for processing before the final detection of the modulating signal is done.

Reasons for use

Conversion to an intermediate frequency is useful for several reasons. When several stages of filters are used, they can all be set to a fixed frequency, which makes them easier to build and to tune. Lower frequencies also permit transistors and amplifiers to provide higher stable gain. Most importantly, selectivity is easier to achieve at lower frequencies: the fractional bandwidth of a filter is what matters for separating adjacent channels, so a fixed filter of modest fractional bandwidth gives much better selectivity at a low IF than a tunable filter could at the original signal frequency.

Common frequencies

Standard intermediate frequencies include 455 kHz for AM broadcast receivers, 10.7 MHz for FM broadcast receivers, and around 38.9 MHz or 455 kHz (as a second IF) in television receivers. Microwave and radar systems commonly use IFs such as 10, 30, 60, or 70 MHz. Higher first IFs, above the top of the tuning range, help reject image responses, and many receivers use double or triple conversion with a high first IF followed by lower IFs for selectivity.
