Conjoint analysis

A choice-based conjoint task: respondents pick one of several product profiles that differ in attributes such as price and brand.

Conjoint analysis is a survey-based statistical technique used in market research that determines how people value different attributes (feature, function, benefits) that make up an individual product or service. The technique decomposes a product into its constituent attributes and infers the contribution — the part-worth utility — of each attribute level to overall preference from respondents' choices, ratings, or rankings of combined profiles.

The objective of conjoint analysis is to determine what combination of a limited number of attributes is most influential on respondent choice or decision making. A controlled set of potential products or services is shown to respondents, and by analysing how they make choices among these products, the implicit valuation of the individual elements making up the product or service can be determined. These implicit valuations (utilities) can then be used to build market simulators that estimate market share, revenue and even profitability of new designs.

History

Conjoint measurement originated in mathematical psychology in the 1960s, notably in the work of Luce and Tukey (1964). It was introduced to marketing in the early 1970s by Paul Green and V. Srinivasan, who developed the approach in a series of influential papers, and it was commercialised by firms such as Sawtooth Software, which popularised computer-administered interviewing.

Design and variants

A conjoint study proceeds by (1) selecting attributes and levels, (2) constructing profiles according to an experimental design — commonly a fractional factorial or D-efficient design — (3) collecting responses, and (4) estimating part-worth utilities, typically by regression, multinomial logit, or hierarchical Bayes methods.

Major variants include:

  • Choice-based conjoint (CBC) — respondents choose one profile from a set (or none); now the dominant form, analysed with logit models.
  • Adaptive conjoint analysis (ACA) — the interview adapts later questions to earlier answers.
  • Full-profile conjoint — respondents rate or rank complete product descriptions.
  • MaxDiff / best–worst scaling — respondents pick the most and least preferred items in a set, a related method often grouped with conjoint.

Applications

Conjoint analysis is used in product design, pricing research, market segmentation, and forecasting demand for new concepts. Outside marketing it has been applied in healthcare (patient preferences), transport economics, environmental valuation and public policy, where it is often called a discrete choice experiment. Its strengths are that it mimics real trade-off decisions and can price individual features; its weaknesses include sensitivity to the chosen attributes and the assumption that preferences are compensatory and stable.
