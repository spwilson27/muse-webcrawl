The Pentland Firth (Scottish Gaelic: An Caol Arcach, meaning the Orcadian Strait) is a strait in northern Scotland that separates the Orkney Islands from Caithness on the Scottish mainland. It lies between Dunnet Head in Caithness and Tor Ness on the island of Hoy, and between Duncansby Head and South Ronaldsay. The name is presumed to be a corruption of the Old Norse "Petlandsfjǫrðr", meaning "the firth of Pictland".

Tides and hazards

The Pentland Firth is well known for the strength of its tides, which are among the fastest in the world; speeds of up to 16 knots (30 km/h) have been reported, and tidal races with standing waves and whirlpools form where the current meets opposing tides or passes around headlands and islands. Famous tidal races include the "Merry Men of Mey" off St John's Point and races around the island of Stroma and the Pentland Skerries. The Swelkie, a whirlpool off Stroma, features in Norse mythology as the grinding of the sea-quern Grotti.

The combination of strong currents, overfalls, and rapidly changing weather has made the firth dangerous to shipping throughout history, and the coasts are littered with wrecks. Even so, the firth is a major shipping route between the North Atlantic and the North Sea, and is crossed by ferries linking the mainland ports of Scrabster and Gills Bay with Orkney.

Marine life

The firth supports seals, porpoises, dolphins, and occasionally killer whales (orcas), as well as large colonies of seabirds on its cliffs and islands, including puffins, guillemots, and gannets. Basking sharks and minke whales are seen in the summer months.

Tidal power

The very strong tidal streams have made the Pentland Firth one of the most promising sites in the world for tidal stream power generation. The MeyGen project in the Inner Sound, between the island of Stroma and the mainland, is one of the largest planned tidal arrays; its first turbines were installed in 2016 and began exporting power to the grid, and the project has progressively set records for tidal energy generation. The Crown Estate leased several sites around the firth and Orkney waters for wave and tidal development.

Islands

Islands within the firth include Stroma, which was permanently inhabited until the 1960s and is now home only to lighthouse keepers' successors and livestock; Swona, likewise uninhabited and known for its feral herd of cattle; and the Pentland Skerries, site of a lighthouse built in 1794.
