Intentional community


Communal buildings and gardens in an intentional community

An intentional community is a voluntary residential community designed to have a high degree of social cohesion and teamwork. The members of an intentional community typically hold a common social, political, religious or spiritual vision, and often follow an alternative lifestyle. They typically also share responsibilities and property. Intentional communities include collective households, cohousing communities, ecovillages, communes, survivalist retreats, kibbutzim, ashrams and housing cooperatives.

Characteristics

Intentional communities vary widely in purpose, structure and belief. Common features include a shared purpose or mission statement, communal living arrangements or shared facilities, collective decision-making (often by consensus), and some degree of income or resource sharing. New members are usually selected by the community's existing membership, unlike in unintentional communities such as ordinary housing developments.

History

Planned communal living has a long history. Religious communities such as monasteries are early examples. In the 19th century, utopian communities proliferated in Europe and North America, including the Shakers, the Oneida Community (founded 1848), Brook Farm, and the Fourierist phalanxes inspired by Charles Fourier and Robert Owen's New Harmony. In the 20th century, the kibbutz movement in Palestine/Israel, beginning with Degania in 1909, became one of the largest communal movements. The counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s produced thousands of communes, and from the 1990s the ecovillage and cohousing movements revived interest in planned sustainable communities.

Types

  • Ecovillages — communities seeking ecological sustainability, such as Findhorn in Scotland and Auroville in India.
  • Cohousing — private homes clustered around shared facilities, originating in Denmark.
  • Religious communities — including ashrams, monasteries and communities such as the Bruderhof.
  • Communes — communities with a high degree of income and resource sharing, such as Twin Oaks in Virginia.
  • Kibbutzim — collective communities in Israel traditionally based on agriculture.

Governance

Many intentional communities use consensus decision-making; others use democratic voting, sociocracy, or leadership by founders or elders. Research on communes suggests that communities with clear membership processes, shared work requirements and strong shared beliefs tend to last longer.
