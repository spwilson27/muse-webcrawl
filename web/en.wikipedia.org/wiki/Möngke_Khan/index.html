Möngke Khan

Möngke Khan (Mongolian: Мөнх хаан, Mönkh khaan; 1209 – 11 August 1259) was the fourth khagan (Great Khan) of the Mongol Empire, reigning from 1251 to 1259. The eldest son of Tolui and grandson of Genghis Khan, he was the last Great Khan to rule a united empire from the capital at Karakorum. His reign saw administrative reforms, religious tolerance, and the great campaigns of his brothers — Hülegü's conquest of Persia and Baghdad, and Kublai's campaigns against the Song dynasty — that brought the empire close to its greatest extent.

Accession

After the death of Güyük in 1248 and a contested interregnum, the Toluid line — backed by Batu, khan of the Golden Horde, and orchestrated by Möngke's mother Sorghaghtani Beki — secured the throne. Möngke was elected at a kurultai in 1251 and carried out purges of the rival Ögedeid and Chagadaid princes, consolidating Toluid power.

Rule and reforms

Möngke strengthened central administration: he conducted censuses across the empire, reformed taxation, restricted the issuing of paiza (tablets of authority) and the abuse of the yam relay system, fixed paper currency issues, and maintained religious tolerance for Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, and Daoism, hosting a famous interfaith debate at Karakorum in 1254 recorded by the Franciscan envoy William of Rubruck.

Conquests

Möngke dispatched his brother Hülegü westward in 1253; Hülegü destroyed the Nizari Ismaili state (Alamut, 1256) and sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid Caliphate. In the east, Möngke and Kublai conquered the Dali Kingdom in Yunnan (1253) and subjugated Korea and raided Đại Việt. In 1258 Möngke personally led a three-pronged invasion of the Song dynasty. While besieging the mountain fortress of Diaoyu in Sichuan in the summer of 1259, he fell ill and died — probably of dysentery or wounds — on 11 August 1259.

Aftermath

His death without a designated heir triggered the Toluid Civil War (1260–1264) between his brothers Kublai and Ariq Böke, after which the empire fragmented into effectively independent khanates: the Yuan dynasty in China, the Ilkhanate in Persia, the Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia, and the Golden Horde in the western steppe.
