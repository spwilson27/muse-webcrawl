The Society of the Spectacle (French: La société du spectacle) is a 1967 work of philosophy and Marxist critical theory by Guy Debord, a central figure of the Situationist International. Written as a series of 221 numbered theses, the book argues that modern capitalist society has replaced authentic social life with its representation: "All that once was directly lived has become mere representation."

Summary

Debord defines the spectacle not as a collection of images but as "a social relation among people, mediated by images". He traces its development from the degradation of being into having, and from having into merely appearing. The spectacle, he argues, is both an outcome and a goal of the dominant mode of production: it pacifies workers, manufactures needs, and turns citizens into passive spectators of commodity life. Debord distinguishes between the concentrated spectacle of authoritarian regimes and the diffuse spectacle of consumer democracies, which later fuse into what he would call the integrated spectacle.

Influence

Published months before the May 1968 unrest in France, the book became a key text of the era's radical movements, and its slogans appeared on Paris walls. It has since influenced media studies, cultural criticism, and critiques of consumer culture and social media. Debord later expanded the theory in his Comments on the Society of the Spectacle (1988) and adapted the book into a film in 1973.
