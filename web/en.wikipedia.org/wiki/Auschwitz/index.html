Auschwitz (German: Konzentrationslager Auschwitz, pronounced [aʊʃvɪt͡s]; also KL Auschwitz or KZ Auschwitz) was a complex of over 40 concentration and extermination camps operated by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland (in a portion annexed into Germany in 1939) during World War II and the Holocaust. It consisted of Auschwitz I, the main camp (Stammlager) in Oświęcim; Auschwitz II-Birkenau, a concentration and extermination camp with gas chambers; Auschwitz III-Monowitz, a labour camp for the chemical conglomerate IG Farben; and dozens of subcamps.

Of the 1.3 million people sent to Auschwitz, 1.1 million were murdered. The number of victims includes 960,000 Jews (865,000 of whom were gassed on arrival), 74,000 non-Jewish Poles, 21,000 Roma, 15,000 Soviet prisoners of war, and up to 15,000 other Europeans. Those not gassed died of starvation, exhaustion, disease, individual executions, or beatings. Others were killed during medical experiments.

History

The camp was established in mid-1940 in former Polish army barracks on the outskirts of Oświęcim, initially for Polish political prisoners. From 1942 it became the largest of the extermination camps where the "Final Solution to the Jewish question" was carried out, with trains bringing Jews from across German-occupied Europe to be murdered in the Birkenau gas chambers or worked to death. Commandants included Rudolf Höss, Arthur Liebehenschel and Richard Baer.

Liberation and aftermath

As Soviet troops approached in January 1945, the SS evacuated some 58,000 prisoners on death marches westward. On 27 January 1945 the Red Army liberated about 7,000 remaining prisoners; the date is now commemorated worldwide as International Holocaust Remembrance Day. In 1947 Poland founded the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum on the sites of Auschwitz I and II, and in 1979 the camp was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
