Memorylessness

Memorylessness
FieldProbability theory, statistics
Continuous exampleExponential distribution
Discrete exampleGeometric distribution

In probability and statistics, memorylessness is a property of certain probability distributions. It describes situations where the time already spent waiting for an event makes no difference to how much longer the wait will be. A distribution of waiting times T is memoryless if, for all s, t ≥ 0:

P(T > s + t  |  T > s) = P(T > t).

Informally: given that the event has not occurred by time s, the probability that it will take at least t more time units is the same as the original probability of waiting at least t — the process "forgets" the elapsed wait.

Characterization

Among continuous distributions, only the exponential distribution is memoryless; among discrete distributions on the positive integers, only the geometric distribution is memoryless. The memoryless property is equivalent to a constant hazard rate and is closely related to the Markov property of Poisson processes: interarrival times of a Poisson process are independent exponential random variables, so the remaining wait for the next arrival never depends on the past.

Applications and misconceptions

Memorylessness models radioactive decay, component lifetimes under purely random failure, and queueing systems. It also corrects a common intuition error: for a memoryless process, a long wait does not make an event "due" — the expected remaining wait stays constant, unlike human intuition in gambler's-fallacy reasoning.
