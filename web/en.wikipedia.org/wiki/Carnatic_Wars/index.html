The Carnatic Wars were a series of military conflicts in the middle of the 18th century fought in the Carnatic region of South India between the British East India Company and the French East India Company, each allied with rival Indian claimants to local thrones. The wars established British dominance in southern India and crippled French ambitions on the subcontinent.

Background

As the Mughal Empire declined, the semi-autonomous Nawab of the Carnatic and the Nizam of Hyderabad became prizes in succession disputes. European trading companies, based at Madras (British) and Pondicherry (French), increasingly intervened with their disciplined sepoy armies, finding that small European-led forces could defeat much larger Indian armies.

The three wars

First Carnatic War (1746–1748), an extension of the War of the Austrian Succession, saw the French under Joseph François Dupleix capture Madras; the war ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, which restored Madras to Britain. Dupleix's skilled defence of Pondicherry and the demonstration of European military methods transformed Indian politics.

Second Carnatic War (1749–1754) arose from succession disputes in Hyderabad and the Carnatic. Dupleix backed Muzaffar Jang and Chanda Sahib; the British supported Muhammad Ali Wallajah. Robert Clive's celebrated seizure and defence of Arcot (1751) turned the tide. Dupleix was recalled in 1754.

Third Carnatic War (1756–1763), part of the global Seven Years' War, ended with the British victory at Wandiwash (1760) under Eyre Coote and the fall of Pondicherry (1761). The Treaty of Paris (1763) returned the French posts but forbade their fortification, leaving Britain the dominant European power in India.

Consequences

The wars proved that European-trained Indian troops could dominate Indian battlefields, pioneered the companies' system of subsidiary alliances, and set the stage for the British conquest of Bengal and, ultimately, of India.
