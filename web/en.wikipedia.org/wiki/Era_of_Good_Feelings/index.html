Era of Good Feelings

The Era of Good Feelings marked a period in the political history of the United States that reflected a sense of national purpose and a desire for unity among Americans in the aftermath of the War of 1812. The era saw the collapse of the Federalist Party and an end to the bitter partisan disputes between it and the dominant Democratic-Republican Party during the First Party System.

The era is closely associated with the presidency of James Monroe, whose two terms (1817–1825) largely spanned it. The phrase "Era of Good Feelings" was coined by the Boston newspaper the Columbian Centinel on July 12, 1817, following Monroe's goodwill tour of New England.

Background

The Federalist Party had been discredited by its opposition to the War of 1812 and the Hartford Convention of 1814–15, which was widely perceived as disloyal. After the war, a surge of nationalism led to policies such as the chartering of the Second Bank of the United States, protective tariffs, and internal improvements — a programme later known as the American System.

Politics of the era

With only one national political party, political conflict shifted to factional and sectional disputes within the Democratic-Republicans. Beneath the surface harmony, serious issues divided the country: the Panic of 1819 caused widespread economic hardship and resentment toward the national bank, while the Missouri Crisis of 1819–1821 exposed the explosive sectional controversy over the expansion of slavery, resolved temporarily by the Missouri Compromise.

End of the era

The era ended with the disputed presidential election of 1824, in which four Democratic-Republican candidates split the vote; the House of Representatives chose John Quincy Adams, whose alleged "corrupt bargain" with Henry Clay embittered supporters of Andrew Jackson and gave rise to the Second Party System.
