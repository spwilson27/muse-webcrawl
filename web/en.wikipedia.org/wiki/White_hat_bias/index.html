White hat bias

White hat bias is a phrase coined by public health researchers David Allison and Mark Cope to describe a bias in scientific research and reporting that is motivated by a perceived righteous cause — bias that is distorted, exaggerated, or selectively presented in service of what the researchers regard as a noble public-health goal. The term plays on the "white hat" worn by the hero in classic Western films.

The "white hat" of the Western hero gives the bias its name

Origin

Allison and Cope introduced the term in a 2010 paper in the International Journal of Obesity, documenting instances in the obesity-research literature in which findings unfavourable to the prevailing narrative (for example, about sugar-sweetened beverages and obesity) were misreported, and favourable findings were embellished, in ways that appeared driven by researchers' and reporters' commitment to a worthy cause rather than by commercial interest.

Forms

White hat bias has been described as operating through selective citation, misreporting of results, exaggeration of weak findings in press releases and media coverage, and undue credulity toward studies supporting the favoured position combined with hyper-scrutiny of contradictory studies. Its proponents argue it differs from ordinary publication bias and industry-funded bias in that the distorting incentive is moral rather than financial, which can make it harder to recognise and correct.
