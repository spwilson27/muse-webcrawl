Money market fund


Total US money market fund assets over time.

A money market fund (also called a money market mutual fund) is an open-end mutual fund that invests in short-term debt securities such as US Treasury bills and commercial paper. Money market funds are managed with the goal of maintaining a stable net asset value (NAV), historically of $1 per share in the United States, while paying dividends to investors.

History

The first money market fund, the Reserve Fund, was founded in 1971 by Bruce R. Bent and Henry B. R. Brown. Money market funds grew rapidly in the late 1970s and early 1980s as they allowed small investors access to money-market yields higher than regulated bank deposit rates.

Regulation and risks

In the US, money market funds are regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission under Rule 2a-7 of the Investment Company Act of 1940, which restricts the maturity, quality and diversification of holdings. US funds hold securities with an average maturity measured in weeks.

Money market funds are not insured by the FDIC and, though designed to be stable, can lose value — "breaking the buck" — as the Reserve Primary Fund did in September 2008 after writing down Lehman Brothers paper during the financial crisis, an event that prompted a run on prime funds and a temporary Treasury guarantee programme. Reforms in 2014 required institutional prime funds to adopt a floating NAV and allowed redemption gates and liquidity fees.

Types

  • Government funds — invest in government securities and repos backed by them.
  • Prime funds — invest in corporate and bank debt as well.
  • Municipal (tax-exempt) funds — invest in short-term municipal obligations.
