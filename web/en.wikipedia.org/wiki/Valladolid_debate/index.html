Valladolid debate

The Valladolid debate (1550–1551) was the first moral debate in European history to discuss the rights and treatment of Indigenous peoples by European colonisers. Held at the Colegio de San Gregorio in Valladolid, Spain, it pitted the Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas, defender of the Indians, against the scholar Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, who argued for their subjugation. Convened by Emperor Charles V, who had suspended the conquests while it proceeded, it is a landmark in the development of international law and human rights thought.

The opposing cases

Sepúlveda, in his treatise Democrates Alter, argued on Aristotelian grounds that the peoples of the Americas were "natural slaves", barbarians whose human sacrifice and cannibalism justified war and Spanish dominion, and whose condition required tutelage. Las Casas, who had witnessed the destruction of the Caribbean populations, countered in his vast Apología that the Indigenous peoples possessed fully rational societies, laws and religion, that they had every right to their lands and self-government, and that conversion must come only by peaceful persuasion.

Outcome and significance

The junta of theologians and jurors issued no clear verdict, and both sides claimed victory. Spanish conquest continued, though the debate strengthened the moral and legal critique of empire, building on the earlier New Laws of 1542 and the papal bull Sublimis Deus (1537), which had affirmed the humanity and rights of the Indigenous peoples. Historians regard Valladolid as an extraordinary event: an empire formally putting its own colonial project on trial.
