Billy Wilder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Billy Wilder on the set of Some Like It Hot (1959), conferring with Marilyn Monroe between takes]

Billy Wilder (born Samuel Wilder; 22 June 1906 – 27 March 2002) was an Austrian-born American film director and screenwriter, one of Hollywood's most brilliant and consistently funny filmmakers. Across six decades he mastered every genre he touched: film noir (Double Indemnity, 1944), drama (The Lost Weekend, 1945; Sunset Boulevard, 1950), war (Stalag 17, 1953), romance (Sabrina, 1954; The Apartment, 1960) and comedy (Some Like It Hot, 1959, routinely voted the greatest comedy ever filmed).

Born in Sucha, Austria-Hungary, Wilder was a journalist and screenwriter in Berlin (he co-wrote People on Sunday, 1930) before fleeing the Nazis in 1933; his mother, stepfather and grandmother died in the Holocaust, a darkness his comedies never quite hide. In Hollywood he first wrote (with Charles Brackett) for Lubitsch and Hawks before directing from 1942.

Method and masterpieces

Wilder's creed — entertain first, smuggle the bitterness in the jokes — produced scripts of clockwork construction ('If there's anything I can do...' is less famous than his working rule that audiences must never be bored, paraphrased here from his many formulations). Sunset Boulevard's dead narrator, Double Indemnity's confession-dictaphone frame, The Apartment's humane farce of corporate adultery: each reinvented its genre's possibilities. He won six Academy Awards across writing and directing.

Late career and legacy

The later films (Irma la Douce, 1963; The Private Life of Sherlock Holmes, 1970, a flop now beloved; Fedora, 1978) kept his bite as fashion moved on. The AFI Life Achievement Award (1986) and a century's worth of one-liners — his advice to actors and writers is quoted in every film school — mark his standing: the émigré who became Hollywood's conscience and clown in one person.
