The Iberian lynx (Lynx pardinus) is a wild cat species endemic to the Iberian Peninsula in southwestern Europe. It is distinguished from the Eurasian lynx by its smaller size, heavily spotted coat, long "beard" of facial hair, and prominent black ear tufts. It feeds almost exclusively on the European rabbit, which makes up the great majority of its diet.

Decline and recovery

Once distributed across the whole peninsula, the Iberian lynx crashed during the twentieth century owing to habitat loss, roadkill, and especially the collapse of rabbit populations caused by myxomatosis and rabbit haemorrhagic disease. By 2002 only about 94 individuals survived in two isolated populations in Andalusia, and the species was listed as Critically Endangered — often described as the world's most endangered cat.

Intensive conservation, including captive breeding, reintroduction into former range in Spain and Portugal, rabbit restocking, and road-mortality measures, produced one of the most celebrated carnivore recoveries: the population climbed above 2,000 individuals by the early 2020s, and in 2024 the IUCN improved its status from Endangered to Vulnerable.

Behaviour

Iberian lynxes are solitary and territorial, active mainly at dawn and dusk, and inhabit Mediterranean scrubland mosaics of dense cover and open pasture. Females give birth to litters of typically two or three kittens in early spring.
