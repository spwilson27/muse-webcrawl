Mescaline

IUPAC name2-(3,4,5-trimethoxyphenyl)ethanamine
FormulaC11H17NO3
ClassPsychedelic phenethylamine
SourcesPeyote, San Pedro, Peruvian torch cacti

Mescaline, also known as 3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine, is a naturally occurring psychedelic protoalkaloid of the substituted phenethylamine class, found in the peyote cactus (Lophophora williamsii), the San Pedro cactus (Trichocereus macrogonus var. pachanoi), and the Peruvian torch cactus. It produces effects similar to those of LSD and psilocybin, including altered perception, vivid visual phenomena, and changes in thought and mood.

History

Mescaline-containing cacti have been used by Indigenous peoples of the Americas for thousands of years; archaeological evidence from Texas indicates peyote use more than 5,000 years ago. The alkaloid was first isolated and identified in 1897 by the German chemist Arthur Heffter, and first synthesized in 1919 by Ernst Späth. Mescaline was the subject of early psychopharmacological research and inspired Aldous Huxley's 1954 essay The Doors of Perception, an account of his mescaline experience supervised by Humphry Osmond. In the United States, ceremonial peyote use is protected for members of the Native American Church.

Pharmacology

Mescaline binds to and activates serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, the mechanism shared by most classic psychedelics, with additional activity at 5-HT2C and adrenergic receptors. A typical oral dose of the hydrochloride salt is 200–400 mg, with effects lasting 10–12 hours. It is metabolised in the liver, in part to trimethoxyphenylacetic acid, and excreted largely unchanged in urine. Mescaline is comparatively low in potency among psychedelics, requiring doses in the hundreds of milligrams rather than micrograms.

Legal status

Mescaline is listed in Schedule I of the United States Controlled Substances Act and in Schedule I of the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971; the peyote cactus itself is exempted in the United States for bona fide religious use by Native American Church members. Possession and sale are prohibited in most countries, though some jurisdictions permit the cultivation of mescaline-containing ornamental cacti.
