Francis Bacon (artist)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Three Studies for Figures at the Base of a Crucifixion (c. 1944): three contorted orange-ground creatures, Bacon's breakthrough triptych]

Francis Bacon (28 October 1909 – 28 April 1992) was an Irish-born British figurative painter, among the most acclaimed and disturbing artists of the twentieth century. Self-taught after an unsettled youth in Ireland and London, he painted the human figure twisted, isolated and screaming — in cage-like spaces, as meat, as popes after Velázquez — with a rawness that made him the dark counterpart to post-war abstraction.

His key works include Three Studies for Figures at the Base of a Crucifixion (c. 1944), the Head series and the 'screaming popes' after Velázquez's Innocent X (from 1949), Three Studies of Lucian Freud (1969) — which in 2013 set a world auction record for a work of art — and the triptychs memorialising his lover George Dyer.

Emergence

Bacon destroyed most of his pre-1944 work and considered the Three Studies, shown in 1945 to a shocked London, his true beginning. Working from photographs, film stills and medical books rather than models, he gambled, drank and painted in legendary chaos at 7 Reece Mews, South Kensington — a studio preserved, contents and all, at Dublin's Hugh Lane gallery.

Themes and reputation

Crucifixions, cruciform meat carcasses, caged businessmen and popes whose mouths open in a scream drawn from Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin: Bacon insisted he sought only 'the brutality of fact'. Long the painter's painter of Soho's Colony Room circle, he is now a blue-chip institution; Three Studies of Lucian Freud sold for $142.4 million in 2013, then a record for art at auction.
