Displacement (fluid)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid mechanics, displacement occurs when an object is largely immersed in a fluid, pushing it out of the way and taking its place. The volume of the fluid displaced can then be measured, and from this, the volume of the immersed object can be deduced: the volume of the immersed object will be exactly equal to the volume of the displaced fluid.

An object that sinks displaces an amount of fluid equal to the object's volume. Thus buoyancy is expressed through Archimedes' principle, which states that the weight of the object is reduced by its volume multiplied by the density of the fluid. If the weight of the object is less than the weight of the displaced fluid, the object rises — a phenomenon known as buoyancy; if more, it sinks. An object that floats, such as a ship, displaces an amount of fluid whose weight equals its own weight (see displacement (ship)).

Measurement

Displacement measurement is a classic method of determining the volume of an irregular solid: the object is submerged in a graduated vessel and the rise in fluid level is read off. A vessel designed for this purpose, with an overflow spout, is called a displacement can or Eureka can. The method also gives the density of the object when combined with a weighing.

Applications

  • Determining the volume and density of irregular solids in laboratory work.
  • Measuring body volume (and hence body composition) by water or air displacement plethysmography.
  • Ship tonnage: a floating vessel's displacement equals its total mass.
