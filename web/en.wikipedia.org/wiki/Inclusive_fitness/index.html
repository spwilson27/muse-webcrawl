Inclusive fitness

Inclusive fitness is a term in evolutionary biology that describes an organism's evolutionary success based on both its own reproductive success and the reproductive success of its genetic relatives. The concept was proposed by W. D. Hamilton in 1964 as a way to explain the evolution of altruistic behaviour — actions that reduce an individual's own direct fitness while increasing the fitness of relatives who share copies of the actor's genes.

Overview

Inclusive fitness is generally divided into two components: direct fitness, gained through one's own offspring, and indirect fitness, gained through aiding relatives to reproduce. Because relatives share genes by common descent, a gene causing an individual to help its kin can spread even when helping carries a personal cost.

Hamilton's rule

The theory is summarised by Hamilton's rule, which states that a social behaviour will be favoured by natural selection when:

rB > C

where r is the coefficient of relatedness between actor and recipient, B is the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and C is the reproductive cost to the actor. The rule provides a predictive framework for when altruism, cooperation, spite, and selfishness should evolve.

Kin selection

Inclusive fitness theory underlies kin selection, the evolutionary strategy that favours the reproductive success of an organism's relatives, even at a cost to the organism's own survival and reproduction. Classic applications include the sterile worker castes of eusocial insects (ants, bees, wasps, and termites), alarm calling in ground squirrels, and cooperative breeding in birds and mammals.

History and debate

J. B. S. Haldane is said to have joked that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins — an informal statement of the relatedness arithmetic Hamilton later formalised. Hamilton's 1964 papers laid the foundation of the field of sociobiology. In 2010, a paper by Martin Nowak, Corina Tarnita, and E. O. Wilson challenged the primacy of inclusive fitness theory, sparking a prominent scientific controversy; many researchers defended the framework, and it remains a central accounting method in evolutionary biology, partly because inclusive fitness and multilevel selection can often be shown to be mathematically compatible descriptions of the same processes.
