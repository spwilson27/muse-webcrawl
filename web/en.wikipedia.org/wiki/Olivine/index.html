The mineral olivine (also called chrysolite and, when gem-quality, peridot) is a magnesium iron silicate with the chemical formula (Mg,Fe)2SiO4. It is a type of nesosilicate or orthosilicate. The primary component of Earth's upper mantle, it is a common mineral in Earth's subsurface, but weathers quickly on the surface. The ratio of magnesium to iron varies between the two endmembers of the solid solution series: forsterite (Mg-endmember) and fayalite (Fe-endmember). The name "olivine" comes from the mineral's usual olive-green colour.

Occurrence

Olivine is one of the most common minerals in the Earth by volume, and is the major constituent of the upper mantle. It crystallizes early from mafic and ultramafic magmas and is abundant in basalt, gabbro, peridotite, and dunite. It has also been identified in meteorites, in lunar and Martian dust, and in the dust of comets; the NASA Stardust mission returned crystalline olivine from comet Wild 2. Olivine-rich mantle xenoliths are frequently carried to the surface in kimberlites and alkali basalts.

Properties and structure

Olivine has an orthorhombic crystal structure consisting of isolated SiO4 tetrahedra linked by divalent cations (Mg2+ or Fe2+) in octahedral sites. It has poor cleavage, conchoidal fracture, and a hardness of 6.5–7 on the Mohs scale. The specific gravity increases with iron content, from about 3.2 for forsterite to 4.4 for fayalite. Olivine weathers readily to serpentine, iddingsite, and iron oxides in the presence of water and carbon dioxide.

Uses

Transparent olive-green olivine is used as the gemstone peridot, the birthstone for August. Industrially, olivine is used as a refractory material in foundry sand and furnace linings, as slag conditioner in steelmaking, and in Norway and elsewhere as a source of magnesium compounds. Olivine weathering has also been proposed as a means of carbon sequestration: finely ground olivine spread on beaches or fields reacts with CO2 to form stable carbonates.
