Norwegian folk music

Norwegian folk music is the traditional music of Norway, performed for both listening and dancing. It divides broadly into vocal music—ballads, hymns, work songs, and the wordless stev—and instrumental dance music played most characteristically on the Hardanger fiddle. Regional traditions vary strongly, with valleys such as Setesdal, Telemark, Hallingdal, and Valdres each maintaining distinctive repertoires.

The Hardanger fiddle, with its sympathetic strings and ornate decoration

Instruments

The Hardanger fiddle (hardingfele), developed in the 17th–18th centuries in western Norway, has four or five understrings that resonate sympathetically and rich mother-of-pearl and ink decoration. Dance tunes are also played on the ordinary fiddle, accordion, and clarinet. Older instruments include the langeleik (a fretted zither), the bukkehorn (goat horn), the seljefløyte (willow flute), and the lur. The Sami joik, a distinct vocal tradition of northern Norway, is one of Europe's oldest song forms.

Dance forms

The main dance tune types are the springar (an asymmetrical triple-time couple dance), the gangar, the halling (a vigorous solo men's dance involving acrobatic kicks at a hat held on a stick), and the rull. Listening pieces (lydarslåttar) include the evocative Fanitullen. Composers such as Edvard Grieg drew heavily on folk melodies collected by L. M. Lindeman, and fiddlers like Myllarguten (Torgeir Augundsson) became national figures. Today the tradition is sustained by local clubs (spelemannslag), competitions such as the Landskappleiken, and artists who fuse folk with jazz and world music.
