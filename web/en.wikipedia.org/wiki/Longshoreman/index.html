Longshoreman

A longshoreman is a worker who loads and unloads ships in a port. The term derives from "longshore", meaning along the shore — originally "longshore-men" were the men who worked along the shore handling cargo. In British usage the equivalent term is docker or stevedore; strictly, a stevedore was historically the contractor or foreman who employed the gangs of longshoremen, though the terms are often used interchangeably.

Work and organisation

Before containerisation, longshoremen moved break-bulk cargo by hand, hook, sling and pallet — dangerous, casual labour often hired by the day through the "shape-up", in which men gathered at the dock gate hoping to be picked. The occupation became strongly unionised: in the United States the International Longshoremen's Association (ILA) organised the East and Gulf coasts, while the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU), led for decades by Harry Bridges, organised the West Coast after the great 1934 West Coast waterfront strike. The 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement on the West Coast accepted containerisation in exchange for job guarantees, transforming the trade.

Today

Modern longshoremen operate ship-to-shore cranes, straddle carriers and terminal tractors, lash containers, and handle lines and cargo documentation. Employment is far smaller than in the break-bulk era but remains strongly unionised and relatively well paid. The word should not be confused with longshore drift, a coastal process.
