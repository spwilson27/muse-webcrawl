Sanctuary movement

Period: c. 1980 – early 1990s; revived 2000s–present

Key figures: Jim Corbett, John Fife

Focus: Shelter for refugees fleeing violence in Central America

The Sanctuary movement was a religious and political campaign in the United States beginning in the early 1980s to provide safe haven for Central American refugees fleeing civil wars in El Salvador and Guatemala. Rooted in the ancient tradition of religious sanctuary and in liberation theology, hundreds of churches and synagogues publicly sheltered asylum seekers whom the U.S. government generally classified as economic migrants and sought to deport.

History

The movement began on the Arizona border, where rancher and Quaker Jim Corbett and others aided Salvadoran refugees crossing from Mexico. On 24 March 1982, Southside Presbyterian Church in Tucson, led by John Fife, publicly declared itself a sanctuary — the movement's public launch. At its height, more than 500 congregations participated, operating an "underground railroad" that moved refugees to sanctuary sites across the country.

Government response and trials

Federal authorities infiltrated the movement, and in 1985–86 sixteen sanctuary workers were tried in Tucson on charges of smuggling and harbouring; eight were convicted, receiving probation. The movement's legal strategy culminated in the 1990 settlement of American Baptist Churches v. Thornburgh, which required the government to re-adjudicate Salvadoran and Guatemalan asylum claims without political bias, and Congress granted Temporary Protected Status to Salvadorans in 1990.

New Sanctuary Movement

In 2007 activists launched a New Sanctuary Movement responding to workplace raids and deportations of undocumented immigrants, and faith communities again offered physical sanctuary to people facing removal in the following decades, influencing municipal sanctuary city policies.
