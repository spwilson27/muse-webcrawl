Choral music

Choral music, in its broadest sense, is music written specifically for a choir — an ensemble of singers divided into voice parts, typically soprano, alto, tenor and bass (SATB) — as opposed to music written for a single voice per part. Choirs may sing unaccompanied (a cappella) or with instruments, and choral music forms a major part of the Western classical tradition as well as of many religious and folk traditions worldwide.

History

Organised choral singing grew out of medieval liturgical chant; the development of polyphony in the Notre Dame school and the Renaissance produced the motets and masses of composers such as Josquin des Prez, Palestrina and Byrd. The Baroque era added large-scale works with orchestra, including Handel's Messiah and Bach's Passions and Mass in B minor. The 19th century saw the rise of amateur choral societies and oratorio festivals, and the 20th century expanded the repertoire from part-songs and spirituals to avant-garde works using extended vocal techniques.

Types of choir

Mixed choirs (SATB) are most common; there are also male-voice, female-voice, children's and youth choirs, chamber choirs of a few dozen singers, and symphony choruses numbering over a hundred. School, church, community and professional choirs sustain an extensive amateur and professional ecosystem.

Repertoire and forms

Principal choral forms include the mass, requiem, motet, anthem, cantata, oratorio, madrigal and part-song. Large works such as Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, Mahler's Eighth Symphony ("Symphony of a Thousand") and Britten's War Requiem combine choir with soloists and orchestra.
