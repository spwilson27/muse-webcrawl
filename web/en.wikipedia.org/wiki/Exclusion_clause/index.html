Exclusion clause

An exclusion clause (or exemption clause) is a term of a contract that seeks to exclude or limit one party's liability for breach, negligence, or other specified risks. Such clauses are common in commercial agreements, insurance policies, tickets, and standard-form consumer contracts, and they attract special scrutiny because of the potential for unfair surprise or imbalance of bargaining power.

Common law controls

At common law, an exclusion clause must first be incorporated into the contract — by signature, by reasonable notice before contracting (the more onerous the clause, the more explicit the notice required, per cases such as Interfoto v Stiletto), or by a course of dealing. Even then, courts construe ambiguities contra proferentem — against the party relying on the clause — and historically demanded very clear words before a clause would be read to exclude liability for negligence or fundamental breach.

Statutory regulation

In the United Kingdom, the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 renders ineffective clauses excluding liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence, and subjects many other exclusions to a reasonableness test; the Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies a fairness test to consumer contracts. Similar protections exist across the European Union (Unfair Terms Directive 93/13/EEC) and in many other jurisdictions. In insurance law, "exclusions" define risks the policy does not cover, and are often the focus of coverage disputes.
