Tin

Tin is the chemical element with the symbol Sn (from Latin stannum) and atomic number 50. It is a soft, silvery, malleable metal with a low melting point (232 °C), famous for alloying with copper to make bronze — the alloy that named the Bronze Age.

Tin resists corrosion, so steel food cans are coated with a whisper of tin ('tin cans'), and tin-lead solder joins electronics. Below 13 °C pure tin can crumble into grey powder — 'tin pest' — which may have plagued organ pipes and, legend says, Napoleon's army buttons.

History

Tin has been mined since about 3000 BCE; Cornwall and the Malay Peninsula were historic sources. The Romans imported it from Britain, and control of tin trade routes shaped ancient commerce.

Allotropes

Ordinary white tin (β-tin) is metallic and stable at room temperature; below 13.2 °C it slowly converts to brittle grey α-tin. Bending a tin bar produces a crackling 'tin cry' as its crystals twin.
