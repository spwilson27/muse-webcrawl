History of the socialist movement in the United States

The history of the socialist movement in the United States spans from early nineteenth-century utopian communities to the Socialist Party of America, the communist movements of the twentieth century, and the renewed democratic socialist organising of the twenty-first century. American socialism drew heavily on European immigrant traditions as well as native currents of labour protest and reform.

Utopian beginnings

In the 1820s–1840s, followers of Robert Owen and Charles Fourier founded communal experiments such as New Harmony, Indiana, and Brook Farm. Although short-lived, these communities introduced socialist ideas to American audiences. After the Civil War, German immigrants fleeing Bismarck's anti-socialist laws brought Marxist politics to cities such as New York, Chicago and Milwaukee.

The Socialist Party era

The Socialist Party of America, founded in 1901, united Eugene V. Debs's followers with other factions. Debs won about 6 percent of the presidential vote in 1912, and the party elected hundreds of local officials, including "sewer socialist" mayors in Milwaukee. Socialists were influential in garment unions and published widely read newspapers such as the Appeal to Reason.

Repression, communism and the New Deal

American opposition to the First World War brought Espionage Act prosecutions and Debs's imprisonment. The Bolshevik Revolution split the movement in 1919, producing rival communist parties. During the Great Depression, communists organised the unemployed and helped build the CIO, while many socialist proposals were absorbed into Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Norman Thomas carried the Socialist Party banner through the 1930s and 1940s as its vote steadily declined.

Cold War decline and later revivals

McCarthyism, anti-communist union purges and post-war prosperity shrank the organised left. Socialist ideas nonetheless influenced the civil rights and anti-war movements; Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) helped inspire the War on Poverty. In the twenty-first century, the Democratic Socialists of America grew rapidly after 2016, and self-described democratic socialists such as Bernie Sanders mounted major presidential campaigns.
