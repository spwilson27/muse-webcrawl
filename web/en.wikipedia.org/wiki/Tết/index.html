Tết

Tết, short for Tết Nguyên Đán, is the Vietnamese Lunar New Year festival and the most important celebration in Vietnamese culture. The word is a shortened form of Tết Nguyên Đán, "Festival of the First Morning of the First Day". Tết falls on the first day of the Vietnamese lunar calendar, usually in January or February, and celebrates the arrival of spring.

Customs

Preparations begin weeks ahead: houses are cleaned and decorated with peach blossom (in the north) or apricot blossom (in the south), kumquat trees, and calligraphy scrolls. Families cook traditional foods, above all bánh chưng (square sticky-rice cakes with mung bean and pork) in the north and bánh tét in the south. On New Year's Eve and the following days, families honour their ancestors at home altars, visit relatives and teachers, wish elders health and prosperity, and give children lucky money (lì xì) in red envelopes. The first visitor of the year (xông đất) is believed to set the family's fortune.

Significance

Tết is a time of family reunion: millions travel to their home towns, and businesses close for a public holiday lasting about a week. It is also a season of remembrance of ancestors and of renewal — debts are settled and past misfortunes left behind. Tết shares its date with the Chinese New Year but has distinctly Vietnamese customs, foods, and legends, such as the tale of the Kitchen Gods (Ông Táo) who ascend to heaven a week before the festival.
