Mark-to-market accounting

Mark-to-market (MTM or M2M) or fair value accounting is an accounting practice in which assets and liabilities are recorded at their current market value rather than their historical cost.

Principles

Under fair value standards such as IFRS 13 and US GAAP's FAS 157, financial instruments are valued using a hierarchy of inputs: quoted prices in active markets (Level 1), observable inputs for similar assets (Level 2), and unobservable model-based inputs (Level 3, sometimes called "mark-to-model").

Controversies

Critics argue mark-to-market can amplify boom–bust cycles: rising prices inflate balance sheets and profits in booms, while forced writedowns in illiquid markets can trigger fire sales and contagion, a debate that intensified during the Enron scandal and the 2008 financial crisis. Supporters counter that it gives investors timely, transparent information and that historical cost hides losses.

History

The practice became widespread in US securities and derivatives markets in the 1990s, and Enron's aggressive use of mark-to-market on long-term energy contracts became emblematic of its accounting manipulation before its 2001 collapse.
