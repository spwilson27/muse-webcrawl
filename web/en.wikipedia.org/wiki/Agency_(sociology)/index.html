Agency (sociology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Agency and structure are central concepts in sociological theory

In sociology, agency is the capacity of individuals to act independently and to make their own free choices. By contrast, structure refers to the recurrent patterned arrangements — such as social class, religion, gender, ethnicity, and institutions — that influence or limit the choices and opportunities available to individuals.

The debate over the relative importance of agency and structure, often framed as the "structure and agency problem", concerns whether social life is primarily shaped by individual initiative or by social forces beyond individual control. If structure is viewed as determining behaviour, agency may be reduced to little more than a product of socialization; if agency is emphasized, individuals are seen as creative actors who remake the social world.

Definitions

Agency has been defined in several overlapping ways. In the work of Anthony Giddens, agency refers to the capacity of individuals to intervene in the world, or to refrain from such intervention, with the effect of influencing a specific process or state of affairs. Giddens's theory of structuration holds that structure and agency are a duality: social structures are both the medium and the outcome of the practices they recursively organize.

Pierre Bourdieu approached the problem through the concept of habitus, the durable dispositions acquired through socialization, which generate practices that are neither wholly free nor wholly determined. Margaret Archer has criticized structuration theory and defended an analytical dualism in which structure and agency operate over time in a "morphogenetic" sequence.

Types of agency

Individual and collective agency

Agency may be exercised by individuals or by collectivities such as social movements, organizations, and states. Collective agency raises additional questions about how individual intentions combine into group action.

Temporal orientations

Some theorists distinguish agency directed toward habit and the past (iteration), toward the future (projectivity), and toward present judgement in changing circumstances (practical evaluation).
