Ria

A ria is a coastal inlet formed by the partial submergence of an unglaciated river valley. It is a drowned river valley that remains open to the sea. The word comes from Galician-Portuguese ría, from río (river), reflecting the famous rías of the Galician coast in north-west Spain, such as the Rías Baixas.

Formation

Most rias were formed at the end of the last ice age, when meltwater raised global sea level by more than 100 metres and flooded valleys that rivers had cut into higher ground. Because the valleys were not scoured by glaciers, a ria typically has a branching, dendritic plan resembling the river network that formed it, and deepens steadily towards its mouth — unlike a fjord, which is glacial, U-shaped and often has a shallow sill at its entrance.

Distribution

Rias occur worldwide where suitable valleys met rising seas. Classic coasts include Galicia and northern Portugal, Brittany, south-west England (the Fal, Dart and Kingsbridge estuaries), southern Ireland, and parts of Australia such as Sydney Harbour and the Hawkesbury River. Chesapeake Bay in the United States is a very large drowned river system of a similar kind. Ria coasts often provide excellent sheltered natural harbours.

Ecology

The mixing of fresh and salt water in rias creates productive estuarine habitats, important for shellfish — the Galician rías are renowned for mussel cultivation on floating rafts (bateas).
