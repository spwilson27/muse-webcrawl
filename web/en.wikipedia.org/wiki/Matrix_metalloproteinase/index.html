Matrix metalloproteinase

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), also known as matrixins, are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that degrade components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and participate in tissue remodeling, wound healing, angiogenesis, and disease processes such as arthritis and cancer metastasis.

Structure and classification

MMPs share a common domain structure: a propeptide domain, a catalytic domain containing a zinc-binding motif (HEXXHXXGXXH), a hinge region, and (in most members) a hemopexin-like domain. They are secreted as inactive zymogens (pro-MMPs) and activated by proteolytic removal of the propeptide. Based on substrate specificity and structure, MMPs are grouped into collagenases (e.g., MMP-1, MMP-8, MMP-13), gelatinases (MMP-2, MMP-9), stromelysins (MMP-3, MMP-10), matrilysins (MMP-7, MMP-26), membrane-type MMPs (such as MT1-MMP/MMP-14), and others.

Function

MMPs degrade collagens, gelatin, elastin, fibronectin, and proteoglycans. Beyond matrix turnover, they process cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and cell-surface receptors, thereby regulating cell migration, proliferation, and signaling. Their activity is tightly controlled by endogenous inhibitors, the tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs).

Clinical significance

Dysregulated MMP activity contributes to osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerotic plaque instability, tumor invasion, and metastasis. MMPs are also involved in normal processes such as embryonic development, reproduction, and wound repair. Pharmaceutical inhibition of MMPs has been explored for cancer and arthritis, though broad-spectrum inhibitors showed limited clinical success.
