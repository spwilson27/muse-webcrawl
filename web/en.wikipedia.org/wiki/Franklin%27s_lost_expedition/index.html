Franklin's lost expedition

Franklin's lost expedition was a failed British voyage of Arctic exploration led by Captain Sir John Franklin that departed England in 1845 aboard two ships, HMS Erebus and HMS Terror. Franklin's expedition was tasked with traversing the last unnavigated sections of the Northwest Passage in the Canadian Arctic and recording magnetic data to help determine whether a better understanding could aid navigation. The expedition met with disaster after both ships and their crews, a total of 129 men, became icebound in Victoria Strait near King William Island in what is today the Canadian territory of Nunavut. After being icebound for more than a year, Erebus and Terror were abandoned in April 1848, by which point two dozen men, including Franklin, had died. The surviving crew attempted a journey to the mainland and to safety, but all died as the group made the arduous trek to the south of King William Island and the mouth of Back's Great Fish River.

Background

The Northwest Passage, a sea route linking the Atlantic and Pacific oceans north of the North American continent, had been sought for centuries. By the 1840s only a small portion remained uncharted. Franklin, a veteran of earlier Arctic expeditions, was 59 years old when appointed to command the voyage. The ships were strengthened against ice, equipped with auxiliary steam engines, retractable iron propellers, and provisions including some 8,000 tins of food, some of them sealed with lead solder — a detail later implicated in hypotheses about lead poisoning among the crew.

Disappearance and searches

The expedition was last seen by whalers in Baffin Bay in July 1845. When the ships failed to return, dozens of search expeditions were launched beginning in 1848. A note found in 1859 by a search team led by Francis Leopold McClintock — the so-called Victory Point Note — is the expedition's only written record: it states that the ships were beset in the ice since September 1846 and that Franklin died on 11 June 1847. Inuit testimony collected by searchers described starving men hauling a boat southward, and later forensic analysis of skeletal remains found evidence consistent with scurvy, starvation, and in some cases cannibalism.

Discovery of the wrecks

In September 2014, the wreck of HMS Erebus was located in Queen Maud Gulf by a Parks Canada-led expedition, confirming Inuit oral histories about a wreck in the region. In September 2016, the wreck of HMS Terror was found farther north in Terror Bay. Both wrecks are remarkably well preserved in the cold Arctic waters and are designated national historic sites of Canada.

Legacy

The fate of the expedition became one of the great mysteries of the Victorian era, inspiring poems, novels, plays, and the 2018 television series The Terror. The searches also spurred extensive mapping of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago.
