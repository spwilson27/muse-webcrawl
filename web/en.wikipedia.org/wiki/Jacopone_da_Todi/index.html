Life

Jacopone da Todi (c. 1230 – 25 December 1306) was an Italian Franciscan mystic and poet, one of the greatest writers of religious verse in medieval Italian. Born Jacopo Benedetti into a noble family of Todi, he trained as a lawyer and lived a worldly life until the sudden death of his wife Vanna in 1268 — when her dress was found to conceal a hairshirt — shocked him into conversion. He gave away his wealth, joined the Franciscan order as a lay brother, and became a leader of the Spiritual Franciscans, the rigorist faction demanding absolute poverty. His opposition to Pope Boniface VIII, whom he attacked in savage satirical laude, led to his excommunication and imprisonment (1298–1303); he was freed only by Benedict XI.

The Laude

Jacopone's Laude (c. 100 poems) are the masterworks of the Italian lauda tradition: dramatic dialogues between the soul and Christ, laments of the Virgin, and blistering satires on clerical corruption, written in a vigorous Umbrian vernacular shot through with Latin. Their emotional extremity — alternating ecstatic love and self-lacerating penitence — made them models for later mystical poetry.

Stabat Mater

Jacopone is traditionally credited with the Stabat Mater ("At the cross her station keeping"), the 13th-century sequence on the Virgin's suffering at the Crucifixion, though the attribution is disputed among scholars. The hymn entered the Roman liturgy and inspired musical settings by Palestrina, Pergolesi, Rossini, Dvořák, and Poulenc.
