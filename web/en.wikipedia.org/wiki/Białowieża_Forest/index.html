Białowieża Forest (Polish: Puszcza Białowieska; Belarusian: Белавежская пушча) is a large forest complex straddling the border between Poland and Belarus. It is one of the last and largest remaining parts of the immense primeval forest that once stretched across the European Plain, and it is home to the largest free-roaming population of European bison, the continent's heaviest land animal.

History

The forest was protected for centuries as a royal hunting ground of Polish kings and later Russian tsars, which preserved its old growth. In the 20th century hunting and war devastated the wildlife: the last wild bison was killed in 1919, and the species was restored from zoo specimens beginning in 1929. Poland established Białowieża National Park in 1932 (formally 1947); the Belarusian side became Belovezhskaya Pushcha National Park. UNESCO inscribed the site as a World Heritage Site in 1979, extended to cover both countries in 1992.

Ecology

The forest is a unique mixed temperate forest of oak, hornbeam, spruce, pine and alder, with enormous standing and fallen dead wood that supports exceptional biodiversity — thousands of species of fungi and invertebrates, woodpeckers, owls, and predators including wolves and lynx. The strictly protected core of the Polish park has had almost no human intervention for nearly a century.

Logging controversy

From 2016, increased logging on the Polish side, justified by the government as a response to a spruce bark beetle outbreak, triggered protests and legal action. In 2018 the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that the logging violated EU nature law and ordered it stopped.
