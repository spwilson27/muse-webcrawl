The Battle of Ascalon took place on 12 August 1099, shortly after the Crusader capture of Jerusalem, and is often considered the last engagement of the First Crusade. A Crusader army led by Godfrey of Bouillon defeated a much larger Fatimid Egyptian army under the vizier al-Afdal Shahanshah outside the coastal city of Ascalon.

Background

After Jerusalem fell on 15 July 1099, the Crusaders learned that a Fatimid army was advancing from Egypt to retake the city. Al-Afdal, who had himself taken Jerusalem from the Seljuks only in 1098, landed at Ascalon with a large force — contemporary estimates are inflated, but it substantially outnumbered the roughly 10,000 Crusaders who marched south to meet it.

The battle

Marching through the night and early morning, the Crusaders achieved complete surprise, attacking the Fatimid camp at dawn before the Egyptians could deploy. The Fatimid army — a polyglot force including Arab, Armenian, Ethiopian and Turkish contingents — collapsed quickly. Al-Afdal fled by ship to Egypt, abandoning his camp, treasure and the Fatimid royal standard, which was captured.

Aftermath

Despite the victory, the Crusaders failed to take Ascalon itself, largely because of rivalry between Godfrey and Raymond of Toulouse over who should hold it. The city remained a Fatimid bridgehead into Palestine for another half-century, until Baldwin III finally captured it in 1153. The victory at Ascalon nonetheless secured the newborn Kingdom of Jerusalem against immediate reconquest.
