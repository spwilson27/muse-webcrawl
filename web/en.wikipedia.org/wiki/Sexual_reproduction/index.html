Sexual reproduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The sexual life cycle: meiosis and fertilisation alternate

Sexual reproduction is a type of reproduction that involves a complex life cycle in which a new organism develops from the combination of genetic information from two individuals of different mating types. It is characteristic of eukaryotes, from single-celled organisms to plants and animals, and is contrasted with asexual reproduction.

Mechanism

The defining processes of the sexual cycle are meiosis, a specialised form of cell division that produces haploid cells called gametes (such as sperm and eggs in animals), and fertilisation, in which two gametes fuse to form a diploid zygote. Because each gamete carries a unique shuffled sample of its parent's genes, offspring are genetically distinct from both parents and from one another.

In many species the sexes produce gametes of markedly different size (anisogamy): small, mobile sperm and large, nutrient-rich eggs. Organisms may be dioecious, with separate male and female individuals, or hermaphroditic, producing both kinds of gamete.

Evolution

The near-universality of sex among eukaryotes, despite its costs, is a classic problem in evolutionary biology. Sex incurs a "two-fold cost" relative to asexual reproduction, since only half of an individual's genes pass to each offspring and males contribute little directly to offspring numbers. Proposed compensating advantages include generating genetic variation that speeds adaptation, purging harmful mutations more efficiently, resisting parasites (the Red Queen hypothesis), and mechanisms of DNA repair. Fisher's principle explains the tendency toward roughly equal investment in male and female offspring in many populations.
