Degree of polymerization

The degree of polymerization, or DP, in polymer chemistry is the number of monomeric units in a macromolecule or polymer or oligomer molecule. For a homopolymer, the number-average degree of polymerization is given by the ratio of the number-average molecular weight to the molecular weight of the monomer unit: DPn = Mn / M0.

Definitions

Because synthetic polymers consist of mixtures of chains of different lengths, the degree of polymerization is an average. The number-average degree of polymerization (DPn) counts all chains equally, while the weight-average (DPw) weights longer chains more heavily; their ratio is related to the dispersity of the sample. For copolymers, an average monomer-unit molar mass is used. In step-growth polymerization, DP is controlled by the extent of reaction through the Carothers equation, which predicts that very high monomer conversions (above 99%) are needed to reach high DP; in chain-growth polymerization, DP is governed by the relative rates of propagation and termination/transfer.

Relation to properties

Physical properties depend strongly on DP. Tensile strength, toughness, melt viscosity, and glass-transition behavior rise with chain length and then level off once chains become long enough to entangle; the critical DP for entanglement in many common polymers is on the order of a few hundred repeat units. Very short chains (DP below ~10) are termed oligomers rather than polymers. Natural polymers illustrate the range: cellulose in cotton has a DP in the thousands, while amylopectin molecules can have DPs in the hundreds of thousands.
