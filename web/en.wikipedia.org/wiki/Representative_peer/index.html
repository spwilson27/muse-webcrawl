Representative peer

A representative peer was a member of the Peerage of Scotland or the Peerage of Ireland elected by their fellow peers to sit in the House of Lords of the United Kingdom (and earlier, for Scotland, of Great Britain). Unlike peers of England and later of the United Kingdom, who held hereditary seats in their own right, Scottish and Irish peers as a body did not have automatic seats; instead they chose representatives.

Scottish representative peers

Under the Acts of Union 1707, the Peerage of Scotland elected sixteen representative peers to sit in the House of Lords at Westminster. They were elected for each Parliament by the assembled Scottish peers, and held their seats only for the duration of that Parliament. The system continued until the Peerage Act 1963 gave all Scottish peers the right to sit in the Lords, ending the elections.

Irish representative peers

Under the Acts of Union 1800, the Peerage of Ireland elected twenty-eight temporal peers (along with four spiritual lords) for life. Elections ceased after the establishment of the Irish Free State in 1922, though surviving Irish representative peers retained their seats for life; the last, the Earl of Kilmorey, died in 1961.

Elections

Elections were conducted at Holyrood Palace in Edinburgh for Scottish peers and in Dublin for Irish peers. In later years peers could vote by proxy or by signed lists. Candidates were generally drawn from the ministerial or opposition groupings of the day, and government influence over Scottish elections was notorious in the 18th century.
