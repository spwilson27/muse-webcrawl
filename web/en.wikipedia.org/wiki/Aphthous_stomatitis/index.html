Aphthous stomatitis

Aphthous stomatitis, or recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), is a common condition characterised by the repeated formation of benign and non-contagious mouth ulcers (aphthae) in otherwise healthy individuals. The informal term "canker sores" is also used, mainly in North America. The ulcers are round or oval, shallow, with a yellow-grey centre and a red border, and occur on the non-keratinised mucosa of the lips, cheeks, floor of mouth, and underside of the tongue. They are distinct from the cold sores of herpes simplex, which occur on the lips and are infectious.

SynonymsCanker sores, recurrent aphthous ulcers
SpecialtyOral medicine, dentistry
SymptomsPainful round mouth ulcers, recurrent episodes
PrevalenceAbout 5–25% of the general population
DurationUsually heal in 7–14 days (minor type)

Classification

  • Minor aphthae — the most common form (about 85% of cases); ulcers under 10 mm, healing without scarring in one to two weeks.
  • Major aphthae — larger than 10 mm, deeper, lasting weeks to months and sometimes scarring.
  • Herpetiform ulcers — crops of numerous tiny ulcers that may coalesce; despite the name, unrelated to herpesvirus.

Causes

The exact cause is unknown. Current evidence points to a localised, T-cell-mediated immune reaction in people with a genetic predisposition. Reported triggers include minor trauma (such as biting the cheek), stress, hormonal changes, certain foods, and toothpastes containing sodium lauryl sulfate. In some patients, recurrent ulcers are associated with deficiencies of iron, folate, or vitamin B12, or with systemic diseases including Behçet's disease, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and HIV infection; persistent or severe ulceration warrants investigation for such causes.

Treatment

Most minor ulcers heal spontaneously without treatment. Management aims at relieving pain and shortening healing: topical corticosteroids (such as triamcinolone dental paste), antiseptic mouthwashes like chlorhexidine, topical anaesthetics, and protective barriers are commonly used. Severe or frequently recurring cases may require systemic therapy, for example colchicine, dapsone, or thalidomide under specialist supervision. There is no permanent cure.

Epidemiology

Aphthous stomatitis is one of the most common oral mucosal conditions worldwide, affecting roughly 5–25% of people, with onset usually in childhood or adolescence and a tendency to lessen with age. It appears to be somewhat more frequent in women and in higher socioeconomic groups.
