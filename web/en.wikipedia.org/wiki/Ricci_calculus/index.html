Ricci calculus

In mathematics, Ricci calculus constitutes the rules of index notation and manipulation for tensors and tensor fields on a differentiable manifold, with or without a metric tensor or connection. It is also the modern name for what used to be called the absolute differential calculus (the foundation of tensor calculus), developed by Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro in 1887–1894, and subsequently popularized in a paper written with his pupil Tullio Levi-Civita in 1900. Jan Arnoldus Schouten developed the modern notation and formalism for this mathematical framework, and made contributions to the application, during his applications to general relativity and differential geometry in the early twentieth century.

A component of a tensor is a real number that is used as a coefficient of a basis element in the tensor space. The tensor is the sum of its components multiplied by their corresponding basis elements. Tensors are denoted with indices using Latin and Greek letters in accordance with the Einstein summation convention, in which a repeated index in a product implies summation over that index.

History

Ricci-Curbastro developed the "absolute differential calculus" in a series of papers beginning in 1887, drawing on the work of Riemann, Christoffel, and Lipschitz on quadratic differential forms. The 1900 paper with Levi-Civita, Méthodes de calcul différentiel absolu et leurs applications, systematised the theory and demonstrated its power in mathematical physics. The calculus came to wide attention when Albert Einstein adopted it for the formulation of general relativity in 1915, after learning of it from Marcel Grossmann. Einstein's field equations, the Ricci tensor (named for Ricci-Curbastro), and the notation of the calculus became standard tools of theoretical physics.

Index notation

Indices are written as superscripts (upper indices) and subscripts (lower indices). A tensor with one upper and one lower index, for example, is written:

Tαβ

Upper indices are associated with contravariant components and lower indices with covariant components. The Einstein summation convention states that when an index appears twice in a single term, once raised and once lowered, a summation over all values of that index is implied:

Tαβ xβ = Σβ Tαβ xβ

Indices that appear once and are not summed over are called free indices; an equation must have the same free indices on both sides.

Raising and lowering indices

On a manifold equipped with a metric tensor gμν and its inverse gμν, indices of a tensor can be raised or lowered by contracting with the metric:

Tα = gαβ Tβ,    Tα = gαβ Tβ

The Kronecker delta δαβ acts as the identity under contraction.

Covariant differentiation

The covariant derivative, denoted by a semicolon in Ricci calculus (for example Tα;β), extends the ordinary partial derivative so that the result remains a tensor. It is built from partial derivatives together with terms involving the Christoffel symbols of a connection. The commutator of covariant derivatives leads to the Riemann curvature tensor, whose contraction gives the Ricci tensor Rμν and the Ricci scalar R, both central to general relativity.

Applications

Ricci calculus is used throughout differential geometry, continuum mechanics, and theoretical physics. It is the standard language of general relativity, where the Einstein field equations relate the curvature of spacetime to the stress–energy tensor:

Rμν − ½ R gμν + Λ gμν = (8πG/c4) Tμν
