Youth language

Youth language (German linguistics: Jugendsprache) refers to the distinctive ways of speaking used by adolescents and young adults within their peer groups. It encompasses slang vocabulary, novel grammar and pronunciation features, discourse styles, and — increasingly — conventions of digital communication. Sociolinguists treat youth language not as a separate language but as a set of registers and styles of a standard language variety, used to mark identity, group belonging, and distance from adult norms.

Features

Typical features include rapid turnover of slang expressions; borrowings from other languages (in Europe frequently from English, and in multilingual cities from immigrant languages); intensifiers and discourse particles; creative word formation, abbreviations, and playful deformation of words; and distinctive prosody. In multiethnic urban neighbourhoods, multiethnolects such as Germany's Kiezdeutsch, Sweden's Rinkeby Swedish, and Multicultural London English have attracted particular research interest.

Functions

Youth language serves social functions: it signals in-group solidarity, marks generational identity, and allows speakers to experiment with style and persona. Most young people style-shift, using peer varieties informally while commanding more standard registers in school or formal settings. Because adolescents are frequent innovators, youth speech is also a major source of language change; features that begin as youth slang sometimes diffuse into general colloquial usage.

Research and public debate

The systematic study of youth language developed from the 1970s onward, notably in German-speaking sociolinguistics. Public debate often frames youth language as a decline of standards, a position researchers generally reject, pointing instead to its creativity and rule-governed nature. In Germany, the Langenscheidt publisher's annual "Youth Word of the Year" (Jugendwort des Jahres) vote has become a popular barometer of adolescent slang.
