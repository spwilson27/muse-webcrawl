Panic disorder

Panic disorder
SpecialtyPsychiatry
SymptomsRecurrent unexpected panic attacks, persistent worry about further attacks, avoidance behaviour
Usual onsetAdolescence to mid-30s
TreatmentCognitive behavioural therapy, SSRIs, other antidepressants
FrequencyRoughly 2–3% of adults (lifetime)

Panic disorder is an anxiety disorder diagnosed when a person experiences recurrent unexpected panic attacks and, for at least a month afterwards, persistently worries about having more attacks, worries about their consequences, or significantly changes behaviour to avoid them. It may occur with or without agoraphobia, which the DSM-5 now classifies as a separate diagnosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires recurrent unexpected panic attacks followed by a month or more of persistent concern about additional attacks or their implications, and/or a maladaptive change in behaviour related to the attacks. The disturbance must not be attributable to substances, another medical condition, or another mental disorder. Because attacks mimic cardiac and other medical emergencies, physical causes are typically excluded first.

Causes

Panic disorder has a substantial heritable component and is associated with heightened sensitivity to bodily sensations ("anxiety sensitivity"), catastrophic misinterpretation of those sensations, and neurochemical factors involving serotonin, norepinephrine, and GABA systems. Stressful life events, trauma, smoking, and caffeine may precipitate onset. It is more common in women than men and frequently co-occurs with depression and other anxiety disorders.

Treatment

First-line treatments are cognitive behavioural therapy (including panic control treatment with interoceptive exposure) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Other options include serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors and, in selected cases, tricyclic antidepressants. Benzodiazepines act quickly but are discouraged for long-term use because of tolerance and dependence. With treatment, many patients achieve remission, though the course can be chronic and relapsing.
