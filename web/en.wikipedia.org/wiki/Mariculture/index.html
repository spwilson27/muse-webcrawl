Mariculture

Mariculture, sometimes called marine farming or marine aquaculture, is a branch of aquaculture involving the cultivation of marine organisms for food and other animal products, in seawater habitats, such as oceans or an enclosed section of an ocean or in tanks, ponds or raceways filled with seawater. It is distinguished from freshwater aquaculture by the salinity of the water. An example is the farming of marine fish, including finfish and shellfish like prawns, or oysters and seaweed in saltwater ponds.

Non-food products produced by mariculture include fish meal, nutrient agar, jewellery (e.g. cultured pearls) and cosmetics. Global mariculture production has grown steadily, supplying a large share of the world's farmed aquatic output, particularly in East and Southeast Asia; China is by far the largest producer.

Categories

Algaculture

Algaculture is the farming of algae species including seaweeds such as kelp, nori and wakame, grown for food, hydrocolloids (agar, carrageenan, alginates) and other industrial products.

Finfish

Marine finfish farming includes Atlantic salmon (largely in Norway, Chile, Scotland and Canada), sea bass, sea bream, yellowtail, cobia and groupers. Salmon are typically raised in hatcheries and then grown out in large net pens or cages moored in sheltered coastal waters.

Shellfish

Shellfish culture covers oysters, mussels, clams, scallops and abalone, as well as crustaceans such as marine shrimp and prawns and crabs. Bivalves are filter feeders and generally require no feed inputs, which makes their culture comparatively low-impact.

Other groups

Sea cucumbers, sea urchins and ornamental species are also cultured, as are broodstock and juveniles for stock enhancement and sea ranching.

Methods

Systems range from extensive pond and raft culture to intensive offshore cages and land-based recirculating systems. Offshore (open-ocean) aquaculture moves cages into deeper, better-flushed waters to reduce coastal impacts. Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) combines fed species such as fish with extractive species — shellfish and seaweed — that absorb waste nutrients, mimicking a natural ecosystem.

Environmental issues

Concerns include nutrient enrichment and benthic impacts beneath cages, escapes of farmed fish and genetic interaction with wild populations, transfer of parasites such as sea lice, disease, and the use of wild-caught fish for feed. Counterbalancing these, mariculture can relieve pressure on over-exploited wild fisheries and, in the case of bivalves and seaweed, provide ecosystem services. Certification schemes and improved siting and husbandry aim to reduce impacts.
