Cairn

[Hero image placeholder: summit cairn]

A cairn is a man-made pile of stones. The word comes from the Scottish Gaelic càrn (plural càirn). Cairns have been built since prehistory for purposes ranging from burial monuments and territorial markers to trail markers and memorials, and they are found all over the world, from the high Arctic to the Himalaya.

Historical uses

In the British Isles and Scandinavia, large prehistoric burial cairns covered chambered tombs; famous examples include the passage graves of Ireland such as Newgrange (technically a cairn-covered mound) and the Clava cairns near Inverness in Scotland. In Norse tradition, cairns marked graves and boundaries. Elsewhere, the Inuit inuksuk serves as a directional marker in the Arctic, and similar stone piles guide travellers in Alpine and desert regions.

Modern uses

Today cairns are most commonly encountered as trail markers and summit markers in mountain areas, where they guide walkers in poor visibility and in terrain where paths are indistinct. Hikers in Scotland traditionally add a stone to a summit cairn for luck. Conservation bodies, however, discourage building unofficial cairns in some national parks, as excessive stone-gathering can disturb habitats and confuse navigation.

Related structures

Cairns differ from dry-stone walls and from natural rock piles. Related forms include burial mounds and tumuli, the ovoö of Mongolia, and the balanced stone stacks sometimes built informally by visitors.
