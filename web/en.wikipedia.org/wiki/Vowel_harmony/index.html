Vowel harmony

Vowel harmony is a phonological process in which all the vowels within a word (or a defined domain) must agree in some feature, such as frontness, backness, rounding or tongue-root position. It is characteristic of Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, Uralic and many other languages of Eurasia, and also occurs in numerous African, Amazonian and other languages. Harmony strongly shapes the form of suffixes in agglutinative languages.

Turkish example

Turkish has a two-way backness harmony and a rounding harmony. The plural suffix appears as -lar after back vowels (evler is an exception showing the pattern with the front-vowel form -ler): at → atlar "horses", ev → evler "houses", kız → kızlar, gün → günler. Some vowels are "transparent" and do not block harmony; others, in loanwords, are disharmonic roots that nonetheless take suffixes matching their last vowel.

Types

Harmony systems may be based on tongue position (front/back, as in Finnish and Hungarian), rounding (lip harmony), tongue root (retracted/advanced tongue root systems in many Niger–Congo and Nilotic languages) or height. Harmony may be root-controlled or dominant–recessive, and its domain may be the word, the stem-plus-affixes, or larger phrases. Finnish harmony notably excludes the neutral vowels e and i from triggering back-vowel suffixes.

Analysis

In phonological theory harmony is modelled as long-distance assimilation or feature spreading, and has been a key test case for autosegmental phonology and Optimality Theory. Historically, harmony systems can be lost — as in most of Mongolic's neighbours and partially in Uzbek under Persian influence — or arise through sound change and analogy.
