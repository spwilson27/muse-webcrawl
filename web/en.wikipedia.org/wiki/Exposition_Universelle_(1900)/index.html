The Exposition Universelle of 1900, better known in English as the 1900 Paris Exposition, was a world's fair held in Paris, France, from 14 April to 12 November 1900, to celebrate the achievements of the past century and to accelerate development into the next. It was the fifth Paris exposition and drew more than 48 million visitors.

Overview

The fair covered a vast area along both banks of the Seine, from the Champ de Mars and the Trocadéro to the Bois de Vincennes. It introduced many features now associated with modern life, including escalators, diesel engines running on peanut oil, talking films, and the trolleybus. Art Nouveau reached the height of its international popularity at the fair, championed by designers such as Hector Guimard, whose entrances for the Paris Métro were unveiled for the occasion.

Notable structures and exhibits

The Grand Palais and Petit Palais, built for the exposition, still stand on the right bank, as does the Pont Alexandre III, laid across the Seine to link them with Les Invalides. The Gare d'Orsay railway station (now the Musée d'Orsay) was also completed for the fair. The Globe Céleste, a giant celestial sphere, and the Grande Roue de Paris, a 100-metre Ferris wheel, were among the most popular attractions. The 1900 Summer Olympics were held as part of the exposition, spread over several months.

Legacy

Despite its popularity, the exposition barely broke even financially and many of its pavilions were demolished afterwards. Its legacy lies in the surviving monuments, the spread of Art Nouveau, and its role as a showcase for the technologies of the new century — electricity above all, displayed in the spectacular Palace of Electricity.
