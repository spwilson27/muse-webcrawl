Latins

The Latins (Latin: Latini) were an ancient Italic people who inhabited Latium, the region of central Italy in which the city of Rome was founded and grew. The Latins spoke Latin, an Italic language of the Indo-European family, and shared a common culture, religion, and set of political institutions, including the Latin League and the common sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris on the Alban Mount. Through the expansion of Rome — traditionally founded in 753 BC by Romulus, himself a Latin — Latin language and culture spread first across Italy and then throughout the Roman Empire, profoundly shaping the later history of Europe; the modern Romance languages descend from the Latin the Latins spoke.

Origins

The Latins belonged to the Italic peoples who entered the Italian peninsula during the 2nd millennium BC. Archaeologically, their emergence is associated with the Latial culture (c. 1000–600 BC), known from cremation burials and hut-urns in the Alban Hills and the plain of Rome. Latin tradition held that the Latins took their name from the legendary king Latinus, who in Virgil's Aeneid receives Aeneas and the Trojans in Italy; the name more plausibly derives from the broad flat plain (latus, "wide") of Latium itself.

History

The Latin cities formed the Latin League, a confederation of about thirty communities united for defence and for the celebration of the Feriae Latinae (Latin Festival). Relations with Rome were codified in the Treaty of Cassius (493 BC). After a general Latin revolt, Rome dissolved the League in 338 BC following the Latin War, and settled the cities with a graded system of statuses — full Roman citizenship, citizenship without the vote (civitas sine suffragio), and the "Latin rights" (ius Latii) — a legal template later extended across Italy and the provinces. The concept of Latin rights long outlived the Latins as a distinct people: under the Empire, "Latin" status became a stepping stone to Roman citizenship granted to provincial communities and individuals.

Culture and religion

The Latins worshipped Jupiter Latiaris at his federal sanctuary on Mons Albanus and shared cults such as that of Diana at Aricia. Their language is attested from inscriptions of the 7th–6th centuries BC (such as the Praeneste fibula, whose authenticity has been debated). Latin society was organised in clans (gentes) and patrician and plebeian orders within each city; Rome's early institutions — kingship, senate, and assemblies — grew out of this Latin world.

Legacy

"Latin" expanded in meaning far beyond the original people of Latium: it came to denote the language of Rome and its descendants (the Romance languages), the Western ("Latin") Church as distinct from the Greek East, and, in modern usage, the peoples and cultures of "Latin" Europe and Latin America. During the Middle Ages, crusader states in the eastern Mediterranean were likewise called "Latin."
