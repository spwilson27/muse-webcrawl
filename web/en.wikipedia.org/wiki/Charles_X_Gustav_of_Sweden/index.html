Charles X Gustav (Swedish: Karl X Gustav; 1622–1660) was King of Sweden from 1654 until his death. A soldier-king of the Palatinate-Zweibrücken dynasty, he succeeded his cousin Queen Christina upon her abdication and spent nearly his entire short reign at war, most famously marching his army across the frozen Danish straits in 1658 and redrawing the map of Scandinavia by the Treaty of Roskilde.

Charles X Gustav, warrior king of Sweden

Early life and military career

Charles Gustav was the son of John Casimir, Count Palatine of Zweibrücken-Kleeburg, and Catherine, daughter of Charles IX of Sweden. Raised at the Swedish court, he served with distinction in the final campaigns of the Thirty Years' War under Lennart Torstensson, fighting at the second Battle of Breitenfeld and at Jankau. Christina named him commander of Swedish forces in Germany and later her heir.

Accession and war with Poland

When Christina abdicated in 1654, Charles was crowned king. In 1655 he invaded the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, beginning the war known in Poland as the Deluge. Swedish forces occupied Warsaw and Kraków, but Polish resistance, guerrilla warfare, and the intervention of other powers forced a long and costly struggle; the war ended only in 1660 with the Treaty of Oliva, which confirmed Swedish Livonia and Polish recognition of Swedish gains.

War with Denmark and the march across the Belts

When Denmark declared war in 1657, Charles marched from Poland across Jutland and, in the bitter winter of early 1658, led his army over the frozen Little and Great Belts to Zealand — one of the most audacious feats of the century. The resulting Treaty of Roskilde (1658) transferred Scania, Halland, Blekinge, and Bohuslän from Denmark to Sweden, giving Sweden roughly its modern southern borders. A second, unsuccessful attempt to conquer Denmark outright followed, including the failed assault on Copenhagen.

Death

Charles X Gustav died suddenly of pneumonia at Gothenburg in February 1660 while preparing further campaigns. He left the throne to his four-year-old son, Charles XI, under a regency. His reign, though brief, secured Sweden's position as the dominant Baltic power for another half-century.
