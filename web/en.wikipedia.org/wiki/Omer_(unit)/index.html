The omer (Hebrew: עֹמֶר, ʿōmer) is an ancient Israelite unit of dry measure used for grain and similar dry commodities, mentioned frequently in the Hebrew Bible. An omer is defined in the Book of Exodus as one-tenth of an ephah. The word also denotes a sheaf of grain itself.

Biblical usage

In Exodus 16, the Israelites in the wilderness are instructed to gather manna — "an omer for each person" — each morning, with a double portion on the sixth day and none on the Sabbath. A jar containing an omer of manna was preserved as a memorial. The unit appears alongside the larger ephah and the smaller measures of the Hebrew system of capacity.

The omer offering

Leviticus 23 commands that on the day after the Sabbath during the festival of Unleavened Bread (Passover), a sheaf — an omer — of the first barley harvest be brought to the priest and waved as an offering (the tenufat ha-omer). Until this offering was made, the new grain crop could not be eaten. In Temple times the reaping of the omer barley was a public ceremony conducted with considerable formality.

Counting of the omer

From the day of the omer offering, Leviticus commands the counting of seven complete weeks — 49 days — culminating in the festival of Shavuot. This practice, the Counting of the Omer (Sefirat HaOmer), remains a daily ritual in Judaism between Passover and Shavuot, during which traditional mourning customs are observed.

Estimates of volume

Because the absolute size of the ephah is not known with certainty, modern estimates of the omer vary, generally falling between roughly 2 and 4 litres; a commonly cited figure is about 2.2 litres. Rabbinic tradition also gives the omer a weight equivalence — the volume of 43.2 eggs — from which several estimates are derived.
