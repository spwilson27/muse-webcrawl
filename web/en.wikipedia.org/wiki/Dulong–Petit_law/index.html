Dulong–Petit law

Dulong–Petit law
Proposed: 1819
By: Pierre Louis Dulong, Alexis Thérèse Petit
Field: Thermodynamics, solid-state physics

The Dulong–Petit law, proposed by French physicists Pierre Louis Dulong and Alexis Thérèse Petit in 1819, states that the molar heat capacity of a solid element is approximately constant, about 3R ≈ 25 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, where R is the gas constant. Equivalently, the product of a solid element's specific heat and its atomic mass is roughly constant.

Classical explanation

In classical statistical mechanics, each atom in a crystal vibrates in three dimensions as a harmonic oscillator with six quadratic energy terms; by equipartition each contributes ½RT per mole, giving a total internal energy of 3RT and a heat capacity of 3R. This made the law a useful tool in the 19th century for determining atomic masses of metals from measured specific heats.

Limitations

The law fails at low temperatures, where quantum effects freeze out vibrational modes and heat capacities fall toward zero as T³ (the Debye law), and for light elements with high vibrational frequencies such as carbon (diamond), boron and beryllium. Einstein's 1907 quantum model of solids and Debye's 1912 refinement explained these deviations.
