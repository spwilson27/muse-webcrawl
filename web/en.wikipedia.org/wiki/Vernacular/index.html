Vernacular
FromLatin vernaculus, "native, domestic"
MeaningEveryday language of ordinary people in a place
Contrasted withStandard language, lingua franca, liturgical/classical language

The vernacular is the language or dialect natively spoken by ordinary people in a particular place or community, as distinct from a formal, literary, classical or foreign language used for official, religious or scholarly purposes. The word comes from the Latin vernaculus, meaning "domestic, native," derived from verna, a slave born in the household.

Usage and history

For most of European history, Latin served as the language of learning, law and the Church, while daily life proceeded in vernaculars such as Old English, Italian dialects, or Occitan. The rise of vernacular literature — Dante's defence of Tuscan in De vulgari eloquentia and his choice to write the Divine Comedy in it, Chaucer's English poetry, Luther's German Bible translation (1522–34) — was a pivotal cultural shift, widened by the printing press and the Reformation, which promoted vernacular scripture and worship over Latin.

In linguistics and other fields

In sociolinguistics, "the vernacular" in Labov's sense denotes the most spontaneous, least self-conscious style of speech, acquired in childhood and used in everyday life; William Labov treated it as the most systematic object of linguistic study. A vernacular name in biology is a common name of an organism, as opposed to its scientific name. In architecture, vernacular architecture refers to traditional, locally adapted building using local materials and craft rather than professional design. "Vernacular" is sometimes used loosely as a synonym for dialect or colloquial speech, though linguists generally reserve it for the native everyday variety of a community, whatever its prestige.
