Cocoa bean

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A split cocoa pod showing white pulp-covered beans inside]
A split cocoa pod showing white pulp-covered beans inside

The cocoa bean is the seed of the cacao tree (Theobroma cacao), native to the tropical Americas, from which Chocolate and cocoa butter are made. Grown in pods on the tree's trunk, the beans are fermented and dried before processing. Cocoa is one of the world's great commodity crops — some five million tonnes annually, dominated by Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana — and the livelihood of millions of smallholder farmers, with persistent controversies over child labour and deforestation.

Cultivation and processing

Cacao grows only in the humid tropics near the equator, mostly on small farms of a few hectares. Harvested pods are split, and the beans ferment in their pulp for several days — developing chocolate's precursor flavours — then sun-dry. At factories the beans are roasted, cracked and ground into cocoa mass, which is pressed into cocoa butter and cocoa powder or conched into chocolate. Varieties divide commercially into bulk Forastero and fine-flavour Criollo and Trinitario, though genetics shows a more complex family tree.

History and trade

Mesoamericans drank cacao for over 3,000 years (the Olmec, Maya and Aztecs used beans as currency and tribute). Carried to Europe after the conquest, sweetened chocolate became an aristocratic drink, then — with the cocoa press (1828), milk chocolate (1875) and mass production — a global industry headquartered in Europe and America on African-grown beans. Prices are set largely on the London and New York exchanges; the 2023–24 cocoa price explosion — multiplying several-fold on West African crop failures — exposed the market's fragility and farmers' small share of chocolate's value.
