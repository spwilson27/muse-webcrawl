Other namesAortic insufficiency
SpecialtyCardiology
Key signEarly diastolic decrescendo murmur; wide pulse pressure

Aortic regurgitation (AR), also called aortic insufficiency, is the leaking of the aortic valve of the heart, which causes blood ejected into the aorta during systole to flow backwards into the left ventricle during diastole. It may result from disease of the valve leaflets themselves or from dilation of the aortic root that prevents the leaflets from meeting.

Causes

Chronic AR is most often caused by a bicuspid aortic valve, calcific degeneration, aortic root dilation (as in Marfan syndrome or hypertension), rheumatic heart disease, or previous infective endocarditis. Acute AR is an emergency typically caused by infective endocarditis, aortic dissection, or trauma.

Signs and symptoms

Chronic AR may remain asymptomatic for years while the left ventricle dilates and hypertrophies; exertional breathlessness, fatigue and palpitations eventually develop. Classic signs include a "water-hammer" (Corrigan's) pulse, a wide pulse pressure, head bobbing (de Musset's sign), and an early diastolic blowing murmur heard best at the left sternal border with the patient leaning forward.

Diagnosis and treatment

Transthoracic echocardiography confirms the diagnosis and grades severity. Vasodilators may be used in selected patients, but definitive treatment of symptomatic severe AR is aortic valve replacement (surgical, or increasingly transcatheter in suitable candidates). Acute severe AR generally requires urgent surgery.
