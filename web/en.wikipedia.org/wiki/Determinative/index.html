Determinative

Hieroglyphic signs; determinatives clarified the meaning of accompanying phonetic signs.

A determinative, also known as a taxogram or semagram, is an ideogram used to mark semantic categories of words in logographic scripts which disambiguate words that have multiple meanings or pronunciations. Determinatives are not pronounced and serve purely as reading aids, indicating the semantic field to which a word belongs.

Ancient Egyptian

In ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs, determinatives were placed at the end of a word to specify its meaning. For example, a seated man determinative indicated words relating to men and their occupations, while a sun disc marked words connected with the sun and time. Because Egyptian writing often omitted vowels, determinatives helped readers distinguish homophones.

Cuneiform

In cuneiform scripts, determinatives (transliterated in superscript, such as dingir for a god or ki for a place) preceded or followed words to classify them as divine names, place names, wooden objects, and so on. Sumerian, Akkadian, Hittite, and Urartian all used systems of determinatives.

Chinese characters

The semantic component of a Chinese phono-semantic compound character functions similarly to a determinative: the radical indicates the general semantic domain, while the other component hints at pronunciation. The great majority of Chinese characters are built in this way.
