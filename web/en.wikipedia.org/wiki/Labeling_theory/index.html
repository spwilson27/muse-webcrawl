Labeling theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Labeling theory is a theory in sociology and criminology which states that deviance is not inherent in an act, but instead arises from the labels that society attaches to it. A person becomes labelled as "deviant" when others — especially authority figures such as police, teachers and courts — react to their behaviour in those terms; the labelled individual may then internalise the label and engage in further deviance. The theory is associated with the symbolic interactionist tradition and with Howard S. Becker's Outsiders (1963).

Key ideas

  • Primary and secondary deviance (Edwin Lemert) — primary deviance is the initial rule-breaking, which may have many causes; secondary deviance occurs when a person reorganises their life and identity around the deviant label.
  • Moral entrepreneurs (Becker) — individuals and groups who create rules and campaign for their enforcement, thereby creating new categories of deviance.
  • Master status — a label such as "criminal" or "addict" can come to dominate how a person is treated, overriding other identities.
  • Stigma — Erving Goffman's work on spoiled identity complements the theory.

Influence and criticism

Labeling theory influenced policies favouring diversion of juveniles away from formal court processing, decriminalisation debates, and restorative justice. Critics argue that it underplays the seriousness of some offending, the causes of primary deviance, and victims' experiences. Later work, such as John Braithwaite's idea of reintegrative shaming, tried to reconcile the theory with crime control by distinguishing stigmatising from reintegrative responses.
