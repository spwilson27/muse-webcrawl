Colony

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The world in 1920, showing colonial empires at their greatest extent.

A colony is a territory subject to a form of foreign rule. Distinct from the sovereign state, a colony is separated from its rulers' country, the metropole. Though dominated by the foreign colonisers, a colony remains separate from the country of origin under international law; its people are governed from the metropole and do not enjoy full citizenship of it.

History

The word derives from the Latin colonia, "a place for agriculture", used for Roman settlements of veteran soldiers in conquered lands, and earlier Greek city-states founded independent colonies (apoikiai) around the Mediterranean and Black Sea. The modern sense arose with the Age of Discovery, when Spain, Portugal, France, England, and the Netherlands established colonies across the Americas, Asia, and Africa. Colonies served as sources of raw materials, markets, and strategic bases, and were administered by chartered companies, crown officials, or settler governments.

Types

Historians distinguish settler colonies, where large numbers of colonists settled permanently (as in North America and Australia), from colonies of occupation or exploitation, where a small ruling class governed an indigenous majority (as in much of Africa and Asia). Penal colonies, trading posts, and plantation colonies were other forms. The 20th century brought waves of decolonization, especially after the Second World War, as colonies in Asia and Africa gained independence.

Modern usage

Few formal colonies remain; most former colonies are now independent states or integrated overseas departments and territories, such as French Guiana or Puerto Rico (an unincorporated territory of the United States). The United Nations maintains a list of Non-Self-Governing Territories whose peoples have yet to exercise self-determination. The term is also used in biology for a colony of organisms.
