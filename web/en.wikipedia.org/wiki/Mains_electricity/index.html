Mains electricity, or utility power, is the general purpose alternating-current (AC) electric power supply. It is the form of electrical power delivered to homes and businesses via the electrical grid in most parts of the world. People often refer to it by the form of a wall socket, as in wall power, or simply by its voltage and frequency, such as "230 volts, 50 hertz".

Terminology

In Britain the supply is officially known as the mains; in the United States it is often called utility power, grid power or line power. The term "mains" derives from the main distribution cables.

Power systems

Electricity is generated at power stations at medium voltage, stepped up by transformers for long-distance transmission over the grid at high voltages (to reduce losses), and stepped down progressively through substations to the utilisation voltage supplied to consumers. Commercial and industrial users may receive three-phase power, while households usually receive a single phase.

Voltage and frequency standards

Two broad standards predominate: North America and parts of Central and South America, Japan and some other countries use nominal voltages of 100–127 V at 60 Hz; most of the rest of the world uses 220–240 V at 50 Hz. Within each system, appliances are built for the local supply, and travellers may need plug adapters and sometimes voltage converters.

Plugs and sockets

Numerous types of AC power plugs and sockets exist worldwide, differing in pin geometry, earthing arrangements and safety features such as shutters and fusing. The diversity is historical, and harmonisation has only been partial.

Safety

Mains voltages are dangerous and can kill. Installations are protected by fuses, circuit breakers, earthing (grounding) systems, and residual-current devices (ground-fault interrupters), and work on fixed wiring is regulated in most jurisdictions.
