Formaldehyde (systematic name methanal) is an organic compound with the formula CH2O. It is the simplest of the aldehydes and is a colourless gas with a pungent odour. Formaldehyde is an important precursor to many materials and chemical compounds; its aqueous solution, formalin, is widely used as a disinfectant and as a preservative for biological specimens. It is produced industrially by the catalytic oxidation of methanol.

Occurrence and chemistry

Formaldehyde occurs naturally in the environment and is produced in small amounts by most organisms, including humans, as part of normal metabolism. In solution it exists largely as the hydrate, methylene glycol, and readily polymerises to paraformaldehyde and trioxane. It was first reported by the Russian chemist Aleksandr Butlerov in 1859 and definitively identified by August Wilhelm von Hofmann.

Uses

The largest use of formaldehyde is in the production of resins — urea-formaldehyde, phenol-formaldehyde and melamine-formaldehyde — used in adhesives for plywood and particleboard, moulding compounds and insulation. It is also a building block for polyacetals and many industrial chemicals, and is used in embalming and tissue fixation.

Health effects

Formaldehyde is an irritant to the eyes, nose and throat and is a known human carcinogen, associated particularly with nasopharyngeal cancer and leukaemia; the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies it in Group 1. Emissions from pressed-wood products have prompted regulation of indoor exposure in many countries.
