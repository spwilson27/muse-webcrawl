Denticity

Denticity refers to the number of donor atoms in a single coordinated ligand that bind to the central atom in a coordination complex. A ligand that binds through one donor atom is monodentate (from the Latin dent-, "tooth"); ligands binding through two, three, or more donor atoms are bidentate, tridentate, and so on, collectively called polydentate or multidentate ligands.

Classes

Monodentate ligands include water, ammonia, chloride, and carbon monoxide. Bidentate ligands include ethylenediamine, bipyridine, and oxalate. Tridentate ligands include diethylenetriamine and terpyridine. Tetradentate ligands include triethylenetetramine and porphyrins. Hexadentate ligands include EDTA, which can wrap around a metal ion using six donor atoms.

Chelate effect

Polydentate ligands form chelate complexes that are usually much more stable than comparable complexes of monodentate ligands, a phenomenon known as the chelate effect, largely attributed to favorable entropy changes on binding. The symbol κ (kappa) is used in nomenclature to specify denticity, e.g. κ2-ethylenediamine denotes binding through two nitrogen atoms.

Ambidentate ligands

Some ligands can bind through more than one kind of donor atom but only one at a time — for example thiocyanate (through S or N) and nitrite (through N or O). Such ambidentate ligands can give linkage isomers.
