The cycle of abuse is a social cycle theory developed in 1979 by psychologist Lenore E. Walker to explain patterns of behaviour in an abusive relationship. The theory describes a repeating pattern of phases through which abusive relationships tend to move, which can help explain why victims may remain in or return to such relationships.

Phases

  1. Tension building — stress and tension rise; the victim may try to placate the abuser and avoid triggers, while minor incidents occur.
  2. Acute incident — the tension erupts in an episode of verbal, emotional, or physical abuse, the shortest but most intense phase.
  3. Reconciliation ("honeymoon") — the abuser apologises, shows affection, makes promises, or denies responsibility; the victim may hope the abuse has ended.
  4. Calm — the relationship appears peaceful for a time, until tension begins building again and the cycle repeats.

Discussion

Walker's model, drawn from interviews with battered women, became foundational in domestic-violence advocacy and in her related theory of learned helplessness in battered women. Later researchers have criticised the cycle as not universal — not all abusive relationships include a honeymoon phase, and the model has been adapted, for example in the Duluth model's emphasis on power and control.
