Pitch accent

Pitch accent is a feature of some languages in which one syllable or mora in a word is made prominent principally through a contrast in pitch (fundamental frequency) rather than through increased loudness or duration. Pitch-accent languages sit between pure tone languages, in which pitch distinguishes lexical items on nearly every syllable, and stress languages such as English, where prominence is mainly a matter of stress.

Examples

Japanese is the best-known pitch-accent language: in the Tokyo dialect, words are distinguished by the position at which pitch falls after an initial rise. The word hashi, for instance, can mean "chopsticks," "bridge," or "edge" depending on the accent pattern. Other examples include Ancient Greek, Vedic Sanskrit, Swedish and Norwegian (with their two tonal word accents), Serbo-Croatian, Lithuanian, Basque, Turkish (in some analyses), and many dialects of Korean.

Characteristics

A typical pitch-accent system has a small inventory of possible patterns — often just accented versus unaccented, or a choice of one of two accent melodies per word — and the accent is described as culminative (at most one peak per word) and obligatory. Unlike tone languages such as Mandarin, a pitch-accent language does not assign an independent tone to every syllable; instead, a single tonal contour spreads across the word from the accented position.

Terminology

The term is used inconsistently in the literature: some linguists restrict "pitch accent" to systems like Japanese, while others apply it to any language where pitch is the main correlate of word prominence, including limited tone systems in West Africa and Mesoamerica. Modern phonological treatments often analyse pitch accent as a restricted type of tonal system.
