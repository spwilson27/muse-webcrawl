Pillbox (military)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pillbox is a type of blockhouse, or concrete dug-in guard-post, often camouflaged, normally equipped with loopholes through which defenders can fire weapons. It is in effect a trench firing step hardened to protect against small-arms fire and grenades, and raised to improve the field of fire. The name derives from the resemblance of the structures to the cylindrical or hexagonal boxes in which pills were once sold.

History

Concrete pillboxes first came into widespread use on the Western Front of the First World War, where German defenders built lines of small reinforced-concrete positions that proved resistant to artillery and difficult to assault. The concept was developed extensively between the wars and during the Second World War: Britain built some 28,000 pillboxes and other anti-invasion defences in 1940–41, of which about 6,500 survive. Similar structures formed part of the French Maginot Line, the German Atlantic Wall, and Japanese island defences in the Pacific.

Design

Pillboxes are typically built of reinforced concrete, sometimes with earth or sandbag protection, and are partly sunk into the ground to present a low profile. A narrow entrance, often at the rear and protected by a blast wall, leads to an internal chamber with one or more embrasures for rifles or machine guns. Standardised designs were produced in quantity — British FW3 types, for example, included designs for rifle sections, Vickers machine guns, and anti-tank guns.

Use and effectiveness

Pillboxes were designed to delay an attacker and channel them into killing zones covered by other positions. Individually they were vulnerable to artillery, armour, flamethrowers, and demolition charges, so they were most effective as parts of mutually supporting defensive lines. Their low cost and speed of construction made them attractive for fixed defences, but mobile warfare after 1940 reduced their military value.

Surviving examples

Thousands of pillboxes survive in Britain, continental Europe, and around the Pacific, where they are recorded by heritage projects such as the Defence of Britain Project. Many are protected as historic structures or reused as bat roosts and stores.
