The Japanese New Year (Japanese: 正月, Hepburn: Shōgatsu) is an annual festival in Japan. Since 1873, the official Japanese New Year has been celebrated according to the Gregorian calendar, on January 1 of each year, New Year's Day (元日, Ganjitsu). Prior to 1872, it coincided with the Chinese New Year on the traditional lunisolar calendar. Shōgatsu is considered by most Japanese to be one of the most important annual festivals, marked by days of family gathering, special foods, shrine visits, and the symbolic sweeping away of the old year's troubles.

Preparations

Before the year turns, households undertake ōsōji, a thorough year-end cleaning, and settle debts and disputes so as not to carry them into the new year. Entrances are decorated with kadomatsu (pine and bamboo arrangements) and shimenawa rope ornaments to welcome the toshigami, the deity of the coming year. Inside, stacked round rice cakes called kagami mochi are displayed. Forgotten-debts parties (bōnenkai) in December and "new year parties" (shinnenkai) in January frame the holiday socially.

New Year's Eve and Day

On Ōmisoka (31 December), families eat toshikoshi soba — long buckwheat noodles symbolizing longevity — and watch the long-running NHK song contest Kōhaku Uta Gassen. Buddhist temples ring their bells 108 times (joya no kane), one toll for each of the worldly desires that Buddhism teaches must be overcome.

On New Year's morning, families eat osechi: elaborate boxed assortments of symbolic preserved foods prepared in advance so no cooking is needed during the holiday, together with ozōni, a soup containing mochi whose recipe varies by region. Children receive money gifts called otoshidama in decorated envelopes.

Hatsumōde and cards

Hatsumōde, the first shrine or temple visit of the year, draws millions in the first three days; major sites such as Meiji Shrine in Tokyo, Fushimi Inari in Kyoto, and Naritasan in Chiba each receive around three million visitors. Worshippers draw omikuji fortune slips and buy new amulets, returning the old ones for ritual burning. The post office delivers millions of nengajō New Year's greeting cards precisely on 1 January when they are posted by the late-December deadline.

Other customs

Traditional pastimes include kite-flying (takoage), spinning tops, battledore (hanetsuki), the poetry card game karuta, and fukuwarai. The Emperor's New Year greeting from the Imperial Palace balcony on 2 January is one of only a few annual occasions when the public enters the palace grounds. Most businesses close from 1 to 3 January, and the holiday period anchors one of Japan's three great travel seasons.
