Surface brightness

In astronomy, surface brightness (SB) quantifies the apparent brightness of an extended astronomical object, such as a galaxy or nebula, per unit area of sky. It is generally measured in magnitudes per square arcsecond.

Definition

The surface brightness of an object is its flux density divided by its angular area. If an object of total magnitude m is spread over an area A square arcseconds, its surface brightness is S = m + 2.5 log10 A. Because it is a ratio of flux to area, surface brightness is (in the absence of cosmological effects) independent of distance: as an object is moved farther away, both its total flux and its angular size shrink in matching proportion.

Applications

Surface brightness is central to the study of galaxies. Freeman's law described the near-constant central surface brightness of spiral galaxy discs, while low surface brightness galaxies fall well below that value and can be hard to detect against the night-sky background. At cosmological distances, surface brightness dims as (1 + z)4, a relation known as the Tolman surface brightness test, which is used to test models of the expanding universe.

Units

Besides magnitudes per square arcsecond, surface brightness is sometimes expressed in solar luminosities per square parsec for the intrinsic brightness of a galaxy's stellar population.
