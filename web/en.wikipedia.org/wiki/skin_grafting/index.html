Skin grafting

A meshed split-thickness skin graft on a healing wound.

Skin grafting, a type of graft surgery, involves the transplantation of skin. The transplanted tissue is called a skin graft. Skin grafting is often used to treat extensive wounding or burns, skin loss due to infection or surgery, chronic ulcers, and areas of prior skin cancer removal. Skin grafts are often employed after serious injuries when some of the body's skin is damaged: surgical removal of the damaged skin (excision or debridement) is followed by grafting, which accelerates healing and reduces scarring and contracture.

Classification

Grafts are classified by source: autografts come from the same individual, allografts from another human donor, xenografts from another species (such as porcine skin), and synthetic or cultured substitutes are also available. By thickness, split-thickness grafts contain epidermis and part of the dermis and are harvested with a dermatome, often meshed to expand coverage; full-thickness grafts contain the entire dermis and give better cosmetic and functional results in smaller areas, such as the face and hands.

Procedure and healing

A graft survives initially by plasmatic imbibition, then by inosculation and revascularization from the wound bed. Successful "take" requires a clean, vascular wound bed, close contact, and immobility; failure can result from hematoma, infection, or shear. Donor sites for split-thickness grafts heal spontaneously within about two weeks but may scar.
