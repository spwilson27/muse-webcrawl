Linux distribution

A Linux distribution (often abbreviated as distro) is an operating system made from a software collection that includes the Linux kernel and often a package management system. Linux users usually obtain their operating system by downloading one of the Linux distributions, which are available for a wide variety of systems ranging from embedded devices and personal computers to powerful supercomputers.

A simplified family tree of major Linux distributions.

Components

A typical distribution consists of the Linux kernel plus supporting system software and libraries, many of which are provided by the GNU Project, along with an init system such as systemd, windowing and desktop software such as the X Window System or Wayland, and application packages managed through formats such as .deb or .rpm.

Major families

Most distributions descend from a handful of early systems. The Debian family includes Ubuntu, Linux Mint and Raspberry Pi OS. The Red Hat family includes Fedora and Red Hat Enterprise Linux. Other influential lines include Arch Linux, Slackware (the oldest surviving distribution, founded 1993), Gentoo, openSUSE and Alpine. Google's Android and ChromeOS are also built on the Linux kernel.

Types and uses

Distributions may follow fixed-release or rolling-release models, target desktops, servers, routers, or security and forensic work, and be community-run or commercially supported. By most counts there are several hundred actively maintained distributions.
