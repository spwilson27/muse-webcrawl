Pre-Romanesque art and architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pre-Romanesque art and architecture is the period in European art from either the emergence of the Merovingian kingdom in about 500 CE or from the Carolingian Renaissance in the late 8th century, to the beginning of the 11th century Romanesque period. The term is generally used in English only for architecture and monumental sculpture, and covers the artistic production of Western Europe after the fall of the Western Roman Empire and before the mature Romanesque style.

Characteristics

Pre-Romanesque building drew on Roman, early Christian, Byzantine, and Germanic traditions. Typical features include basilican plans adapted into cruciform churches, the use of the horseshoe arch in Visigothic and Mozarabic Spain, massive stone barrel vaults in some regions, and rich programmes of carved decoration, metalwork, and illuminated manuscript painting — the Insular art of Britain and Ireland, Carolingian art, and Ottonian art being the best-known schools.

Regional schools

Iberian peninsula

Visigothic churches such as San Juan de Baños (661) display horseshoe arches and sculpted friezes. After the Muslim conquest, Christian building in the north produced the Asturian pre-Romanesque of the 9th century, exemplified by Santa María del Naranco, and the Mozarabic churches of the Duero valley.

Carolingian and Ottonian lands

Charlemagne's Palatine Chapel at Aachen (c. 796–805) revived monumental Roman forms. Ottonian architecture in Germany, with westworks, alternating supports, and early galleries, laid foundations for the Romanesque.

Italy and the Lombards

Lombard building preserved late-antique techniques and produced distinctive stuccowork and sculpture, as at Cividale del Friuli.
