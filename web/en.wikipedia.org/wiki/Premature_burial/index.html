Premature burial

Premature burial, also known as live burial or burial alive, is the burial of a living person. It may occur accidentally, when a person is mistakenly declared dead, or intentionally, as a form of torture, murder, or execution. The fear of being buried alive is called taphophobia.

History

Accounts of accidental premature burial date to antiquity; Pliny the Elder recorded cases of persons reviving on their funeral pyres. In the 18th and 19th centuries, public anxiety about the reliability of signs of death — before modern diagnostic criteria — led to the design of "safety coffins" equipped with bells, flags, ladders, or breathing tubes, and to waiting mortuaries in Germany where corpses were kept under observation until putrefaction confirmed death. Patents for safety coffins were numerous in the late 19th century.

Deliberate live burial

Live burial has been used historically as a punishment and execution method in several cultures, and it occurred in wartime atrocities in the 20th century. In literature, premature burial is a recurring theme, most famously in Edgar Allan Poe's short story "The Premature Burial" (1844), which drew on contemporary reports and popular fears.

Modern assessment

With modern medical determination of death, accidental burial of a living person has become extremely rare in developed countries, though isolated mistaken-death incidents are still reported. Hair and nail growth after death — caused by skin retraction — helped sustain older myths of corpses reviving in graves.
