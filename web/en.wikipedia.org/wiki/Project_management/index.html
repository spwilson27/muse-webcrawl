Project management


Illustration placeholder: a Gantt chart, a classic project scheduling tool

Project management is the process of supervising the work of a team to achieve all project goals within the given constraints. This information is usually described in project documentation, created at the beginning of the development process. The primary constraints are scope, time and budget. The secondary challenge is to optimize the allocation of necessary inputs and apply them to meet pre-defined objectives.

The objective of project management is to produce a complete project which complies with the client's objectives. In many cases, the objective of project management is also to shape or reform the client's brief to feasibly address the client's objectives. Once the client's objectives are established, they should influence all decisions made by other people involved in the project — for example, project managers, designers, contractors and subcontractors.

History

Until 1900, civil engineering projects were generally managed by creative architects, engineers, and master builders. The formal discipline emerged from Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management, Henry Gantt's Gantt chart (1910s), the PERT and critical path method (CPM) techniques developed for the US Navy's Polaris programme in the 1950s, and the foundation of the Project Management Institute (PMI) in 1969, whose PMBOK Guide became the field's standard reference.

Project management processes

The traditional phased approach identifies five process groups: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. Not all projects will have every phase, and not all will visit phases in the same order. Common tools include the work breakdown structure (WBS), the Gantt chart, the critical path, earned value management, and risk registers.

Initiating

Defines the project at a broad level: the business case, the project charter, and the identification of stakeholders.

Planning

Sets scope, schedule, cost, quality, communications, and risk management plans; produces the project management plan.

Executing and monitoring

Carries out the plan while tracking progress, managing changes, and resolving issues; performance is compared against the baseline.

Closing

Formally accepts the deliverables, releases resources, and records lessons learned.

Approaches

Waterfall methodology proceeds through fixed sequential phases; agile methods (notably Scrum and Kanban), originating in software development and codified in the 2001 Agile Manifesto, proceed in short iterations with continuous customer feedback. Lean project management adapts manufacturing's waste-reduction principles. The "triple constraint" — scope, time, and cost — and its extension to a project management triangle with quality, summarises the central trade-offs.
