Colonisation of Africa

The colonisation of Africa by European powers began with coastal trading posts in the 15th century and culminated in the 'Scramble for Africa' of the late 19th century, when Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Italy, Portugal and Spain partitioned nearly the whole continent. Most colonies won independence between the 1950s and 1970s.

Scramble for Africa

European penetration of the interior accelerated with quinine, steamships and telegraphs; King Leopold II's personal Congo Free State (1885–1908) became notorious for atrocities in the rubber trade. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 set rules requiring 'effective occupation', and within 30 years borders were drawn with little regard for African peoples, languages or states.

Colonial rule and resistance

Systems ranged from British indirect rule to French assimilation and Portuguese forced labour; economies were restructured around extraction of minerals and cash crops, using taxes, concessions and coercion. Resistance was constant, from the Maji Maji and Herero wars to later nationalist movements. Italy's defeat by Ethiopia at Adwa (1896) preserved Ethiopian independence.

Decolonisation and legacy

Sudan and Ghana opened the wave in 1956–57; 1960, the 'Year of Africa', saw 17 nations independent, while Portuguese colonies held out until 1975. Legacies include inherited borders, extractive institutions, underdevelopment debates and neocolonial economic ties.
