A tape drive is a data storage device that reads and writes data on a magnetic tape. Data storage on magnetic tape has been used since the earliest days of computing — tape was the primary storage medium of the UNIVAC I in 1951 — and tape drives remain in widespread use today for system backup, data archiving, and other offline, high-capacity storage, because tape offers very low cost per bit and low power consumption for data at rest.

Operation

A tape drive moves tape past read/write heads at controlled speed and tension. Because tape is a sequential-access medium, locating a particular file may require winding through much of the reel, so tape is poorly suited to random-access workloads but excellent for streaming large volumes of data in order. Early drives used open reels of half-inch tape with formats such as nine-track; later designs enclosed the tape in cartridges and cassettes, including QIC, DAT/DDS, AIT, and DLT. Modern enterprise drives use single-reel cartridges with serpentine linear recording, writing many parallel tracks back and forth along the length of the tape.

Modern formats

The dominant standard is Linear Tape-Open (LTO), developed jointly by HP, IBM, and Certance (later Quantum) and first released in 2000. Successive LTO generations have roughly doubled native cartridge capacity; LTO-9 cartridges hold 18 TB of uncompressed data, with a roadmap extending to far larger capacities. LTO drives support hardware compression, encryption, and the LTFS file system, which makes a tape appear as a browsable volume. IBM's enterprise TS series serves mainframe and high-end markets. Tape libraries automate cartridge handling with robotics, scaling to exabytes in the largest archival installations, such as those operated by hyperscale cloud providers for cold storage.

Advantages and disadvantages

Tape's strengths are economics and durability: media cost per terabyte is a fraction of that of disk, drives consume power only when active, cartridges stored offline are immune to ransomware and network attack (an "air gap"), and modern tape has a rated shelf life of 15–30 years. Its weaknesses are slow seek times (tens of seconds on average), the need for periodic migration to new media generations, and sensitivity to storage conditions. The long-predicted death of tape has repeatedly failed to arrive; shipments of tape capacity have continued to grow with the explosion of archival data.

History

Magnetic tape data storage began with the UNIVAC's Uniservo drive (1951), which used metal tape, and IBM's 726/727 vacuum-column drives of the 1950s, which popularised plastic tape on 7-track and later 9-track reels. DEC's DECtape and home-computer cassette storage are notable descendants. By the 1990s tape had retreated from primary storage to its enduring backup and archive niche.
