Nestorius (c. 386 – c. 450) was the Archbishop of Constantinople from 428 to 431. A monk and priest from Antioch formed in the theological tradition of Theodore of Mopsuestia, he became embroiled in a controversy over the title Theotokos ("God-bearer") for the Virgin Mary, which led to his condemnation and deposition at the Council of Ephesus in 431 and gave rise to the doctrine known as Nestorianism.

Early life

Nestorius was born at Germanicia in Roman Syria (now Kahramanmaraş, Turkey). He studied at Antioch, became a monk, and gained a reputation as an eloquent preacher. Emperor Theodosius II appointed him Archbishop of Constantinople in 428, where he quickly moved against heretical groups in the capital.

The Theotokos controversy

Nestorius opposed applying the popular title Theotokos ("Mother of God") to Mary, arguing that she bore the man Jesus (and so might be called Christotokos, "Christ-bearer"), while God the Word could not be born or suffer. His preaching was attacked by Cyril of Alexandria, who defended the unity of Christ's person and accused Nestorius of dividing Christ into two persons — a "loose conjunction" of the divine Word and the man Jesus. The dispute combined doctrinal differences between the Alexandrian and Antiochene schools with church-political rivalry.

Council of Ephesus and exile

Theodosius II convened a council at Ephesus in 431. Cyril's party opened proceedings before the Antiochene bishops arrived, condemned Nestorius, and deposed him; the rival party in turn condemned Cyril. The emperor ultimately confirmed Nestorius's deposition. Nestorius retired to his monastery near Antioch and was later exiled to the Great Oasis of Kharga in Egypt, where he died around 450.

Later reputation and writings

The Church of the East, centred in Persia, honoured Nestorius and Theodore and was long labelled "Nestorian" by its opponents, though scholars note it did not necessarily teach the two-person doctrine attributed to him. Nestorius's own major surviving work, the Bazaar of Heracleides, written near the end of his life and preserved in Syriac, presents a Christology closer to the later orthodox definition, leading some scholars to argue that he was not "Nestorian" in the sense condemned. In 1994 the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East signed a Common Christological Declaration partly resolving the ancient dispute.
