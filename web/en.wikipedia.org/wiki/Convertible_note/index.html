Convertible note

A convertible note (or convertible debt) is a type of short-term debt, most often issued by early-stage companies, that converts into equity — typically in conjunction with a future financing round. Investors loan money to a startup and instead of receiving principal and interest back, they receive equity in the company at the time of conversion.

Convertible notes became a popular seed-stage financing instrument because they allow a company and its investors to defer the question of valuation until a later priced round, avoiding the cost and delay of negotiating a valuation for a very young company.

Features

Key terms of a convertible note typically include:

  • Principal and interest — the amount loaned, which accrues a modest rate of interest until conversion or maturity.
  • Maturity date — the date by which the note must be repaid or converted if no financing event has occurred.
  • Conversion trigger — usually a qualified equity financing above a stated threshold.
  • Discount — noteholders often convert at a discount (commonly 10–20%) to the price paid by new investors, rewarding them for early risk.
  • Valuation cap — a maximum company valuation at which the note converts, protecting early investors from dilution if the company's value rises sharply.

Alternatives

In 2013 Y Combinator introduced the SAFE (simple agreement for future equity), an instrument similar in purpose to a convertible note but structured as a warrant rather than debt: it carries no interest and no maturity date. SAFEs and convertible notes coexist as the two dominant seed financing instruments.
