Auditory hallucination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An auditory hallucination, or paracusia, is a form of hallucination that involves perceiving sounds without auditory stimulus. A common form of auditory hallucination involves hearing one or more voices without a speaker present, known as an auditory verbal hallucination. This may be associated with psychotic disorders, most notably schizophrenia, and this phenomenon is often used to diagnose these conditions. However, individuals without any psychiatric disease may hear voices, including those under the influence of mind-altering substances, such as cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines, and PCP.

There are three main categories into which the hearing of talking voices can fall: a person hearing a voice speak one's thoughts, a person hearing one or more voices arguing, or a person hearing a voice narrating their own actions. These three categories do not account for all types of auditory hallucinations.

Causes

Auditory hallucinations are a hallmark symptom of schizophrenia, occurring in an estimated 60–80% of individuals with the condition. They also occur in bipolar disorder during manic or depressive episodes, in severe major depression with psychotic features, in post-traumatic stress disorder, and in some neurological conditions. Non-clinical voice hearing is also reported by a significant minority of the general population.

Mechanisms

Neuroimaging studies implicate the primary auditory cortex, superior temporal gyrus, and speech production areas such as Broca's area. One influential model holds that auditory verbal hallucinations arise from a failure of source monitoring: inner speech is misattributed to an external source. Elevated dopamine signalling in subcortical regions has also been proposed to confer aberrant salience on internally generated percepts.

Treatment

Where hallucinations are distressing or linked to a psychotic disorder, antipsychotic medication is the mainstay of treatment. Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for psychosis can help people develop coping strategies, and avatar therapy, in which a person dialogues with a digital embodiment of their persecutory voice, has shown promising results in trials.

Cultural aspects

The interpretation of voices varies widely across cultures. In some contexts, hearing voices is regarded as a spiritual gift, as in shamanic traditions or among certain religious communities, whereas in Western clinical settings it is primarily framed as a symptom. Research by anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann and others suggests that cultural expectations shape whether voices are experienced as harsh and violent or benign and playful.
