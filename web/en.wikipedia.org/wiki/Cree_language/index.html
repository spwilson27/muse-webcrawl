Cree language

Cree is written in Canadian Aboriginal syllabics and in Latin script

Cree (also known as Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi) is a group of closely related Algonquian languages spoken by approximately 117,000 people across Canada, from the Northwest Territories and Alberta to Labrador, making it the Indigenous language with the largest number of speakers in Canada. It is spoken by the Cree and related peoples and forms a dialect continuum in which neighbouring varieties are mutually intelligible but distant ones may not be.

Dialects

Major varieties include Plains Cree (y-dialect), Woods Cree (ð/th-dialect), Swampy Cree (n-dialect), Moose Cree (l-dialect), and East Cree or Eeyou, alongside the closely related Montagnais (Innu-aimun) and Naskapi. The dialects are often named for how the Proto-Algonquian sound *l is realised in each — as y, th, n, l, or r.

Writing system

Cree syllabics were devised in the 1840s by the Methodist missionary James Evans, working with Cree and Ojibwe speakers; the syllabary uses rotated characters to indicate vowels and was adapted to several other Indigenous languages, becoming the basis of Canadian Aboriginal syllabics. In the Plains, a standardised Latin roman orthography is also widely used.

Grammar

Cree is a polysynthetic language: verbs are highly complex, encoding subject, object, direction, and manner in a single word. Nouns are classified as animate or inanimate, and the language distinguishes proximate and obviative third persons. Word order is flexible, with emphasis marked rather than fixed grammatical roles.

Status

Cree has official status in the Northwest Territories and is used in education, broadcasting (such as CBC North), and publishing. Like other Indigenous languages in Canada it faces pressure from English and French, and revitalisation programs — immersion schools, dictionaries, and teacher training — are under way in many communities.
