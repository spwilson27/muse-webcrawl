Bulla (seal)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Bulla

A bulla (from Latin, "bubble, amulet"; plural bullae) is a lump of clay, or a disc of metal (typically lead), that was moulded around a cord and stamped with a seal. Bullae were used in antiquity and the Middle Ages to secure documents and containers, to authenticate goods, and to record transactions. Because the clay was baked hard or the lead crimped shut, a bulla could not be removed without breaking it, making tampering evident.

Origins and administrative use

Clay bullae are among the earliest administrative artefacts of the ancient Near East, appearing by the 8th millennium BC as hollow clay envelopes enclosing tokens used in accounting. With the invention of writing, bullae came to bear seal impressions and sometimes cuneiform inscriptions recording commodities and parties to a transaction. In the biblical world, clay bullae bearing Hebrew seal impressions — several naming officials known from the Book of Jeremiah, and the famous, though debated, bulla of King Hezekiah — are important archaeological evidence for administration in ancient Judah.

Papal and medieval bullae

In the medieval West the best-known bullae are the lead seals of the papacy. A papal document known as a "bull" takes its name from the leaden bulla attached to it, stamped on one side with the heads of Saints Peter and Paul and on the other with the name of the issuing pope. Papal bullae were cast in lead (gold for exceptional documents) and attached with cords of hemp or silk. Byzantine emperors likewise used lead seals, of which tens of thousands survive, providing a major source for the study of Byzantine administration — a discipline called sigillography.

Related uses

The term also denotes the round amulet worn by Roman boys (bulla), a distinct object sharing only the name's sense of a rounded lump. In commerce, lead bale seals and customs bullae continued the same function into modern times.
