Persistent depressive disorder

Persistent depressive disorder
Other name: Dysthymia
Specialty: Psychiatry
Duration: Depressed mood most of the day, for at least 2 years
Treatment: Psychotherapy, antidepressant medication

Persistent depressive disorder (PDD), historically known as dysthymia, is a mental disorder characterised by a chronically depressed mood lasting for at least two years in adults (one year in children and adolescents). The DSM-5 consolidated the former diagnoses of dysthymia and chronic major depressive disorder under this single category.

Signs and symptoms

In addition to depressed mood, symptoms may include poor appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy or fatigue, low self-esteem, poor concentration or difficulty making decisions, and feelings of hopelessness. The symptoms are generally less intense than those of a major depressive episode but are long-lasting and can seriously impair work, school, and relationships. When a major depressive episode is superimposed on PDD it is sometimes called "double depression".

Causes and risk factors

The causes are multifactorial, involving genetic vulnerability, neurochemistry, early life stress, chronic medical illness, and co-occurring conditions such as anxiety disorders and substance use. Onset is often in childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood, and the disorder frequently goes untreated because sufferers come to regard the low mood as part of their personality.

Treatment

Treatment commonly combines antidepressant medication — particularly SSRIs — with psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy or the Cognitive Behavioral Analysis System of Psychotherapy (CBASP), which was developed specifically for chronic depression. Combined treatment tends to be more effective than either alone.

Epidemiology

Lifetime prevalence is estimated at roughly 2–6 percent of the general population, with higher rates among women.
