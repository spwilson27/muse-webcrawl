Dialectical behavior therapy

DBT combines individual therapy, skills training, and coaching.

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) is a type of talk therapy (psychotherapy) based on cognitive behavioral therapy, adapted for people who experience emotions very intensely. It was developed by psychologist Marsha Linehan in the 1980s, originally to treat borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidality, for which it has the strongest evidence base of any psychotherapy.

Principles

DBT rests on a dialectical worldview emphasising the synthesis of opposites — above all, acceptance and change. Its biosocial theory holds that emotional dysregulation arises from a transaction between a biologically sensitive individual and an invalidating environment. Therapy balances validation and acceptance strategies with problem-solving and change strategies, drawing also on mindfulness practices adapted from Zen Buddhism.

Components and skills

Comprehensive DBT includes individual therapy, group skills training, between-session phone coaching, and a therapist consultation team. Skills are taught in four modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. Treatment is organised in stages, prioritising life-threatening behaviours first, then therapy-interfering behaviours, then quality-of-life issues.

Evidence and uses

Randomised trials support DBT for reducing self-harm and suicidal behaviour in borderline personality disorder. Adaptations are used for adolescents, substance use disorders, eating disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Complete, adherent DBT is resource-intensive, and research continues on which components drive its effects.
