Population attributable fraction

The population attributable fraction (PAF), also called the population attributable risk or population attributable proportion, is a measure used in epidemiology. It is the proportion of cases (incidence) in a population that can be attributed to a particular exposure — the fraction of disease that would theoretically be eliminated if the exposure were completely removed, assuming the exposure is causal.

Calculation

For a binary exposure, PAF can be computed as Pe(RR − 1) / (Pe(RR − 1) + 1), where Pe is the prevalence of exposure in the population and RR is the relative risk. Levin's formula, published in 1953, is the classical expression. Adjustments are needed when confounding is present or when multiple risk factors act together.

Interpretation and uses

PAF depends both on the strength of association and on how common the exposure is, so a strong risk factor that is rare may produce a smaller PAF than a weaker but widespread one — as with smoking and lung cancer, where PAF is often around 85–90%. Policy-makers use PAF estimates to prioritise public health interventions; related measures include the attributable fraction among the exposed and the preventable fraction.
