Coliform bacteria

Coliform bacteria are defined as all aerobic and facultatively anaerobic, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped bacteria that ferment lactose with gas and acid production within 48 hours at 35 °C. They are a commonly used indicator of sanitary quality of foods and water. Coliforms can be found in the aquatic environment, in soil and on vegetation; they are universally present in large numbers in the feces of warm-blooded animals, including humans.

Genera and significance

Typical genera include Escherichia, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, and Serratia. While coliform bacteria themselves do not usually cause serious illness, their presence indicates that other disease-causing organisms (pathogens) may also be present. The subgroup of fecal coliforms, which ferment lactose at 44.5 °C, is more specifically associated with fecal contamination; among them, Escherichia coli is regarded as the best indicator of recent fecal pollution and of the possible presence of enteric pathogens such as Salmonella and viruses.

Testing

Standard methods for detecting coliforms in water include the multiple-tube fermentation (most probable number) test, membrane filtration onto selective media, and defined-substrate tests such as Colilert, which detect total coliforms and E. coli simultaneously by colour and fluorescence changes. Drinking-water regulations in many countries require the absence of E. coli in 100 mL samples.

Indicator concept

Coliforms became the classic bacterial indicator because they are abundant in feces, easy and cheap to detect, and survive in water about as long as most enteric pathogens. Limitations are recognised: some pathogens, especially viruses and protozoan cysts such as Cryptosporidium, are more resistant to disinfection than coliforms, so coliform-free water is not an absolute guarantee of safety, and environmental coliforms can regrow in distribution systems and biofilms.
