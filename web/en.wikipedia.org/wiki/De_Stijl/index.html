De Stijl

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (1930) by Piet Mondrian: black grid lines and planes of primary colour on white]

De Stijl (Dutch for 'The Style'), also known as Neoplasticism, was a Dutch art and design movement founded in 1917 in Leiden around the journal of the same name edited by Theo van Doesburg. Its adherents — Piet Mondrian, Gerrit Rietveld, Bart van der Leck and others — sought a universal visual language of straight lines, right angles, and primary colours plus black, white and grey, applied across painting, architecture, furniture and typography.

Mondrian's mature grid paintings and Rietveld's Red-Blue Chair (1917–18) and Schröder House in Utrecht (1924) are its emblematic works; van Doesburg, restless, introduced diagonals ('Elementarism') and carried De Stijl ideas across Europe until his death in 1931.

Principles

Neoplasticism grew from the theosophical-tinged belief that art should express the underlying harmony of the universe, stripped of individual emotion and natural appearance. The journal De Stijl published manifestos proclaiming a new, collective, machine-age consciousness after the First World War.

Influence

Brief-lived — the journal ended in 1932 — De Stijl profoundly influenced the Bauhaus (van Doesburg taught informally in Weimar), modernist architecture and graphic design; its red-blue-yellow geometry remains an instantly recognised design vocabulary.
