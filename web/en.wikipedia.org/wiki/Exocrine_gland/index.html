Exocrine gland

Exocrine glands are glands that secrete their products onto an epithelial surface — such as the skin or the lining of the gastrointestinal tract — by way of a duct. They are distinguished from endocrine glands, which are ductless and release hormones into the bloodstream. Examples of exocrine glands include sweat glands, salivary glands, mammary glands, sebaceous glands, lacrimal glands, and the exocrine pancreas.

Classification

Exocrine glands are classified by the nature of their secretion (serous, mucous, or mixed/seromucous), by structure (simple or compound ducts; tubular, alveolar/acinar, or tubuloalveolar secretory units), and by the mechanism of secretion:

  • Merocrine — products released by exocytosis (e.g., eccrine sweat glands, pancreas).
  • Apocrine — the apical portion of the cell is shed with the secretion.
  • Holocrine — the whole cell disintegrates to release its contents (e.g., sebaceous glands).

Function

Exocrine secretions include sweat, saliva, tears, milk, sebum, mucus, and digestive enzymes. The pancreas is a mixed gland: its acinar tissue is exocrine, secreting digestive juice into the duodenum, while its islets are endocrine.
