Autosomal dominant

In genetics, autosomal dominant is one of several ways that a trait or disorder can be passed down through families. In an autosomal dominant disease, if you get the variant gene from only one parent, you will have the disease or trait. The gene in question is located on one of the numbered, or non-sex, chromosomes (autosomes). A person with an autosomal dominant condition has a 50% chance with each pregnancy of passing the variant to a child.

Characteristics

  • The trait appears in every generation (vertical inheritance), unless it arises from a new mutation or shows reduced penetrance.
  • Affected individuals are usually heterozygotes; homozygous cases, when viable, are often more severely affected.
  • Males and females are affected equally, and father-to-son transmission occurs (excluding X-linkage).
  • An affected person has, on average, a 1-in-2 chance of passing the variant to each offspring.
  • Unaffected family members do not usually transmit the condition.

Mechanisms

Dominant disorders arise through several molecular mechanisms: haploinsufficiency, in which one working copy cannot produce enough protein; dominant-negative effects, in which the abnormal protein interferes with the normal one; and gain-of-function mutations, in which the altered protein acquires new or excessive activity. Many dominant conditions show variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance, so severity differs between relatives carrying the same variant.

Examples

Well-known autosomal dominant conditions include Huntington's disease, Marfan syndrome, achondroplasia, neurofibromatosis type 1, familial hypercholesterolemia, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, and hereditary forms of breast and ovarian cancer due to BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants.
