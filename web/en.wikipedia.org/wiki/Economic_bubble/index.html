An economic bubble (also a speculative bubble or financial bubble) is a situation in which asset prices rise far above the assets' intrinsic values — the income or utility they can plausibly generate — driven by exuberant expectations that prices will keep rising, until the bubble "bursts" and prices collapse, often causing financial crisis and recession. Because intrinsic value is hard to measure, economists dispute whether particular episodes were true bubbles, and bubbles are far easier to identify in hindsight than in real time.

Stages and causes

Charles Kindleberger, building on Hyman Minsky, described a recurring pattern: a displacement (a new technology, financial innovation or policy change) creates new profit opportunities; credit-fuelled buying produces a boom; euphoria sets in as speculation detaches from value and latecomers pile in on "greater fool" logic; insiders begin profit-taking; and finally doubt triggers panic selling. Contributing mechanisms studied by economists include cheap credit and low interest rates, herd behaviour and momentum trading, overconfidence and other behavioural biases, agency problems (managers risking others' money), and financial deregulation. Some rational-bubble theories show that even fully rational investors may ride a bubble if they expect to sell before it bursts.

Historical examples

  • Tulip mania (Dutch Republic, 1636–37) — the canonical early bubble, though modern historians debate the scale of the price rises and damage.
  • Mississippi and South Sea bubbles (1719–20) — John Law's scheme in France and the South Sea Company's debt conversion in Britain crashed together, ruining thousands.
  • Railway Mania (Britain, 1840s) and the panic cycles of 1873, 1907 and 1929.
  • Japanese asset price bubble (late 1980s) — land and equity prices collapsed from 1990, ushering in the "Lost Decades".
  • Dot-com bubble (1995–2000) — internet stocks peaked in March 2000 and lost most of their value.
  • United States housing bubble (2000s) — inflated by subprime lending and securitisation, its collapse produced the 2007–08 global financial crisis.

Effects and policy

Bursting bubbles destroy wealth, leave households and banks burdened with debt, and can cause banking crises and prolonged recessions ("balance-sheet recessions"). Policy responses are contested: central banks debate whether to "lean against" bubbles with tighter money or merely "clean up" afterwards, and macroprudential tools (loan-to-value limits, countercyclical capital buffers) aim to restrain credit-driven bubbles without targeting prices directly.
