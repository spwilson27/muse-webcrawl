Substituted tryptamine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Substituted tryptamines are a class of organic compounds based on the tryptamine skeleton — an indole ring joined to an aminoethyl side chain — with chemical substitutions at various positions. The parent compound, tryptamine itself, occurs naturally as a trace amine in the mammalian brain. Many substituted tryptamines are psychoactive, acting principally as agonists at serotonin receptors, especially the 5-HT2A receptor, and include both naturally occurring compounds and synthetic designer drugs.

Natural compounds

Substituted tryptamines are widespread in nature. Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) and the sleep-regulating hormone melatonin are essential physiological examples. Psychedelic natural products include psilocybin and psilocin from "magic mushrooms", N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) from numerous plants, bufotenin from certain toads and seeds, and 5-MeO-DMT. Ergot alkaloids such as ergine, and the beta-carboline-containing preparations used in ayahuasca, are structurally related.

Synthetic compounds and pharmacology

Synthetic substituted tryptamines were extensively explored by the chemist Alexander Shulgin, who documented dozens of compounds — including DiPT, DPT, 5-MeO-DiPT, and alpha-methyltryptamine (AMT) — in his book TiHKAL ("Tryptamines I Have Known and Loved"). Substitutions on the indole ring (particularly at the 4- and 5-positions), on the side chain, and on the nitrogen atom produce compounds ranging from classic psychedelics to entactogens and research chemicals. Many have been placed under drug-control legislation in various countries, in some cases under analogue or blanket-ban provisions.

Medical relevance

Beyond recreational and research use, the tryptamine framework underlies important medicines: the triptans used to treat migraine, such as sumatriptan, are substituted tryptamines engineered to act on serotonin 5-HT1B/1D receptors. Psilocybin and related compounds have been investigated in clinical trials for depression, anxiety, and addiction, receiving "breakthrough therapy" designations for some indications in the United States.
