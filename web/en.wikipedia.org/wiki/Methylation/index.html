Methylation

In chemistry and biology, methylation is the addition of a methyl group (–CH3) to a substrate, or the substitution of an atom or group by a methyl group. Methylation is a fundamental biochemical modification affecting DNA, RNA, proteins, and small molecules, and an important reaction in organic synthesis.

In biochemistry

Biological methylation is catalysed by methyltransferase enzymes, which usually transfer the methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the substrate. DNA methylation — typically the addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon of cytosine in CpG dinucleotides in vertebrates — is a central epigenetic mechanism regulating gene expression, genomic imprinting, and X-chromosome inactivation; aberrant DNA methylation patterns are hallmarks of cancer. Protein methylation modifies lysine and arginine residues, notably on histones, where it forms part of the "histone code" that modulates chromatin. RNA methylation (for example m6A) has likewise emerged as a major regulatory layer.

In chemistry

In organic chemistry, methylation uses reagents such as methyl iodide, dimethyl sulfate, diazomethane, and methyl triflate, or reductive methylation (Eschweiler–Clarke) of amines. Catalytic methylation with methanol is an industrial process of growing interest.

Demethylation

Removal of methyl groups is called demethylation; in DNA it may be passive (dilution through replication) or active (involving TET enzymes), and is essential to epigenetic reprogramming in early development.
