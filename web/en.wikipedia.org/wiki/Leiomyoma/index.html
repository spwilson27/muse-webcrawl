Leiomyoma

[Image: Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids)]

A leiomyoma (plural leiomyomas or leiomyomata), also known as a fibroid, is a benign smooth muscle neoplasm that very rarely becomes cancer (0.1%). They can occur in any organ, but most commonly arise in the uterus, small bowel and the oesophagus. The word derives from Greek leio- (smooth) + myo- (muscle) + -oma (tumour).

Uterine leiomyomas

Uterine fibroids are the most common type, affecting a large proportion of women by the age of 50. They grow from the smooth muscle of the myometrium under the influence of oestrogen and progesterone, and are classified by location: subserosal (beneath the outer surface), intramural (within the uterine wall) and submucosal (beneath the endometrium). Many are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally; others cause heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, urinary symptoms, pain and infertility or pregnancy complications. Risk factors include early menarche, nulliparity, obesity and African ancestry, in whom fibroids tend to be larger and more numerous.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by pelvic examination and ultrasound; MRI is used for detailed mapping before surgery. Treatment depends on symptoms, size, number and fertility wishes, and ranges from watchful waiting and hormonal therapies (including GnRH analogues and selective progesterone receptor modulators) to procedures such as myomectomy, hysterectomy, uterine artery embolization and focused ultrasound ablation. Malignant transformation is rare; the malignant counterpart is leiomyosarcoma, which usually arises independently.

Other sites and forms

Leiomyomas of the skin (including angioleiomyomas and pilar leiomyomas) present as small, sometimes painful nodules. Gastrointestinal leiomyomas occur most often in the oesophagus. Rare syndromes such as hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer (HLRCC), caused by fumarate hydratase mutations, feature multiple cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas and an increased risk of kidney cancer.
