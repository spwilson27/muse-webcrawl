Attrition warfare

Attrition warfare is a military strategy consisting of belligerent attempts to win a war by wearing down the enemy to the point of collapse through continuous losses in personnel and materiel. The word attrition comes from the Latin root atterere, meaning "to rub against", similar to the "grinding down" of the opponent's forces in attrition warfare.

Strategic considerations

Attrition warfare represents the art of fighting to exhaust the enemy. The side that can replace its losses more quickly, or sustain them longer, is expected to prevail. It is usually pursued when one belligerent enjoys a material advantage in manpower, industry or logistics, or when the opposing force holds strong defensive positions that cannot easily be outflanked. The strategy contrasts with manoeuvre warfare, which seeks victory through movement, surprise and the dislocation of the enemy rather than his destruction in place.

History

Early examples

During the American Civil War, Union general Ulysses S. Grant waged a campaign of continuous engagement against Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia in 1864–65, accepting heavy Union casualties in the knowledge that the Confederacy could not replace its losses. Fabian strategy, named for the Roman general Quintus Fabius Maximus who avoided pitched battle against Hannibal, is a related but distinct approach based on delay and harassment.

First World War

The static trench lines of the Western Front made attrition almost unavoidable. At the Battle of Verdun in 1916, German Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn sought to "bleed France white" by attacking a position the French would feel compelled to defend at all costs; the battle produced roughly 700,000 casualties on both sides. The battles of the Somme and Passchendaele are also frequently cited as attritional contests.

Later conflicts

Attrition featured in the Iran–Iraq War (1980–88), the later stages of the Korean War, and the Russo-Ukrainian War, where artillery-heavy fighting, drone warfare and the targeting of logistics and infrastructure have been described by analysts as a war of attrition.

Counter-strategies

Forces facing an attrition strategy may attempt to break the deadlock through new technology or tactics — as with the infiltration tactics and combined-arms methods developed late in the First World War — to open a new front, to strike at the enemy's sources of supply, or to seek a political settlement before exhaustion sets in.
