Tilt table test

A tilt table test (TTT), occasionally called a head-up tilt test (HUTT), is a medical procedure used to investigate the cause of unexplained fainting (syncope). The patient lies flat on a special table with safety straps and a footrest while heart rate and blood pressure are monitored, and the table is then tilted to a near-standing position—typically 60 to 80 degrees—for a period of time. The resulting pooling of blood in the legs places stress on the autonomic nervous system and may reproduce the patient's symptoms, revealing conditions such as vasovagal syncope, orthostatic hypotension, or postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).

Procedure

After a period of supine rest with baseline measurements, the table is raised smoothly and the patient remains tilted, usually for 20 to 45 minutes. If no symptoms occur, a provocative agent such as sublingual nitroglycerin or intravenous isoproterenol may be given and the tilt continued. A test is generally considered positive if the patient develops their typical symptoms together with a marked fall in blood pressure and/or heart rate (a vasovagal response), a sustained heart-rate increase of at least 30 beats per minute without hypotension (suggesting POTS), or a progressive fall in blood pressure on assuming the upright posture (orthostatic hypotension). The patient is returned to the horizontal position, and recovery is usually rapid.

Uses and limitations

The test is most useful when autonomic dysfunction is suspected after initial evaluation—including history, examination, lying and standing blood pressures and an ECG—has not established a cause of syncope. It can also help distinguish syncope from epilepsy in difficult cases. Limitations include variable sensitivity and specificity, day-to-day reproducibility issues, and false positives in people without syncope, so results must be interpreted in clinical context.
