Hāngī

Hāngī is a traditional New Zealand Māori method of cooking food using heated rocks in an earth oven (called an umu in other Polynesian cultures). Heated stones are placed in a pit, baskets of food are set upon them, and the whole is covered with wet cloths or sacks and then with earth, trapping steam and smoke so the food cooks slowly over several hours.

Method

A shallow pit is dug and a fire built in it with stones — traditionally volcanic rocks that do not shatter when heated — piled on top. When the fire has burned down and the stones are white hot, the embers are removed and baskets of food are placed on the stones: meats such as pork, lamb, chicken, and seafood, together with vegetables including potato, kūmara (sweet potato), pumpkin, and corn. Water is sprinkled over the food to generate steam; in geothermal areas such as Rotorua, hāngī may instead be cooked over natural steam vents or in boiling alkaline pools. The pit is covered with wet sacking and sealed with the excavated earth, and the food steams for typically three to four hours, acquiring a distinctive smoky, earthy flavour. Traditionally the food was wrapped in flax or cabbage leaves; modern hāngī commonly use wire baskets, foil, and cabbage or banana leaves.

Cultural role

Hāngī is prepared for gatherings on the marae — for weddings, funerals (tangihanga), and festivals — and the work of preparing it is a communal activity overseen by experienced cooks. It remains one of the most widely recognised elements of Māori cuisine for visitors to New Zealand and is also sold commercially and served at cultural events.
