SMAD (protein)

SMAD proteins are a family of intracellular signal-transducing proteins that mediate signalling by the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily of ligands, which includes TGF-βs, activins and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). The name is a contraction of the names of the first two identified members: Sma in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans and Mad ("Mothers against decapentaplegic") in Drosophila.

Structure

SMADs share two conserved domains: an N-terminal MH1 (Mad homology 1) domain, which can bind DNA, and a C-terminal MH2 domain, which mediates protein–protein interactions, including binding to receptors and to other SMADs. The domains are separated by a variable proline-rich linker region that is targeted by regulatory phosphorylation.

Classes

  • Receptor-regulated SMADs (R-SMADs) — SMAD1, 2, 3, 5 and 8/9. SMAD2 and SMAD3 transduce TGF-β/activin signals; SMAD1, 5 and 8/9 transduce BMP signals. They are phosphorylated directly by type I receptors.
  • Common-mediator SMAD (Co-SMAD) — SMAD4, the obligate partner that forms complexes with R-SMADs and translocates to the nucleus.
  • Inhibitory SMADs (I-SMADs) — SMAD6 and SMAD7, which antagonise signalling, in part by promoting receptor degradation.

Signalling pathway

On ligand binding, the type II receptor kinase activates the type I receptor, which phosphorylates R-SMADs at a C-terminal SSXS motif. The phosphorylated R-SMAD binds SMAD4, and the complex accumulates in the nucleus, where it regulates target gene transcription together with DNA-binding co-factors. Because SMADs themselves bind DNA weakly and with low specificity, partner transcription factors largely determine which genes are activated or repressed.

Clinical significance

SMAD4 (also known as DPC4) is a tumour suppressor frequently inactivated in pancreatic and colorectal cancers. Germline mutations in SMAD4 cause juvenile polyposis syndrome, and mutations in SMAD3 are associated with aneurysms–osteoarthritis syndrome.
