Date9–14 June 1667 (Old Style: 19–24 June 1667)
LocationChatham, Kent, England
ResultDecisive Dutch victory
BelligerentsDutch Republic; England
CommandersMichiel de Ruyter; Cornelis de Witt (political deputy); Duke of Albemarle
Strength62 warships, 15 smaller vessels, 12 fireships; c. 1,750 men ashore; some 17 English warships laid up, shore batteries
CasualtiesDutch: about 50–150 killed and wounded, 8 fireships expended. English: 13 ships destroyed, 2 captured (incl. HMS Royal Charles)

The Raid on the Medway, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War in June 1667, was a successful attack conducted by the Dutch navy on English warships laid up in the fleet anchorages off Chatham Dockyard and Gillingham in the county of Kent. At the time, the fortress of Upnor Castle and a barrier chain called the "Gillingham Line" were supposed to protect the English ships.

The Dutch, under nominal command of Lieutenant-Admiral Michiel de Ruyter, bombarded and captured the town of Sheerness, sailed up the River Thames to Gravesend, then up the River Medway to Chatham and Gillingham, where they engaged fortifications with cannon fire, burned or captured three capital ships and ten more ships of the line, and captured and towed away the flagship of the English fleet, HMS Royal Charles.

Politically, the raid was disastrous for the war plans of Charles II of England. It led to a quick end to the war and a favourable peace for the Dutch. It was one of the worst defeats in the history of the Royal Navy and of the British military. Horace George Franks called it the "most serious attack that the Royal Navy has ever received, more serious even than the bombing of Pearl Harbor".

Background

In 1667, the funds of Charles II were running short, and the English Royal Navy's strength was weakened by a reduced budget, plague, and the Great Fire of London. Because of this, Charles decided to enter peace negotiations with the Dutch at Breda, and to prevent a raid, ordered that most of the fleet be laid up at Chatham, with only a small squadron at sea. The defences at Chatham depended on the chain across the Medway at Gillingham, shore batteries, and the guns of Upnor Castle.

The raid

Sheerness

On 6 June 1667 the Dutch fleet, about 62 frigates and men-of-war, 15 smaller vessels and 12 fireships, reached the Thames Estuary. On 10 June the Dutch attacked the fort under construction at Sheerness. Eighty Dutch marines captured the fort, its 16 guns, and the stores within.

Up the Medway

On 12 June Dutch frigates and fireships attacked the Gillingham Line, breaking the great chain across the river — tradition holds that a fireship or the frigate Pro Patria severed it, though the chain appears to have been jumped or sunk under the weight of attack. The Dutch bombarded Upnor Castle and destroyed the ships Unity, Matthias and Charles V with fireships.

Burning of the fleet

On 13 June the Dutch sailed further upriver and burned three of the largest ships in the English navy: HMS Loyal London, HMS Royal James and the new HMS Royal Oak. The flagship HMS Royal Charles, the pride of the fleet, was boarded and carried off to the Netherlands as a prize, lightly defended because her guns had been removed. Her stern piece, bearing the royal arms, is still on display in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam.

Aftermath

News of the raid caused panic in London; Samuel Pepys recorded fears of a Dutch landing and of a general collapse. The humiliation hastened the conclusion of the Treaty of Breda on 31 July 1667, on terms favourable to the Dutch. The raid led directly to improvements in the defences of Chatham, Sheerness and the Thames, and to the building of new fortifications along the Medway.
