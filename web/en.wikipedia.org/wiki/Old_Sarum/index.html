Old Sarum, in Wiltshire, England, is the site of the earliest settlement of Salisbury. A mighty Iron Age hillfort, it was occupied successively by Romans, Saxons and Normans, who built a royal castle and a cathedral within its earthworks, before the city moved to the plain below in the 13th century.

History

The hillfort, originally called Sorviodunum, dates to around 400 BC. After the Norman Conquest, William the Conqueror built a motte-and-bailey castle here and in 1075 the see was transferred to Old Sarum, where a cathedral was completed in 1092 (and rebuilt after storm damage). Disputes between the garrison and the clergy, shortage of water, and the cramped site led Bishop Richard Poore to found a new cathedral in the Avon valley in 1220; the city of New Sarum — Salisbury — grew around it, and Old Sarum was gradually abandoned and quarried for stone.

The rotten borough

Despite having virtually no inhabitants, Old Sarum continued to elect two Members of Parliament until the Reform Act 1832, becoming the most notorious of the "rotten boroughs". Its owner could effectively sell the seats, a symbol of the unreformed electoral system.
