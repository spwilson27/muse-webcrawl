The Isle of Eigg (Scottish Gaelic: Eige) is one of the Small Isles in the Scottish Inner Hebrides, south of the Isle of Skye. It is famous for its dramatic pitchstone ridge, An Sgùrr, its archaeological sites, and its pioneering community buyout and renewable energy system.

Geography and geology

Eigg measures about 9 km by 5 km. Its most striking feature is An Sgùrr, a steep-sided ridge of columnar pitchstone formed by a lava flow that filled a river valley during the Palaeogene volcanic activity that also shaped Skye and Mull. The coastline includes the Singing Sands at Cleadale and the Massacre Cave and Cathedral Cave on the south shore.

History

The island has Bronze Age and Iron Age remains, an early Christian monastery at Kildonnan associated with St Donnán, who was martyred with his monks, tradition says, in 617, and later centuries under Clanranald lordship. In 1577, according to tradition, a party of MacLeods suffocated a large number of islanders hiding in the Massacre Cave. In the nineteenth century the island passed through a succession of private landlords, clearance and emigration reduced the population, and the twentieth century brought a series of controversial owners.

Community buyout and sustainability

In 1997 the islanders, together with the Scottish Wildlife Trust and Highland Council through the Isle of Eigg Heritage Trust, bought the island — a landmark in Scottish land reform. In 2008 Eigg launched an off-grid electricity system combining wind, hydro and solar power with battery storage and backup generators, cutting reliance on diesel; the project won international attention and awards as a model of island sustainability.
