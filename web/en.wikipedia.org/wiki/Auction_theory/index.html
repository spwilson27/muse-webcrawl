Auction theory is an applied branch of economics and game theory that deals with how people act in auction markets and investigates the theoretical properties of different auction formats — the rules determining who wins, what they pay, and how much revenue the seller obtains.

Standard auction types

Four standard single-item formats dominate the theory. In an English auction, bidding rises openly until one bidder remains. In a Dutch auction, the price starts high and falls until someone accepts it. In a first-price sealed-bid auction, the highest bid wins and pays its own bid. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction, the highest bid wins but pays the second-highest bid, making truthful bidding a dominant strategy.

Key results

William Vickrey's 1961 paper founded the modern field. Its most famous consequence, the revenue equivalence theorem (developed by Riley and Samuelson, and Myerson, 1981), shows that under the benchmark model — risk-neutral bidders with independent private values drawn from a common distribution — all four standard formats yield the same expected revenue. Departures from the benchmark (risk aversion, affiliated or common values, asymmetries) break the equivalence and guide practical design. The winner's curse — the tendency of the winner in a common-value auction to overpay — is a central empirical phenomenon the theory explains.

Applications

Auction theorists designed the radio spectrum auctions used by governments worldwide from the 1990s onward, raising hundreds of billions in revenue; Paul Milgrom and Robert B. Wilson received the 2020 Nobel Memorial Prize for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats, such as the simultaneous multiple-round auction. Auctions also allocate Treasury securities, electricity, emissions permits, and online advertising impressions sold in real-time bidding.
