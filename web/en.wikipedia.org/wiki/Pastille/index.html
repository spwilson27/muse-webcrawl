Pastille

A pastille is a type of sweet or medicinal lozenge made of a thick, paste-like liquid mixture that has been solidified, typically by pouring drops onto a surface to cool and harden. The word derives from the Latin pastillus, a small loaf or roll, by way of Spanish pastilla. Pastilles may be intended to be chewed or allowed to dissolve slowly in the mouth.

Types and history

Gum pastilles, made with gum arabic, were historically produced by pharmacists as a vehicle for medicines; fruit pastilles such as Rowntree's Fruit Pastilles (introduced in England in 1881) became a popular confection. Throat pastilles containing menthol, eucalyptus, or local anaesthetics remain a common form of over-the-counter remedy. A related sense of the word refers to a small cone or tablet of aromatic paste that is burned like incense to perfume a room, popular in the 19th century.

Production

Confectionery pastilles are typically made by cooking a mixture of sugar or glucose syrup with gelling agents such as gum arabic, starch, or gelatin, adding fruit juice, flavourings, and colours, then depositing the mass into moulds or dropping it onto trays to set before coating in sugar.
