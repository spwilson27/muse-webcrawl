Transdermal patch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transdermal patch is a medicated adhesive patch that is placed on the skin to deliver a specific dose of medication through the skin and into the bloodstream. One advantage is controlled, steady release of the drug over hours or days, avoiding first-pass metabolism in the liver and the peaks and troughs of oral dosing.

Design

Patches are typically built up from a backing layer, a drug reservoir or drug-in-adhesive matrix, a rate-controlling membrane in some designs, and a skin adhesive, covered by a release liner removed before use. Only relatively small, lipophilic molecules that can cross the stratum corneum are suitable for transdermal delivery.

Uses

The first transdermal patch, delivering scopolamine for motion sickness, was approved in the United States in 1979. Well-known applications include nicotine patches for smoking cessation, fentanyl patches for severe chronic pain, nitroglycerin patches for angina, estradiol and combined contraceptive hormone patches, and rivastigmine patches for dementia associated with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases.

Safety

Because a patch continues to deliver drug after application, misuse can cause overdose — fentanyl patches are a particular hazard if diverted or if heat increases absorption. Used patches may retain substantial residual drug and require careful disposal. Skin irritation at the application site is the most common adverse effect.
