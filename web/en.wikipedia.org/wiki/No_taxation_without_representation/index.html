No taxation without representation

"No taxation without representation" is a political slogan that originated in the American Revolution and expressed one of the primary grievances of the American colonists against Great Britain. In short, the colonists believed that, as they were not represented in the distant Parliament of Great Britain, taxes imposed on them by that body (such as the Stamp Act and the Townshend Acts) were unconstitutional and a denial of the colonists' rights as Englishmen.

Background

The phrase is often attributed to James Otis, who reportedly declared in 1761, during arguments against the writs of assistance, that "taxation without representation is tyranny." In Britain, defenders of the colonial taxes argued that the colonists were "virtually represented" in Parliament, as were many British subjects who could not vote. Colonists such as Daniel Dulany acknowledged virtual representation for some purposes but rejected its application to taxation, arguing that representation and taxation were inseparable in the British constitution.

American Revolution

The Stamp Act Congress of 1765 declared that no taxes could constitutionally be imposed on the colonists except by their own legislatures. The dispute escalated through the Boston Tea Party of 1773 and the Intolerable Acts, becoming a rallying cry of the Revolution.

Modern usage

The slogan is used today by advocates of voting representation for the District of Columbia, whose license plates bear the variant "Taxation Without Representation," and by residents of U.S. territories such as Puerto Rico who pay certain federal taxes without voting representation in Congress.
