Hyperthyroidism is the condition that occurs when the thyroid gland produces and releases excessive amounts of thyroid hormones (thyroxine, T4, and triiodothyronine, T3), speeding up the body's metabolism. It is the opposite of hypothyroidism. The term thyrotoxicosis refers to the clinical state of excess thyroid hormone action from any cause, including hormone released by an inflamed gland or taken in tablet form.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include weight loss despite normal or increased appetite, palpitations and rapid or irregular heartbeat, heat intolerance and sweating, fine tremor of the hands, anxiety and irritability, frequent bowel movements, menstrual disturbance, and an enlarged thyroid (goitre). In Graves' disease, the most common cause, patients may also develop bulging eyes (exophthalmos) and thickened skin over the shins. In older people symptoms may be muted ("apathetic hyperthyroidism"). A rare life-threatening complication is thyroid storm.

Causes

Graves' disease, an autoimmune disorder in which antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor, accounts for roughly 60–80% of cases in iodine-sufficient regions. Other causes include toxic multinodular goitre, a single toxic adenoma, thyroiditis (which transiently releases stored hormone), excessive iodine intake, and pituitary tumours secreting TSH.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood tests showing a suppressed TSH with elevated free T4 and/or T3. Treatment options include antithyroid drugs such as methimazole or propylthiouracil, radioactive iodine ablation, and partial or total thyroidectomy; beta blockers control symptoms such as tremor and palpitations. Choice of treatment depends on the cause, severity, age, and patient preference.
