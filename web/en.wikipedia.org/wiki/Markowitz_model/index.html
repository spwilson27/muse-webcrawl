Modern portfolio theory

Modern portfolio theory (MPT), or mean-variance analysis, is a mathematical framework for assembling a portfolio of assets such that the expected return is maximized for a given level of risk. It was introduced by Harry Markowitz in his 1952 paper "Portfolio Selection", published in The Journal of Finance, and developed in his 1959 book. The framework formalises the idea that an investor can reduce risk through diversification, because assets that are not perfectly correlated offset one another's fluctuations.

The Markowitz model

In the Markowitz model, a portfolio is described by the vector of weights invested in each asset. The portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of the assets' expected returns:

E(Rp) = Σ wi E(Ri)

The portfolio variance depends not only on each asset's variance but on the covariances between all pairs of assets:

σp² = Σi Σj wi wj σiσj ρij

where ρij is the correlation between assets i and j. When correlations are less than 1, the portfolio's risk is lower than the weighted average of the individual risks — the mathematical basis of diversification.

Efficient frontier

Solving the optimisation problem — minimising variance for each target expected return — yields the efficient frontier: the set of portfolios offering the lowest risk for their level of return. Portfolios below the frontier are inefficient; no rational risk-averse investor should hold them. The upper part of the hyperbola-shaped minimum-variance frontier is efficient. Adding a risk-free asset transforms the efficient set into a straight line tangent to the frontier at the "tangency portfolio", the basis of the capital market line and the later Capital Asset Pricing Model developed by Sharpe, Lintner and Mossin.

Assumptions

The model assumes investors are rational and risk-averse, markets are frictionless (no taxes or transaction costs), returns are adequately described by their means and variances (e.g. normally distributed), and all investors share the same expectations and investment horizon.

Criticism

MPT has been criticised for relying on historical estimates of returns and covariances that may not persist; for treating variance as the whole of risk, though investors may care more about downside loss; and for assuming normally distributed returns when real markets exhibit fat tails. Post-modern portfolio theory and behavioural finance develop alternatives, but mean-variance optimisation remains widely used in practice. Markowitz shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work.
