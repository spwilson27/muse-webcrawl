Automatic dependent surveillance – broadcast

Automatic dependent surveillance – broadcast (ADS-B) is a surveillance technology in which an aircraft periodically broadcasts its position, velocity, identity and other information derived from on-board systems, without the need for interrogation by ground radar. The broadcasts can be received by air traffic control ground stations and by other aircraft, improving situational awareness in the cockpit and on the ground.

Principle

ADS-B is automatic because it requires no pilot or controller input, dependent because it relies on the aircraft's own navigation systems to determine position, and a broadcast surveillance system because data are transmitted to all receivers in range. The principal implementation uses the 1090 MHz Mode S Extended Squitter; in the United States, aircraft operating below 18,000 feet may instead use the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT).

Functions

ADS-B Out is the broadcast of an aircraft's own state vector and is the function mandated by regulators. ADS-B In is the reception and display of other traffic and of uplinked information; in the US, ground stations rebroadcast traffic information (ADS-R and TIS-B) and broadcast weather and aeronautical data (FIS-B) to equipped aircraft. Space-based ADS-B receivers on satellite constellations extend coverage over oceans and remote regions where ground stations are impractical.

Adoption and mandates

The FAA's NextGen programme made ADS-B Out equipage mandatory in most US controlled airspace from 1 January 2020. The European Union, Australia and other states have adopted similar mandates. ADS-B progressively replaces or supplements secondary surveillance radar, offering higher update rates, lower infrastructure cost and coverage in mountainous or offshore areas.

Security considerations

ADS-B messages are unauthenticated and unencrypted, so they can be received by hobbyists — enabling public flight-tracking services — but can also in principle be spoofed or jammed. Researchers have demonstrated these vulnerabilities, and regulators continue to study authentication measures while retaining radar and multilateration as independent surveillance sources.
