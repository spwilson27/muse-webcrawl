Eye movement in reading

A record of fixations and saccades as a reader scans a passage

Eye movement in reading is the visual processing that occurs as the eyes move across written text. Contrary to the impression of a smooth sweep, the eyes move in a series of rapid jumps called saccades, separated by brief pauses called fixations during which visual information is acquired. The study of these movements, pioneered in the late nineteenth century by Louis Émile Javal and Edmund Huey, is a central method in the psychology of reading.

Characteristics

In skilled reading of English, fixations last on average about 200–250 milliseconds, and saccades carry the eyes forward roughly seven to nine character spaces. About 10–15 per cent of saccades are regressions back to earlier text, which increase when the text is difficult. Readers do not fixate every word: short, frequent and predictable words are often skipped, while long and unfamiliar words attract longer and repeated fixations. The region from which useful information is extracted, the perceptual span, extends only a few characters to the left of fixation and some 14–15 characters to the right in English, and is reversed in scripts read right to left.

Skilled and developing readers

Children and poor readers make longer fixations, shorter saccades and more regressions than skilled adults, patterns that change systematically as reading skill develops. Eye movements are also affected by text difficulty, word frequency, predictability from context and typographical factors such as font and spacing, making eye-tracking a sensitive tool for studying language comprehension in real time. Computational models such as E-Z Reader and SWIFT attempt to capture how linguistic processing and oculomotor control interact to determine when and where the eyes move.
