Later Stone Age

The Later Stone Age (LSA) is a period in African prehistory that follows the Middle Stone Age. It is characterised by smaller, more refined stone tools known as microliths, and by the spread of fully modern human behaviour across Africa. The Later Stone Age began roughly 50,000 to 40,000 years ago, though some of its characteristic technologies appear considerably earlier at certain sites, and it continued in some regions until historical times, ending at different dates with the local adoption of pastoralism, agriculture, and ironworking. The period is broadly analogous to the Upper Paleolithic of Europe and Asia, though African archaeologists prefer the regional terminology because the sequence of change in Africa was different in timing and character.

Microliths — small, standardised stone blades — are a hallmark of the Later Stone Age.

Characteristics

Later Stone Age assemblages are dominated by microlithic industries: tiny blades and geometric pieces (crescents, trapezoids, and triangles) that were hafted in rows into wood or bone handles to make composite tools such as arrows, spears, sickles, and saws. Characteristic industries include the Robberg, Wilton, and Albany industries of southern Africa and the Capsian of North Africa. Other hallmarks of the period include:

  • Widespread use of the bow and arrow
  • Bone tools, including fish hooks, harpoons, and arrow points
  • Ostrich eggshell beads and other personal ornaments
  • Rock art traditions, notably the paintings of the San peoples of southern Africa
  • Formal burial of the dead and intensified exploitation of fish, shellfish, and small game

Regional sequences and lifeways

Later Stone Age peoples were hunter-gatherers, many of them ancestors of historically known groups such as the San (Bushmen) and Khoikhoi of southern Africa and the Pygmy peoples of the central forests. In southern Africa the LSA persisted longest; in the Sahara and the Horn of Africa, some hunter-gatherer groups adopted livestock herding during the African Humid Period, producing "pastoral Neolithic" cultures that still used stone tools. The transition out of the Later Stone Age therefore varied: in some places it ended with the arrival of farming Bantu-speaking peoples and iron technology during the first millennium AD, while isolated foraging communities retained LSA technologies into the colonial era.

Origins

The boundary between the Middle and Later Stone Ages is gradual and debated. Innovations such as microlith production, long-distance exchange of materials, and symbolic artefacts appear sporadically in late Middle Stone Age contexts (for example at Pinnacle Point and Blombos Cave in South Africa) tens of thousands of years before they became widespread, suggesting that the LSA emerged from processes internal to Africa rather than from any outside stimulus.
