A primary flight display or PFD is a modern aircraft instrument dedicated to flight information. It integrates onto a single electronic screen the information traditionally provided by several electromechanical instruments — the attitude indicator, airspeed indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator and heading indicator. PFDs are a core element of the glass cockpit and of the electronic flight instrument system (EFIS), and they greatly reduce pilot scan workload by presenting flight data in a consolidated, consistent format.

Layout

Although exact layouts vary between manufacturers (Boeing, Airbus, Garmin, Honeywell, Collins Aerospace), the standard arrangement places a large attitude indicator in the centre, most often using the conventional blue-over-brown ("sky over ground") colour scheme with a fixed aircraft symbol and flight director command bars. Along the left edge runs a vertical airspeed tape; along the right edge, an altitude tape with a vertical speed indicator beside it; and across the bottom, a horizontal situation display arc showing heading and course. Additional elements typically include:

  • Flight mode annunciations across the top, indicating active and armed autopilot and autothrottle modes
  • Radio altimeter height below 2,500 ft
  • Instrument landing system (ILS) localiser and glideslope deviation scales
  • Selected speed, altitude and heading "bugs"
  • Slip/skid indicator and, in many installations, a flight path vector

Data sources

The PFD is driven by air data computers and inertial reference systems, which supply pressure altitude, airspeed, Mach number, attitude and heading. General aviation systems such as the Garmin G1000 combine the same sensors into integrated avionics suites, bringing PFDs to light aircraft, and synthetic vision systems can overlay a computer-generated terrain view behind the attitude symbology.

History

Electronic attitude and navigation displays entered airline service in the early 1980s with the Boeing 757 and 767 and the Airbus A310, which replaced some electromechanical instruments with cathode-ray tubes. Full PFDs with integrated speed and altitude tapes became standard on late-1980s types such as the Airbus A320 and Boeing 747-400, and LCD units have since displaced CRTs. Regulatory requirements (e.g. for standby instruments) ensure that an independent backup attitude indicator remains available in case of PFD failure.
