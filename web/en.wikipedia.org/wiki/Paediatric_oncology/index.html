Paediatric oncology

Paediatric oncology (American spelling: pediatric oncology) is the branch of medicine concerned with the diagnosis and treatment of cancer in children. Worldwide, it is estimated that childhood cancer incidence is roughly 150–200 cases per million children per year in high-income settings, and survival has improved dramatically since the 1960s: in developed countries, more than 80 per cent of children with cancer now survive five years or more.

Types of childhood cancer

The cancers of childhood differ from adult cancers. The most common are leukaemias (about a quarter to a third of cases), followed by tumours of the brain and central nervous system, lymphomas, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma), rhabdomyosarcoma and bone tumours such as osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma. Carcinomas typical of adults are rare in children.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines imaging, laboratory studies and biopsy, often with molecular and genetic characterisation that increasingly guides therapy. Treatment is usually multimodal — chemotherapy, surgery and radiotherapy, together with newer targeted agents and immunotherapies — delivered according to cooperative clinical-trial protocols in specialised centres. Because late effects of treatment (including cardiac toxicity, infertility and second malignancies) may appear decades later, long-term follow-up of survivors is a core part of the discipline.

History

The modern discipline is often dated to Sidney Farber's demonstration in 1948 that aminopterin could induce remissions in childhood acute leukaemia, work that helped establish chemotherapy. The founding of St. Jude Children's Research Hospital (1962) and cooperative groups such as the Children's Oncology Group and SIOP (founded 1969) drove steady improvements in outcomes through randomised trials.

Global disparities

Survival remains much lower in many low- and middle-income countries, where most of the world's children with cancer live and where diagnosis is often late and treatment abandonment common. International initiatives aim to raise global childhood cancer survival to at least 60 per cent by 2030.
