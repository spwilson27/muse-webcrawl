Precision and recall

In pattern recognition, information retrieval, and classification (machine learning), precision and recall are performance measures used to evaluate how well a system retrieves or classifies relevant items. Precision (also called positive predictive value) is the fraction of retrieved instances that are relevant, while recall (also known as sensitivity) is the fraction of relevant instances that were actually retrieved. Both are defined in terms of the confusion matrix counts of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN).

Definitions

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)   ·   Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

Perfect precision (1.0) means everything the system returned was relevant, though it may have missed much; perfect recall (1.0) means everything relevant was returned, possibly amid much irrelevant material. Typically the two must be traded off: widening a search raises recall at the cost of precision. The F₁ score combines them as their harmonic mean, which penalises imbalance more strongly than the arithmetic mean; the general Fβ score weights recall β times as much as precision.

Precision–recall curves

For classifiers that rank results or output scores, performance is summarised by a precision–recall curve plotting precision against recall as the decision threshold varies; the area under this curve (average precision) is a standard benchmark measure, especially for imbalanced datasets where ROC curves and overall accuracy can be misleading.

History and use

The concepts originated in information retrieval research in the 1950s–60s, notably in Cyril Cleverdon's Cranfield experiments, and were systematised by Gerard Salton. They remain standard in evaluating search engines, recommender systems, information extraction, medical testing (where recall corresponds to sensitivity and precision to positive predictive value), and machine-learning classification.
