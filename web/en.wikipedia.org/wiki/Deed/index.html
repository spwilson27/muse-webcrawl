Deed

A deed is a legal document that is signed and delivered, especially concerning the ownership or legal rights relating to property. In the context of real property, a deed transfers title from a grantor to a grantee. The term contrasts with a simple contract: traditionally a deed is a formal instrument, often requiring sealing or attestation, and in many jurisdictions it must be delivered to take effect.

Requirements

At common law, a deed traditionally had to be written, sealed, and delivered. Modern statutes in most jurisdictions have simplified the requirements: typically the deed must identify the parties, describe the property, express an intent to convey, and be signed by the grantor, with delivery — physical or constructive — completing the transfer. Recording the deed in a public registry protects the grantee against later claimants.

Types of deeds

  • Warranty deed — the grantor guarantees good title and promises to defend it against claims.
  • Special warranty deed — the grantor warrants title only against defects arising during their own ownership.
  • Quitclaim deed — transfers whatever interest the grantor may have, without any warranty of title.
  • Deed of trust — conveys title to a trustee as security for a loan, common in several U.S. states instead of a mortgage.
  • Gift deed — transfers property voluntarily without consideration.

Deeds in other contexts

Historically, deeds were also used for covenants and unilateral undertakings; a "deed poll" is a deed made by one party alone. In English law a promise made by deed can be enforceable even without consideration.
