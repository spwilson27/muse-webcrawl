North Region, Brazil

The North Region of Brazil (Região Norte) is one of the five regions of Brazil defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). Comprising the states of Acre, Amapá, Amazonas, Pará, Rondônia, Roraima, and Tocantins, it is the largest region by area — about 45% of the national territory — but the second least populous. It is dominated by the Amazon rainforest and the Amazon River basin.

The Amazon rainforest covers most of the North Region.

Geography

The region lies almost entirely within the Amazon basin, a low, equatorial plain drained by the Amazon and its tributaries (the Negro, Madeira, Xingu, Tapajós, and Tocantins rivers). Exceptions include the higher Guiana Shield lands in the far north and the cerrado savannas of Tocantins and southern Pará. The climate is hot and humid year-round, and the forest supports the richest biodiversity on Earth, although deforestation for cattle, soy, mining, and logging remains a major pressure.

History and economy

Indigenous peoples have inhabited the region for millennia. Portuguese expansion followed the Amazon from Belém (founded 1616); the late-19th-century rubber boom made Manaus fabulously wealthy, leaving landmarks such as the Amazonas Opera House, before collapsing when Asian plantations undercut wild rubber. Today the economy rests on mining (iron ore at Carajás, bauxite), the Manaus Free Trade Zone's electronics assembly, timber, açaí and Brazil nuts, and hydroelectric dams such as Belo Monte and Tucuruí — development that repeatedly conflicts with Indigenous rights and conservation.

Demographics

The region has Brazil's highest proportion of Indigenous people and of pardo (mixed-ancestry) residents, with riverine caboclo culture shaped by the flood-pulse of the great rivers. Most of the population is concentrated in the metropolitan areas of Manaus and Belém.
