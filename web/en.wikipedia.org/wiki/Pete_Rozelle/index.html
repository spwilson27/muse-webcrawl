Pete Rozelle

Alvin Ray "Pete" Rozelle (March 1, 1926 – December 6, 1996) was an American businessman and sports executive who served as commissioner of the National Football League (NFL) from 1960 to 1989. During his nearly three decades in charge, the NFL grew from 12 teams to 28, and professional football became the most popular spectator sport in the United States.

Career

Rozelle began in sports public relations, working for the University of San Francisco and then the Los Angeles Rams, where he became general manager in 1957. Elected commissioner in January 1960 as a compromise candidate at age 33, he persuaded team owners to accept league-wide television contracts with shared revenue — underpinned by the Sports Broadcasting Act of 1961 — a model that enriched all clubs equally.

Achievements

Rozelle negotiated the AFL–NFL merger of 1966 and the creation of the Super Bowl, oversaw the launch of Monday Night Football in 1970, and founded NFL Films' league partnership and NFL Properties, turning the league into a marketing powerhouse. His decision to play games two days after the assassination of President Kennedy in 1963 remained controversial, one he later called his greatest regret. He was inducted into the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1985, while still commissioner, and the Pete Rozelle Radio-Television Award is named for him. He retired in 1989, succeeded by Paul Tagliabue.
