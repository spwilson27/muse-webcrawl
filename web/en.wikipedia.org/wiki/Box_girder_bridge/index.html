Box girder bridge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prestressed concrete box girders are built segmentally by balanced cantilevering (image placeholder)

A box girder bridge is a bridge in which the main girders have a hollow box cross-section — a top deck slab, inclined or vertical webs, and a bottom flange forming a closed tube. The closed section gives high torsional stiffness, making box girders well suited to curved alignments and long spans. They are built in structural steel, reinforced concrete or prestressed concrete.

History

The principle appeared in wrought-iron tubular bridges such as Robert Stephenson's Britannia Bridge (1850), whose trains ran inside rectangular tubes. Modern box girders proliferated after the Second World War, especially in Germany and France, with advances in prestressing and segmental construction; the Bendorf Bridge (1965) over the Rhine reached a main span of 208 m.

Construction

Concrete box girders are frequently erected as precast or cast-in-place segments joined by prestressing tendons, using the balanced cantilever method from the piers outward, or launched incrementally from one abutment. Single-cell boxes suit moderate widths; multi-cell and twin-box decks serve wide highways. Internal diaphragms and inspection access allow maintenance of tendons and bearings.

Failures

A series of erection collapses of steel box-girder spans around 1970 (including the Milford Haven and West Gate bridges) led to the Merrison rules for box-girder design in the United Kingdom and major revisions of buckling practice for stiffened plate elements.
