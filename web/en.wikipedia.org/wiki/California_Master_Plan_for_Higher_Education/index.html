The California Master Plan for Higher Education of 1960 was a landmark plan for developing a coordinated system of public higher education in the US state of California. Developed under Governor Pat Brown and largely drafted by a team led by University of California president Clark Kerr, it was enacted through the Donahoe Higher Education Act of 1960.

Differentiation of function

The Plan assigned distinct missions to California's three public segments. The University of California (UC) became the primary research institution, drawing freshmen from the top one-eighth (12.5%) of high-school graduates and holding exclusive jurisdiction over doctoral degrees and professional schools of law, medicine, dentistry and veterinary medicine. The state colleges (later California State University, CSU) would draw from the top one-third of graduates and focus on undergraduate and master's-level instruction. The junior (community) colleges would admit any student "capable of profiting from the instruction offered" and provide transfer, vocational and general education.

Access and finance

The Plan promised a tuition-free education to state residents (students paid only modest fees) and guaranteed a place for every eligible Californian somewhere in the public system, with transfer pathways allowing community college students to move to UC or CSU. It has been widely studied and emulated internationally as a model for mass higher education, although rising fees and capacity constraints have strained its guarantees in recent decades.
