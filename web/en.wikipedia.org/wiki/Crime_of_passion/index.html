Crime of passion

A crime of passion (French: crime passionnel) is a violent crime, especially a murder, committed in a state of intense emotion such as jealousy, rage or despair, as opposed to a premeditated offence. The classic case is the killing of a spouse or lover — or a partner's lover — upon discovering infidelity.

Legal treatment

In the legal tradition of France, the crime passionnel was for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries treated with notable leniency: Article 324 of the Napoleonic Penal Code of 1810 excused a spouse who killed the other spouse and their accomplice caught in the act of adultery at home. The provision was repealed in 1975, though "passion" continues to inform sentencing and jury sympathy in practice.

In the common-law world, the related doctrine is the defence of provocation, which can reduce murder to manslaughter where the defendant acted in the "heat of passion" upon adequate provocation. In England and Wales provocation was replaced by the partial defence of "loss of control" under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, which expressly excludes sexual infidelity as a qualifying trigger.

Several Latin American jurisdictions historically had statutes or doctrines — sometimes framed as "violent emotion" or "honour" defences — mitigating killings committed after discovering adultery; most have been repealed or narrowed following campaigns against femicide. In some Middle Eastern penal codes, "honour" remains a mitigating factor, a position widely criticised by human-rights organisations.

Criticism

Feminist legal scholars and campaigners against domestic violence argue that the "crime of passion" framing romanticises or excuses the killing of women by partners, and that leniency historically reflected the treatment of wives as property. Modern reforms in many countries have accordingly abolished adultery-based excuses and reclassified such killings within domestic-homicide frameworks.
