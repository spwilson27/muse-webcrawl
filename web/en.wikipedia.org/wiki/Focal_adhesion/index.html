Focal adhesion
Macromolecular assemblies linking the extracellular matrix to the actin cytoskeleton through integrin receptors.

In cell biology, focal adhesions (also cell–matrix adhesions or adhesion plaques) are macromolecular assemblies through which the extracellular matrix (ECM) is connected, mechanically and chemically, to the cell's cytoskeleton. They form where bundles of actin filaments (stress fibres) are anchored to transmembrane receptors of the integrin family, which in turn bind to ECM proteins such as fibronectin, collagen, and laminin through their extracellular domains.

Focal adhesions are dynamic structures that assemble, mature, and disassemble as cells migrate, allowing them to exert traction forces on their environment, sense its mechanical properties, and convert those cues into biochemical signals.

Structure and composition

A focal adhesion is a flat, elongated structure roughly 2–5 μm long and a few hundred nanometres wide, located at the cell periphery on the ventral surface. Proteomic studies have identified more than 150 component proteins, collectively termed the "adhesome." Layers of the assembly span from the ECM to the actin network:

  • Integrins — heterodimeric α/β transmembrane receptors whose occupancy clusters them at adhesion sites
  • Talin and kindlin — link integrin cytoplasmic tails to actin and activate integrins ("inside-out" signalling)
  • Vinculin — reinforces the talin–actin linkage under mechanical tension, a key force sensor
  • Paxillin — a scaffold protein and target of adhesion signalling
  • Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) — a tyrosine kinase that autophosphorylates (at Tyr397) on integrin clustering and recruits Src-family kinases, initiating downstream signalling
  • Zyxin, VASP, α-actinin and other actin-binding proteins that connect to and organize stress fibres

Assembly and turnover

New adhesions begin as small, dot-like focal complexes at the edge of the lamellipodium. Under tension generated by the actomyosin cytoskeleton and resistance from the substrate, some mature into focal adhesions and, with further tension, into larger fibrillar adhesions. Adhesions at the rear of a migrating cell disassemble through mechanisms involving FAK, dynamin, calpain-mediated cleavage of talin and FAK, and microtubule targeting. The cycle of assembly at the front and disassembly at the back is essential for cell migration.

Mechanosensing and signalling

Focal adhesions are central to mechanotransduction: cells on stiffer substrates form larger, more stable adhesions and generate greater contractile force. Stretch of talin exposes cryptic vinculin-binding sites, so adhesion strengthening is directly force-sensitive. Signals from focal adhesions — through FAK/Src, Rho-family GTPases (RhoA, Rac, Cdc42), and the YAP/TAZ pathway — regulate cell survival (anoikis resistance), proliferation, differentiation, and gene expression. Dysregulated adhesion signalling contributes to cancer invasion and metastasis, fibrosis, and cardiovascular disease, and FAK inhibitors have been investigated as anticancer drugs.
