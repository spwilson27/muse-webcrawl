Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) is an aviation surveillance technology and form of electronic conspicuity by which an aircraft determines its position via satellite navigation (GNSS) or other sensors and periodically broadcasts it, enabling it to be tracked by air traffic control ground stations and by other aircraft. The information can also be received by the general public via receiver networks and flight-tracking websites.

How it works

ADS-B is "automatic" in that no pilot or controller input is required, "dependent" because it relies on the aircraft's navigation systems for position, and "broadcast" because data are transmitted openly rather than in reply to interrogation like a traditional transponder. ADS-B Out transmits identity, position, altitude and velocity about once per second, typically on 1090 MHz using Mode S Extended Squitter, or on 978 MHz UAT in the United States for lower-altitude general aviation. ADS-B In allows suitably equipped aircraft to receive traffic (TIS-B) and weather (FIS-B) information.

Adoption

The FAA required ADS-B Out in most US controlled airspace from 1 January 2020, and the European Union and many other states have similar equipage mandates. Space-based ADS-B, hosted on the Iridium NEXT satellite constellation by Aireon, provides global oceanic and remote coverage beyond the reach of ground radar. Because ADS-B signals are unencrypted and unauthenticated, security researchers have noted vulnerabilities to spoofing; nonetheless it is the foundation of next-generation air traffic management programmes such as NextGen and SESAR.
