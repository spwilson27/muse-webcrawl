Load factor (structural design)

In structural engineering, a load factor is a multiplier applied to the expected (nominal or service) loads on a structure to account for uncertainties in the magnitude, distribution, and combination of those loads. Designing against factored loads ensures that the structure has an adequate margin of safety against overload and failure.

Load and resistance factor design

Load factors are a central feature of load and resistance factor design (LRFD), also called limit state design. In this method, different factors are applied to different kinds of load — dead load (the structure's own weight, which is comparatively predictable) typically receives a smaller factor, while live load (occupancy, vehicles, wind, snow) receives a larger one because it is more variable. Typical LRFD combinations in North American practice use factors such as 1.2 on dead load and 1.6 on live load, alongside a resistance (strength reduction) factor applied to the member capacity.

Partial safety factors

In Eurocode-based practice, the equivalent quantities are called partial safety factors (denoted γ), applied to actions and to material strengths. The philosophy is the same: separate uncertainties are handled separately rather than by a single global factor of safety as in older allowable-stress design.

Purpose

Load factors account for the possibility that actual loads may exceed the design values, for inaccuracies in load models, and for the statistical variability of loads over the life of the structure. Combined with resistance factors, they allow engineers to calibrate reliability consistently across different materials and structural types.
