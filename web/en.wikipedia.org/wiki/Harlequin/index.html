Harlequin (Italian: Arlecchino) is the best-known of the zanni or comic servant characters from the Italian commedia dell'arte, a form of theatre that arose in Italy in the sixteenth century and spread throughout Europe. The Harlequin character came to England early in the seventeenth century and took centre stage in the derived genre of the harlequinade, developed by John Rich.

Harlequin is identified by his chequered costume. His role is that of a light-hearted, nimble and astute servant, often acting to thwart the plans of his master and pursuing his own love interest, Columbina, with wit and resourcefulness, often competing with the sterner Pierrot. His frequent physical antics, acrobatics, and his wooden sword or slapstick — from which the term "slapstick comedy" derives — define the character.

History

The character's name may derive from a black-faced devil figure of medieval French passion plays, or from the Old French hellequin, a leader of a troop of demon horsemen. Tristano Martinelli is traditionally credited with introducing the role in France in the 1580s and giving it the name Arlecchino. Notable later interpreters included the Italian actor-manager Domenico Biancolelli and, in England, John Rich, whose silent mime harlequinades made the character a fixture of English pantomime.

Costume and characteristics

Originally a patched costume of a poor peasant, Harlequin's rags evolved into a tight-fitting suit covered in regular diamond-shaped patches (lozenges), usually red, green, and blue. He wears a black half-mask, carries a batte (slapstick), and in the English tradition acquired magical powers of transformation, using his bat to change scenes and objects in pantomime.

Legacy

Harlequin influenced the clown traditions of European theatre, the English Punch and pantomime clown, and later figures in ballet, opera, and modernist art — notably Picasso's Harlequin paintings and the commedia-inspired works of the Ballets Russes.
