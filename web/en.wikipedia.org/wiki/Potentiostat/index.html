Potentiostat

A potentiostat is the electronic hardware required to control a three-electrode cell and run most electroanalytical experiments. It maintains the potential of the working electrode at a constant level with respect to the reference electrode by adjusting the current at an auxiliary (counter) electrode.

Operation

The instrument uses a feedback amplifier circuit: it measures the potential between the reference electrode and the working electrode and drives current through the counter electrode so that this potential matches a set value, while recording the resulting current. This permits techniques such as cyclic voltammetry, chronoamperometry, and linear sweep voltammetry. A galvanostat performs the complementary function, controlling current rather than potential; many modern instruments combine both modes, along with impedance spectroscopy modules.

History and uses

The first potentiostats were built in the 1940s (notably by Archie Hickling in 1942) as polarographs and corrosion-testing instruments replaced manual control. Today they are standard tools in corrosion science, battery and fuel-cell research, sensor development, electroplating, and analytical chemistry. Bench-top research instruments offer potential ranges of ±10 V and currents from picoamperes to amperes; low-cost open-source designs have also been developed for teaching and field work.
