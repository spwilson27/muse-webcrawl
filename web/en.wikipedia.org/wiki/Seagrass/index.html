Seagrass

GroupMarine flowering plants
Speciesc. 60
HabitatShallow coastal waters worldwide

Seagrasses are the only flowering plants that grow fully submerged in marine environments. About 60 species, in four families (Posidoniaceae, Zosteraceae, Hydrocharitaceae and Cymodoceaceae), form dense underwater meadows in shallow coastal waters on all continents except Antarctica. Familiar genera include Zostera (eelgrass), Posidonia and Thalassia (turtle grass).

Ecology

Seagrass meadows are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. They stabilise sediments, improve water clarity, store large amounts of "blue carbon" in their roots and soils, and provide food and nursery habitat for fish, shellfish, dugongs, manatees and sea turtles. A single clone of Posidonia australis in Shark Bay, Australia, stretches some 180 km and is considered the largest known plant on Earth.

Threats and conservation

Seagrasses are in global decline from nutrient pollution and eutrophication, coastal development, dredging, trawling and warming seas; large die-offs followed a wasting disease epidemic in North Atlantic eelgrass in the 1930s. Restoration by transplanting and seeding has become an active conservation field, with notable recoveries in places such as Virginia's coastal bays.
