Outburst flood

An outburst flood is a sudden, often catastrophic release of water impounded behind a barrier — an ice dam, a moraine, a landslide deposit or a volcanic crater rim. Because the stored volume can be enormous and the release abrupt, outburst floods include the largest known floods on Earth, and outflow channels attributed to them have also been identified on Mars.

Glacial lake outburst floods

A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) occurs when a lake dammed by a glacier or its moraine drains suddenly, through overtopping, internal erosion ("piping"), ice-dam flotation or the collapse of the dam. In Iceland, subglacial lakes over volcanic systems produce the jökulhlaup: geothermal heat or an eruption melts ice, water accumulates under the ice cap, and the resulting flood crosses the coastal sandur plains — as in the great 1996 Gjálp eruption flood beneath Vatnajökull, whose peak discharge briefly rivalled that of the Amazon. In the Himalayas and Andes, growing moraine-dammed lakes above populated valleys are a major natural hazard, monitored with satellite imagery and sometimes lowered artificially by siphons or outlet cuts.

Megafloods of the Ice Age

During the last ice age, outburst floods from ice-dammed lakes reshaped whole landscapes. The Missoula Floods repeatedly drained glacial Lake Missoula through the Columbia River Gorge, carving the Channeled Scablands of Washington State — the evidence J Harlen Bretz used, against furious opposition, to establish catastrophic flooding as a geological process. Comparable floods drained Lake Bonneville into the Snake River plain and spilled from ice-dammed lakes in the Altai Mountains of Siberia.

Mechanisms

  • Ice-dam failure by flotation, cracking or subglacial tunnel enlargement
  • Moraine-dam failure by overtopping (often triggered by rock or ice avalanches into the lake), piping, or earthquake shaking
  • Volcanic melting of ice and snow, including crater-lake breakout (lahar generation)
  • Landslide-dam failure after river blockage
  • Failure of artificial dams — distinguished in usage, though physically similar
