The 8.8 cm Flak 18/36/37/41 family was a series of German 88 mm anti-aircraft and anti-tank guns of the Second World War, widely known simply as the "eighty-eight". Developed by Krupp, the Flak 18 entered service in the 1930s, followed by the improved Flak 36 and Flak 37, and the longer-barrelled Flak 41.

Design

The guns were mounted on a cruciform platform allowing 360-degree traverse and high elevation, and were transported on a two-axle trailer. The Flak 36 introduced a two-piece barrel for easier replacement of the worn section; the Flak 37 featured improved fire-control data transmission; the Flak 41, with a longer barrel and higher muzzle velocity, arrived in smaller numbers from 1943.

Anti-tank role

First used by the Condor Legion in the Spanish Civil War against ground targets, the "eighty-eight" gained fame in the Second World War as a tank killer, notably in the North African campaign, where its armour-piercing shells could defeat almost any Allied tank at long range. Its success led directly to tank guns of the same calibre, such as the KwK 36 of the Tiger I.

Legacy

More than 21,000 examples of the Flak 18/36/37 series were built. The 88 became one of the most recognisable German weapons of the war, and captured examples served with several armies for decades afterward.
