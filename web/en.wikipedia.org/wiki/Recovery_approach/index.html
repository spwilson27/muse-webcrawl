Recovery approach

The recovery approach to mental disorder or substance dependence emphasises and supports a person's potential for recovery rather than only the reduction of symptoms. Recovery is generally considered a personal journey rather than a set outcome, and may involve developing hope, a secure base and sense of self, supportive relationships, empowerment, social inclusion, coping skills and meaning. In the recovery approach, "recovery" does not necessarily mean complete clinical remission: a person can live a meaningful and satisfying life while still experiencing symptoms.

The concept grew out of the civil-rights-era consumer/survivor/ex-patient movement, the experience of people recovering from alcohol and drug dependence (including Alcoholics Anonymous, founded 1935), and longitudinal research — notably Courtenay Harding's Vermont study — showing that many people diagnosed with schizophrenia improved substantially over decades. William Anthony's 1993 definition of recovery as "a deeply personal, unique process of changing one's attitudes, values, feelings, goals, skills and/or roles" became canonical for services.

Policy adoption

Recovery became official policy in several countries: New Zealand's Mental Health Commission adopted a recovery blueprint in 1998; in the United States the 2003 President's New Freedom Commission report declared recovery the goal of the mental-health system, and SAMHSA published a working definition in 2011. In England, the 2011 strategy No Health Without Mental Health endorsed recovery, and "recovery colleges" — educational services co-produced and co-delivered by people with lived experience — spread from 2009 onward. Peer support workers became an increasingly common, formally employed role.

Frameworks and measures

A widely used synthesis (Leamy et al., 2011) summarised personal recovery as CHIME: Connectedness, Hope and optimism, Identity, Meaning in life, and Empowerment. Instruments such as the Recovery Assessment Scale and the Questionnaire about the Process of Recovery measure self-rated recovery, while service-level tools (e.g. the Recovery Self Assessment) audit how far organisations embody recovery values. Wellness Recovery Action Planning (WRAP), developed by Mary Ellen Copeland, is a structured self-management method within the tradition.

Criticism

Critics argue that "recovery" has sometimes been co-opted by services to justify cuts, to shift responsibility onto individuals, or reduced to a managerial slogan; others note tensions between personal recovery and clinical models centred on symptom remission, and that some people find the language alienating or feel written off by services in its name. Defenders respond that authentic implementation requires co-production and a shift of power toward service users.
