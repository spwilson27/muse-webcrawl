Lambeth degree

A Lambeth degree is an academic degree conferred by the Archbishop of Canterbury under authority granted by the Ecclesiastical Licences Act 1533, which transferred the papal legate's power to grant dispensations and degrees to the Archbishop following the English Reformation. The degrees take their name from Lambeth Palace, the Archbishop's London residence, and are full academic degrees in law, not honorary titles.

History

Before the Reformation, papal legates in England could confer degrees; the 1533 Act preserved the power in the Archbishop of Canterbury so that the practice could continue without reference to Rome. Holders of Lambeth degrees historically enjoyed privileges such as eligibility for certain offices and, in the case of medical degrees, the right to practise medicine. The most frequently conferred degrees are in divinity (DD, BD), law (LLD, LLB), arts (MA) and music (DMus), and the MA by special conferment became particularly well known in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Modern practice

Today Lambeth degrees are awarded sparingly, usually to clergy and others who have made distinguished contributions to theology, the Church or public life, and who have undertaken study or work of degree standard without following a university course. Candidates for examined awards follow the Archbishop's Examination in Theology, established in the 1990s, which leads to the MPhil and other qualifications administered from Lambeth Palace. Recipients are entitled to use the post-nominal letters of the degree, with the name of Canterbury (Cantuar) sometimes used to identify the awarding authority.
