Hubbert's bell-shaped curve models the rise, peak and decline of oil production in a region.

Peak oil is the point in time when the maximum rate of global petroleum extraction is reached, after which production enters a decline. The concept derives from geophysicist M. King Hubbert's 1956 prediction that United States oil production would peak between 1965 and 1971; US conventional production did peak in 1970, lending the theory lasting influence.

Hubbert's theory

Hubbert modelled production from a finite resource as a bell-shaped curve: output rises as discoveries are developed, peaks when roughly half the recoverable resource has been extracted, then falls as the remaining oil becomes harder to produce. He correctly forecast the US lower-48 peak but global predictions by later analysts repeatedly proved premature.

Unconventional oil and demand peak

From the late 2000s, hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling unlocked vast shale (tight oil) resources, making the United States the world's largest producer by 2018 and pushing global output to about 100 million barrels per day. Attention consequently shifted from a supply-driven peak to a possible demand peak, as electric vehicles, efficiency gains and climate policy threaten to cap consumption; the International Energy Agency has projected global oil demand plateauing before 2030 under stated policies.

Implications

Debates over peak oil involve energy security, price volatility, the economics of oil-exporting states, and the pace of the transition to renewable energy.
