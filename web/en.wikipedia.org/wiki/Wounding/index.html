Wounding

Legal contextCriminal law (England and Wales and other common law jurisdictions)
Related offencesGrievous bodily harm, assault occasioning actual bodily harm
Key statute (UK)Offences against the Person Act 1861, ss. 18 and 20

Wounding is a term used in criminal law, particularly in England and Wales and other common law jurisdictions, for the unlawful infliction of a wound — that is, an injury that breaks the continuity of the skin — upon another person. It forms the basis of serious offences against the person.

Definition

In law, a wound requires a break in the whole skin, both dermis and epidermis; internal bleeding or a fracture alone, without broken skin, is not a wound, though it may constitute grievous bodily harm. Scratches that break only the surface and bruises do not qualify.

Offences in England and Wales

The Offences against the Person Act 1861 contains the two principal wounding offences. Section 18 covers wounding (or causing grievous bodily harm) with intent — one of the most serious non-fatal offences, carrying a maximum sentence of life imprisonment. Section 20 covers unlawful and malicious wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm without the specific intent, carrying a maximum of five years' imprisonment. The mens rea for section 20 requires foresight of some physical harm, though not necessarily of a wound itself.

Distinction from related offences

Wounding overlaps with, but is distinct from, causing grievous bodily harm (GBH) — really serious harm, which need not involve a break in the skin — and from assault occasioning actual bodily harm (ABH) under section 47, which covers lesser injuries such as bruising and minor cuts. A wound stripe, by contrast, is an unrelated military distinction for soldiers wounded in action.
