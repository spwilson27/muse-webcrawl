Coin silver

Coin silver is an alloy of silver containing 90% silver and 10% copper by weight, corresponding to the standard used for United States silver coinage for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The term is also used for objects — especially American silverware of the early nineteenth century — made by melting down silver coins, a common practice when bullion was scarce.

Composition and standards

The Coinage Act of 1792 set United States silver coins at a fineness of 892.4 parts per thousand; the Coinage Act of 1837 standardised the composition to 900 fine (90% silver, 10% copper), the proportion thereafter called "coin silver". It is slightly less pure than the British sterling silver standard of 92.5% and less pure than Britannia silver (95.8%), but purer than the 800 and 830 grades common in continental Europe.

Use in silverware

Before industrial silver production expanded after the Civil War, American silversmiths frequently melted foreign and domestic coins as their raw material, and pieces so made are described and collected as "coin silver". Flatware and hollowware of the period is often marked simply "COIN" or with the maker's name and city. After about 1870, sterling became the standard for American silverware and coin silver production declined.
