Korteweg–De Vries equation

A soliton solution of the KdV equation

In mathematics and fluid dynamics, the Korteweg–De Vries (KdV) equation is a nonlinear partial differential equation that models waves on shallow water surfaces. It is notable as one of the earliest equations found to possess soliton solutions—localised waves that maintain their shape through collisions—and it launched the modern theory of integrable systems.

∂t u + 6u ∂x u + ∂x3 u = 0

History

The equation derives from work by Joseph Boussinesq in the 1870s and was given its standard form by Diederik Korteweg and Gustav de Vries in 1895, who sought to explain the solitary wave observed by John Scott Russell in 1834. Interest revived in 1965 when Norman Zabusky and Martin Kruskal showed numerically that its wave solutions interact elastically, coining the term "soliton." The inverse scattering transform, developed shortly after by Gardner, Greene, Kruskal and Miura, provided a method for solving the equation exactly.

Solutions

The KdV equation admits single-soliton solutions of sech² form, multi-soliton solutions, and periodic cnoidal wave solutions expressible in terms of elliptic functions. It possesses infinitely many conserved quantities and can be written as an infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian system.

Applications

Beyond shallow water waves, the equation and its generalisations appear in plasma physics (ion acoustic waves), internal ocean waves, anharmonic lattices and optical fibres.
