Spy film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The spy film is a genre of film dealing with the subject of fictional espionage, either in a realistic way (such as the adaptations of John le Carré) or as a basis for fantasy (such as the James Bond series). Many novels in the spy fiction genre have been adapted as films, including the works of John Buchan, le Carré, Ian Fleming, and Len Deighton. The genre is a significant aspect of British cinema and has produced some of the medium's most enduring franchises.

History

Silent-era films such as Fritz Lang's Spione (1928) and Alfred Hitchcock's British thrillers of the 1930s — The 39 Steps (1935), Secret Agent (1936), and The Lady Vanishes (1938) — established many conventions of the genre. The popularity of the spy film peaked during the Cold War. The debut of James Bond in Dr. No (1962) launched a wave of glamorous, gadget-filled imitations across Europe and Hollywood, including the Eurospy cycle and television series such as The Man from U.N.C.L.E. and Mission: Impossible. Against the Bond model, a realist counter-tradition produced The Spy Who Came In from the Cold (1965), The Ipcress File (1965), and Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (2011), emphasising moral ambiguity and bureaucratic tradecraft.

Themes and conventions

Common elements include a secret agent protagonist; an intelligence agency issuing assignments; exotic locations; ingenious gadgets; double agents and betrayals; and an antagonist pursuing world domination, stolen secrets, or nuclear blackmail. Post–Cold War spy films have turned to terrorism, surveillance, and corporate espionage, and franchises such as Mission: Impossible and the Jason Bourne films have emphasised physical action and realistic stunt work.

Notable franchises

  • James Bond (1962–present)
  • Mission: Impossible (1996–present)
  • Jason Bourne (2002–2016)
  • Harry Palmer films (1965–1967)
  • Jack Ryan films
  • Kingsman (2014–present)
