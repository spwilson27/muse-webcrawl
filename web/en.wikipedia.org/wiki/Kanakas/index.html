Kanakas

Kanaka (plural Kanakas) was a term used in the 19th and early 20th centuries for Pacific Islander workers employed in British and French colonies, especially the South Sea Islanders recruited — often coercively, in the practice known as "blackbirding" — to work on sugar plantations in Queensland, Australia, and in Fiji, Samoa, New Caledonia and elsewhere. The word comes from Hawaiian kanaka, simply meaning "person, human being".

Queensland trade

Between 1863 and 1904, over 62,000 Islanders, mainly from Vanuatu, the Solomon Islands and New Caledonia, were brought to Queensland. Recruitment ranged from genuine indentured labour agreements to kidnapping and deception. Working conditions on the plantations were harsh and mortality was high. After Federation, the Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901 ordered the deportation of most Islanders, though some remained and formed the basis of today's Australian South Sea Islander community, formally recognised by the Queensland and Commonwealth governments in the 1990s–2000s.

Other uses

The term was also applied to Native Hawaiian sailors and fur-trade labourers in the Pacific Northwest (for example at Fort Vancouver), and "Kanake" survives in German as a slur derived from colonial-era usage. In New Caledonia, the Indigenous Melanesian people reclaimed the related word as Kanak, now a proud self-designation.
