The Yugoslav Partisans, officially the National Liberation Army and Partisan Detachments of Yugoslavia, were the communist-led resistance movement against the Axis occupation of Yugoslavia during the Second World War. Led by Josip Broz Tito, the Partisans grew from small guerrilla detachments in 1941 into a conventional army of several hundred thousand that liberated most of Yugoslavia with limited outside help. They are widely considered the most effective resistance movement in occupied Europe, and their victory laid the foundation of the postwar Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia.

Formation and early uprising

After the Axis invasion and partition of Yugoslavia in April 1941, the Communist Party of Yugoslavia — illegal before the war — prepared armed resistance, launching a general uprising following Germany's invasion of the USSR in June 1941. In Serbia the Partisans briefly held a liberated territory around Užice (the "Republic of Užice") before German offensives drove them into Bosnia. They fought simultaneously against German and Italian occupiers, the fascist Ustaše regime of the Independent State of Croatia (whose racial persecution of Serbs, Jews, and Roma drove recruits to the Partisans), and, increasingly, their royalist rivals the Chetniks under Draža Mihailović, who prioritised fighting the communists and collaborated tactically with the occupiers.

The People's Liberation War

In November 1942 the Partisans convened the Anti-Fascist Council for the National Liberation of Yugoslavia (AVNOJ) at Bihać, and at its second session at Jajce in November 1943 proclaimed a provisional government, forbade the king's return, and laid the basis for a federal Yugoslavia of equal nations. German offensives repeatedly came close to destroying the movement: at the Neretva (Case White, early 1943) Tito's forces escaped across a demolished bridge with their wounded, and at the Sutjeska (Case Black, May–June 1943) they broke out of encirclement at heavy cost; Tito himself was wounded. A German airborne raid on his headquarters at Drvar in May 1944 narrowly failed to capture him. Uniquely among major resistance movements, the Partisans organised across ethnic lines under the slogan "Brotherhood and Unity", and enrolled large numbers of women as fighters, nurses, and organisers.

Allied support and liberation

Frustrated by Chetnik inaction and collaboration, the Western Allies shifted support to the Partisans after missions by Fitzroy Maclean and others confirmed their effectiveness; at the Tehran Conference in late 1943 the Allies recognised them as the principal Yugoslav resistance. British and American air power and supplies flowed via the island of Vis. In autumn 1944 the Partisans, advancing alongside the Red Army, liberated Belgrade on 20 October 1944; by May 1945 they controlled the whole country and had advanced into Trieste and Carinthia, precipitating a crisis with the Western Allies. The Partisans' victory enabled Tito to establish a communist federation largely independent of Stalin — a self-reliance that underpinned Yugoslavia's defiance in the Tito–Stalin split of 1948. The war's aftermath included Partisan reprisals against captured collaborators and Axis troops, notably the Bleiburg repatriations and subsequent killings, which remain politically contested in the successor states.
