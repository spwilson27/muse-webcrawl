Saddle point

In mathematics, a saddle point or minimax point is a point on the surface of the graph of a function where the slopes (derivatives) in orthogonal directions are all zero (a critical point), but which is not a local extremum of the function. An example is a point where there is a stationary point in one direction (which may be a local extremum) and an inflection point in another direction.

The hyperbolic paraboloid z = x² − y² has a saddle point at the origin.

Mathematical discussion

A simple criterion for checking whether a given stationary point of a twice-differentiable real-valued function F(x, y) is a saddle point is to compute the function's Hessian matrix at that point: the point is a saddle if the Hessian is indefinite, that is, if it has both positive and negative eigenvalues. Equivalently, the determinant of the Hessian — the product of its eigenvalues — is negative.

The prototype example is the function z = x² − y², whose only critical point is at the origin. Along the x-axis the function curves upward (a minimum in that direction), while along the y-axis it curves downward (a maximum in that direction). The surface resembles a horse's saddle, or a mountain pass, which gives the point its name.

Other uses

In dynamical systems, a saddle point is a fixed point of a system that attracts trajectories in some directions and repels them in others. In game theory, a saddle point of a payoff matrix corresponds to a pair of optimal strategies in a two-player zero-sum game, formalised in von Neumann's minimax theorem. In optimization, saddle points pose a challenge for gradient-based methods, which may slow near them even though they are neither minima nor maxima.
