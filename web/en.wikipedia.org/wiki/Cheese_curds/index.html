Cheese curd

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of fresh cheese curds, with a plate of poutine in the background]

Cheese curds are the moist pieces of curdled milk that separate from the whey early in cheesemaking, before being pressed into blocks and aged. Eaten fresh, they have a mild, slightly salty flavour and a distinctive springy, rubbery texture that produces a "squeak" against the teeth — a quality prized by connoisseurs and lost within days as the curds dry out.

Production

Curds form when milk is acidified and treated with rennet, causing the casein proteins to coagulate. In Cheddar production, the curds are cut, cooked, drained, and stacked in the "cheddaring" process; some of the fresh curds are diverted and sold before pressing and ageing.

Culinary use

Cheese curds are a signature food of the American Midwest, particularly Wisconsin, where they are sold at farmers' markets and county fairs, often battered and deep-fried. In Quebec they are an essential component of poutine, in which fresh curds are layered over fries and covered with hot gravy, which softens them without fully melting them. Their use is also traditional in parts of Canada and the upper Midwest of the United States where dairy farming is prominent.
