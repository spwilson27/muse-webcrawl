Sheep dog trials

A sheep dog trial — also called a herding trial or dog trial — is a competitive sport in which herding dogs, usually border collies, move sheep around a field and through obstacles under the direction of their handler. The handler gives commands by whistle, voice and gesture while remaining at a fixed post for much of the run.

The course

A standard trial run comprises several judged elements. In the outrun the dog runs wide around the sheep so as not to startle them; in the lift and fetch it brings the flock straight to the handler through a set of gates. The drive moves the sheep away from the handler around a triangular course; the shed separates specified sheep from the group; and the run ends with penning, in which the dog holds the sheep while the handler opens a pen gate and the flock is guided inside. Points are deducted for deviations from straight lines, missed obstacles and rough handling, and each element must be completed within a time limit.

History and organisation

The first recorded trials were held at Bala in Wales in 1873. The International Sheep Dog Society, founded in 1906, organises national and international championships in the British Isles; the supreme championship tests dogs over a larger course with a double lift. Trials are also a major tradition in New Zealand, Australia, North America and South Africa, where local rules — such as New Zealand's huntaway events — reflect regional working styles.
