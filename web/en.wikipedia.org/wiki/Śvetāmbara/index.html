Śvetāmbara

Śvetāmbara (Sanskrit: श्वेताम्बर, "white-clad") is one of the two main traditions of Jainism, the other being Digambara ("sky-clad"). The names refer to a central point of difference: Śvetāmbara monks and nuns wear simple white garments, whereas Digambara monks renounce clothing entirely.

Origins

Jain tradition dates the schism to the centuries after Mahavira (6th–5th century BCE). According to Śvetāmbara accounts, a famine in Magadha led one group of monks, under Bhadrabahu, to migrate south, while those who remained under Sthulabhadra adopted white clothing during the famine; when the migrants returned, the differences had hardened into separate sects. The Śvetāmbara canon (Agamas) was written down at a council at Valabhi in Gujarat in the 5th century CE, traditionally dated to 453 or 466 CE.

Beliefs and differences

Śvetāmbaras differ from Digambaras on several doctrinal points: they hold that the canonical scriptures survive in the Agamas; that women can attain liberation (and that the 19th Tirthankara, Mallinatha, was female); that Mahavira married and had a daughter; and that an omniscient being (kevalin) still takes food. Śvetāmbara monks wear white robes, carry an alms bowl and a whisk broom (rajoharana), and may wear a mouth-cloth (muhapatti) to avoid harming airborne life.

Sub-traditions

The tradition divides into image-worshipping Murtipujaka and the later reformist, non-image-worshipping Sthanakavasi and Terapanth orders, the latter founded by Acharya Bhikshu in the 18th century. Śvetāmbara communities are concentrated in western India and in the Jain diaspora worldwide.
