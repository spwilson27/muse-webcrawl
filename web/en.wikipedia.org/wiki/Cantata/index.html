Cantata

A cantata (from Italian cantare, "to sing") is a vocal composition with an instrumental accompaniment, typically in several movements, often involving a choir. The term originated in the early 17th century, used in contrast to a piece written purely for instruments (sonata) and to distinguish it from the solo song with continuo. Cantatas have been written for sacred (church) and secular purposes.

History

Baroque secular cantata

The early cantata was a short piece for one or two solo voices with basso continuo, developed in Italy by composers such as Alessandro Grandi and Barbara Strozzi. Alessandro Scarlatti composed over 600 secular cantatas, usually consisting of recitative and da capo aria pairs. The form spread to France, where composers including Jean-Baptiste Morin and Louis-Nicolas Clérambault developed the cantate françoise.

Sacred cantata

In Lutheran Germany the church cantata became a central liturgical genre, combining biblical text, chorales and free poetry in movements for chorus, soloists and orchestra. Dieterich Buxtehude wrote notable examples, but the form reached its height with Johann Sebastian Bach, who composed several annual cycles — more than 200 of his sacred cantatas survive, including works such as Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme and the Christmas Oratorio (a set of six cantatas). Bach also wrote secular cantatas, among them the Coffee Cantata.

Later cantatas

After the Baroque era the term was applied more loosely to large-scale dramatic vocal works. Examples include Beethoven's Cantata on the Death of Emperor Joseph II, works by Mendelssohn and Brahms, and in the 20th century such pieces as Prokofiev's Alexander Nevsky, Britten's Cantata academica and Carl Orff's Carmina Burana (described as a scenic cantata).
