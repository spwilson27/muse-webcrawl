Chartjunk
Coined byEdward Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983)
DefinitionNon-informative or redundant visual elements in charts
Related ideasData-ink ratio; data visualization; information design

Chartjunk refers to visual elements in charts and graphs that are not necessary for understanding the data, or that distract the reader from the information presented. The term was coined by the information design theorist Edward Tufte in his 1983 book The Visual Display of Quantitative Information. Chartjunk includes heavy gridlines, decorative illustrations, three-dimensional effects, cross-hatching ("vibrating" moiré patterns), and redundant labels.

Excess decoration can obscure the data

Tufte's argument

Tufte argued that graphics should maximize the "data-ink ratio" — the share of ink devoted to representing data — and that decoration serves the designer's vanity rather than the reader. He criticized the charts in news magazines for low data density and gimmickry, coining the deliberately dismissive word "chartjunk" for effects such as pseudo-3D bars and pictorial backgrounds. The principle "above all, show the data" and advice to "erase non-data-ink" became foundational in data visualization teaching.

Critiques and debate

Later researchers complicated the picture. Studies by Bateman and colleagues (2010) found that charts embellished with images were no less accurate to read and were more memorable than minimalist equivalents. Nigel Holmes, whose magazine graphics Tufte had criticized, defended illustration as engaging readers. Visualization researchers now often distinguish useless decoration from "useful chartjunk" — annotations, icons, and styling that aid comprehension, memory, or audience engagement — while still warning against distortions such as 3D perspective that misrepresent values.
