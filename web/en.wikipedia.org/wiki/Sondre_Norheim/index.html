Sondre Norheim

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sondre Auverson Norheim (10 June 1825 – 9 March 1897), born Sondre Auverson Øverbø, was a Norwegian skier and pioneer of modern skiing. Regarded as the father of Telemark skiing, Norheim revolutionised ski technique and equipment in the mid-19th century and demonstrated the new methods to astonished audiences in Christiania (Oslo).

Life and innovations

Norheim grew up at Øverbø farm in Morgedal, Telemark, a valley where skis were everyday winter transport. He developed the Telemark turn, in which the skier advances one ski and bends both knees to carve through a descent, and the Christiania turn for stopping. Equally important was his equipment: he introduced bindings with stiff heel straps of twisted willow that held the boot firmly enough to steer, sidecut (waisted) ski profiles that made turning easier, and shorter skis suited to hilly terrain. In 1868 the 42-year-old Norheim travelled to Christiania and decisively won the capital's ski competition, combining cross-country speed, jumping, and controlled turns on steep slopes; the event is often treated as the birth of competitive skiing as a sport.

In 1884, seeking a better life, Norheim emigrated to the United States, first to Minnesota and later to North Dakota, where he farmed and helped build a Lutheran church. He died in 1897 and was buried in an unmarked grave near Minot; his remains were later honoured with a memorial, and in 1984 his grave marker was dedicated in the presence of King Olav V of Norway. A statue of Norheim stands in Morgedal, and the Olympic flame for the 1952, 1960, and 1994 Winter Games was lit in the fireplace of his birthplace.
