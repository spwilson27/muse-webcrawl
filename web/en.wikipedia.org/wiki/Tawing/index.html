Tawing

Tawing is a method of preserving animal hides using aluminium salts — traditionally alum (potassium aluminium sulfate), often with salt, flour and egg yolk — rather than the vegetable or chromium compounds used in tanning. Tawing produces a soft, white, supple leather especially prized for gloves, bookbinding and garments, but unlike true tanning the process is partly reversible: prolonged washing can remove the alum and allow the skin to revert toward rawhide.

Process

After liming, fleshing and drenching, the prepared skins are worked in a paste or liquor of alum and salt (the flour and egg-yolk mixture, when used, also nourishes and softens the pelt). The aluminium ions bind loosely to the collagen fibres; the skins are then dried and staked — pulled and worked over a blunt blade to soften them — and may be dyed. Tawed leathers, such as traditional white kid for gloves, are characteristically pale, soft and stretchy, and tolerate dry conditions well but stiffen and degrade if repeatedly wetted.

History

Alum tawing is ancient — alum was traded for the purpose in the ancient Mediterranean — and in medieval England the tawers formed separate guilds from the tanners, handling the lighter skins for gloves, purses and clothing while tanners made heavy sole and harness leather from oak-bark liquors. In modern usage, mineral "tannages" with aluminium, zirconium and chromium salts blur the distinction, and some authorities restrict the term tawing to the alum process and its white-leather products.
