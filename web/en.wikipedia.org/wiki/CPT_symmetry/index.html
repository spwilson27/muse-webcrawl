Charge, parity, and time reversal symmetry (CPT symmetry) is a fundamental symmetry of physical laws under the simultaneous transformations of charge conjugation (C), parity transformation (P), and time reversal (T). CPT is the only combination of C, P, and T that is an exact symmetry of nature at the fundamental level. The CPT theorem says that CPT symmetry holds for all physical phenomena, or more precisely, that any Lorentz invariant local quantum field theory with a Hermitian Hamiltonian must have CPT symmetry.

History

The effort to establish the CPT theorem began in the work of Julian Schwinger in 1951 to prove the connection between spin and statistics. In 1954, Gerhart Lüders and Wolfgang Pauli derived a more explicit theorem, so it is sometimes referred to as the Lüders–Pauli theorem. At the same time, and independently, John Stewart Bell derived the theorem. These proofs are based on the validity of Lorentz invariance and the principle of locality in the interaction of quantum fields. Subsequently, Res Jost gave a more general proof within the framework of axiomatic quantum field theory.

Derivation and implications

An informal sketch of the proof follows from considering how a Lorentz-invariant scalar Lagrangian transforms under the combined operations. Charge conjugation replaces particles with antiparticles; parity reflects spatial coordinates; time reversal reverses the direction of time and complex-conjugates amplitudes. Under the combined transformation, the interaction terms of any local, Lorentz-invariant Hermitian theory return to their original form, so the dynamics are unchanged.

An important consequence of CPT symmetry is that a particle and its antiparticle must have exactly the same mass, the same mean lifetime, equal and opposite electric charge, and equal and opposite magnetic moment. These predictions have been tested to extraordinary precision, for example by comparing the charge-to-mass ratios of protons and antiprotons and by measurements of the neutral kaon system.

CPT violation and Lorentz violation

Because CPT violation would imply violations elsewhere, searches for CPT violation form a part of precision tests of Lorentz invariance. No violation of CPT has ever been observed. Extensions of the Standard Model, such as the Standard-Model Extension, parametrize possible CPT- and Lorentz-violating effects so that experiments — including comparisons of matter and antimatter and precision spectroscopy — can bound them.

In 2002, Oscar Greenberg proposed that CPT violation would imply the violation of Lorentz symmetry; string theory and some theories of quantum gravity have been discussed as possible settings in which CPT might not be exact, though no evidence supports this.
