Slater determinant

In quantum mechanics, a Slater determinant is an expression that describes the wave function of a multi-fermionic system. It satisfies the antisymmetry requirement, and consequently the Pauli exclusion principle, by changing sign upon exchange of two electrons (or other fermions).

Definition

Given a set of N single-particle spin-orbitals χ1, …, χN, the Slater determinant for an N-electron system is the determinant of the matrix whose entries are the spin-orbitals evaluated at each electron's coordinates:

Ψ(x₁,…,x_N) = (1/√N!) det [ χᵢ(xⱼ) ]

The factor 1/√N! normalises the wavefunction. The determinant was proposed by John C. Slater in 1929 as a compact way of writing an antisymmetrised product of orbitals.

Properties

If two electrons occupy the same spin-orbital, two rows (or columns) of the determinant coincide and the wavefunction vanishes — the Pauli exclusion principle. Exchanging the coordinates of two electrons swaps two columns and flips the sign of the wavefunction, as required for fermions. A single Slater determinant is exact for a system of non-interacting fermions; for interacting systems it serves as the starting approximation of the Hartree–Fock method, in which the determinant's orbitals are varied to minimise the energy. More accurate methods such as configuration interaction, coupled cluster and Møller–Plesset perturbation theory build on or correct the single-determinant description.

Two-electron case

For two electrons in spin-orbitals χa and χb, the determinant reduces to (1/√2)[χa(x₁)χb(x₂) − χb(x₁)χa(x₂)], making the antisymmetry under exchange explicit.
