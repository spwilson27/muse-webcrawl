Herbert Spencer

Herbert Spencer (27 April 1820 – 8 December 1903) was an English philosopher, biologist, anthropologist and sociologist famous for applying evolutionary theory to philosophy, psychology and the study of society. He coined the expression "survival of the fittest", which he introduced in his Principles of Biology (1864) after reading Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species.

Life

Spencer was born in Derby and educated largely by his father and uncle, both dissenters and educational reformers. He worked as a railway engineer, then as a journalist on The Economist, before devoting himself to writing. He never married and suffered from chronic ill health, yet produced an enormous body of work.

Synthetic Philosophy

Over four decades Spencer built his vast "System of Synthetic Philosophy", spanning biology, psychology, sociology and ethics. He argued that evolution — the integration of matter and dissipation of motion, from homogeneity to heterogeneity — was a universal law governing everything from stars to human societies.

Social theory and legacy

A leading advocate of laissez-faire, Spencer opposed state poor relief and public education in works such as The Man Versus the State (1884). His views were enormously influential in the late 19th century, especially in the United States, and were later grouped (somewhat unfairly) under the label "social Darwinism". By the mid-20th century his reputation had sharply declined, though scholars continue to study his systematic ambitions and his role in founding sociology.
