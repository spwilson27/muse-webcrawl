Big Stick ideology, or Big Stick diplomacy, describes the foreign policy of U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt, summarized by his saying "speak softly and carry a big stick; you will go far" — the practice of negotiating peacefully while simultaneously threatening with the "big stick", a strong military, in case negotiations fail.

Origins

Roosevelt first used the proverb — which he attributed to a West African saying — in a letter of January 1900 and then publicly in a speech at the Minnesota State Fair on 2 September 1901, shortly before he became president.

Applications

The doctrine underpinned the Roosevelt Corollary (1904), which asserted a U.S. right to intervene in Latin American countries to stabilize their affairs, leading to interventions and protectorates in the Caribbean, including the Dominican Republic, Nicaragua, and Haiti. It also informed U.S. support for Panama's separation from Colombia and the construction of the Panama Canal, the acquisition of rights in Cuba under the Platt Amendment, and Roosevelt's mediation of the Treaty of Portsmouth (1905) ending the Russo-Japanese War, as well as the dispatch of the Great White Fleet (1907–1909) as a display of naval power.

Legacy

"Big stick" policy is commonly contrasted with the "dollar diplomacy" of President Taft and remains a standard reference in discussions of coercive diplomacy and gunboat diplomacy.
