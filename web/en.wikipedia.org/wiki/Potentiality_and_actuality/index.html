Potentiality and actuality

Potentiality and actuality (Greek: dynamis and energeia) are a pair of closely connected principles in Aristotle's philosophy, used to analyse motion, causality, ethics and physiology in his Physics, Metaphysics, Nicomachean Ethics and De Anima.

Overview

Aristotle distinguished a thing's potentiality (dynamis), its capacity to be in a certain state or to bring about change, from its actuality (energeia or entelecheia), the realisation or fulfilment of that capacity. An acorn, for example, is potentially an oak tree; when it grows, that potential is actualised.

Types of potentiality

Aristotle recognised both passive potentiality, the capacity of matter to receive a form, and active potentiality, the capacity of an agent to produce change. Rational capacities, such as the skills of a builder or physician, can be exercised in contrary ways, unlike non-rational capacities which produce a single effect.

Actuality and entelechy

Aristotle coined the term entelecheia (entelechy) for actuality as the state of a thing having its end within itself. The soul, he argued, is the first actuality of a living body — the form by which an organism is alive and capable of its characteristic activities.

Priority of actuality

In Metaphysics Book Θ, Aristotle argues that actuality is prior to potentiality in definition, in substance, and in a qualified sense in time: a mature organism precedes the seed from which a new one grows, and eternal actual beings such as the unmoved mover are prior to perishable potential beings.
