Helena, mother of Constantine I

Flavia Julia Helena (Greek: Ἑλένη, Helénē; c. AD 246/248 – c. 330), also known as Helena of Constantinople and later as Saint Helena, was an Augusta (empress) of the Roman Empire and mother of Emperor Constantine the Great. She is an important figure in the history of Christianity: tradition credits her pilgrimage to the Holy Land in 326–328 with the discovery of relics of the Passion, including the True Cross, and with founding churches at the sites of Christ's birth and ascension.

Helena is venerated as the finder of the True Cross.

Life

Helena was of humble origin, possibly from Drepanum in Bithynia in Asia Minor. She became the wife or concubine of the officer Constantius Chlorus and bore him Constantine around 272. When Constantius rose to become Caesar in 293, he set Helena aside to marry Theodora, stepdaughter of the emperor Maximian. Helena returned to prominence when her son became emperor in 306; Constantine honoured her with the title Augusta in 325, struck coins in her name, and renamed her birthplace Helenopolis.

Pilgrimage and the True Cross

A convert to Christianity, Helena made a pilgrimage to Syria Palaestina and Jerusalem in 326–328, an extraordinary journey for a woman of about eighty. Eusebius of Caesarea records her church-building: the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem and the Church of Eleona on the Mount of Olives. Later tradition — reported by Ambrose, Rufinus and Socrates Scholasticus — holds that during excavations at Golgotha she discovered the True Cross and the nails of the Crucifixion, and identified the site of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre built by Constantine.

Veneration

Helena is venerated as a saint, regarded in Orthodox tradition as equal to the apostles along with her son. Numerous churches are dedicated to St Helena, and the island of Saint Helena in the South Atlantic — where Napoleon was exiled — bears her name.
