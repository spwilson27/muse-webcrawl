Soft power

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Soft power is the ability of a state or actor to shape the preferences of others through appeal and attraction rather than coercion (hard power: military and economic might) or payments. The concept was coined by Joseph Nye in 1990 and developed in Soft Power: The Means to Success in World Politics (2004). Its sources, in Nye's account, are an actor's culture (where attractive), its political values (where genuinely lived up to at home and abroad) and its foreign policies (where perceived as legitimate and moral).

Use and criticism

Governments invest in soft power through cultural institutes (British Council, Goethe-Institut, Confucius Institutes — the latter controversial), international broadcasting, education exchanges, development aid and nation branding; indices attempt to rank national soft power, with France, the UK, the US, Japan and others perennially near the top. Critics question the concept's measurability, its conflation of attraction with statecraft, and the convertibility of appeal into outcomes; the rise of "sharp power" (authoritarian manipulation of open societies' information environments) and social-media-era public diplomacy have extended the debate. Nye's later "smart power" denotes combining hard and soft resources effectively.
