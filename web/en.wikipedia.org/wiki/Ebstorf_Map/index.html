Ebstorf Map

Ebstorf Map
Datec. 13th century (c. 1235, traditionally attributed)
LocationCreated at Ebstorf Abbey, Lower Saxony
SizeAbout 3.6 m in diameter, on 30 goatskins
StatusOriginal destroyed in 1943; known from photographs and reproductions

The Ebstorf Map was a medieval mappa mundi — the largest known example of its kind — created around the thirteenth century, probably in the Benedictine convent at Ebstorf in Lower Saxony, Germany. It depicted the known world as a great circle roughly 3.6 metres across, drawn and painted on thirty sewn-together goatskins, and presented the world as the body of Christ: the head of Christ appeared at the top (east), his hands at the north and south edges, and his feet at the west.

Contents

Like other T-O maps, the Ebstorf Map divided the circular world-ocean into three continents — Asia across the top, Europe at lower left, and Africa at lower right — separated by T-shaped waters, with Jerusalem near the centre. It contained about 1,600 inscriptions, some 1,500 places, numerous rivers and mountains, and hundreds of pictures: cities, biblical scenes, historical events, mythical peoples and animals, and vignettes from natural history drawn from authors such as Pliny and Isidore of Seville. The Earthly Paradise was shown at the top in Asia, near Christ's head.

History and loss

The map was discovered in 1830 in a storeroom at Ebstorf Abbey and passed into the state archives at Hanover. The original was destroyed in an Allied bombing raid on Hanover in 1943 during the Second World War. Fortunately it had been photographed in colour and reproduced in facsimile before the war, and several reconstructions exist, including a full-scale copy displayed at Ebstorf. The map is traditionally associated with Gervase of Tilbury, though the attribution is debated.
