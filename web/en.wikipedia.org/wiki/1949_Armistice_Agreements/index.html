The 1949 Armistice Agreements were a set of armistice agreements signed during 1949 between Israel and neighbouring Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria to formally end the hostilities of the 1948 Arab–Israeli War and establish armistice lines between Israeli forces and Arab forces. The agreements were negotiated under United Nations auspices on the island of Rhodes, mediated by the UN Acting Mediator Ralph Bunche, who later received the 1950 Nobel Peace Prize for his work.

The agreements

The first agreement, with Egypt, was signed on 24 February 1949; Lebanon followed on 23 March, Jordan on 3 April, and Syria on 20 July 1949. Iraq, whose forces had fought in the war, withdrew without signing an agreement, handing its positions to Jordan. The agreements fixed armistice demarcation lines — the Egyptian and Jordanian lines became known as the "Green Line" — and created demilitarised zones and Mixed Armistice Commissions supervised by the UN Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO).

The agreements explicitly stated that the armistice lines were not political or territorial boundaries and were agreed "without prejudice" to the parties' claims in an ultimate peace settlement. Under them, Israel controlled about 78 per cent of the territory of former Mandatory Palestine; Jordan held the West Bank (annexed in 1950) and East Jerusalem, and Egypt administered the Gaza Strip.

Subsequent history

Intended as temporary arrangements pending peace treaties, the armistice lines instead endured for 18 years, until the Six-Day War of 1967. No final peace treaties followed from them; Israel's peace treaty with Egypt came only in 1979 and with Jordan in 1994. The Green Line remains a central reference line in negotiations over Israeli–Palestinian final status.
