Folk culture

Folk culture describes the unifying expressive components of everyday life of any specific group, as distinguished from mass culture and popular culture. It generally refers to the products and practices of relatively homogeneous and isolated small-scale social groups living in rural locations. Thus, folk culture is often associated with tradition, historical continuity, and a sense of place and belonging. It is often characterized by face-to-face interaction, strong social networks, and storytelling as a means of transmitting shared values.

Characteristics

Folk culture comprises traditional customs, tales, sayings, dances, legends, oral history, proverbs, jokes, popular beliefs, and other forms of expression passed down within a community, largely through oral tradition. Because it is transmitted informally, folk culture tends to change slowly and varies from place to place, in contrast to popular culture, which diffuses rapidly and widely through mass media.

Relation to other cultures

Scholars contrast folk culture with popular culture, which is produced by mass media and consumed by heterogeneous groups, and with mass culture, a related concept. The study of folk culture is a central concern of folklore studies and cultural geography, which examine how folk practices relate to landscape, identity, and community.

Decline and preservation

As societies urbanize and mass media spread, many folk traditions have declined or been absorbed into popular culture. At the same time, festivals, museums, and cultural heritage programs work to preserve folk customs, crafts, music, and foodways as part of national and regional identity.
