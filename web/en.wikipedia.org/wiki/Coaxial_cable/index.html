Coaxial cable

Coaxial cable, or coax (pronounced /ˈkoʊ.æks/), is a type of electrical cable consisting of an inner conductor surrounded by a concentric conducting shield, with the two separated by a dielectric (insulating material); many coaxial cables also have a protective outer sheath or jacket. The term coaxial refers to the inner conductor and the outer shield sharing a geometric axis.

Construction and operation

The concentric geometry confines the electromagnetic field of the signal to the space between the conductors, so losses by radiation and interference from external fields are small. The characteristic impedance is determined by the ratio of the conductor diameters and the permittivity of the dielectric. Two impedances dominate: 50 ohms for radio and data applications, and 75 ohms for video and cable television distribution.

Coaxial cable was the original medium for Ethernet networks and remains ubiquitous for distributing television signals to homes, where hybrid fibre-coaxial networks carry broadband internet service (DOCSIS). It is also used for connecting radio transmitters and receivers to antennas, in scientific instruments, and in military and avionics systems.

History

English engineer Oliver Heaviside patented the design in 1880. The first transatlantic coaxial telephone cable, TAT-1, entered service in 1956. Herbert H. "Hobby" and others standardized the RG (Radio Guide) designations during the Second World War.

Limitations

Signal attenuation increases with frequency and distance, and bulky cables are stiff and expensive to install around bends. For high-bandwidth data, twisted-pair copper and optical fibre have largely superseded coax in local networks, though coax remains competitive for last-mile television and broadband delivery.
