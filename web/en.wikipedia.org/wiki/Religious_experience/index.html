Religious experience

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Depictions often use imagery of light

A religious experience (also sometimes known as a spiritual experience, sacred experience, or mystical experience) is a subjective experience which is interpreted within a religious framework. The concept originated in the 19th century, as a defense against the growing rationalism of Western society. William James popularised the concept in his The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902).

Many religious and mystical traditions see religious experiences (particularly the knowledge which comes with them) as real encounters with God or gods, or real contact with higher-order realities of which humans are not ordinarily aware. Scientists may hold that religious experience is an evolved feature of the human brain amenable to normal scientific study.

Definitions

William James described four characteristics of mystical experience: ineffability (the experience cannot adequately be described in words), noetic quality (a sense of gaining knowledge), transiency, and passivity. Rudolf Otto used the term numinous for the experience of the holy, characterized by mysterium tremendum et fascinans — a mystery that is at once terrifying and fascinating.

Examples and causes

Religious experiences have been reported in prayer, meditation, worship, and near-death experiences, and have also been associated with fasting, sensory deprivation, entheogenic substances, epilepsy (particularly temporal lobe epilepsy), and mental illness. Conversion experiences, such as Paul's on the road to Damascus, are central narratives in several traditions.

Study

The psychology of religion, neurotheology and cognitive science of religion study religious experience from empirical perspectives, while philosophers of religion debate whether such experiences can serve as evidence for religious claims. Critics such as Wayne Proudfoot have argued that experiences are shaped by the concepts and expectations of the traditions in which they occur.
