Appeal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An appeal is a legal procedure in which a case is brought before a higher court for review of the decision of a lower court. Appeals correct errors of law, procedure or (within limits) fact-finding, unify the law's interpretation, and legitimate outcomes by providing a second look. Most legal systems grant at least one level of appeal in criminal cases (a right recognised in the ICCPR), and civil appeals subject to varying filters.

Procedure

Appellate courts generally do not retry cases: they review the record, hear argument on alleged errors, and apply standards of review — strict for legal questions (de novo), deferential for factual findings and discretionary rulings. Outcomes include affirming, reversing, modifying or remanding for retrial. Access is controlled by leave/permission requirements (notably for second appeals and supreme courts, such as US certiorari), filing deadlines, and in some systems security for costs. Interlocutory appeals (mid-case) are restricted to prevent delay; final-judgment rules dominate in US practice.

Significance

Because most litigation ends below, appellate courts exert outsized influence in declaring law through published (precedential) opinions. Wrongful-conviction review bodies, such as the UK's Criminal Cases Review Commission, supplement ordinary appeals where fresh evidence emerges after they are exhausted.
