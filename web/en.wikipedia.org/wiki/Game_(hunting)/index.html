Game (hunting)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Game or quarry is any wild animal hunted for animal products (primarily meat), for recreation, or as a trophy. The species of animals hunted, whether as food or for sport, vary in different parts of the world and by local contexts and traditions. In many legal systems game is distinguished from other wildlife by specific seasons, licences and bag limits, and the meat obtained is termed game meat.

Types of game

Big game traditionally refers to large mammals — in North America deer, elk, moose, bear and bighorn sheep; in Africa the celebrated "Big Five" (lion, leopard, rhinoceros, elephant and African buffalo). Small game includes rabbits, hares, squirrels and furbearers. Game birds divide into upland birds (pheasant, grouse, partridge, quail) and waterfowl (ducks and geese hunted in wetlands), while in Britain "game" in culinary and legal usage classically means pheasant, partridge, grouse, ptarmigan, hare and deer. Wild boar are prized quarry in Europe and Asia.

Regulation

Hunting of game is usually governed by conservation law: open and closed seasons protect breeding periods, bag limits cap harvest, and tags or licences fund habitat management — in the United States, through mechanisms such as the Pittman–Robertson Act (1937) excise taxes and the Duck Stamp. In the United Kingdom, game law descends from historic forest and Game Acts, with seasons fixed by statute (famously, red grouse shooting opens on the "Glorious Twelfth" of August). Gamekeepers manage habitat and control predators on shooting estates.

Game meat

Game meat is generally leaner and more strongly flavoured than domestic meat; venison (deer), wild boar, rabbit and game birds are valued in traditional and modern cuisine. Fresh game is traditionally hung to tenderise and develop flavour. Food-safety rules govern commercial sale and inspection in many jurisdictions.
