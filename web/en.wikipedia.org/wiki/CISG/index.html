United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods

The United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG), sometimes known as the Vienna Convention, is a multilateral treaty that establishes a uniform framework for international commerce. Adopted by a diplomatic conference in Vienna on 11 April 1980, the convention entered into force on 1 January 1988 after ratification by eleven states. As a uniform international sales law, it has been ratified by more than ninety countries, representing a large majority of world trade, and is considered one of the most successful instruments of international commercial law.

Background and adoption

Efforts to unify the law of international sales began under the League of Nations and produced two Hague Conventions of 1964 (ULIS and ULF), which attracted few ratifications, mainly from Western European states. The newly created UNCITRAL took up the task in 1968, working from the Hague texts but broadening participation to include developing and socialist countries. After a decade of drafting under a working group that included scholars such as John Honnold, the text was adopted at a diplomatic conference in Vienna in 1980 attended by 62 states. The CISG is often described as a bridge between civil law, common law, and other legal traditions; its drafting deliberately avoided terminology peculiar to any one system.

Scope and structure

The Convention applies to contracts for the sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different states, when those states are Contracting States (Article 1(1)(a)) or when private international law leads to the law of a Contracting State (Article 1(1)(b)). It excludes consumer sales, auctions, electricity, ships, and aircraft, and it does not govern the validity of the contract or the effect of the contract on property in the goods (Article 4), nor liability for death or personal injury (Article 5). Parties may exclude the Convention entirely or vary its provisions (Article 6) — a freedom frequently exercised in practice.

The 101 articles fall into four parts: (I) sphere of application and general provisions, including interpretation of the Convention with regard to its international character and the need to promote uniformity (Article 7); (II) formation of the contract, dispensing with any writing requirement (Article 11) and modifying common-law rules such as the mirror-image rule via Article 19; (III) the obligations of seller and buyer — delivery of conforming goods (Article 35), examination and notice of non-conformity (Articles 38–39), and a graduated system of remedies including price reduction, cure, avoidance for fundamental breach (Article 25), and damages measured on common principles (Articles 74–77); and (IV) final treaty provisions.

Interpretation and influence

Because the CISG is applied by national courts, achieving uniform interpretation is a standing challenge; aids include the UNCITRAL Digest of case law, the CLOUT reporting system, the CISG Advisory Council opinions, and large academic databases. The Convention has influenced domestic reforms (including in China, Germany, and the Nordic states), regional instruments, and soft-law codifications such as the UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts. Notable non-parties include the United Kingdom and India, whose traders frequently encounter the CISG through foreign governing law or counterparty states.
