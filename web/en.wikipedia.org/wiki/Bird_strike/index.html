A bird strike — sometimes called bird ingestion (for an engine), bird hit, or bird aircraft strike hazard (BASH) — is a collision between an airborne animal (usually a bird or bat) and a moving vehicle (usually an aircraft). The term is also used for other wildlife strikes with vehicles. Bird strikes are a significant threat to flight safety and have caused a number of accidents with human casualties.

Risk and damage

Most bird strikes occur at low altitude during takeoff and landing, when birds are most concentrated and aircraft are slow and low. The force of impact depends on the bird's weight and the speed differential: a 5 kg bird striking an aircraft travelling at 240 km/h exerts a force comparable to a one-tonne weight dropped from three metres. Engines are particularly vulnerable — ingestion can cause compressor stall or complete failure — and windshields, radomes, and wing leading edges are frequently damaged. In the United States alone, reported strikes number in the tens of thousands annually, and global damage costs are estimated in the billions of dollars.

Notable incidents

On 15 January 2009, US Airways Flight 1549 struck a flock of Canada geese shortly after departure from New York's LaGuardia Airport, losing thrust in both engines; Captain Chesley Sullenberger ditched the Airbus A320 in the Hudson River with no fatalities — the "Miracle on the Hudson". Earlier, in 1960, Eastern Air Lines Flight 375 crashed after ingesting starlings, killing 62 people and prompting major regulatory changes.

Prevention

Mitigation includes airport wildlife management — habitat modification, pyrotechnics, trained raptors and dogs, and lethal control — as well as bird-detecting radar, aircraft certification standards requiring engines and airframes to withstand impacts from specified bird weights, and reporting programmes such as the Smithsonian's feather-identification service, which identifies species from remains ("snarge") to guide prevention.
