Pyrite is an iron sulfide mineral with the chemical formula FeS2 (iron(II) disulfide). It is the most abundant sulfide mineral on Earth. Its metallic lustre and pale brass-yellow hue give it a superficial resemblance to gold, from which it earned the nickname "fool's gold". The name pyrite derives from the Greek pyrítēs lithos, "stone which strikes fire," because it emits sparks when struck against steel.

Properties and identification

Pyrite crystallises in the isometric system, commonly forming cubes—often striated—octahedra, and twelve-faced pyritohedra. Despite its golden look, it is easily distinguished from gold: it is much harder (6–6.5 on the Mohs scale, versus gold's malleability), brittle rather than ductile, and gives a greenish-black streak. Its related polymorph marcasite shares the same formula but has an orthorhombic structure.

Occurrence and uses

Pyrite occurs in sedimentary rocks such as shale and coal beds, in hydrothermal veins, and as an accessory mineral in igneous and metamorphic rocks. Famous localities include Spain (Rio Tinto), Italy (Elba), and Peru. Historically it was mined as a source of sulfur and sulfuric acid, and was used in wheellock firearms to strike sparks. Oxidation of pyrite in mines and waste rock generates acid mine drainage, a significant environmental problem. In recent research, pyrite has attracted interest as a cheap semiconductor material for photovoltaics.
