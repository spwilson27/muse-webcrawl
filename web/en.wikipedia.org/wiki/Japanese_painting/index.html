Japanese painting

Image placeholder: Japanese painting

Japanese painting (日本画, Nihonga) is one of the oldest and most highly refined of the Japanese visual arts, encompassing a wide variety of genres and styles. As with the history of Japanese arts in general, the long history of Japanese painting combines the influence of Chinese painting and the development of a distinct Japanese tradition.

History

Painting in Japan dates to the prehistoric period, with simple graphic designs found on Jōmon pottery and Yayoi bronze bells. The introduction of Buddhism from China and Korea in the 6th century brought Chinese painting techniques and religious subject matter, which developed into Buddhist painting of the Nara and Heian periods.

In the Heian period, the secular style known as yamato-e ("Japanese pictures") emerged, used for narrative handscrolls (emaki) such as the Genji Monogatari Emaki, illustrating scenes from The Tale of Genji. During the Muromachi period, Zen Buddhism inspired monochrome ink painting (suiboku-ga), exemplified by the works of Sesshū Tōyō.

The Azuchi–Momoyama and Edo periods saw the rise of decorative schools: the Kanō school, official painters to the shogunate; the Rinpa school, known for bold designs and gold-leaf backgrounds; the Tosa school; and the Maruyama–Shijō school of naturalistic painting. In the Meiji period, Japanese-style painting (Nihonga) was distinguished from Western-style painting (Yōga).

Formats and materials

Traditional Japanese painting is executed with brushes on paper or silk, in formats including hanging scrolls (kakemono), handscrolls (emaki), folding screens (byōbu), sliding doors (fusuma), and albums. Pigments are derived from natural minerals, shells, and plants, bound with animal glue (nikawa).
