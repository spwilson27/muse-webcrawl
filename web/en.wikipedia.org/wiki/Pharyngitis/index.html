Pharyngitis

Symptoms: Sore throat, pain on swallowing, fever

Causes: Usually viral infection; also group A Streptococcus and other bacteria

Treatment: Supportive care; antibiotics for bacterial causes

Pharyngitis is inflammation of the pharynx, the part of the throat behind the mouth and nasal cavity, and is one of the most common reasons for medical visits. Its cardinal symptom is a sore throat. Most cases are caused by viral infections associated with the common cold or influenza, and resolve without specific treatment within about a week.

Causes

Viruses — including rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, adenoviruses, and the Epstein–Barr virus (infectious mononucleosis) — account for the great majority of cases (roughly 50–80%). The most important bacterial cause is group A Streptococcus pyogenes ("strep throat"), responsible for approximately 15–30% of cases in children and 5–15% in adults; other bacterial causes include Fusobacterium necrophorum, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Corynebacterium diphtheriae (diphtheria). Non-infectious causes include allergens, irritants such as smoke or dry air, and gastroesophageal reflux.

Diagnosis

Clinical prediction rules such as the Centor criteria (fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical lymph nodes, absence of cough, and age) help estimate the probability of streptococcal infection. Rapid antigen detection tests and throat culture confirm group A streptococcus. Distinguishing viral from bacterial causes matters because antibiotics are ineffective against viruses and unnecessary use drives antimicrobial resistance.

Treatment and complications

Management is mainly symptomatic: analgesics such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, hydration, and rest. Confirmed streptococcal pharyngitis is treated with penicillin or amoxicillin (cephalosporins or macrolides for the penicillin-allergic), mainly to prevent complications. Untreated streptococcal infection can lead to suppurative complications such as peritonsillar abscess, and to immune-mediated sequelae including acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Most viral cases resolve in 3–7 days, though mononucleosis may cause prolonged symptoms.
