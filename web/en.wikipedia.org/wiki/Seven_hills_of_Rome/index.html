Seven hills of Rome

The seven hills of Rome (Italian: Sette colli di Roma) are the hills east of the river Tiber around which the ancient city of Rome was built. They are traditionally listed as the Aventine, Caelian, Capitoline, Esquiline, Palatine, Quirinal and Viminal hills. The hills were a prominent feature of Roman topography and remain central to the geography of the modern city, though centuries of building have greatly reduced their original prominence.

The seven hills

  • Palatine — the centremost hill and, in Roman tradition, the site of the city's founding by Romulus; later the seat of imperial palaces, giving English the word "palace".
  • Capitoline — the smallest hill, site of the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus and the citadel of Rome.
  • Aventine — associated with the plebeians and their secessions from the patrician order.
  • Caelian — a fashionable residential district in imperial times.
  • Esquiline — the largest hill, once the site of Rome's cemetery for the poor, later covered by Nero's Golden House.
  • Quirinal — the northernmost hill, today home to the Quirinal Palace, residence of the President of Italy.
  • Viminal — the smallest of the seven, between the Quirinal and the Esquiline.

History

The hills were originally occupied by separate settlements whose inhabitants eventually merged — a process commemorated in the festival of the Septimontium ("seven hills"). The Servian Wall, built in the 4th century BC, enclosed all seven hills within a single fortified city. Rome's identity as the "City on Seven Hills" became a commonplace of Latin literature and has been echoed by many later cities that claim seven hills of their own, including Lisbon, Istanbul and Jerusalem.
