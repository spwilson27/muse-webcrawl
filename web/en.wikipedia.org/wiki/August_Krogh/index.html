August Krogh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schack August Steenberg Krogh (15 November 1874 – 13 September 1949) was a Danish professor at the department of animal physiology at the University of Copenhagen from 1916 to 1945. He contributed a number of fundamental discoveries within several fields of physiology, and is famous for developing the Krogh principle — the idea that for every physiological problem there is an organism in which it can most conveniently be studied.

He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1920 for the discovery of the mechanism of regulation of the capillaries in skeletal muscle.

Life and work

Krogh was born in Grenaa, Denmark, the son of a shipbuilder. He studied zoology at Copenhagen and completed his doctorate in 1903 with work on respiration through the skin and lungs of frogs. With his wife, the physiologist Marie Krogh (née Jørgensen), he demonstrated that gas exchange in the lungs occurs by passive diffusion rather than secretion, settling a long-running debate with work conducted with Christian Bohr.

His Nobel-winning work showed that capillaries open and close in response to the metabolic needs of the tissues they supply — the so-called capillary recruitment mechanism. Krogh also developed precision instruments, including improved tonometers and respirometers, and his measurements of oxygen diffusion gave rise to the concept of the Krogh cylinder model of tissue oxygenation.

Insulin and later years

After his wife developed diabetes, Krogh travelled to North America in 1922 and obtained permission to produce insulin in Scandinavia, co-founding the Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium — a forerunner of Novo Nordisk — with Hans Christian Hagedorn. He was also a pioneer of isotope tracer studies in physiology and of research expeditions, including work in Greenland.
