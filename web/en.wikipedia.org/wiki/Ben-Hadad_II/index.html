Ben-Hadad II (or Benhadad II) was a king of Aram-Damascus in the 9th century BC, the son and successor of Hazael. His name, meaning "son of Hadad", invokes the storm god Hadad, and is the dynastic name borne by several Aramean kings of Damascus. His reign is known from the Hebrew Bible, from Assyrian royal inscriptions, and from Aramean monumental inscriptions such as the Zakir Stele.

Reign

Ben-Hadad II inherited a kingdom that his father Hazael had raised to regional dominance, dominating much of the southern Levant including the northern kingdom of Israel. During his reign, however, Aram-Damascus came under renewed pressure from the Neo-Assyrian Empire. The Assyrian king Adad-nirari III campaigned westward and, according to his inscriptions, received tribute from Damascus, an event sometimes dated to around 796 BC. Assyrian pressure weakened Aramean control over Israel, allowing King Jehoash of Israel to recover cities his father had lost.

Biblical account

In the biblical narrative of 2 Kings 13, Ben-Hadad II (called simply Ben-Hadad) is defeated three times by Jehoash of Israel, fulfilling a prophecy attributed to the dying prophet Elisha, who had promised that Israel would strike Aram at Aphek.

Zakir Stele

The Zakir Stele, an Aramaic inscription commissioned by Zakir, king of Hamath and Luhuti, records that a "Bar-Hadad, son of Hazael, king of Aram" led a coalition of kings against Zakir and besieged him at Hazrak, but that the god Baal-shamayn delivered Zakir. This Bar-Hadad is generally identified with Ben-Hadad II, making the stele an important extra-biblical witness to his reign and to the shifting alliances of the Aramean city-states.
