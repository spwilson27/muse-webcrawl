Ovulation

Ovulation is the release of a mature egg cell (ovum) from an ovarian follicle in the ovary, an event that occurs once in each menstrual cycle in women of reproductive age. The released oocyte is swept into the fallopian tube, where it remains viable for fertilisation for roughly 12–24 hours. Ovulation marks the transition between the follicular and luteal phases of the cycle and defines the peak of the fertile window — the few days during which conception is possible.

Mechanism

During the follicular phase, rising follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) from the pituitary gland stimulates the growth of a cohort of ovarian follicles. The dominant follicle secretes increasing amounts of oestradiol. When oestradiol levels stay high for a sustained period, feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary switches from negative to positive, provoking a sharp surge of luteinising hormone (LH) — and a smaller surge of FSH — about 24–36 hours before ovulation. The LH surge triggers the resumption of meiosis in the oocyte, weakens the follicle wall through prostaglandin- and protease-mediated changes, and causes the follicle to rupture at a weak point called the stigma, expelling the oocyte surrounded by its cumulus cells. Fimbriae at the end of the fallopian tube sweep the egg into the tube.

Timing and fertility

In a textbook 28-day cycle, ovulation occurs around day 14, but cycle lengths and ovulation days vary considerably between women and between cycles. Because sperm can survive in the female reproductive tract for up to about five days, the fertile window comprises the five days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation itself, with the highest probability of conception in the two days before ovulation. After ovulation the remains of the follicle form the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone; if fertilisation and implantation do not occur within about two weeks, the corpus luteum regresses and menstruation follows.

Signs and detection

Signs of approaching ovulation include a change in cervical mucus to a clear, slippery, stretchy consistency, a small rise in basal body temperature (which occurs after ovulation, confirming it retrospectively), and, in some women, mild one-sided pelvic pain known as mittelschmerz. Over-the-counter ovulation predictor kits detect the urinary LH surge. Clinically, ovulation can be confirmed by ultrasound monitoring of follicular growth or by a mid-luteal serum progesterone measurement.

Disorders

Failure to ovulate (anovulation) is a major cause of infertility. Causes include polycystic ovary syndrome, hypothalamic amenorrhoea (from stress, low body weight or excessive exercise), hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid disorders and premature ovarian insufficiency. Hormonal contraceptives work principally by suppressing the LH surge and thus preventing ovulation.

In other mammals

Most female mammals ovulate cyclically, but the pattern varies: some species are spontaneous ovulators, like humans, while others — including cats, rabbits and camels — are induced ovulators, in which copulation itself triggers the LH surge. Many species conceal ovulation, whereas some primates advertise it with visible signs; humans are generally classed as having relatively concealed ovulation, though subtle behavioural and physical changes across the cycle have been documented.
