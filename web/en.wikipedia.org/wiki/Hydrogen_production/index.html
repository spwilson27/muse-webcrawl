Hydrogen production is the family of industrial methods used to generate molecular hydrogen (H2). Although hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, it occurs on Earth almost entirely bound in compounds such as water and hydrocarbons, so it must be manufactured. Roughly 97 million tonnes of hydrogen are produced annually, overwhelmingly from fossil fuels, and used mainly for ammonia fertiliser production and petroleum refining.

Steam methane reforming

The dominant process reacts methane with steam at 700–1,000 °C over a nickel catalyst to give hydrogen and carbon monoxide (synthesis gas); a subsequent water–gas shift reaction converts the carbon monoxide with more steam into carbon dioxide and additional hydrogen. Reforming accounts for the great majority of world production but emits roughly 9–10 kg of CO2 per kilogram of hydrogen unless coupled with carbon capture and storage.

Colour codes

The industry informally classifies hydrogen by production route: grey hydrogen from unabated fossil fuels, blue hydrogen from fossil fuels with carbon capture, green hydrogen from electrolysis powered by renewable electricity, and pink hydrogen from nuclear-powered electrolysis.

Electrolysis

Electrolysis splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity. Alkaline electrolysers are the mature technology; proton-exchange membrane (PEM) and solid-oxide electrolysers offer higher efficiency or better dynamic response. Although currently a small share of production, electrolysis is the focus of most low-carbon hydrogen strategies.

Other methods

Other routes include partial oxidation of heavy hydrocarbons, coal gasification, biomass gasification, methane pyrolysis (which yields solid carbon rather than CO2), thermochemical water-splitting cycles such as the sulfur–iodine cycle, and biological production by algae and bacteria.
