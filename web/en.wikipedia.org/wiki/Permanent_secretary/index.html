Permanent secretary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A permanent secretary is the most senior civil servant of a British government ministry, responsible for running the department on a day-to-day basis and as its accounting officer for public funds. Unlike the Secretary of State (the politician who heads the department), the permanent secretary is a career official, appointed on merit, politically impartial and — as the title says — permanent across changes of government, providing continuity and institutional memory.

The role emerged as British administration professionalised after the Northcote–Trevelyan reforms (1854): a permanent head advising ministers, commanding the department's officials, and answering to Parliament (the Public Accounts Committee) for value for money. Permanent secretaries sit at the apex of the civil service under the Cabinet Secretary and Head of the Home Civil Service. The model spread through the Commonwealth (Australia's departmental secretaries, India's cabinet secretary system, Canada's deputy ministers are analogous). Tension between permanence and ministerial demands for responsiveness is a permanent theme of British constitutional debate — sharpened in recent years by rapid turnover in the role.
