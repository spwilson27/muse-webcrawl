Carina (constellation)

Carina in the southern sky, showing Canopus and the Carina Nebula

Carina is a constellation in the southern sky. Its name is Latin for the keel of a ship, and it was formerly part of the larger constellation Argo Navis, the ship of Jason and the Argonauts, before that constellation was divided into Carina, Puppis (the stern) and Vela (the sails) by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille in the 1750s. Carina contains Canopus, the second-brightest star in the night sky, and the giant Carina Nebula.

Notable features

Canopus (Alpha Carinae) is a bright giant roughly 310 light-years away, long used for spacecraft navigation. The constellation also contains Eta Carinae, a highly luminous and unstable massive star system embedded in the Carina Nebula (NGC 3372), which underwent a "great eruption" in the 1840s that briefly made it the second-brightest star in the sky. Other notable objects include the Diamond Cross and False Cross asterisms — the latter sometimes mistaken for the Southern Cross — and the open clusters NGC 2516 and the Southern Pleiades (IC 2602).

History and mythology

As part of Argo Navis, Carina was one of the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy. Because of its far southern declination it is invisible from most of Europe and was little known to northern observers until the voyages of exploration of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when Dutch navigators charted the southern stars.

Meteor showers

The Eta Carinids, a minor meteor shower with a radiant near Eta Carinae, peak in January. The constellation also lies across a rich stretch of the Milky Way, making it a favourite target for southern-hemisphere observers.
