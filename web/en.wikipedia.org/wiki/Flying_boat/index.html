Flying boat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Boeing 314 Clipper flying boat at its moorings in a harbour, 1939 photograph style]
A Clipper flying boat at anchor.

A flying boat is a type of seaplane whose fuselage is a watertight, boat-like hull providing buoyancy and planing surfaces, usually with stabilising floats under the wings. Flying boats opened the world\'s air routes before long runways existed: Sikorsky and Martin \'Clippers\', Short Empires and the Boeing 314 carried mail and wealthy passengers across the Atlantic and Pacific in the 1930s, and Second World War patrol boats (Catalina, Sunderland) hunted submarines across the oceans.

Golden age

Imperial Airways\' Short Empire boats linked Britain to Australia and South Africa in stages; Pan Am\'s Sikorsky S-42, Martin M-130 (China Clipper) and Boeing 314 (Yankee Clipper) crossed the Pacific from 1935 and the Atlantic from 1939, with sleeper berths and dining rooms. Howard Hughes\' giant H-4 Hercules (1947) came too late — landplanes from wartime airfields took the routes.

Wartime and decline

The PBY Catalina was the most numerous flying boat (over 3,300 built), famed for spotting the Bismarck and rescuing downed airmen; Sunderlands and H8Ks ranged whole oceans. Post-war, only niches survived: firefighting conversions, the ShinMaywa PS-1/US-2 lineage in Japan, Russia\'s Be-12 and Beriev jets, and the Martin Mars water bombers (retired from firefighting in the 2010s, now museum-bound).

Design

A two-step hull breaks water suction at takeoff; sponsons or wing floats stabilise the boat on the surface. Marine equipment (anchors, bilge pumps, sea drogues) and corrosion protection mark the type. Modern amphibians add retractable wheels for land operation.
