Eugene Onegin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Onegin and Tatyana in the garden: the young heroine confessing her love in a letter, in an illustration after the novel's most famous scene]

Eugene Onegin (Russian: Евгений Онегин) is a novel in verse by Alexander Pushkin, published in full in 1833 and regarded as the central work of Russian literature. Written over some eight years in a distinctive fourteen-line stanza of iambic tetrameter (the 'Onegin stanza'), it tells of the bored St Petersburg dandy Eugene Onegin, the country girl Tatyana Larina who falls in love with him, the poet Lensky whom he kills in a duel, and their final, reversed encounter years later.

The narrative is as famous for its digressive, witty narrator — Pushkin's most charming mask — as for its plot: theatre, balls, duels, dreams and footnotes on Russian life make it an encyclopedia of its era. Vladimir Nabokov's massive literal translation and commentary (1964) is a landmark of translation polemic; Tchaikovsky's opera (1879) carried the story worldwide.

Plot in brief

Onegin, weary of society, inherits a country estate and befriends the young poet Lensky, who is engaged to Olga Larina. Olga's elder sister Tatyana writes Onegin the famous letter declaring her love; he coolly lectures her on the danger of candour. Months later, piqued at a name-day ball, Onegin flirts with Olga; the ensuing duel kills Lensky — the novel's still point of guilt. Tatyana, married to a stout general in Moscow, meets Onegin again: now he burns and writes, and now she, still loving him, refuses — 'I am another's; I shall be faithful to him all my life' (in summary; the novel ends unresolved).

Reception

Belinsky called it an 'encyclopedia of Russian life'. Its stanza has been borrowed by poets from Vikram Seth (The Golden Gate, 1986) onward, and its portrait of the 'superfluous man' founded a Russian archetype. Untranslatable in its ease, it remains the touchstone against which Russian prose and poetry are judged.
