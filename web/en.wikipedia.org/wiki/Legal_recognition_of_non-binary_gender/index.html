Legal recognition of non-binary gender

Legal recognition of non-binary gender refers to the extent to which legal systems allow people who identify as neither exclusively male nor female to have their gender recognized in law, including through a third or non-binary gender marker on identity documents such as passports, driver's licences, and birth certificates. Recognition varies widely between jurisdictions, from full self-declaration regimes to no recognition at all.

Markers on documents

Many countries permit an "X" marker on passports, a format standardized by ICAO for unspecified or indeterminate sex. Australia introduced an X passport category in 2011; Canada has offered an X designation on passports since 2019; the United States began issuing passports with an X gender marker in 2022, though U.S. policy on the option has shifted with changes of administration. Germany's civil status law has allowed a "diverse" (divers) entry since 2018, following a Federal Constitutional Court ruling concerning intersex people.

Self-declaration laws

Argentina's Gender Identity Law of 2012 pioneered a model based purely on self-declaration, permitting change of legal gender without medical or judicial requirements, though it initially framed gender in binary terms. Later statutes in countries such as Spain (2023) and Germany (the Self-Determination Act of 2024) addressed non-binary recognition more explicitly, allowing registrants to choose a diverse entry or none.

Subnational recognition

In the United States, states including California, Oregon, Colorado, New York, and others have provided non-binary ("X") options on driver's licences and birth certificates by statute or administrative rule, while other states recognize only binary markers. In Australia, several states and territories allow non-binary descriptors on birth certificates.

Intersex and third-gender traditions

Some recognitions long predate the modern term "non-binary": India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal legally recognize hijra and third-gender communities, with India's Supreme Court affirming a third-gender right in the 2014 NALSA judgment. Indigenous categories such as Two-Spirit in North America and fa'afafine in Samoa illustrate that gender diversity beyond the binary exists in many cultures, though legal recognition of these identities varies.

Debates

Advocates argue recognition prevents the distress and practical problems — exposure to questioning, discrimination, or danger when documents "out" the holder — caused by mismatched identification. Critics raise questions about sex-based statistics, prisons, sport, and data systems designed around binary fields. Courts and legislatures continue to work through these issues, and the legal landscape remains in flux in many countries.
