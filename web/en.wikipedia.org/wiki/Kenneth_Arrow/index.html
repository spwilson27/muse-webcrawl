Kenneth Joseph Arrow (August 23, 1921 – February 21, 2017) was an American economist, mathematician and political theorist, widely regarded as one of the most influential economic theorists of the twentieth century. He won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1972, jointly with John Hicks; at 51, he remains the youngest person to have received the economics prize.

Education and career

Arrow was born in New York City and graduated from the City College of New York in 1940. He took a master's degree in mathematics at Columbia University, then served as a weather officer in the U.S. Army Air Forces during the Second World War. Returning to Columbia, he completed his PhD in economics in 1951 under Harold Hotelling. He held professorships at Stanford University and Harvard University.

Contributions

Arrow's impossibility theorem, the subject of his doctoral dissertation published as Social Choice and Individual Values (1951), proved that no ranked voting system can satisfy a short list of seemingly reasonable fairness criteria simultaneously when there are three or more options. It founded the modern field of social choice theory.

With Gérard Debreu, Arrow gave the first rigorous proof of the existence of a general competitive equilibrium (the Arrow–Debreu model), a cornerstone of neoclassical economics. He also pioneered the economics of uncertainty and information, analysed the economics of medical care in a celebrated 1963 paper, founded (with Kenneth Arrow and Frank Hahn) work on stability of equilibrium, and introduced the concept of learning-by-doing in growth theory. His students included several Nobel laureates, among them Eric Maskin, Roger Myerson and Michael Spence.
