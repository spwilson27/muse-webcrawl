Arthur Guinness (24 September 1725 – 23 January 1803) was an Irish brewer, entrepreneur and philanthropist, the founder of the Guinness brewery business and family dynasty. In 1759, at the age of 34, he signed a 9,000-year lease at £45 per annum for the unused St James's Gate Brewery in Dublin — the origin of one of the most famous beer brands in the world.

Early life

Guinness was born in Celbridge, County Kildare, where his father Richard was land steward to the Archbishop of Cashel, Arthur Price; the boy was named after his godfather, the archbishop, and was reportedly left £100 in Price's will, which he used to start brewing. By 1755 he was running a small brewery in Leixlip, County Kildare, before taking the lease on St James's Gate in Dublin on 31 December 1759.

The St James's Gate Brewery

At St James's Gate Guinness first brewed ale, but around 1770 he began brewing porter, the dark beer popular with London market porters, and by 1799 he had abandoned ale production entirely to concentrate on it. His porter — the ancestor of Guinness stout — was exported to England and beyond, and the brewery grew steadily. Guinness fought a long legal battle with Dublin Corporation over water charges, at one point in 1775 famously threatening the sheriff's men, but ultimately secured his water supply.

Family and legacy

In 1761 Guinness married Olivia Whitmore, a relative of his godfather; of their 21 children, 10 survived to adulthood, and his second son, Arthur Guinness II, succeeded him in the business. A committed philanthropist and supporter of Irish causes, he helped found the Hibernian Bible Society, supported Daniel O'Connell's Catholic emancipation campaign financially through his descendants' generation, and was known for relatively enlightened treatment of his workers. He died in Dublin in 1803 and is buried at Oughterard, County Kildare. His birthday, 24 September, is celebrated by the company as "Arthur's Day" (2009–2013).
