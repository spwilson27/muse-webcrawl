The Hijra (Arabic: الهِجْرَة, romanized: al-Hijra, lit. "the Migration") was the journey undertaken in 622 CE by the Islamic prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to the city of Yathrib, which he renamed Medina ("the City"). The Hijra marks the founding of the first Muslim community (ummah) governed as a city-state, and the year of the event was later chosen as the epoch of the Islamic (Hijri) calendar, whose years are designated AH (anno Hegirae).

Background

After Muhammad began preaching Islam in Mecca around 610 CE, he and his followers faced growing persecution from the Quraysh tribe who controlled the city. Following the deaths of his wife Khadija and his uncle and protector Abu Talib in 619 (the "Year of Sorrow"), Muhammad sought a new base. Envoys from Yathrib, an oasis town some 320 km north whose Arab tribes (Aws and Khazraj) had accepted Islam at the pledges of al-Aqaba, invited him to act as an arbitrator and leader.

The migration

Muslims emigrated in small groups during 622; Muhammad and Abu Bakr were among the last to leave, departing secretly after a plot to assassinate Muhammad was reported. According to tradition, Muhammad's cousin Ali lay in his bed as a decoy, and the two men hid in the cave of Thawr for three days before travelling on little-used routes, guided by Abdullah ibn Urayqit, reaching Quba and then Medina in September 622.

Consequences

In Medina Muhammad established the Charter of Medina, regulating relations among the Muslim emigrants (Muhajirun), the Medinan helpers (Ansar), and the Jewish tribes of the city. The first mosque was built at Quba and then the Prophet's Mosque in Medina. The Hijra transformed Islam from a persecuted religious movement into a political community; the second caliph, Umar, later fixed its year as year 1 of the Islamic calendar.

The term hijra also came to mean emigration for the sake of faith more generally, and the emigrants to Medina hold an honoured place in Islamic tradition.
