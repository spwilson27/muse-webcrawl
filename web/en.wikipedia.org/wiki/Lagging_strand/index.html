Lagging strand

A lagging strand is one of the two strands of DNA found at the replication fork, or junction, in the double helix; the other strand is called the leading strand. Because DNA polymerase can only synthesise DNA in the 5′ → 3′ direction, and the two template strands run antiparallel, only the leading strand can be copied continuously toward the fork. The lagging strand is instead synthesised discontinuously, in short fragments that grow away from the fork and are later joined together.

Mechanism

As helicase unwinds the parental duplex and the fork advances, primase repeatedly lays down short RNA primers on the lagging-strand template. DNA polymerase extends each primer away from the fork, producing fragments of roughly 100–200 nucleotides in eukaryotes (longer, 1,000–2,000 nucleotides, in bacteria) known as Okazaki fragments, after Reiji and Tsuneko Okazaki, who demonstrated discontinuous synthesis in 1968. When one fragment reaches the primer of the previous fragment, maturation begins: in eukaryotes the strand-displacement activity of polymerase δ, the flap endonuclease FEN1 and RNase H remove the RNA primer, DNA fills the gap, and DNA ligase seals the nick to create a continuous strand. In bacteria, DNA polymerase I removes the primer with its 5′ → 3′ exonuclease before ligase acts.

Coordination with the leading strand

To keep both strands growing at the same rate, the lagging-strand template forms a loop (the "trombone model"), allowing the lagging-strand polymerase, physically tethered to the leading-strand polymerase in the replisome, to release and re-prime repeatedly without leaving the fork. The lagging strand's repeated priming, fragment processing and exposure of single-stranded DNA make it a frequent site of replication stress and are relevant to telomere maintenance and genome instability.
