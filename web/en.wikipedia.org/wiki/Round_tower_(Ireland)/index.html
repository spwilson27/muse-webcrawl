Irish round towers (Irish: cloigtheach, meaning "bell house") are early medieval stone towers found mainly at monastic sites in Ireland, with related examples in Scotland and on the Isle of Man. Built between roughly the 10th and 12th centuries, they are slender, tapering circular towers, typically 18 to 40 metres tall, capped with a conical stone roof and entered through a raised doorway several metres above the ground.

Construction and features

The towers are built of coursed rubble masonry with dressed stone around openings. The doorway, usually round-headed or lintelled, is placed well above ground level and was reached by a ladder or wooden stair that could be drawn up. Inside, wooden floors divided the tower into storeys connected by ladders, with small windows lighting the upper levels; the topmost storey generally has several openings, traditionally interpreted as being for bells. The conical cap is frequently a later restoration.

Purpose

The Irish name cloigtheach indicates the towers' primary function as belfries from which a handbell was rung to summon monks to prayer. They also served as landmarks for pilgrims, treasuries for reliquaries, books, and valuables, and — with their raised doors — places of refuge during raids, including Viking attacks, although they offered limited protection against fire. Nineteenth-century antiquarians proposed fanciful origins (including pagan fire temples), theories definitively refuted by George Petrie's 1845 essay on the towers' ecclesiastical character.

Distribution and examples

Around 65 towers survive in varying states of repair. Notable complete examples include the towers at Clonmacnoise, Monasterboice, Devenish Island, Glendalough, and the 34-metre tower at Kilmacduagh, famous for leaning noticeably from vertical. The tower at St Andrews in Scotland and Peel Castle's tower on the Isle of Man show the style's spread through the Gaelic world.
