Frau Perchta

Frau Perchta, also known as Berchta or Bertha, is a figure of Alpine and Germanic folklore, the female counterpart or companion of the Perchten tradition. Her name likely derives from Old High German beraht, "bright" or "shining."

Folklore

Perchta is said to roam the countryside during the Twelve Nights of Christmas, especially on the eve of Epiphany (Perchta's Night, January 5–6). She knows whether children and servants have behaved well and completed their spinning during the year. The diligent she rewards with a small silver coin found in a shoe or pail; the lazy she punishes horribly — in the oldest tales, by slashing open their bellies, removing the innards, and stuffing them with straw and stones. She also punishes those who eat something other than the traditional meal of fish and dumplings on her feast day.

Appearance and the Perchten

Perchta appears in two forms: as a beautiful, radiant woman in white, and as a hideous old hag with a beaked or iron nose, one oversized (goose or swan) foot, and ragged clothes. She is accompanied by the Perchten, a retinue of beautiful and ugly spirits, and by the souls of unbaptized children. In modern Alpine custom, Perchtenläufe (Perchten runs) are held in winter, in which masked figures — the beautiful Schönperchten and the terrifying Schiachperchten — parade to drive out winter and evil spirits.

Origins

Jacob Grimm traced Perchta to a pre-Christian goddess of the southern Germanic peoples, cognate with Frau Holle of the north. Her cult was condemned by church authorities from the early medieval period onward, but the customs surrounding her persisted in Austria, Bavaria, Switzerland, and Slovenia.
