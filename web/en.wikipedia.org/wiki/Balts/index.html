The Balts are a group of Indo-European peoples speaking the Baltic languages, native to the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea in Northern Europe. The only surviving Baltic peoples are the Lithuanians and the Latvians; numerous other tribes — including the Old Prussians, Curonians, Semigallians, Selonians, Samogitians and Yotvingians — were assimilated or extinguished between the Middle Ages and the early modern period.

History

Baltic-speaking peoples settled the region between the Vistula and the upper Dnieper by the Bronze Age, trading amber — "the gold of the north" — with the Roman world; Tacitus described the amber-gathering Aestii on the Baltic shore. During the 12th and 13th centuries the German Northern Crusades conquered and Christianised the western Balts: the Old Prussians were subdued by the Teutonic Order and eventually Germanised, their language dying out around 1700. The Lithuanians, by contrast, formed a powerful state — the Grand Duchy of Lithuania — and were Europe's last pagans, accepting Christianity in 1387.

Languages and culture

The Baltic languages, Lithuanian especially, are famously conservative and of central importance to Indo-European linguistics for their preservation of archaic features such as pitch accent and a rich case system. Baltic culture is noted for its polyphonic folk songs (dainos), amber jewellery, and a mythology centred on the thunder god Perkūnas and the sun goddess Saulė.
