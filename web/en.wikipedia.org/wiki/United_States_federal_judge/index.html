United States federal judge

In the United States, the title federal judge generally refers to a judge appointed by the president of the United States and confirmed by the United States Senate pursuant to the Appointments Clause in Article II of the U.S. Constitution. Article III judges serve on the Supreme Court of the United States, the United States courts of appeals, and the United States district courts, as well as the Court of International Trade.

Article III judges

Article III judges hold their offices during good behaviour — effectively for life — and their compensation cannot be diminished while in office. These protections are designed to ensure judicial independence. There are nine justices of the Supreme Court, 179 authorized judgeships on the courts of appeals, and 677 authorized district court judgeships.

Appointment process

Candidates are typically evaluated by the White House with input from home-state senators, whose views historically mattered through the informal "blue slip" practice in the Senate Judiciary Committee. After nomination, the committee holds hearings and votes, followed by a vote of the full Senate. The American Bar Association's Standing Committee on the Federal Judiciary has traditionally rated nominees' qualifications, though its formal pre-nomination role has varied by administration.

Other federal judicial officers

Not all federal judicial officers are Article III judges. Bankruptcy judges and magistrate judges serve under Article I for fixed terms, as do judges of the Court of Federal Claims, the Tax Court, and the territorial courts. Administrative law judges preside over hearings within federal agencies.

Removal and senior status

Article III judges may be removed only through impeachment by the House of Representatives and conviction by the Senate; only a handful have been removed in U.S. history. Eligible judges may take "senior status," a form of semi-retirement in which they continue to hear a reduced caseload, creating a vacancy for a new appointee.
