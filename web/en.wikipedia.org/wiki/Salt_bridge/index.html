Salt bridge

A salt bridge, in electrochemistry, is a laboratory device used to connect the oxidation and reduction half-cells of a galvanic cell (voltaic cell), a type of electrochemical cell. It maintains electrical neutrality within the internal circuit. If no salt bridge were present, the solution in one half cell would accumulate negative charge and the other would accumulate positive charge as the reaction proceeded, quickly preventing further reaction and hence the production of electricity.

A galvanic cell with a salt bridge (the inverted U-tube) completing the circuit between the two half-cells.

Function

In a typical Daniell cell, zinc is oxidized at the anode, releasing Zn2+ ions into solution, while Cu2+ ions are reduced and plate out as copper at the cathode. Without a salt bridge, the anode compartment would build up excess positive charge and the cathode compartment excess negative charge, and the cell would stop working almost immediately. The salt bridge allows anions to flow toward the anode compartment and cations toward the cathode compartment, balancing charge while keeping the two solutions from mixing freely.

Types

Glass tube bridges

One type of salt bridge consists of a U-shaped glass tube filled with a relatively inert electrolyte, commonly potassium chloride or ammonium nitrate, whose ions have similar mobilities so as to minimize the liquid junction potential. The electrolyte is often gelled with agar-agar to prevent the liquid from flowing out when the tube is inverted. The conducting solution does not react with either half-cell solution.

Filter paper bridges

The second type consists of a strip of filter paper soaked in a suitable electrolyte such as potassium chloride. Conductivity depends on the concentration of the electrolyte, the porosity of the paper, and its roughness. Such bridges are cheap and disposable but have higher junction potentials and dry out quickly.

Requirements

The electrolyte chosen for a salt bridge should be chemically inert toward both cell solutions, and its cation and anion should have approximately equal ionic mobilities so that no significant junction potential develops. Potassium chloride is widely used because K+ and Cl− move at nearly the same speed; ammonium nitrate and potassium nitrate are alternatives when chloride would react with a cell component (for example, silver ions).
