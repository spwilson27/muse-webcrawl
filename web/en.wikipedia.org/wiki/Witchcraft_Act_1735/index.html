Witchcraft Act 1735
Citation: 9 Geo. II c. 5
Royal assent: 24 March 1736 (passed 1735 session)
Repealed earlier Witchcraft Acts
Repealed by: Fraudulent Mediums Act 1951

The Witchcraft Act 1735 (9 Geo. II c. 5) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that abolished the legal crime of witchcraft. Where earlier statutes — the English Witchcraft Act 1604 and the Scottish Act of 1563 — had punished practising witchcraft as a real offence punishable by death, the 1735 Act assumed the Enlightenment position that witchcraft was impossible, and instead made it an offence to pretend to exercise witchcraft, tell fortunes, or claim to find stolen goods by occult means, treating such claims as fraud punishable by imprisonment.

Provisions and prosecutions

The Act repealed the 1563 Scottish act and the 1604 English act, ending centuries of witch trials in Britain; the last executions had already occurred decades earlier (the last in England in 1682, and in Scotland in 1727). Under the new Act, prosecutions targeted fortune-tellers, mediums and "cunning folk" as fraudsters. The most famous late prosecution was that of the medium Helen Duncan, imprisoned for nine months in 1944 after allegedly revealing wartime naval secrets at a séance — the last person jailed under the Act.

Repeal and legacy

The Act was repealed by the Fraudulent Mediums Act 1951, secured largely through the efforts of Spiritualist MP Walter Monslow, which required proof of intent to defraud. Similar witchcraft legislation across the British Empire was modelled on the 1735 Act, and its fraud-based approach influenced jurisdictions in Canada, Australia and elsewhere, some retaining such laws into the 21st century.
