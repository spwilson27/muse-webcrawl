The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III is a black limestone Neo-Assyrian sculpture from Nimrud (ancient Kalhu) in northern Iraq. Commissioned by the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III (reigned 858–824 BC), it records his military campaigns and the tribute brought to him over a period of about thirty-one years. It is one of the most complete Assyrian obelisks ever discovered.

Description

The four-sided obelisk stands nearly two metres tall. Each face carries five registers of relief carving, twenty in total, depicting foreign rulers and envoys presenting tribute. Above and below the reliefs run long cuneiform inscriptions narrating the king's annals. The tribute scenes include gold, silver, tin, exotic animals, and other luxury goods from regions including Tyre, Sidon, and the land of Musri.

Jehu panel

The second register from the top is famous for depicting a king of Israel: an inscription identifies the tribute-bearer as "Jehu, son of Omri" (or his emissary), shown kneeling before Shalmaneser. It is the earliest known surviving visual depiction of an Israelite and one of the few contemporary extra-biblical references to a king of Israel.

Discovery

The obelisk was excavated by the British archaeologist Austen Henry Layard in 1846 at Nimrud and shipped to London, where it remains on display in the British Museum. Its depiction of exotic animals, including an elephant and an ape-like creature long misidentified, also attracted early attention.
