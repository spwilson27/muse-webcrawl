Syntax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, syntax is the study of how words and morphemes combine to form larger units such as phrases and sentences. Central concerns of syntax include word order, grammatical relations, hierarchical structure, agreement, and the ways different languages mark these structures. The term also refers to the rules themselves that govern sentence formation in a particular language.

History

The study of grammar has ancient roots, from Pāṇini's grammar of Sanskrit (c. 4th century BC) through the Greek and Arabic traditions. Modern syntax emerged in the 20th century with structuralism and, decisively, with Noam Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957), which launched generative grammar — the programme of describing the implicit rules by which speakers produce and understand an unbounded set of sentences from finite means.

Key concepts

  • Constituency: sentences are organised into hierarchical phrases (noun phrases, verb phrases, etc.), often represented as tree diagrams.
  • Grammatical relations: subjects, objects, and other functions borne by phrases in a clause.
  • Word order typology: languages are classified by the basic order of subject, verb, and object (SVO, SOV, VSO, and so on).
  • Movement and dependencies: displaced elements (as in questions and passives) and long-distance relations between words.

Theoretical approaches

Major frameworks include transformational/generative grammar and its descendants (Government and Binding theory, the Minimalist Program), dependency grammar, Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar (HPSG), Lexical Functional Grammar (LFG), and construction and cognitive grammars, which treat constructions rather than rules as primary.
