Atlas (architecture)

In European architectural sculpture, an atlas (also known as an atlant, or atlante, plural atlantes) is a support sculpted in the form of a man, which may take the place of a column, a pier or a pilaster. The Roman term for such a sculptural support is telamon.

The term atlantes is the Greek plural of the name Atlas—the Titan in Greek mythology condemned to hold up the heavens for eternity—whose representation as a straining figure bearing a great weight made him a natural model for architectural supports.

History

Atlantes originated in Greek architecture, where colossal male figures were used at temples such as the Olympieion at Agrigento in Sicily, whose massive telamons were among the largest figures of Greek antiquity. The form passed into Roman architecture and was revived enthusiastically in the Renaissance and Baroque periods, when atlantes appeared on palaces, churches and fountains across Europe, often in dramatic poses of effort.

Related forms

The female counterpart of the atlas is the caryatid, a sculpted female figure serving as a support, famous from the Erechtheion on the Acropolis of Athens. A related form is the herm, a square pillar topped with a carved head, often of Hermes.
