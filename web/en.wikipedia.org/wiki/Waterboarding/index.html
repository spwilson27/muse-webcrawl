Waterboarding

Waterboarding is a form of torture in which water is poured over a cloth covering the face of an immobilised captive, causing the sensation of drowning. The technique induces an immediate gag reflex and a sense of suffocation and has been used historically by regimes including the Spanish Inquisition, the Khmer Rouge, and Japanese forces in the Second World War.

Technique

The captive is restrained on their back with the head inclined downward, and water is poured intermittently over cloth or plastic wrap covering the mouth and nose. The subject experiences the feeling of drowning even though water does not necessarily enter the lungs. Medical and psychological harms can include lung damage, broken bones from struggling, and lasting psychological trauma.

Legal status and controversy

Waterboarding is regarded as torture under international law by organisations including the United Nations, and its use by the United States Central Intelligence Agency on terrorism suspects after the 11 September 2001 attacks became a major political and legal controversy. U.S. officials who authorised "enhanced interrogation techniques" argued the practice was lawful, while critics, courts-martial precedents, and later official reviews — including the 2014 Senate Intelligence Committee report — concluded it was torture and ineffective. President Barack Obama banned its use by executive order in 2009.

Historical use

Japanese soldiers were prosecuted after the Second World War for water tortures against Allied prisoners, and a U.S. soldier was court-martialled during the Vietnam War after a photograph of waterboarding appeared in print. The practice has also been demonstrated publicly by journalists and critics, notably Christopher Hitchens in 2008, who concluded after undergoing it that it was torture.
