École de Nancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The École de Nancy (School of Nancy) was a group of Art Nouveau artists, artisans, and manufacturers active in the French city of Nancy, in Lorraine, at the turn of the 20th century. Founded in 1901 as the Alliance provinciale des industries d'art, the movement made Nancy one of the great centres of the Art Nouveau style in Europe, rivalling Paris and Brussels.

Origins

The movement grew out of Nancy's strong traditions in glassmaking, furniture, and the decorative arts. Glassmaker and furniture designer Émile Gallé was its leading figure and first president; other key members included glassmakers Antonin Daum and the Daum brothers, furniture maker Louis Majorelle, architect Lucien Weissenburger, and designer Eugène Vallin.

Style

The Nancy school drew its inspiration from the flora of Lorraine — thistles, ginkgo, water lilies, dragonflies — translating natural forms into sinuous lines in glass, wood, iron, and stained glass. Gallé's motto, "Ma racine est au fond des bois" ("My root is at the bottom of the woods"), is carved on the door of his workshop. Works often carried symbolic or literary inscriptions, and the school pursued a union of art and industry, producing both unique pieces and high-quality serial manufacture.

Legacy

The movement declined after Gallé's death in 1904 and the outbreak of war in 1914, but Nancy remains dense with Art Nouveau architecture. The Musée de l'École de Nancy, opened in 1964 in the former home of collector Eugène Corbin, preserves the largest collection of its works.
