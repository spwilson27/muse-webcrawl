The Phaistos Disc (also spelled Phaestos Disc) is a disc of fired clay from the Minoan palace of Phaistos on the island of Crete, bearing a spiral inscription of stamped pictorial signs on both sides. Discovered in 1908 by the Italian archaeologist Luigi Pernier, it is one of the most famous undeciphered artefacts of the ancient world, and its purpose, meaning and even authenticity have been debated for over a century.

Description

The disc is about 15 centimetres (5.9 in) in diameter and a little over a centimetre thick. Its two faces, conventionally called A and B, carry 241 stamped impressions made from 45 distinct signs, arranged in a spiral running from the rim toward the centre, grouped by incised lines into "words". The signs depict recognisable objects — human figures, a fish, birds, a ship, tools and plants. Because the signs were impressed with individual punches or seals rather than written by hand, the disc is often described as an early example of movable-type printing.

Dating and context

The disc was found in a basement room of the palace complex at Phaistos, in a deposit that also contained a Linear A tablet. It is usually dated to the middle or late Minoan Bronze Age, roughly the second millennium BC, though estimates range widely. No other artefact bearing the same script has ever been found, which is the principal obstacle to decipherment: there is no second text for comparison.

Decipherment attempts

Dozens of decipherments have been proposed, reading the text as Greek, Luwian, Semitic, Egyptian, a hymn, a prayer, a calendar, a game board or a geometric theorem. None has won scholarly consensus. The brevity of the text and the absence of any bilingual or parallel inscription mean that proposed readings cannot be verified, and mainstream scholarship regards the disc as undeciphered.

A small number of scholars have questioned its authenticity, but the consensus remains that it is a genuine Bronze Age artefact.
