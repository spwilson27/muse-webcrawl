General of the Army (United States)

General of the Army (abbreviated GA) is a five-star general officer rank in the United States Army, equivalent to a field marshal in many other armies. It ranks immediately above general and is equivalent to the Navy's fleet admiral and the Air Force's General of the Air Force. The rank has been conferred only during periods of large-scale war and is senior to all other Army ranks except the special grade of General of the Armies.

History

A rank titled "General of the Army of the United States" was first created in 1866 for Ulysses S. Grant, and was subsequently held by William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan; it lapsed with Sheridan's death in 1888. The modern five-star grade was established by an Act of Congress on 14 December 1944, initially as a temporary wartime measure, to give senior American commanders rank parity with British field marshals during the Second World War. It was made permanent in 1946.

Holders

Five officers have held the modern rank:

  • George C. Marshall — promoted 16 December 1944
  • Douglas MacArthur — promoted 18 December 1944
  • Dwight D. Eisenhower — promoted 20 December 1944
  • Henry H. Arnold — promoted 21 December 1944 (later made the only General of the Air Force)
  • Omar Bradley — promoted 22 September 1950, the last appointee

Officers holding the rank were considered to remain on active duty for life, with full pay and allowances. In 1976 President Gerald Ford posthumously promoted George Washington to General of the Armies, with precedence over all other officers past or present, in connection with the United States Bicentennial.

Insignia

The insignia consists of five silver stars arranged in a pentagonal pattern, worn with the coat of arms of the United States above them on the shoulder boards of the dress uniform.
