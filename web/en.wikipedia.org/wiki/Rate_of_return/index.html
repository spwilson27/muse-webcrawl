In finance, return is a profit on an investment. It comprises any change in value of the investment, and/or cash flows (or securities, or other investments) which the investor receives from that investment over a specified time period, such as interest payments, coupons, cash dividends and stock dividends. It may be measured either in absolute terms (e.g., dollars) or as a percentage of the amount invested. The latter is also called the rate of return or holding period return. A loss instead of a profit is described as a negative return, assuming the amount invested is greater than zero.

Calculation

For a single period with no external cash flows, the rate of return is:

R = (Vfinal − Vinitial) / Vinitial

For example, if an investor buys a share for $100, receives $4 in dividends, and sells for $110, the rate of return is (110 − 100 + 4) / 100 = 14%. Returns over multiple periods cannot simply be added: they compound. The geometric mean of periodic returns gives the compound annual growth rate (CAGR), which measures the constant rate at which an investment would have grown over the period.

Adjustments and variants

Internal rate of return

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes the net present value of all cash flows from an investment equal to zero, and is widely used in capital budgeting and private equity. It generalises the rate of return to irregular cash flows and multiple periods.

Time-weighted and money-weighted returns

When investors add or withdraw money during the measurement period, the simple return is distorted by those flows. Time-weighted return breaks the period at each external cash flow and compounds the sub-period returns, eliminating the effect of flow timing; it is the standard for comparing fund managers. Money-weighted return (essentially the IRR of the investor's actual cash flows) reflects the investor's personal experience instead.

Risk, tax and inflation adjustments

Returns are commonly compared after adjusting for risk (as in the Sharpe ratio), after tax, and in real terms after inflation. A nominal return of 8% with 3% inflation corresponds to a real return of roughly 5% (more precisely 1.08/1.03 − 1 ≈ 4.85%).

Uses

Rates of return underpin nearly every investment decision: comparing assets and funds, setting portfolio policy, evaluating managers, appraising projects, and regulating disclosures such as standardised fund performance figures. Because past returns can be presented in flattering ways — by cherry-picking periods or ignoring fees, taxes and risk — regulators require standard calculation methods for advertised performance.
