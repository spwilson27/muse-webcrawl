Parental investment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parental investment, in evolutionary biology, is any parental expenditure (e.g. time, energy, resources) that benefits offspring. Parental investment may be performed by both males and females (biparental care), females alone (exclusive maternal care) or males alone (exclusive paternal care). Care can be provided at any stage of the offspring's life, from pre-natal (e.g. egg guarding and incubation in birds, and gestation in mammals) to post-natal (e.g. food provisioning and protection).

The concept was developed by Robert Trivers in a 1972 paper, building on the earlier work of A. J. Bateman and Ronald Fisher. Trivers defined parental investment as "any investment by the parent in an individual offspring that increases the offspring's chance of surviving (and hence reproductive success) at the cost of the parent's ability to invest in other offspring."

Theory

Parental investment theory predicts that the sex investing more in offspring will be more selective in mating, while the sex investing less will compete more intensely for access to mates — a driver of sexual selection. Because female mammals invest obligately through gestation and lactation, females are typically the more investing sex, and males compete for them; in species where males invest more, such as some seahorses and jacanas, the pattern can reverse.

In humans, parental investment includes prolonged provisioning, teaching and protection of children with a very long period of dependency. The theory has been applied to explain patterns of mate choice, mating competition, and differences in reproductive strategies, though its application to humans is debated and it is not the only framework used.

Costs and trade-offs

Investment in one offspring trades off against investment in others and against the parent's own survival and future reproduction. Parents may adjust investment according to offspring quality, environmental conditions, and the presence of helpers. Brood reduction, sex allocation and parent–offspring conflict (also theorised by Trivers) arise from these trade-offs.
