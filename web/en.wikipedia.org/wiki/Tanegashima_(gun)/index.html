Tanegashima (gun)


A Japanese tanegashima matchlock

The tanegashima (種子島), also known as the hinawajū (火縄銃, "matchlock gun"), was a type of matchlock-configured arquebus firearm introduced to Japan through the Portuguese Empire in 1543. It takes its name from Tanegashima Island, where a Chinese junk carrying Portuguese adventurers — including Fernão Mendes Pinto, according to tradition — was driven ashore by a storm.

History

The lord of the island, Tanegashima Tokitaka, purchased two matchlocks from the Portuguese and put his swordsmith to work copying the design. Within a few years the weapons had spread across Japan. The gunsmith Yaita Kinbei is credited with reproducing the crucial breech screw mechanism, reportedly with the help of a Portuguese smith who instructed Japanese craftsmen in return for Tokitaka's daughter's hand in marriage, according to legend.

The tanegashima transformed Japanese warfare during the Sengoku period. Oda Nobunaga made extensive use of ashigaru (foot soldier) arquebus units, most famously at the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, where rotating volleys of gunfire devastated the Takeda cavalry charges. By some estimates, Japan possessed more firearms per capita than any European country by the end of the sixteenth century.

Design

The Japanese matchlock adopted the European serpentine lock, in which a smoldering slow match is lowered into the priming pan. Japanese guns were produced in a wide range of calibres and lengths, from short pistols to large-calibre ōzutsu wall guns. Distinctive features included brass or iron barrels often signed by their makers, stocks of lacquered wood, and mechanisms adapted to work reliably in wet weather, including protective covers for the priming pan.

Decline and legacy

During the Edo period under the Tokugawa shogunate, firearms production was tightly controlled and concentrated at centres such as Sakai and Kunitomo. With Japan at peace, gun development stagnated, and the weapons remained in use largely unchanged until the modernisation of the Meiji era. Antique tanegashima are now prized collectibles and museum pieces, and the term itself remains the everyday Japanese word for the matchlock.
