The will to power (German: der Wille zur Macht) is a central concept in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche. Nietzsche defined it as the main driving force in human beings — not merely a will to survive or reproduce, but a striving for achievement, ambition, growth and the overcoming of resistance, which he extended into a general principle governing all living things and, in some passages, the whole of reality.

Development of the concept

The concept emerged against the background of Schopenhauer's metaphysics of the "will to live", which Nietzsche came to reject: self-preservation, he argued, is only an indirect consequence of a deeper drive to discharge strength, expand and impose form. The will to power is first presented prominently in Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–85), where Zarathustra declares that wherever he found the living, there he found will to power. In Beyond Good and Evil (1886) Nietzsche proposed it as a principle of psychology and even, tentatively, of physics — "the world viewed from inside... would be 'will to power' and nothing else" — and in On the Genealogy of Morality (1887) he used it to explain the history of moral values, including the reactive, vengeful form of power-seeking he called ressentiment.

The Will to Power (book)

Nietzsche planned a major work under the title but abandoned the project. After his collapse and death, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche and editors assembled selections from his notebooks (the Nachlass) and published them as The Will to Power (1901, expanded 1906). Later scholarship, notably the Colli–Montinari critical edition, showed this compilation to be an editorial construction that does not represent a book Nietzsche wrote; the notebooks themselves remain an important source for scholars.

Interpretations and influence

Scholars debate whether the will to power is a metaphysical doctrine, a psychological hypothesis, or a heuristic for interpreting drives and values. Martin Heidegger treated it as the completion of Western metaphysics; Gilles Deleuze gave it an influential reading as a principle of difference and interpretation; others emphasise self-overcoming and creativity rather than domination. The concept influenced Alfred Adler's individual psychology (the striving for superiority), depth psychology, and much 20th-century thought. Because of its later appropriation in Nazi-era propaganda — promoted by Nietzsche's sister and editors of the period — the phrase acquired political associations that most scholars regard as a distortion of Nietzsche's anti-nationalist, anti-antisemitic writings.
