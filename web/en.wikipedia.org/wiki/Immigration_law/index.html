Immigration law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Immigration law is the national and international body of statutes, regulations and case law governing immigration, asylum, nationality and the removal of aliens. It determines who may enter and remain in a state, in what status (visitor, worker, student, family member, refugee), and the rights and duties attaching to each status — an area where states claim wide sovereign discretion, qualified by human-rights and refugee-law obligations.

Framework

The modern regime grew with passport controls after the First World War and quota and points systems in settler states; the 1951 Refugee Convention (and its 1967 Protocol) imposes non-refoulement — no return to persecution — as its cornerstone, elaborated by regional instruments and human-rights courts. Labour migration is organised through visa categories, employer sponsorship and, regionally, free-movement regimes (the EU's being the deepest; the UK left it after Brexit). Enforcement topics include border control, detention, deportation, carrier sanctions and regularisation programmes; recurring policy debates address economic effects, demographic decline, integration, irregular migration and smuggling, and the politics of asylum backlogs.
