Children's poetry

Children's poetry is poetry written for, or appropriate for, children. It ranges from traditional nursery rhymes to comic verse, nonsense poetry, and serious lyrical work. Children's poetry is often characterised by strong rhythm and rhyme, vivid imagery, wordplay, and themes drawn from childhood experience, nature, animals, and fantasy.

History

Some of the earliest poetry encountered by English-speaking children is the body of nursery rhymes, many of which circulated orally for centuries before being collected in print in the 18th century. John Newbery's A Little Pretty Pocket-Book (1744) was among the first books published specifically for children.

The 19th century produced classics such as Edward Lear's nonsense verse in A Book of Nonsense (1846), Lewis Carroll's poems embedded in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), and Robert Louis Stevenson's A Child's Garden of Verses (1885). In the 20th century, poets such as A. A. Milne, Eleanor Farjeon, Walter de la Mare, and later Roald Dahl and Shel Silverstein brought humour and contemporary subjects to the field. Silverstein's Where the Sidewalk Ends (1974) became one of the best-selling poetry collections for children.

Forms and themes

Children's poetry includes limericks, haiku, shape poems, acrostics, narrative verse, and free verse. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century poets such as Michael Rosen, Roger McGough, Carol Ann Duffy, Jacqueline Woodson, and Kwame Alexander have expanded the range of subjects to include family life, school, identity, and social issues.
