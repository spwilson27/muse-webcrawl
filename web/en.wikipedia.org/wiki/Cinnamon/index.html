Cinnamon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Cinnamon sticks and ground cinnamon with a small spoon]

Cinnamon is a spice obtained from the inner bark of trees of the genus Cinnamomum. The bark is stripped, the outer layer removed, and the inner bark curls into rolls ("quills") as it dries. "True cinnamon" (Cinnamomum verum, Ceylon cinnamon) comes mainly from Sri Lanka and is mild and delicate; most of the world's cheaper cinnamon is cassia (C. cassia and relatives) from China, Vietnam and Indonesia, stronger and spicier. Cinnamon is used in sweet and savoury cooking nearly everywhere, and was once among the most valuable commodities in world trade.

History

Cinnamon was traded into Egypt by around 2000 BCE and was prized in the ancient world for embalming, perfume and ritual; its sources were guarded trade secrets, which is why fanciful stories (Herodotus repeats one about giant birds) circulated. Arab traders carried it along the spice routes; European powers fought over Ceylon's cinnamon in the 16th–18th centuries, with Portuguese, Dutch and British control following one another. Today it is an everyday kitchen spice rather than a luxury.

Production

Trees are coppiced: shoots are cut, the bark loosened in two layers, and the inner bark scraped and rolled by hand into quills which are dried and graded. Ground cinnamon loses aroma quickly. Cassia contains more coumarin, a compound regulators advise consuming in moderation, than Ceylon cinnamon.

Uses

Cinnamon flavours desserts and baking (cinnamon rolls, apple pie, rice pudding), drinks (mulled wine, chai, hot chocolate), and savoury dishes from Moroccan tagines and Indian curries to Mexican mole and Greek moussaka's béchamel. Cinnamon oil and extract are used in confectionery and liqueurs.
