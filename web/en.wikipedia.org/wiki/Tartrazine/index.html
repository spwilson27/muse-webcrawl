Tartrazine

Tartrazine, a synthetic lemon-yellow azo dye used as a food colour

Tartrazine is a synthetic lemon-yellow azo dye used as a food colouring, designated E102 in the European Union and FD&C Yellow 5 in the United States. It is a water-soluble powder, chemically a pyrazolone dye (trisodium 5-hydroxy-1-(4-sulfonatophenyl)-4-[(E)-(4-sulfonatophenyl)diazenyl]-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxylate), and is among the most widely used yellow colourants in foods, beverages, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals.

Uses

Tartrazine colours soft drinks, confectionery, jams, cereals, snack foods, mustards, soups, and many other products, often in combination with other dyes to produce green or orange shades. It is also used to colour medicines and cosmetics, and was historically used as a wool and silk dye. It is cheap, stable to light, heat and acid, which accounts for its popularity despite periodic controversy.

Health concerns and regulation

A small minority of people — estimates commonly range from about 0.01% to 0.1% of the population, with higher rates among aspirin-sensitive individuals — experience intolerance reactions to tartrazine, such as urticaria (hives). In the United States, foods and drugs containing it must declare it by name on the label, a requirement dating from concerns about such sensitivities.

A 2007 study commissioned by the UK Food Standards Agency (the "Southampton study") associated mixtures of certain food colours, including tartrazine, with sodium benzoate, with increased hyperactivity in some children. As a result, in the European Union, foods containing tartrazine must carry the warning that it "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children". The European Food Safety Authority has maintained an acceptable daily intake for tartrazine, having reviewed the evidence. Some countries, such as Norway and Austria, previously restricted its use in foods.
