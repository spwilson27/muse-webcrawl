Counterfactual

[Image placeholder: branching possible worlds]

A counterfactual is a statement, conditional, or line of reasoning about what would have been the case if something contrary to fact had occurred: "If Oswald had not shot Kennedy, Kennedy would not have died that day." Counterfactuals are studied in philosophy, linguistics, psychology, economics, statistics and history, and they are central to accounts of causation, because to say that one event caused another seems to imply that, had the cause not occurred, the effect would not have occurred either.

Philosophy

The most influential philosophical analysis is David Lewis's possible-worlds semantics: a counterfactual "if A had been the case, C would have been the case" is true when, in the possible worlds most similar to the actual world in which A holds, C also holds. Robert Stalnaker developed a related account. Counterfactual reasoning has been applied to theories of causation (Lewis's counterfactual theory), free will, dispositions, and the semantics of conditionals more generally.

Statistics and causal inference

In statistics, the potential outcomes framework associated with Jerzy Neyman and Donald Rubin defines causal effects through counterfactual outcomes — the outcome a unit would have shown under treatment compared with the outcome it would have shown under control — only one of which can ever be observed. Judea Pearl's structural causal models and "do-calculus" provide a formal logic for counterfactual queries and place them at the highest level of his "ladder of causation".

History

Counterfactual history (or virtual history) explores alternative timelines: what might have happened had pivotal events gone otherwise. Academic historians are divided on its value; some treat it as a tool for assessing the weight of causes, others as mere speculation.
