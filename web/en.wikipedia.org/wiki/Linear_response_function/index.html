Linear response function

A linear response function describes the input–output relationship of a signal transducer, such as a radio turning electromagnetic waves into music or a neuron turning a stimulus into a response. In many areas of physics—particularly statistical mechanics and condensed matter—the linear response of a system to a weak external perturbation is a central quantity, tied to equilibrium fluctuations by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

In the linear regime the output of a system is proportional to its input.

Definition

Consider a system whose observable output x(t) responds to an applied input (driving force) h(t'). If the response is linear and time-translation invariant, the output is a convolution of the input with a response function χ(t − t'), often called a susceptibility:

x(t) = ∫−∞t χ(t − t′) h(t′) dt′.

The upper limit enforces causality: the system cannot respond before the perturbation acts. In the frequency domain, the response function is the Fourier transform of the time-domain susceptibility, and its real and imaginary parts are connected by the Kramers–Kronig relations.

Examples

Examples of linear response functions include the electric susceptibility relating polarization to an applied electric field, the magnetic susceptibility, the complex impedance of a circuit, and Green's functions in quantum field theory and many-body physics.

Kubo formula

The Kubo formula, derived by Ryogo Kubo in 1957, gives a general microscopic expression for a linear response function in terms of equilibrium correlation functions of operators in the unperturbed system. Schematically, the response of an observable A to a perturbation coupling to B is proportional to the retarded commutator ⟨[A(t), B(0)]⟩ evaluated in thermal equilibrium. The imaginary part of the susceptibility describes dissipation (absorption of energy), while the fluctuation–dissipation theorem relates it to spontaneous fluctuations.
