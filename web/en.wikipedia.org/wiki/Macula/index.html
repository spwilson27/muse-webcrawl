Macula

The macula, or macula lutea ("yellow spot" in Latin), is a pigmented oval area near the centre of the retina of the human eye, about 5.5 mm in diameter. It is specialised for high-acuity central vision: at its centre lies the fovea centralis, a small pit densely packed with cone photoreceptors that provides the sharpest detailed sight used in reading, driving and recognising faces.

Structure

The macula is divided into concentric zones: the umbo at the very centre, surrounded by the foveola, the fovea centralis (about 1.5 mm across), and the parafoveal and perifoveal belts. Its yellow colour comes from the carotenoid pigments lutein and zeaxanthin, which filter blue light and act as antioxidants. Unlike the rest of the retina, the central fovea (the foveal avascular zone) contains no blood vessels, minimising light scatter.

Function

Cones in the fovea are wired almost one-to-one to ganglion cells, giving the brain a high-resolution sample of the central visual field. The macula covers only a small fraction of the retina's area but is served by a disproportionately large share of the visual cortex — roughly half of it is devoted to processing macular input.

Clinical significance

The macula is vulnerable to age-related macular degeneration (AMD), the leading cause of severe vision loss in older adults in developed countries, in which drusen deposits and, in the "wet" form, abnormal blood-vessel growth damage central vision. Other disorders include macular holes, epiretinal membranes ("macular pucker") and diabetic macular oedema. Because peripheral vision is preserved in most macular disease, patients rarely become completely blind but lose the ability to read and recognise faces.
