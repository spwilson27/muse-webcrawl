History of the Philippines (1565–1898)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the Philippines from 1565 to 1898 covers the Spanish colonial period, from the arrival of Miguel López de Legazpi and the founding of the first lasting Spanish settlements to the Philippine Revolution and the transfer of sovereignty to the United States at the end of the Spanish–American War.

Conquest and early colonization

Ferdinand Magellan's expedition reached the islands in 1521, but permanent colonization began in 1565 when Legazpi established a settlement on Cebu and claimed the archipelago for Spain. Manila was founded in 1571 and became the capital. The colony was administered as part of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, and from 1565 to 1815 the Manila galleon trade linked Asia and the Americas, exchanging Chinese silk and porcelain for Mexican silver.

Colonial society

Spanish rule spread Catholicism, which became the dominant religion outside the Muslim south (the Moro regions of Mindanao and Sulu resisted full incorporation for centuries). The encomienda and later hacienda systems organized labor and land; the friar orders — Augustinians, Franciscans, Dominicans, and Jesuits — wielded great local power. Chinese merchants and Chinese mestizos became central to the economy, while revolts such as those of Tamblot and Dagohoy punctuated the period.

Reform and revolution

The opening of Philippine ports to world trade in the 19th century spurred an export economy (sugar, abacá, tobacco) and the rise of an educated ilustrado class. The Propaganda Movement, including José Rizal, Graciano López Jaena, and Marcelo H. del Pilar, demanded reforms; Rizal's execution in 1896 galvanized the secret Katipunan society of Andrés Bonifacio, which launched the Philippine Revolution. Emilio Aguinaldo proclaimed Philippine independence on 12 June 1898, but the Treaty of Paris (1898) ceded the islands to the United States, setting the stage for the Philippine–American War.
