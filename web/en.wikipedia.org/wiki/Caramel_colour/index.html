Caramel colour

E numberE150 (classes E150a–d)
TypeFood colouring
Made fromHeat-treated carbohydrates
AppearanceDark brown liquid or solid

Caramel colour or caramel coloring is a water-soluble food coloring. It is made by heat treatment of carbohydrates (sugars), in general in the presence of acids, alkalis, or salts, in a process called caramelization. It is more fully oxidized than caramel candy, and has an odour of burnt sugar and a somewhat bitter taste. Its colour ranges from pale yellow to amber to dark brown.

Production and classes

Commercial caramel colour is produced by heating sugars such as glucose, sucrose, or invert sugar under controlled conditions with reactant chemicals that determine its class:

  • Class I (E150a, plain caramel) — no ammonium or sulfite compounds used; used in spirits such as whisky.
  • Class II (E150b, caustic sulfite caramel) — made with sulfite compounds.
  • Class III (E150c, ammonia caramel) — made with ammonium compounds; used in beer and sauces.
  • Class IV (E150d, sulfite ammonia caramel) — made with both sulfite and ammonium compounds; the most widely used class, standard in soft drinks such as cola.

Uses

Caramel colour is the most widely used food colouring in the world by weight. It colours colas and other soft drinks, beer, brown bread, balsamic vinegar, soy sauce, gravy browning, confectionery, and pet food, and is also used in cosmetics. Unlike caramel candy, commercial caramel colour contributes colour with minimal sweetness or flavour at typical use levels.

Safety

Regulators including the FDA, EFSA, and JECFA have approved caramel colour classes with acceptable daily intakes. Attention has focused on 4-methylimidazole (4-MEI), a trace by-product of the ammonia classes; California's Proposition 65 listing of 4-MEI prompted major cola manufacturers in 2012 to reformulate production processes to reduce its levels.
