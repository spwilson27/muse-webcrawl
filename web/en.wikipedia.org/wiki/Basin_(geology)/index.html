Basin (geology)

Structural basin
DisciplineStructural geology, sedimentology
ExamplesParis Basin; Illinois Basin; Congo Basin

A basin in geology is a large low-lying region, often below sea level, or a large-scale structural formation of rock strata formed by tectonic warping of previously flat-lying strata. Structural basins are geological depressions — the inverse of domes — and are frequently also sedimentary basins in which large volumes of sediment accumulate over millions of years, preserving thick sequences of layered rock.

Formation

Basins form by several mechanisms, including crustal extension and rifting, flexure of the lithosphere under the load of mountain belts (foreland basins), thermal subsidence after rifting, and broad downwarping of cratons. As the basin subsides, sediments deposited within it become thicker toward the centre and may be gently folded so that strata dip inward on all sides — a pattern easily seen on geological maps as concentric outcrops, with the youngest rocks at the centre.

Economic significance

Sedimentary basins host most of the world's oil and natural gas fields, because they combine organic-rich source rocks, porous reservoir rocks and seals. They also commonly contain coal seams and major aquifers of groundwater.
