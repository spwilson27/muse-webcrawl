Ecosystem

[Image: a pond ecosystem at dawn — reeds, dragonflies and a heron, with mist over the water]

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms (plants, animals and microbes) in conjunction with the non-living components of their environment — air, water, soil, sunlight — interacting as a system. Ecosystems range from a pond or a rotting log to a rainforest or the whole biosphere, and are studied in Ecology through the flows of energy and the cycling of nutrients that link their parts.

The term was coined by the British ecologist Arthur Tansley in 1935, to emphasise that organisms cannot be separated from the physical environment with which they exchange materials and energy.

Functioning

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight, is fixed by producers (plants, algae, photosynthetic bacteria) into food, and passes along food chains to consumers and decomposers, being lost as heat at each step — which is why food chains are short and top predators rare. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and water cycle between organisms and the physical world, assisted by microbes at every stage.

Ecosystems change through succession after disturbance — fire, flood, abandonment of farmland — and can flip between alternative states (clear to turbid lakes, forest to savanna) when pushed past thresholds.

Services and threats

Humans depend on ecosystem services: pollination, water purification, flood control, fisheries, timber, carbon storage and climate regulation, valued by economists in the trillions of dollars annually. Habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change are degrading ecosystems worldwide, motivating conservation and ecological restoration.
