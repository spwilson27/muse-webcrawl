The Pinaceae, or pine family, is the largest family of conifers, comprising about 225–250 species in 11 genera, including pines (Pinus), spruces (Picea), firs (Abies), larches (Larix), hemlocks (Tsuga), Douglas-firs (Pseudotsuga) and true cedars (Cedrus). Members dominate vast areas of the Northern Hemisphere, from the boreal taiga to mountain forests and Mediterranean scrub.

Characteristics

Almost all are evergreen trees (larches are the notable deciduous exception) with needle-like leaves, resin canals, and separate male and female cones on the same tree. Female cones are woody, made of overlapping scales each bearing two winged seeds. Many species are adapted to fire, some holding serotinous cones that open only after burning.

Ecology and importance

Pinaceae form the backbone of the world's boreal forests and are of enormous commercial value as sources of softwood timber, pulp, resin, turpentine and Christmas trees. Their forests store large quantities of carbon and support specialised wildlife. The family has a fossil record extending to the early Cretaceous, and molecular studies place its origin earlier still.
