Field: Classical mechanics

Conservation of angular momentum

The conservation of angular momentum is a fundamental law of physics stating that the total angular momentum of a closed system remains constant unless acted upon by an external torque. Angular momentum is the product of a body's rotational inertia and its angular velocity.

Examples

A figure skater spins faster when pulling in her arms, because reducing the moment of inertia must be compensated by an increase in angular speed to keep angular momentum constant. The same law explains why planets move faster near perihelion (Kepler's second law), why gyroscopes resist tilting, and how collapsing stars spin up to form rapidly rotating neutron stars.

Theoretical basis

By Noether's theorem, conservation of angular momentum follows from the rotational symmetry of the laws of physics — their invariance under rotations in space. Together with conservation of energy and linear momentum, it forms a cornerstone of mechanics, and its quantum-mechanical analogue governs atomic structure and selection rules.
