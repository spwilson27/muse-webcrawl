Neurophilosophy

Neurophilosophy or the philosophy of neuroscience is the interdisciplinary study of neuroscience and philosophy that explores the relevance of neuroscientific studies to the arguments traditionally categorized as philosophy of mind. The term was popularized by Patricia Churchland's 1986 book Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain.

Neurophilosophy brings neuroscientific evidence to bear on questions in the philosophy of mind.

Approaches

Neurophilosophy is often associated with eliminative materialism, the view held by Patricia and Paul Churchland that many folk-psychological categories such as "belief" and "desire" may be replaced by neuroscientific concepts as neuroscience matures, just as earlier scientific concepts replaced older folk theories. Other philosophers of neuroscience take a more integrative approach, examining concepts, methods, and explanatory practices within neuroscience itself, including debates about reductionism, the interpretation of neuroimaging, and the neural basis of consciousness, free will, personal identity, and moral judgment.

Related fields

Neurophilosophy overlaps with neuroethics, which addresses the ethical implications of neuroscience, and with the broader philosophy of science and cognitive science. Critics caution against "neuroessentialism" — drawing philosophical conclusions from neuroscientific data too directly.
