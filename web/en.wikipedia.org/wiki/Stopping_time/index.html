Stopping time

In probability theory, in particular in the study of stochastic processes, a stopping time (also known as a Markov time) is a specific type of "random time"—a random variable whose value is interpreted as the time at which a given stochastic process exhibits a certain behaviour of interest. The defining condition is that the decision whether or not to stop at time t can be made using only information available up to time t: formally, for a filtered probability space, τ is a stopping time if {τ ≤ t} belongs to the σ-algebra Ft for every t.

Examples

The classic example is a hitting time: the first time a random walk or Brownian motion reaches a specified level. Constant times are trivially stopping times, as is the minimum of two stopping times. By contrast, "the time at which the process attains its maximum over the whole interval" is not a stopping time, because deciding it requires knowledge of the future.

Theory

Stopping times underpin the optional stopping theorem, which states conditions under which a fair game (a martingale) stopped at a stopping time retains its expected value—showing that no betting strategy based on stopping can beat a fair game. They are also central to optimal stopping problems, such as the secretary problem, and to the strong Markov property.
