Wartburg was a marque of automobiles manufactured by Automobilwerk Eisenach (AWE) in Eisenach, East Germany, from 1956 to 1991. Named after Wartburg Castle overlooking the town, Wartburg cars were the more upmarket counterpart to the smaller Trabant, and were widely exported within the Eastern Bloc and in smaller numbers to Western markets.

History

The Eisenach works had built cars since 1898 and, as part of BMW before the Second World War, produced the BMW 321 and 340; under Soviet occupation the plant passed to the state-owned AWE. In 1956 the factory relaunched the Wartburg name — first used on an 1898 licence-built Decauville — for the Wartburg 311, a handsome sedan derived from the IFA F9/DKW design with a three-cylinder two-stroke engine.

Models

The 311 (1956–1965) was built in numerous body styles, including limousine, estate (Kombi), coupé, cabriolet and pickup. It was succeeded by the angular Wartburg 353 (1966–1988), also sold in some markets as the Wartburg Knight, with a 993 cc three-cylinder two-stroke engine driving the front wheels and a freewheel device; production exceeded 1.2 million. The final model, the Wartburg 1.3 (1988–1991), replaced the two-stroke with a Volkswagen-designed 1.3-litre four-stroke engine. After German reunification, production ended on 10 April 1991, as the car could not compete with Western models; the Eisenach plant was taken over by Opel.

Character and reputation

Two-stroke Wartburgs were noted for their characteristic exhaust note and blue smoke, simple construction, roomy interior and durability, as well as for the very long waiting lists East German buyers faced. Surviving examples are now collected as classics of East German industrial design.
