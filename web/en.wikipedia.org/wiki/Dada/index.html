Dada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dada or Dadaism was an art movement of the European avant-garde in the early 20th century, with early centres in Zürich (at the Cabaret Voltaire, founded 1916) and — independently — in New York, Berlin, Cologne, Hanover and Paris. Born of disgust at the social, political and cultural values that Dadaists believed had led to the First World War, Dada rejected reason and logic, embracing nonsense, irrationality and chance. Its products included performance, poetry (sound poems, cut-up texts), collage, photomontage, assemblage and "readymades".

Practice and legacy

Key figures included Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Tristan Tzara, Hans (Jean) Arp, Marcel Duchamp, Man Ray, Hannah Höch, Raoul Hausmann, Kurt Schwitters and Max Ernst. Duchamp's Fountain (1917) — a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt" and submitted to an exhibition — became the movement's most famous (and then, infamous) gesture and one of the most influential artworks of the century. Berlin Dada was sharply political, using photomontage against militarism and the Weimar establishment. By the mid-1920s Dada had burned itself out, but it seeded Surrealism (many Dadaists, Ernst and Arp among them, joined André Breton's new movement) and its anti-art provocations echo through conceptual art, pop art, punk and performance art. Hannah Höch's collages and Duchamp's readymades anticipate much of what modern art would become; the movement's collage sensibility also influenced later artists discussed under Surrealism, including the circle of René Magritte.
