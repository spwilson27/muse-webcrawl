Seckel syndrome

Characteristic features include severe microcephaly and a "bird-headed" facial profile.

Seckel syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterised by severe intrauterine and postnatal growth retardation, very short stature, marked microcephaly with intellectual disability, and distinctive facial features including a large eyes, a beak-like protruding nose and a receding jaw. It is one of the microcephalic primordial dwarfisms and was described by Helmut Paul George Seckel in 1960.

Specialty: Medical genetics

Symptoms: Primordial dwarfism, microcephaly, intellectual disability, distinctive facial features

Usual onset: Prenatal

Causes: Autosomal recessive mutations in DNA-damage-response and centrosomal genes (e.g. ATR, RBBP8, CENPJ, CEP152)

Frequency: Very rare (fewer than a few hundred cases reported)

Signs and symptoms

Babies with Seckel syndrome are born very small and grow slowly throughout life, reaching adult heights well below average. Head circumference is disproportionately small. Facial features include a sloping forehead, large eyes, a prominent beak-like nose and micrognathia, giving the historical (now avoided) description "bird-headed dwarfism". Intellectual disability is typical, and skeletal abnormalities such as hip dislocation and scoliosis may occur.

Genetics

The syndrome is genetically heterogeneous and inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. The first identified gene was ATR, which encodes a kinase central to the cellular response to DNA replication stress; other types involve genes controlling centrosome function and the DNA damage response, including RBBP8 (CtIP), CENPJ, CEP152, CEP63, NIN and DNA2. This links Seckel syndrome biologically to other microcephaly and genome-instability disorders.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on clinical features and confirmatory genetic testing. There is no cure; care is supportive, addressing feeding, development, orthopaedic problems and associated conditions such as haematological abnormalities reported in some patients.
