The three-tier system of alcohol distribution is the system for distributing alcoholic beverages set up in the United States after the repeal of Prohibition in 1933. Under the system, producers (breweries, wineries, distilleries and importers) may sell their products only to licensed wholesale distributors, who in turn sell only to licensed retailers; retailers are the only businesses permitted to sell to consumers. With some exceptions for direct-to-consumer shipping and brewpubs, no tier is allowed to operate in another tier — the "tied-house" prohibitions that the system was designed to enforce.

History

Before Prohibition, producers commonly owned saloons outright ("tied houses"), and the aggressive sales practices that resulted were blamed for excessive consumption and political corruption. When the Twenty-first Amendment repealed national Prohibition in 1933, it gave the states broad power to regulate alcohol. Most states adopted the three-tier model recommended by the influential study Toward Liquor Control (1933), intended to prevent vertical integration, promote orderly markets, facilitate tax collection, and discourage the marketing abuses of the pre-Prohibition era. Seventeen or so "control" jurisdictions instead run state monopolies over wholesaling (and in some cases retail); the rest are "licence" states.

Operation and exceptions

In practice the system is riddled with state-specific exceptions: many states let wineries ship directly to consumers (a practice protected for interstate commerce in part by the Supreme Court's decision in Granholm v. Heald, 2005), permit breweries to sell on-premises in taprooms, or allow limited self-distribution for small producers. Federal law, administered by the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB), still requires basic permits and prohibits producers from holding interests in retail licences.

Criticism and debate

Critics — especially craft brewers, small winemakers and direct-to-consumer advocates — argue that the system protects large distributors and producers, raises prices, limits consumer choice, and no longer fits e-commerce. Defenders, notably distributor trade groups, contend that it ensures accountability, product safety, reliable tax collection and a level playing field for small retailers. The system's treatment of interstate commerce remains an active area of litigation, including the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Wine and Spirits Retailers Assn v. Thomas (2019), which struck down a state residency requirement for retail licences.
