Laugh track

A laugh track (or laughing track) is a separate soundtrack for a recorded comedy show containing the sound of audience laughter. In some productions, the laughter is a live audience response instead; in the United States, where it is most commonly used, the term usually implies that the laughter is artificial ("canned laughter"), and was often produced using a machine invented for the purpose. The process of inserting artificial or supplemented laughter is called sweetening.

History

The practice began in radio, where engineers sweetened comedy programmes whose studio audiences reacted inconsistently, then carried into early television. Sound engineer Charley Douglass built the "Laff Box" in the early 1950s — a typewriter-like instrument loaded with tape loops of recorded laughter, titters, and applause that he played live during post-production. Douglass's company Northridge Electronics dominated the craft for decades, and his secret techniques were closely guarded. Shows such as The Hank McCune Show pioneered laugh tracks on series filmed without audiences, while filmed sitcoms like I Love Lucy used genuine studio-audience reactions — an approach that became the prestige alternative.

Use and decline

Laugh tracks were standard on American sitcoms and even animated series (such as those of Hanna-Barbera) through the 1960s and 1970s, sometimes imposed by networks over creators' objections — CBS famously added one to the war comedy-drama M*A*S*H in its domestic broadcasts. From the 2000s onward, single-camera comedies shot without audiences and without laugh tracks, such as The Office and 30 Rock, became dominant, and the laugh track came to be widely viewed as dated, though multi-camera sitcoms recorded before live audiences continue to use sweetening. Studies in psychology suggest laughter tracks increase perceived funniness, particularly for weak jokes, an effect sometimes cited in their defence.

Criticism

Critics have long argued that canned laughter manipulates viewers, insults their intelligence, or papers over weak writing. The device is frequently listed among television's most notorious inventions, and scripted "no laugh track" presentation has become a selling point for comedy aimed at younger audiences.
