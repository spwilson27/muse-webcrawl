Wars of Liberation

The Wars of Liberation (German: Befreiungskriege) were the campaigns of 1813–1814 in which Prussia, Russia, Austria, Sweden and other states fought to free the German states from French domination during the Napoleonic Wars. They culminated in Napoleon's defeat at Leipzig and his first abdication.

Background

After Napoleon's disastrous Russian campaign of 1812, Prussia broke with France under the Convention of Tauroggen and the Treaty of Kalisch, and declared war in March 1813. Popular enthusiasm — volunteers, the Landwehr militia, and patriotic poetry — gave the conflict its "liberation" character, though the monarchs' aims remained largely conservative.

Course

After initial setbacks at Lützen and Bautzen, an armistice and the entry of Austria into the coalition led to the Battle of Leipzig (16–19 October 1813), the largest battle in Europe before the First World War, in which Napoleon's army was decisively defeated. The allies invaded France in early 1814, entered Paris in March, and Napoleon abdicated in April.

Legacy

The wars became a founding myth of German nationalism, commemorated in monuments such as the Völkerschlachtdenkmal at Leipzig, though the Congress of Vienna restored much of the old order. The term is also used for other independence struggles, including the German War of Liberation against Napoleon and, in a different context, Bangladesh's Liberation War of 1971.
