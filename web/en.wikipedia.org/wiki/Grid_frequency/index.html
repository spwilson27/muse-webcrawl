Utility frequency

The utility frequency, also called power line frequency or mains frequency, is the nominal frequency of the oscillations of alternating current (AC) in a wide area synchronous transmission grid. Most of the world uses 50 Hz, while the Americas and parts of Asia use 60 Hz. The frequency is a real-time indicator of the balance between generation and load: frequency rises when generation exceeds demand and falls when demand exceeds generation.

Frequency control

Grid operators continuously regulate generator output to keep frequency close to its nominal value. Small deviations are corrected by primary frequency response from generators, secondary control (automatic generation control), and, in some systems, time error correction to keep the long-term average exact — historically important for synchronous electric clocks. Large disturbances, such as the sudden loss of a power station, can cause frequency to fall rapidly, triggering under-frequency load shedding if uncorrected.

50 Hz versus 60 Hz

The two standards emerged in the late 19th century: AEG in Germany settled on 50 Hz, while Westinghouse in the United States adopted 60 Hz. Japan famously uses both — eastern Japan (including Tokyo) uses 50 Hz and western Japan (including Osaka) uses 60 Hz — a legacy of early equipment purchases from different suppliers. Aircraft and ships often use 400 Hz systems to reduce the weight of transformers and motors.

Stability and measurement

Maintaining frequency stability becomes more challenging as grids integrate variable renewable generation and lose the inertia of large rotating generators. Grid frequency can be measured anywhere on the network with an ordinary outlet and provides a shared signal of grid health; researchers have used tiny frequency signatures recorded in audio and video recordings for forensic verification.
