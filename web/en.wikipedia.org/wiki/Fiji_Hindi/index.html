Fiji Hindi (Devanagari: फ़िजी हिन्दी) is an Indo-Aryan language spoken by Indo-Fijians, the descendants of indentured labourers (girmitiyas) brought by the British from northern India to Fiji between 1879 and 1916. It is also spoken by Fiji Indian diaspora communities in Australia, New Zealand, the United States and Canada.

History

The language developed as a plantation koine from the dialects the labourers brought—chiefly Awadhi and Bhojpuri, with elements of other Hindi varieties and Standard Hindi—levelled through daily contact on sugarcane plantations. It also absorbed loanwords from iTaukei (Fijian) and English. Because many speakers were Muslim or from diverse regions, Fiji Hindi became the community's common tongue distinct from both Standard Hindi and Fijian.

Features

Fiji Hindi differs from Standard Hindi in pronunciation (e.g., merger of some sibilants), vocabulary (borrowing extensively from Fijian and English), and grammar, including simplified verb inflections and distinctive pronouns. Religious and formal contexts often use more standardised registers influenced by Urdu and Standard Hindi.

Status and media

Fiji's 2013 Constitution names English, iTaukei and "Hindustani" (under which Fiji Hindi is understood) as official languages, with parliamentary interpretation provided. Fiji Hindi is widely used in radio broadcasting, film and everyday commerce, though literacy is commonly in English, and the language competes with Standard Hindi taught in schools and temples.
