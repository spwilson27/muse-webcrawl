Sankin-kōtai

Sankin-kōtai (Japanese: 参覲交代, "alternate attendance") was a system of the Tokugawa shogunate in Edo-period Japan requiring daimyo (feudal lords) to spend alternate years in the capital, Edo (now Tokyo), while their families remained there permanently as hostages.

Operation

Formalised under Tokugawa Iemitsu in the 1630s and 1640s, the system obliged each daimyo to travel to Edo with a large retinue every other year and reside there, returning to his domain in the alternate year. His wife and heir stayed in Edo year-round, effectively as hostages guaranteeing loyalty.

Purpose and effects

The system drained daimyo finances through the cost of processions and dual residences, limiting their ability to wage war, and kept them under shogunal surveillance. It also stimulated the economy: the great highways (such as the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō), post towns, inns and markets flourished, and Edo grew into one of the world's largest cities. The elaborate daimyo processions became iconic spectacles.

End

Sankin-kōtai was relaxed in 1862 as the shogunate weakened and effectively ended with the fall of the Tokugawa regime in 1868.
