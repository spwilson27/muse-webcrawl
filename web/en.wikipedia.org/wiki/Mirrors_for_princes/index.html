Mirrors for princes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mirrors for princes (Latin: specula principum) are works of political literature — instruction manuals in the art of government addressed to kings, princes and rulers. The genre flourished in medieval and early modern Europe and has parallels in Byzantine, Islamic and Indian political writing. Such texts typically held up a "mirror" in which the ruler could see both the ideal of kingship and his own shortcomings.

Medieval genre

Early examples include the Irish Audacht Morainn (7th century) and Carolingian works such as Jonas of Orléans' De institutione regia. The genre peaked in the 12th–13th centuries with works like John of Salisbury's Policraticus (1159), the pseudo-Aristotelian Secretum secretorum (widely translated from Arabic), and Giles of Rome's De regimine principum (c. 1280), one of the most widely read books of the late Middle Ages. Typical themes were the cardinal virtues of the ruler, justice, counsel, the education of heirs, and the duties of kingship under God.

Renaissance and parallels

Renaissance humanists adapted the genre: Erasmus wrote the Education of a Christian Prince (1516) for the future Charles V. Machiavelli's The Prince (1513) is often read as a subversion of the mirror tradition, advising on power as it is rather than as it ought to be. Outside Latin Europe, the genre includes the Byzantine advice of Kekaumenos, Islamic works such as Nizam al-Mulk's Siyasatnama and al-Ghazali's Nasihat al-muluk, and the Indian Arthashastra and Panchatantra traditions.
