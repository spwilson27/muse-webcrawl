Deworming

Deworming (sometimes called drenching) is the giving of an anthelmintic drug to a human or animal to rid them of helminths — parasitic worms such as roundworms, hookworms, whipworms, flukes and tapeworms. Common drugs include albendazole, mebendazole, ivermectin and praziquantel.

Mass deworming

Because over 1.5 billion people are infected with soil-transmitted helminths, the World Health Organization recommends periodic mass drug administration to at-risk groups — especially school-age children — without prior diagnosis, since the drugs are cheap and safe. School-based programmes have been associated with improved attendance and nutritional outcomes, though the size of long-term cognitive benefits is debated among researchers.

Veterinary use

Deworming is routine in livestock, horses, dogs and cats. Overuse has driven anthelmintic resistance in sheep and horse parasites, prompting strategies such as targeted selective treatment and the FAMACHA system for detecting anaemia from barber's pole worm.

History

Traditional vermifuges included chenopodium oil and santonin; modern benzimidazoles arrived in the 1960s–70s. Large donations by pharmaceutical companies since the 2012 London Declaration have expanded treatment to hundreds of millions of people annually.
