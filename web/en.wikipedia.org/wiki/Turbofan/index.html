A turbofan or fanjet is a type of airbreathing jet engine that is widely used in aircraft propulsion. It is a gas turbine engine in which a large ducted fan at the front, driven by the turbine core, pushes a substantial portion of the incoming air around the core — the bypass flow — producing additional thrust. Because it accelerates a larger mass of air to a lower velocity than a pure turbojet, a turbofan is more fuel-efficient and much quieter at subsonic speeds. Turbofans power virtually all modern airliners.

Principles of operation

Air entering the engine first passes through a large-diameter fan. A fraction of the air enters the core, where it is compressed further, mixed with fuel and burned in the combustor, and expanded through turbines that drive the fan and compressors, before exiting through the core nozzle. The remainder — the bypass flow — is accelerated by the fan alone and exhausted through the annular bypass duct, in cold-stream designs mixing with the core flow only downstream or not at all.

The bypass ratio (BPR) is the ratio of the mass flow through the bypass duct to that through the core. Low-bypass engines (BPR below about 2) are used in military aircraft, often with afterburners, where compactness and high specific thrust matter. High-bypass engines (BPR 5 and above) dominate commercial aviation: on modern types such as the GE9X or Rolls-Royce Trent XWB, the fan produces the great majority — often around 80 per cent — of total thrust.

History

The concept was explored during the Second World War — the German Daimler-Benz DB 670 and the British Metrovick F.2/4 were early ducted-fan designs — but the first production turbofan was the Rolls-Royce Conway, which entered service in 1960 on variants of the Boeing 707 and Douglas DC-8, with a modest bypass ratio below 1. The General Electric TF39, developed for the C-5 Galaxy and first run in the 1960s, pioneered the true high-bypass configuration; its civil derivative, the CF6, along with the Pratt & Whitney JT9D and Rolls-Royce RB211, powered the first generation of wide-body airliners in the 1970s and made long-haul jet travel dramatically cheaper per seat.

Design variations

Geared turbofan

In a geared turbofan, exemplified by the Pratt & Whitney PW1000G family, a reduction gearbox between the fan and the low-pressure turbine allows each to rotate at its optimum speed, enabling still larger fans and higher bypass ratios of 12:1 or more.

Afterburning turbofans

Military low-bypass engines such as the Pratt & Whitney F100 and the Eurojet EJ200 use afterburners (reheat) to achieve supersonic performance, accepting high fuel consumption in exchange for thrust.

Unducted fan

The unducted fan or "propfan", tested in the 1980s, removes the duct to allow very high effective bypass ratios; renewed interest in open-rotor designs has emerged with efforts to cut aviation emissions.

Efficiency and noise

Propulsive efficiency improves as exhaust velocity approaches flight velocity, so high-bypass fans are markedly more efficient at airliner cruise speeds (around Mach 0.8) than turbojets. The shrouding of the hot, fast core jet by the slower bypass stream also greatly reduces jet noise, one reason modern airports are far quieter than in the early jet age, though fan noise has become the dominant remaining source and drives nacelle acoustic treatment design.
