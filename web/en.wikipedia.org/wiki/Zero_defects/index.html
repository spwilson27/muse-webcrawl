Zero defects

Zero defects is a quality management philosophy and performance standard associated with Philip B. Crosby, popularised in his 1979 book Quality Is Free. It holds that organisations should aim for work done right the first time, with no defects, rather than budgeting for an "acceptable" level of errors, scrap and rework.

Origins

Crosby developed the concept in the early 1960s while working on the Pershing missile programme at the Martin Company, where a zero-defects campaign motivated workers to eliminate errors and delivered missiles ahead of schedule with very high reliability. The idea spread through U.S. defence and industrial firms during the decade.

Principles

In Crosby's framework, quality means conformance to requirements, the system of quality is prevention rather than appraisal, the performance standard is zero defects, and the measurement of quality is the "price of non-conformance" — the cost of doing things wrong. Management, he argued, must take the standard seriously and not implicitly accept defects by setting acceptable quality levels.

Criticism

Critics, including some associated with W. Edwards Deming's approach, have argued that slogans and motivational campaigns aimed at workers miss the point, since most defects arise from the system and processes that only management can change, and that an absolute zero-defects target can be unrealistic or demoralising. Others regard it as a useful aspirational direction compatible with continuous improvement, statistical process control and later approaches such as Six Sigma.
