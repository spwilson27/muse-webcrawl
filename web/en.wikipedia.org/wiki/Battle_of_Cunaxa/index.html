The Battle of Cunaxa was fought in 401 BC between Cyrus the Younger, satrap and younger brother of the Persian king, and Artaxerxes II, for the throne of the Achaemenid Empire. It took place at Cunaxa, on the left bank of the Euphrates about 70 km north of Babylon. Cyrus was killed in the battle, ending the revolt at a stroke, but his Greek mercenaries — the famous Ten Thousand — were undefeated and fought their way out of Mesopotamia, an odyssey immortalised by Xenophon in the Anabasis.

Background

Cyrus, governor of the western satrapies since 408 BC, had supported Sparta in the final phase of the Peloponnesian War. On the death of Darius II in 404 BC the crown passed to his elder son Artaxerxes, and Cyrus, accused of plotting against the new king, withdrew to Asia Minor and quietly recruited some 13,000 Greek mercenary soldiers under the Spartan exile Clearchus. In the spring of 401 BC he marched inland under the pretext of a campaign against the Pisidians.

Battle

Artaxerxes met the rebels at Cunaxa with a much larger army drawn up across the plain, the king in the centre with his guard cavalry and scythed chariots. Cyrus placed the Greeks on his right, by the river. Their charge routed the Persian left opposite them at almost no cost, the enemy fleeing before contact. Cyrus, seeing Artaxerxes exposed, led a charge of 600 cavalry at the royal centre; he reached the king and wounded him slightly, but was struck down and killed by a javelin. On the opposite flank, Tissaphernes broke through Cyrus's Asiatic troops and plundered the rebel camp, though he too failed to dislodge the Greeks.

Aftermath

With Cyrus dead the rebellion collapsed, but the Ten Thousand refused to surrender their arms. Under threat from Tissaphernes — who later treacherously seized and executed Clearchus and the other generals — the mercenaries elected new leaders, including Xenophon, and marched north through the mountains of Armenia in winter to reach the Greek cities on the Black Sea, famously greeting the sight of the water with the cry "Thálatta! Thálatta!" ("The sea! The sea!"). The march demonstrated to Greek observers the internal weakness of the Persian Empire, a lesson not lost on Alexander the Great two generations later.

Sources

The principal account is Xenophon's Anabasis, written by an eyewitness; the battle is also treated by Diodorus Siculus (drawing on Ephorus and Ctesias) and in Plutarch's Life of Artaxerxes.
