Fundamental theorem of asset pricing

The fundamental theorem of asset pricing is a result in mathematical finance that provides necessary and sufficient conditions for a market to be arbitrage-free and for a market to be complete. It links the economic concepts of no-arbitrage and market completeness to the probabilistic concepts of equivalent martingale measures.

Statement

In a discrete-time, finite-state model, the theorem has two parts:

First fundamental theorem. A market model is arbitrage-free if and only if there exists at least one risk-neutral probability measure — a probability measure equivalent to the physical measure under which the discounted price process of every traded asset is a martingale.

Second fundamental theorem. An arbitrage-free market model is complete (every contingent claim can be replicated by a self-financing trading strategy) if and only if there exists exactly one such risk-neutral measure.

A discounted asset price process S is a martingale under the risk-neutral measure Q when EQ[St+1 | Ft] = St for all t.

Intuition

If a risk-neutral measure exists, then any portfolio whose discounted value is a martingale has zero expected gain, so no strategy can produce a riskless profit from nothing — the market admits no arbitrage. Conversely, if two distinct risk-neutral measures existed, some contingent claim would have two different candidate prices, and the gap between them corresponds to payoffs that cannot be hedged, so the market is incomplete.

Continuous-time models

In continuous time the statement requires more care: the appropriate no-arbitrage condition is "no free lunch with vanishing risk" (NFLVR), introduced by Delbaen and Schachermayer, who proved in the 1990s that NFLVR holds if and only if there exists an equivalent local martingale measure for the price process. Earlier formulations for finite markets are due to Harrison, Kreps and Pliska around 1979–1981.

Applications

The theorem underlies derivative pricing: the price of a contingent claim is the discounted expectation of its payoff under the (unique, in a complete market) risk-neutral measure. It justifies the Black–Scholes option pricing framework, in which the geometric Brownian motion model has a unique equivalent martingale measure obtained by the Girsanov change of measure.
