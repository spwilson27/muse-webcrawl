Merneptah Stele

The Merneptah Stele, now in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo.

The Merneptah Stele, also known as the Israel Stele or the Victory Stele of Merneptah, is an inscription by the ancient Egyptian pharaoh Merneptah (reigned 1213–1203 BCE), son of Ramesses II. It was discovered by Flinders Petrie in 1896 in Merneptah's mortuary temple at Thebes and is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

Contents

The black granite slab, over three metres tall, is mostly devoted to Merneptah's victory over the Libyans and their Sea Peoples allies in his fifth regnal year (c. 1208 BCE). Twenty-eight lines of hieroglyphic text, largely poetic, close with a short stanza about a separate campaign in Canaan, celebrating the subjugation of Ashkelon, Gezer and Yenoam.

The Israel reference

The stele is famous for containing the earliest generally accepted mention of Israel outside the Bible. The closing lines read, in a common translation: "Israel is laid waste and his seed is not." Crucially, the name Israel is written with the hieroglyphic determinative for a people rather than a place or state — unlike the city-states named beside it — suggesting a tribal group newly established in Canaan. Most scholars date the reference to around 1208 BCE and regard it as key evidence in debates over Israelite origins, though a minority dispute the reading.

The reverse of the stele was reused from an earlier monument of Amenhotep III, bearing a large carved scene. Transcriptions and translations were produced by Petrie's team, with Wilhelm Spiegelberg deciphering the text.
