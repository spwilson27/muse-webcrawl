William James (1842–1910) was an American philosopher and psychologist, and the first educator to offer a psychology course in the United States. James is considered to be a leading thinker of the late nineteenth century, one of the most influential philosophers of the United States, and the "Father of American psychology". Along with Charles Sanders Peirce, he is a founder of pragmatism.

Life

James was born in New York City into a wealthy, intellectually intense family; his father Henry James Sr. was a Swedenborgian theologian, and his younger brother was the novelist Henry James. After studies in painting, chemistry, and medicine — he earned an MD from Harvard in 1869 but never practiced — he joined the Harvard faculty in 1872, teaching physiology, then psychology and philosophy. Recurrent depression and back and eye problems dogged him; he described a philosophical crisis resolved by reading Charles Renouvier's defense of free will.

The Principles of Psychology

James's masterwork, The Principles of Psychology (1890), ran to some 1,400 pages and defined the field: it attacked associationist psychology, introduced the famous metaphor of the stream of consciousness, treated habit, attention, the self, and emotion — including the James–Lange theory that emotion is the perception of bodily change. An abridged "briefer course" (1892) served generations of students, and his Talks to Teachers shaped American education.

Psychology of religion

In his Gifford Lectures, published as The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), James examined conversion, saintliness, and mysticism through individual case reports, distinguishing the "healthy-minded" from the "sick soul" and evaluating religion by its fruits rather than its roots. It remains a classic of religious studies.

Pragmatism and philosophy

As a philosopher James developed pragmatism — the view that the meaning and truth of an idea lie in its practical consequences — in Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking (1907). He defended the "will to believe" in genuine options that cannot be decided on evidence, promoted "radical empiricism" (the doctrine that relations are themselves experienced), and sketched a pluralistic universe against monistic idealism in A Pluralistic Universe (1909).

Legacy

James influenced John Dewey, American functionalist psychology, and later thinkers from Wittgenstein to Richard Rorty. His blend of empirical psychology and philosophical breadth remains a touchstone for both disciplines.
