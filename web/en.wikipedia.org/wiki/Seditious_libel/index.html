Seditious libel

Seditious libel was a criminal offence under common law in England and its colonies, consisting of the publication of writing intended to bring the sovereign, government, or constitution into hatred or contempt, to excite disaffection, or to promote ill will between classes of subjects. Uniquely, under the doctrine as developed by the Star Chamber, truth was no defence; indeed, a true statement was treated as more libellous than a false one, because it was more likely to provoke unrest — summarised in the maxim "the greater the truth, the greater the libel."

History

The offence took shape in the early seventeenth century, notably in the case De Libellis Famosis (1606). Prosecutions were a frequent instrument against opposition writers in the eighteenth century, including the proceedings against John Wilkes over issue 45 of The North Briton in 1763. In British North America, the 1735 acquittal of printer John Peter Zenger in New York — after his lawyer Andrew Hamilton argued that truth should be a defence — became a celebrated landmark for press freedom, although it did not change the formal law.

Reform

Fox's Libel Act 1792 restored to juries the right to decide whether words were libellous, rather than leaving that question to judges. Lord Campbell's Libel Act 1843 allowed truth published for the public benefit as a defence. Prosecutions became rare in the twentieth century; the last notable trials in England occurred in the 1940s. The offence of sedition and seditious libel was abolished in England and Wales by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009.

Other jurisdictions

Variants of the offence appeared across the Commonwealth. Canada abolished seditious libel considerations in most contexts, and New Zealand repealed its sedition offences in 2007. In the United States, the Sedition Act of 1798 criminalised false statements against the government but expired in 1801, and twentieth-century Supreme Court jurisprudence, culminating in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), sharply limited punishment of political speech.
