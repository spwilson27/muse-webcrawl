Majdanek (Polish: Majdanek) was a Nazi concentration and extermination camp built and operated by the SS during German occupation of Poland in World War II. It was located about four kilometres south-east of the centre of Lublin.

History

The camp was established in October 1941, initially as a prisoner-of-war camp for Soviet soldiers and a forced-labour camp. From 1942 it functioned as part of Operation Reinhard, the Nazi plan to murder the Jews of occupied Poland: Jews deported from Poland, Slovakia, Bohemia, the Netherlands, France and elsewhere were murdered there, many in gas chambers using Zyklon B or carbon monoxide. Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and others also perished from starvation, disease, beatings and executions.

On 3 November 1943, during Aktion Erntefest ("Harvest Festival"), SS and police units shot approximately 18,400 Jews at Majdanek in a single day — the largest single-day massacre of the Holocaust carried out at one camp.

Liberation and memorial

Soviet forces reached the camp on 23 July 1944, capturing it almost intact, as the SS had little time to destroy the evidence. Majdanek became the first major camp to be liberated and reported to the world press. In November–December 1944, some of its personnel were tried in Lublin in one of the first war-crimes trials.

A state museum was founded on the site in 1944 — the first memorial museum of its kind in Europe. Historian Tomasz Kranz of the Majdanek State Museum estimates about 130,000 prisoners passed through the camp and at least 78,000 died, including about 59,000 Jews.
