Madchester was a British music and cultural scene that developed in Manchester in the late 1980s and peaked around 1989–1991, in which indie rock and alternative rock fused with acid house, rave culture, and 1960s psychedelia. The term — a pun on "mad" and Manchester, popularized by Factory Records' Tony Wilson and the Happy Mondays' circle — became shorthand for the city's explosion of bands, club culture, and youthful hedonism at the end of the Thatcher era.

The scene

The scene's hub was the Haçienda nightclub, owned by Factory Records and New Order, where acid house nights from 1987–88 converted indie audiences to dance music. Its leading bands were the Stone Roses, whose 1989 debut album is routinely ranked among the greatest British records ever, and the Happy Mondays, whose groove-heavy fusion peaked with Pills 'n' Thrills and Bellyaches (1990). Other acts included Inspiral Carpets, The Charlatans (from the West Midlands but adopted by the scene), James, 808 State, A Guy Called Gerald, and Northside. The associated look — baggy jeans, loose T-shirts, bucket hats ("Reni hats"), and flares — gave the sound its other label, "baggy".

Peak and decline

The Stone Roses' open-air concert at Spike Island in May 1990 drew some 27,000 people and was seen as the scene's Woodstock-like apex. The Madchester moment faded by 1991–92 as the Roses became mired in legal disputes, the Mondays collapsed amid Shaun Ryder's drug problems, the Haçienda's finances and gang troubles deepened (it closed in 1997), and media attention shifted first to grunge and shoegaze, then to Britpop — which Manchester answered with Oasis, formed in 1991 from the scene's milieu. Madchester is remembered as a defining moment when guitar music and rave culture merged, and it reshaped Manchester's civic identity.
