Scrambling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scrambling is a method of ascending rocky faces and ridges. It is an ambiguous term that lies somewhere between hiking, hillwalking, and easy mountaineering and rock climbing. Sure-footedness and a head for heights are essential. Scrambling is a British term; equivalent ascending techniques elsewhere include the lower grades of climbing in alpine terrain, and in North America similar ground may simply be described by class 3 terrain in the Yosemite Decimal System.

Grading

In Britain, scrambles are graded 1, 2, or 3. Grade 1 routes are essentially steep, rough walks where hands are needed for balance — famous examples include Striding Edge on Helvellyn, Crib Goch on Snowdon, and the Aonach Eagach ridge in Glen Coe (sometimes rated grade 2). Grade 2 routes involve moves where a fall could be serious and some climbers use a rope for security. Grade 3 scrambles approach the standard of easy rock climbing (roughly "Moderate" grade) and a rope is normal. Guidebooks by authors such as Terry Marsh and Brian Evans popularised graded scrambling routes in the Lake District, Snowdonia, and Scotland.

Hazards

Because scrambles sit between walking and climbing, they are disproportionately represented in mountain rescue statistics: parties equipped only for walking can be caught out by exposure, loose rock, bad weather, or by straying onto harder ground. Route-finding errors, wet rock, and underestimating the descent are common causes of accidents. Many scramblers carry a short rope and basic equipment to safeguard nervous party members.

Notable scrambles

Classic British scrambles include Bristly Ridge and Crib Goch in Snowdonia, Sharp Edge on Blencathra and Jack's Rake in the Lake District, and the Cuillin ridge traverses on Skye, which stand at the top of the scrambling scale. In North America, routes on peaks such as Capitol Peak's Knife Edge in Colorado involve comparable terrain.
