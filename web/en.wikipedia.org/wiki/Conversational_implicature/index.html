Conversational implicature

In pragmatics, conversational implicature is information implied by a speaker in conversation, which is not part of the literal, conventional meaning of the words used. The concept was introduced by the philosopher H. P. Grice in his 1967 William James lectures and developed in his 1975 paper "Logic and Conversation."

Cooperative principle and maxims

Grice proposed that conversation is governed by a cooperative principle: participants make their contributions such as is required by the accepted purpose of the exchange. He articulated this through four categories of maxims:

  • Quantity — be as informative as required, and no more.
  • Quality — say only what you believe to be true and adequately supported.
  • Relation — be relevant.
  • Manner — be perspicuous; avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly.

When a speaker appears to flout a maxim while the listener still presumes cooperation, the listener infers an implicature. For example, a reference letter saying only "The candidate has excellent handwriting" implicates, by flouting Quantity, that the candidate is weak in the relevant discipline.

Properties

Conversational implicatures are cancelable (a speaker can add "...but I don't mean to suggest that..."), non-detachable (any rephrasing with the same literal content carries the same implicature), and calculable from the literal meaning plus context and the maxims. A subclass, scalar implicatures, arises when a weaker term on a scale (e.g., "some") implicates the negation of a stronger one ("not all").
