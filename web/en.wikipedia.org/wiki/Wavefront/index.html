Wavefront

In physics, the wavefront of a time-varying wave field is the set (locus) of all points having the same phase of the sinusoid. The term is generally meaningful only for fields that, at each point, vary sinusoidally in time with a single temporal frequency. Wavefronts usually move with time; for waves propagating in a straight line in homogeneous media they are planes or spheres perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Wavefronts (surfaces of constant phase) and the rays normal to them.

Simple wavefronts

A point source in an isotropic medium radiates spherical wavefronts; far away, a small portion approximates a plane wavefront. A line source gives cylindrical wavefronts. Rays — lines normal to the wavefront — show the direction of energy flow, a picture formalised in Huygens' principle, in which every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets whose envelope forms the next wavefront.

Wavefront aberrations

In optics, deviations of a real wavefront from the ideal reference sphere or plane are called aberrations; they are measured with instruments such as Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensors and corrected in adaptive optics systems used in astronomy and retinal imaging. Wavefront-guided LASIK surgery uses a map of the eye's aberrations to shape the corrective ablation. In seismology, wavefronts of earthquake waves refract and reflect at layer boundaries within the Earth, revealing its interior structure. See also wave interference and diffraction.
