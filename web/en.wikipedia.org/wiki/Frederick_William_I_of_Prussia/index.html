Frederick William I of Prussia

Frederick William I (German: Friedrich Wilhelm I.; 14 August 1688 – 31 May 1740), known as the Soldier King (Soldatenkönig), was King in Prussia and Elector of Brandenburg from 1713 until his death in 1740, as well as Prince of Neuchâtel. He transformed Prussia into a tightly administered fiscal-military state and built the army that his son, Frederick the Great, would use to make Prussia a European great power.

Domestic reforms

Succeeding his father Frederick I in 1713, Frederick William slashed court expenditure, sold royal jewels and dismissed most of the court. He centralised administration in the General Directory (1723), promoted cameralist economic policy, encouraged immigration and industry, and famously resettled East Prussia — devastated by plague — with Protestant refugees from Salzburg. He introduced compulsory primary schooling in principle and made long-service conscription systematic through the canton system of 1733.

The army

The army grew from about 38,000 men to over 80,000 — the fourth largest in Europe for a state only thirteenth in population — funded by his frugal accumulation of a treasury surplus. The king took a personal, obsessive interest in his regiments, most famously the Potsdam Giants, a unit of exceptionally tall grenadiers recruited from across Europe. Because he avoided major wars, however, his fine army was rarely tested in battle.

Family and character

Frederick William was devout, blunt, violent-tempered and notorious for his brutal treatment of his son, the crown prince Frederick, whose attempt to flee in 1730 led to the execution of his friend Hans Hermann von Katte before his eyes and his own imprisonment. The king died at Potsdam in 1740 and was succeeded by Frederick II.
