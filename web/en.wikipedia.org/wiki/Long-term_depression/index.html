Long-term depression

In neurophysiology, long-term depression (LTD) is an activity-dependent reduction in the efficacy of neuronal synapses lasting hours or longer following a long patterned stimulus. LTD occurs in many areas of the central nervous system and is, along with long-term potentiation (LTP), one of the principal cellular mechanisms underlying learning and memory.

Mechanisms

In the hippocampus, LTD is typically induced by prolonged low-frequency stimulation (about 1 Hz) and depends on NMDA receptor activation, modest calcium influx, and phosphatase signalling, leading to the internalisation of AMPA receptors and a lasting weakening of the synapse. A distinct form depends on metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR-LTD) and involves rapid protein synthesis. In the cerebellum, LTD at parallel fibre–Purkinje cell synapses — requiring coincident climbing fibre activity — is a key mechanism of motor learning.

Functions

LTD is thought to refine neural circuits by weakening unused connections, to prevent saturation of synaptic strengths, and to support memory updating and extinction. In the striatum, endocannabinoid-mediated LTD contributes to habit learning. Impaired LTD has been implicated in neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions, including fragile X syndrome, where mGluR-LTD is exaggerated.

History

Homosynaptic LTD in the hippocampus was first characterised in the late 1970s and 1980s, following the discovery of LTP by Bliss and Lømo in 1973. The Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro (BCM) theory of 1982 provided an influential framework in which the direction of plasticity depends on the history of postsynaptic activity.
