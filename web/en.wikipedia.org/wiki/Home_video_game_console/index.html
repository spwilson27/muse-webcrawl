Home video game console

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A home video game console is a video game console designed to be connected to a display device, such as a television, and an external power source, so as to play video games at home. Unlike personal computers, which can serve many purposes, consoles are dedicated gaming machines, typically controlled with gamepads or other specialised controllers. Games are distributed on cartridges, optical discs or by digital download.

History

The first home console was the Magnavox Odyssey, released in 1972. Dedicated consoles such as Atari's Pong (1975) followed, and the Fairchild Channel F (1976) introduced interchangeable ROM cartridges. The Atari 2600 (1977) popularised the format. After the video game crash of 1983, Nintendo's Nintendo Entertainment System (1985) revived the industry in North America. Successive generations brought the Sega Genesis and Super Nintendo in the 16-bit era; Sony's PlayStation (1994) and the Nintendo 64 in the move to 3D graphics and optical media; the PlayStation 2 (2000), the best-selling console of all time; and later the Wii, Xbox 360, PlayStation 4, Nintendo Switch and PlayStation 5.

Generations

Consoles are conventionally grouped into generations of roughly five to seven years, each marked by advances in processor power, graphics, media format and features such as online play. The ninth generation, beginning in 2020 with the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S, emphasises solid-state storage, ray tracing and 4K output.

Hardware and business model

A console contains a CPU, GPU, memory and storage in a fixed configuration, giving developers a stable target platform. Manufacturers often sell hardware at a small profit or a loss and make money from game licensing, accessories and subscription services such as online multiplayer. Handheld consoles differ in having a built-in screen and battery; hybrid systems such as the Nintendo Switch serve both roles.
