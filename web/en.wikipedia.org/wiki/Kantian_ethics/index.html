Kantian ethics is the deontological moral theory of Immanuel Kant, set out principally in his Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). Kant held that morality is grounded in reason alone: an action has genuine moral worth only when done from duty, in accordance with a principle that reason gives itself, rather than from inclination or in pursuit of outcomes.

The categorical imperative

The theory's centre is the categorical imperative — an unconditional command of reason — expressed in several formulations Kant took to be equivalent: universalisability (act only on maxims you can at the same time will to become universal law); humanity (treat humanity, in yourself and others, always also as an end and never merely as a means); and autonomy (act as a legislating member of a kingdom of ends). Unlike hypothetical imperatives, which command conditionally on one's desires, the categorical imperative binds rational agents as such.

Concepts and influence

Kant's ethics underwrites the modern language of human dignity and rights: persons have worth (dignity) beyond price. It forbids lying and promise-breaking even for good ends, prompting the famous problem of the "murderer at the door" and centuries of debate about conflicts of duty. Developed by Kantians such as Christine Korsgaard and Onora O'Neill, and central to contemporary deontology and to Rawls's theory of justice, Kantian ethics stands with Utilitarianism and Virtue ethics as one of the three great frameworks of normative ethics.
