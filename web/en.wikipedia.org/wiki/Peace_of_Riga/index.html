The Peace of Riga, signed on 18 March 1921 in Riga, Latvia, was the treaty that ended the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921. It was concluded between Poland on one side and Soviet Russia and Soviet Ukraine on the other, following an armistice and preliminary peace signed the previous October.

Terms

The treaty fixed Poland's eastern frontier roughly 250 kilometres east of the Curzon Line proposed by the Allies, awarding Poland large territories inhabited mainly by Ukrainians and Belarusians — including western Belarus and Volhynia — while leaving millions of Ukrainians and Belarusians inside the Soviet republics. Soviet Russia agreed to pay Poland 30 million gold roubles in compensation for Poland's contribution to the economy of the former Russian Empire, and to return Polish cultural treasures looted since 1772, though these provisions were never fully carried out. Both sides renounced war reparations claims and pledged to respect each other's sovereignty.

Aftermath

The settlement disappointed both Józef Piłsudski's federalist vision of an alliance of states between Germany and Russia and the Soviet hope of exporting revolution westward. The border it created lasted until 1939, when the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland erased it; after 1945 Poland's eastern frontier moved west to approximately the Curzon Line, and the Riga settlement's territorial arrangements were formally undone.
