Multiplicative function

In number theory, a multiplicative function is an arithmetic function f(n) of a positive integer n such that f(1) = 1 and f(ab) = f(a)f(b) whenever a and b are coprime. A function satisfying the identity for all a, b, not merely coprime ones, is called completely multiplicative. Because of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, a multiplicative function is determined entirely by its values on prime powers.

Euler's totient function φ(n) counts integers up to n coprime to n; it is multiplicative but not completely multiplicative.

Examples

  • The identity function and the constant function 1.
  • Euler's totient function φ(n), counting numbers ≤ n coprime to n.
  • The Möbius function μ(n), central to Möbius inversion.
  • The divisor functions: d(n), the number of divisors of n, and σ(n), the sum of its divisors.
  • The Dirichlet character modulo q (completely multiplicative and periodic).

Properties

The Dirichlet convolution of two multiplicative functions is multiplicative, and the multiplicative functions form a group under Dirichlet convolution. This structure underlies the theory of Dirichlet series: the Dirichlet series of a multiplicative function factorises as an Euler product over the primes. For example, the Riemann zeta function, the Dirichlet series of the constant function 1, factors as ζ(s) = Πp (1 − p−s)−1.

Applications

Multiplicative functions pervade analytic number theory: summatory results such as estimates for the average order of d(n) and φ(n), and identities such as Σd|n φ(d) = n, are standard tools. The study of their mean values connects to deep results such as the prime number theorem.
