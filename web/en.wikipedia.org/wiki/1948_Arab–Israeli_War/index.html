The 1948 Arab–Israeli War, also known in Israel as the War of Independence and in Arabic as the Nakba ("catastrophe"), was fought between the newly declared State of Israel and a coalition of Arab states and Palestinian Arab forces. It was the first war of the Israeli–Palestinian and Arab–Israeli conflicts, following the civil war phase of 1947–48 in Mandatory Palestine.

Background

In November 1947 the United Nations adopted Resolution 181, proposing the partition of Palestine into Jewish and Arab states. Jewish leaders accepted the plan; Arab leaders rejected it. Fighting broke out between Jewish (Yishuv) and Palestinian Arab militias, with the Haganah moving to secure the territory allotted to the Jewish state (Plan Dalet). On 14 May 1948, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel; the next day the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq invaded.

Course of the war

The war was fought in phases separated by UN-brokered truces. Israel established air and naval superiority and, supplied in part by arms from Czechoslovakia, expanded beyond the partition borders — capturing the Galilee, the Negev (Operation Yoav and Operation Horev), Lydda and Ramle, and holding West Jerusalem, while Transjordan's Arab Legion held East Jerusalem and the West Bank and Egypt held the Gaza Strip. Armistice agreements signed in 1949 at Rhodes drew the "Green Line" borders.

Consequences

Israel emerged controlling about 78% of the former Mandate. More than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from their homes in what Palestinians call the Nakba, and over 400 Palestinian villages were depopulated; the refugees' right of return remains a core dispute. Jewish exodus from Arab countries accelerated in the war's aftermath. No Palestinian state was created, and the armistice lines held until 1967.
