Kenaf

Hibiscus cannabinus flowering

Kenaf (Hibiscus cannabinus L.), also called Deccan hemp and Java jute, is a plant in the family Malvaceae cultivated for its fibre in India, Bangladesh, the United States and many other countries for more than 6,000 years. The name also applies to the fibre obtained from the plant. Kenaf is one of the allied fibres of jute and shows similar characteristics.

Description

Kenaf is an annual herbaceous plant growing to 1.5–3.5 metres tall with a woody base. The stems are 1–2 cm in diameter, often branched. The leaves are variable, simple on the lower stem and deeply palmately lobed higher up, and the flowers — about 8–15 cm across — are white, yellow or purple with a dark crimson centre. The stalk consists of an outer bast layer of long fibres and an inner core ("hurd") of short woody fibres.

Uses

Kenaf bast fibre is traditionally made into rope, twine, coarse cloth and sacking. In recent decades it has attracted interest as a fast-growing, renewable raw material: the whole stalk can be pulped for paper (including newsprint), the core absorbs liquids and is used as animal bedding and oil-absorbent material, and the fibres are used in composite panels, insulation and bioplastics. Kenaf seeds yield an edible vegetable oil also used in cosmetics, and the leaves are consumed as a vegetable in parts of Africa and Asia.

Cultivation

Kenaf grows rapidly — maturing in about 100–125 days — and requires relatively little fertiliser or pesticide compared with some fibre crops. Major producers include India, China, Thailand and the United States, where it is grown in the southeast. Because a single crop can produce both long bast fibre and bulky core material, it is promoted as an efficient source of industrial biomass and cellulose.
