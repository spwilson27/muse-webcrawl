Epiphenomenalism

Epiphenomenalism is the view in the philosophy of mind that mental phenomena are caused by physical phenomena but do not themselves cause anything physical. Mental events are epiphenomena — by-products like the steam whistle of a locomotive, in T. H. Huxley's famous analogy — which accompany physical processes without influencing them.

History

The doctrine emerged in the nineteenth century as science appeared to show that the physical world is causally closed. Huxley defended a version of it in 1874, arguing that consciousness accompanies brain activity as a collateral product. In the twentieth century it was associated with some forms of behaviourism and later revived in qualia-focused debates; Frank Jackson's knowledge argument introduced the character Mary, a scientist who knows all physical facts about colour but learns something new on first seeing red, which Jackson initially took to support the reality — though not the causal efficacy — of qualia.

Arguments

Supporters point to the causal closure of physics: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, leaving no work for the mental to do. Critics object that epiphenomenalism is self-undermining — if beliefs have no effects, our belief in epiphenomenalism itself cannot be caused by the reasons we give — and that it makes knowledge of one's own mind inexplicable. Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument pressed the problem for non-reductive physicalism generally.
