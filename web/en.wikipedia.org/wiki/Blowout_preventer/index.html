Blowout preventer

A blowout preventer (BOP) is a specialised valve assembly installed at the wellhead of an oil or gas well during drilling and completion to seal the well and prevent a blowout — the uncontrolled release of formation fluids. BOP stacks combine ram-type preventers, which use steel rams to close around or shear through the drill pipe, and annular preventers, which use a rubber packing element that can seal around irregular shapes or an open hole.

Types

Ram preventers include pipe rams, sized to seal around a specific pipe diameter; blind rams, which seal an open hole; and shear rams, intended to cut the pipe and seal the well in an emergency. Subsea BOPs installed on the seabed for offshore drilling are very large devices — stacks can weigh hundreds of tonnes and are controlled from the rig through hydraulic and electronic multiplex systems, with acoustic and remotely operated vehicle (ROV) backup controls.

History and failures

The first successful ram-type preventer was developed in the 1920s by James S. Abercrombie and Harry S. Cameron, whose company became Cameron International. BOPs are tested regularly during drilling, but their failure contributed to disasters including the 2010 Deepwater Horizon blowout in the Gulf of Mexico, where the BOP's blind shear ram failed to seal the well because the drill pipe had buckled off-centre; the resulting investigation led to strengthened "well control rule" requirements in the United States.
