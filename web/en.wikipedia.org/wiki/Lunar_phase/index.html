A lunar phase is the shape of the Moon's directly sunlit portion as viewed from Earth. The phases change cyclically as the Moon orbits the Earth and the relative positions of the Sun, Moon and Earth shift, over a synodic month averaging 29.53 days — the basis of the lunar month in many calendars.

The phases

The eight conventionally recognised phases (Northern Hemisphere view) are:

  • New moon — the Moon is between Earth and Sun; the dark side faces Earth.
  • Waxing crescent — a sliver of light grows on the right side.
  • First quarter — half the disc is illuminated.
  • Waxing gibbous — more than half, growing.
  • Full moon — the entire Earth-facing side is lit.
  • Waning gibbous, last quarter (half lit, on the left), and waning crescent — the light then shrinks back to a new moon.

"Waxing" means the illuminated fraction is increasing; "waning", decreasing. In the Southern Hemisphere the sequence appears mirrored left-to-right.

Related phenomena

At new moon a solar eclipse may occur, and at full moon a lunar eclipse, when the alignment coincides with a lunar node. Earthshine — the faint illumination of the Moon's dark limb by sunlight reflected from Earth — is most visible near new moon. The phase cycle drives the timing of lunar calendars and festivals and is linked approximately to the spring–neap cycle of the tides.
