Metempsychosis

In philosophy, metempsychosis (Greek: μετεμψύχωσις) is the transmigration of the soul, especially its reincarnation after death. The term derives from Greek meta ("change") and empsychōsis ("ensouling"), and is associated particularly with Greek traditions — Orphism, Pythagoreanism, and Platonism — though similar doctrines appear in many cultures.

Greek philosophy

Pythagoras was said to have claimed he could remember his past lives, and the doctrine was central to the Pythagorean way of life, including its vegetarianism, which rested on the kinship of all ensouled beings. Empedocles likewise described himself as a fallen daimon wandering through successive incarnations as punishment. Plato adopted the doctrine in myths such as the Myth of Er at the end of the Republic, and in the Phaedrus and Timaeus, where souls choose or are assigned new lives according to their character and past conduct.

Other traditions

Similar beliefs occur in Indian religions as saṃsāra, the cycle of rebirth governed by karma in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism, though these traditions differ from the Greek on whether an enduring soul exists at all. Elements of transmigration also appear in some Egyptian, Celtic, and later esoteric and Theosophical teachings. In the Roman world the doctrine was transmitted through Virgil's Aeneid (Book VI) and Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book XV), which puts a long speech on the doctrine into the mouth of Pythagoras.

In literature

Metempsychosis has been a recurring literary device, notably in James Joyce's Ulysses, where the word is explained to Molly Bloom and becomes a motif of the novel.
