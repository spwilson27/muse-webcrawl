Constantinian dynasty

The Constantinian dynasty is an informal name for the ruling family of the Roman Empire from Constantius Chlorus (died 306) to Julian the Apostate (died 363). It is named after its most famous member, Constantine the Great, who founded Constantinople and legalised Christianity.

Members and reigns

Constantius I Chlorus, a Tetrarch, was succeeded in the west by his son Constantine (r. 306–337), who reunified the empire, won the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312), issued the Edict of Milan (313) and founded Constantinople (330). His sons Constantine II, Constantius II and Constans divided the empire and fought among themselves; Constantius II emerged sole emperor. His cousin Julian (r. 361–363), the last non-Christian emperor, attempted a pagan revival before dying on campaign in Persia, ending the dynasty.

Legacy

The dynasty transformed the empire's religion, capital and administration, setting patterns that shaped Byzantine and medieval Europe.
