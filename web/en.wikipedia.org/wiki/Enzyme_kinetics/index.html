Enzyme kinetics

Enzyme kinetics is the study of the rates of enzyme-catalysed chemical reactions. In enzyme kinetics, the reaction rate is measured and the effects of varying the conditions of the reaction are investigated. Studying the kinetics of an enzyme reveals its catalytic mechanism, its role in metabolism, how its activity is controlled, and how a drug or a modifier (inhibitor or activator) might affect its behaviour.

Michaelis–Menten kinetics

Many enzymes follow the model proposed by Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten in 1913, in which a substrate S binds reversibly to the enzyme E to form a complex ES, which then breaks down to release product P:

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P

Under steady-state assumptions (derived by Briggs and Haldane in 1925), the rate of reaction is:

v = (Vmax [S]) / (Km + [S])

Here Vmax is the maximum rate, approached when the enzyme is saturated with substrate, and Km (the Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax. A low Km indicates high apparent affinity of the enzyme for its substrate. The turnover number kcat = Vmax/[E]T gives the maximum number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme per unit time, and the ratio kcat/Km measures catalytic efficiency.

Plots and measurement

Kinetic parameters are estimated from initial-rate measurements at varying substrate concentrations. Historically, double-reciprocal Lineweaver–Burk plots were used to linearise the data, but modern practice favours direct nonlinear fitting of the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Inhibition

Enzyme inhibitors alter kinetics in characteristic ways: competitive inhibitors raise the apparent Km but leave Vmax unchanged; non-competitive inhibitors lower Vmax; and uncompetitive inhibitors lower both. Many drugs, including statins and many antibiotics, work as enzyme inhibitors, and kinetic analysis is central to their development.

Multi-substrate and cooperative enzymes

Reactions with two or more substrates follow sequential or ping-pong mechanisms. Allosteric enzymes with multiple interacting subunits deviate from Michaelis–Menten behaviour and show sigmoidal rate curves, described by the Hill equation.
