The Rutherford model is a model of the atom devised by the physicist Ernest Rutherford in 1911. Rutherford's model arose from his analysis of the Geiger–Marsden experiments (1909), in which alpha particles fired at a thin sheet of gold foil were occasionally deflected through very large angles — a result incompatible with J. J. Thomson's plum pudding model, in which positive charge was spread diffusely through the atom.

Rutherford concluded that almost all of the atom's mass, and all of its positive charge, is concentrated in a minute central region he called the nucleus, roughly 10,000 times smaller than the atom itself, with the electrons moving in the largely empty space around it. Although the model was quickly found to be unstable by classical electromagnetic theory — an orbiting electron should radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus — it correctly identified the atomic nucleus and laid the foundation for the Bohr model (1913) and, ultimately, modern quantum mechanics.

Background

In 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working under Rutherford at the University of Manchester, bombarded gold foil with alpha particles. Most passed straight through, but about 1 in 8,000 was deflected by more than 90 degrees. Rutherford later recalled that it was "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." A diffuse positive charge, as in the plum pudding model, could not produce such strong deflections; only a concentrated charge could.

The model

Rutherford's 1911 paper proposed that the atom contains a central charge (+Ne or −Ne) concentrated in a volume very small compared with the atom, with an equal opposite charge distributed in a sphere of radius equal to the atom's radius (~10−10 m). The scattering formula he derived — the Rutherford scattering formula, giving the number of particles scattered per unit area as a function of angle — matched the experimental data remarkably well.

Limitations and legacy

The model could not explain the stability of atoms, the discrete spectra of elements, or chemical bonding. Niels Bohr's 1913 model retained Rutherford's nucleus but imposed quantised electron orbits. Despite its shortcomings, the Rutherford model is regarded as one of the great turning points in physics: it established the nuclear atom, the picture still used as the starting point for atomic theory.
