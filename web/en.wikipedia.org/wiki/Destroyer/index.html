In naval terminology, a destroyer is a fast, manoeuvrable, long-endurance warship intended to escort larger vessels in a fleet, convoy, or carrier battle group and defend them against a wide range of general threats. Originally, in 1885, it was a torpedo boat destroyer — a ship created to counter the threat of torpedo boats.

A modern guided-missile destroyer, among the most capable surface combatants afloat.

History

The first vessels explicitly called torpedo boat destroyers appeared in the 1890s, notably in the Royal Navy. By the First World War destroyers screened capital ships, laid and swept mines, and fought submarines. In the Second World War they were the workhorses of convoy escort and fleet actions, armed with guns, torpedoes, depth charges and radar.

Modern destroyers

After 1945, guided missiles transformed the type: modern destroyers carry vertical-launch systems for anti-air, anti-ship and land-attack missiles, advanced radar (such as the Aegis combat system on U.S. Arleigh Burke-class ships), helicopters, and sophisticated sonar. At 8,000–10,000 tonnes, today's destroyers rival Second World War cruisers in size and capability, and the line between "destroyer" and "cruiser" is largely a matter of national naming.

Roles

  • Anti-aircraft and missile defence of fleets and carrier groups
  • Anti-submarine warfare with sonar, torpedoes and helicopters
  • Anti-surface warfare and land attack with cruise missiles
  • Escort, patrol, and independent operations
