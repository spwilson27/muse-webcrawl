Tsesarevich

Tsesarevich (Russian: Цесаре́вич, IPA: [tsɨsɐˈrʲevʲɪtɕ]) was the title given to the heir apparent or presumptive to the throne of the Russian Empire. It derives from tsesár', an archaic Slavic form of "Caesar/emperor", plus the patronymic suffix -evich, meaning "son of the tsar". The first to bear the title in its formal sense was Paul Petrovich, son of Catherine the Great, after the succession statute of 1797.

Usage

Under the Fundamental Laws codified by Paul I, the title of Tsesarevich was reserved for the direct heir in the line of succession, and was typically borne together with the older title of Grand Duke (Veliky Knyaz). The heir's wife bore the title Tsesarevna. Earlier in the 18th century, heirs were more loosely styled Tsarevich, properly the title of any son of a tsar.

The last Tsesarevich

The most famous bearer was Alexei Nikolaevich (1904–1918), the only son of Nicholas II. Alexei suffered from haemophilia, a fact concealed by the imperial family and one of the channels through which the mystic Grigori Rasputin gained influence at court. After Nicholas II's abdication in March 1917 — first in favour of Alexei, then of Grand Duke Michael — the title ceased to exist with the empire itself. Alexei was murdered with his family at Yekaterinburg in July 1918.

Other uses

The name was also given to the Russian pre-dreadnought battleship Tsesarevich, built in France and launched in 1901, which fought in the Russo-Japanese War, and to the Fabergé "Tsesarevich" Easter egg of 1912, made for the Dowager Empress and containing a double-headed eagle.
