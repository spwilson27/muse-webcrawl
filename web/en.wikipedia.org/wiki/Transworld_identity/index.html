Transworld identity

Identity across possible worlds: the same individual existing in different worlds.

Transworld identity is the philosophical question of whether, and in what sense, an individual can be identical across different possible worlds. In the semantics of modal logic, evaluating statements such as "Humphrey might have won the election" seems to require us to consider the same person — Humphrey — in a world in which events went differently. Transworld identity is the doctrine that it is literally the same individual who exists in more than one world.

The debate

The doctrine is defended by Saul Kripke, who argued in Naming and Necessity (1972) that possible worlds are stipulated rather than discovered, so no problem arises about "identifying" individuals across worlds from qualitative descriptions. A rigid designator like a proper name picks out the same individual in every world in which it exists.

By contrast, David Lewis rejected transworld identity in his modal realism, replacing it with counterpart theory: individuals exist in only one world, but have counterparts — sufficiently similar individuals — in others. Critics of identity across worlds, beginning with issues raised about indiscernibility and contingent identity, include proponents of overlapping and perduring accounts, while defenders such as Alvin Plantinga handled the question through individual essences (haecceities).

Related problems

The issue connects to the metaphysics of identity, essentialism, the necessity of origins, and to formal work on quantification into modal contexts in quantified modal logic.

References: Kripke, S., Naming and Necessity (1972); Lewis, D., On the Plurality of Worlds (1986).
