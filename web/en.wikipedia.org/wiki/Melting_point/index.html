Melting point

The melting point (or, rarely, liquefaction point) of a substance is the temperature at which it changes state from solid to liquid at atmospheric pressure. At the melting point the solid and liquid phases coexist in equilibrium. When considered as the temperature of the reverse change (from liquid to solid), it is referred to as the freezing point.

Fundamentals

Unlike the boiling point, the melting point is relatively insensitive to pressure. For most substances, melting and freezing points are approximately equal: ice melts and water freezes at 0 °C at standard pressure. A few substances, such as agar, show thermal hysteresis between melting and freezing. The melting point of a pure substance is a characteristic physical property used for identification, and melting-point depression is used to judge purity.

Melting requires an input of energy — the enthalpy of fusion (latent heat) — because the ordered structure of the solid must be disrupted. At the melting point, the temperature remains constant until all the solid has melted, despite continued heating. Amorphous materials such as glass and many polymers do not have a sharp melting point but soften gradually through a glass transition.

Examples

Mercury melts at −38.83 °C, the lowest of any metal; water at 0 °C; aluminium at 660 °C; iron at 1,538 °C; and tungsten at 3,422 °C, the highest melting point of all metals. Helium remains liquid at atmospheric pressure down to absolute zero and solidifies only under pressure.
