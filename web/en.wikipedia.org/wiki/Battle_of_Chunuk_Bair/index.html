The Battle of Chunuk Bair (Turkish: Çanakkale — Conk Bayırı) was a battle of the Gallipoli campaign fought between 6 and 10 August 1915, the high-water mark of the Allied August Offensive. Chunuk Bair, one of the main peaks of the Sari Bair range, was the only objective of the offensive actually captured and held — for two days — by troops of the New Zealand Expeditionary Force, before a massive Ottoman counter-attack led by Mustafa Kemal retook it.

Background

By August 1915 the Anzac position around Anzac Cove was deadlocked. General Hamilton's plan called for a night assault up the dere valleys to seize the Sari Bair ridge — Chunuk Bair, Hill Q, and Hill 971 — while a fresh corps landed at Suvla Bay and diversionary attacks pinned the Ottomans at Lone Pine and Helles. The approach marches, delayed and confused in broken country, fell hours behind schedule.

The summit

After fierce fighting on Rhododendron Spur, the Wellington Battalion under Lieutenant-Colonel William Malone reached the summit of Chunuk Bair at dawn on 8 August; from the crest the men could see the Dardanelles — the campaign's objective — glinting beyond. Malone was killed that evening, and the Wellington and Gloucester troops on the peak endured two days of shellfire (including, at one point, Allied naval shells falling short), heat, and thirst. On 9 August they were relieved by the Otago Battalion and the Wellington Mounted Rifles.

Kemal's counter-attack

Mustafa Kemal, newly appointed to command the Anafarta sector by Liman von Sanders, prepared a massed dawn assault. At 4:30 a.m. on 10 August thousands of Ottoman infantry swept over the summit and down the slopes, overwhelming the defenders; the New Zealanders on Chunuk Bair were virtually annihilated. A British battalion's brief foothold on Hill Q was likewise eliminated. The offensive — and with it the last real Allied chance at Gallipoli — had failed.

Legacy

Chunuk Bair is New Zealand's principal Gallipoli memorial site; the names of 852 New Zealand soldiers with no known grave are inscribed there. Malone's stand and the 10 August counter-attack are commemorated in both New Zealand and Turkish national memory, the battle standing beside Lone Pine as the campaign's most bitterly contested height.
