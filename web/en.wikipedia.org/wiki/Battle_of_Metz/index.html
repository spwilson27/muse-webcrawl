Battle of Metz

The Battle of Metz was a battle fought during the Second World War as part of the Lorraine campaign, from September to December 1944, between the United States Army and the German Army for the fortified French city of Metz. The city's ring of forts, many built under German rule before 1914, made it one of the toughest nuts of the Western Front campaign.

Background

General George S. Patton's Third Army, racing across France in August 1944, ran out of fuel and momentum at the Moselle. Metz, defended by the German First Army with garrison troops including officer-cadet units in modernised forts such as Driant and Jeanne d'Arc, blocked the route into the Saar.

The battle

Early September attacks across the Moselle failed bloodily — notably the assault on Fort Driant in late September–early October, abandoned after heavy losses. Through October the Americans reduced the forts one by one with artillery, air support and direct-fire assaults. In November XX Corps attacked in earnest, crossing the Moselle north and south of the city and encircling it; Metz itself was cleared by 22 November 1944. Several outlying forts held out into December; Fort Jeanne d'Arc, the last, surrendered on 13 December.

Aftermath

The battle cost the U.S. XX Corps roughly 7,000 casualties (estimates vary) and German losses were considerably higher including prisoners. Critics have debated whether Metz could have been masked rather than stormed, but its capture opened the way for the Third Army's advance to the Saar and the subsequent fighting on Germany's western frontier.
