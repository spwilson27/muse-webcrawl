Advanced Audio Coding (AAC) is an audio coding standard for lossy digital audio compression. Standardised by the Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG) as part of the MPEG-2 and MPEG-4 specifications, it was designed as the successor to MP3 and generally achieves better sound quality than MP3 at the same bit rate, thanks to techniques such as improved transform windowing, joint stereo coding, and psychoacoustic modelling.

History

AAC was developed in the mid-1990s by a consortium including Fraunhofer IIS, Dolby Laboratories, AT&T, Sony and Nokia, and was first specified in 1997. It gained mass adoption when Apple selected it as the default format for the iTunes Store and iPod in 2003. It later became the standard audio format for YouTube, the Nintendo and PlayStation consoles, digital radio (DAB+), and digital television broadcasts in many countries.

Profiles and versions

The family includes Low Complexity AAC (AAC-LC), High Efficiency AAC (HE-AAC, which adds spectral band replication and is marketed as aacPlus), HE-AAC v2 (adding parametric stereo), and AAC-LD for low-delay speech communication. The successor standard, xHE-AAC, extends the format for adaptive streaming at very low bit rates.

Licensing

AAC was covered by patents administered through a licensing pool; no licence is required for distributing content in AAC format. With the expiry of the core patents in the 2020s, the format has become effectively patent-free in most jurisdictions.
