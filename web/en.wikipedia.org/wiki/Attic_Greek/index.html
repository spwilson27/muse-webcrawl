Attic Greek is the Greek dialect of the ancient region of Attica, which includes the polis of Athens. Often called classical Greek, it was the prestige dialect of the Greek world and is now the standard form taught to students of Ancient Greek. It is closely related to Ionic Greek. Attic and Ionic together formed the Attic–Ionic dialect group of Ancient Greek.

Attic Greek inscription, typical of classical Athens.

History

Attic Greek first became prominent in the archaic period, but it rose to dominance in the 5th century BC, when Athens' political and cultural supremacy spread the dialect across the Aegean and the wider Greek world. The works of the tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, the comedies of Aristophanes, the histories of Thucydides and Xenophon, and the philosophy of Plato and the speeches of Demosthenes are all written in Attic.

In the Hellenistic period, Attic merged with Ionic and other dialects to form the basis of Koine Greek, the common language of the eastern Mediterranean. Centuries later, Atticising prose writers of the Second Sophistic deliberately imitated classical Attic style.

Features

Attic differs from other dialects in a number of phonological and morphological respects. Characteristic features include the retention of long ā after e, i, r (where Ionic shifted ā to ē), the contraction of adjacent vowels, the use of -tt- where other dialects have -ss- (e.g. glôtta "tongue"), and distinctive forms such as the endings of the first declension and particular pronouns and particles. Attic prose of the classical period is marked by a precise use of particles and a complex system of moods and tenses.
