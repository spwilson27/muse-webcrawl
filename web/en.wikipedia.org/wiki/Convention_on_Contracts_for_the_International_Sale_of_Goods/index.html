The United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG), sometimes called the Vienna Convention, is a multilateral treaty establishing a uniform framework for the international sale of goods. Developed by UNCITRAL, it was signed in Vienna in 1980 and entered into force on 1 January 1988. Its parties account for the large majority of world trade.

The CISG supplies default rules for most international goods contracts.

Scope

The CISG applies to contracts for the sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different Contracting States, or where private international law leads to the law of a Contracting State. It excludes consumer sales, auctions, and sales of ships, aircraft, and electricity. Notably, it governs contract formation and the rights and obligations of buyer and seller, but not the validity of the contract or the effect of the contract on title to the goods (Article 4).

Key provisions

  • Formation — an offer must be sufficiently definite; acceptance is effective when it reaches the offeror; the "battle of the forms" is handled by a modified mirror-image approach (Article 19).
  • Obligations of the seller — delivery of conforming goods and documents, and transfer of property.
  • Obligations of the buyer — payment of the price and taking delivery.
  • Remedies — avoidance for fundamental breach, price reduction, and damages under the foreseeability rule of Article 74.
  • Risk of loss — passes, in carriage contracts, when goods are handed to the first carrier (Article 67).

Adoption

The Convention is regarded as one of the most successful uniform commercial law instruments. Some major trading nations, including the United Kingdom and India, are not parties, and parties frequently exclude it by agreement under Article 6.
