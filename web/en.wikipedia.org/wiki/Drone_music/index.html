Drone music, also called drone-based music or simply drone, is a minimalist genre characterised by the use of sustained or repeated sounds, notes or tone clusters — drones — held for long durations, with harmonic change occurring slowly or not at all. Attention shifts instead to timbre, overtones, beating patterns and the physical sensation of sound. Drones are ancient and cross-cultural — underpinning the tanpura of Indian classical music, the bagpipe, the didgeridoo and Byzantine chant's ison — but drone as a self-conscious genre emerged from 1960s New York minimalism.

Origins

Composer La Monte Young, influenced by Hindustani singing and the static harmonies of his teacher Pandit Pran Nath, is regarded as the genre's founding figure: his Trio for Strings (1958) and the Theatre of Eternal Music (Dream Syndicate) with Tony Conrad, John Cale, Angus MacLise and Marian Zazeela explored just-intonation drones lasting hours, and his Dream House installations sustain sound environments for years. Éliane Radigue composed long-form electronic drones on the ARP 2500 synthesiser from the 1970s, and Phill Niblock built dense microtonal walls of sound from recorded instruments.

Developments

Drone seeped into rock through the Velvet Underground (Cale's viola drones), Krautrock, and Brian Eno's ambient music. From the 1990s, "drone metal" bands such as Earth and Sunn O))) applied amplifier volume and guitar feedback to Sabbath-derived riffs stretched to glacial tempos, while ambient-drone acts such as Stars of the Lid and William Basinski made works from decaying tape loops. Drone elements pervade post-rock, doom metal, dark ambient, shoegaze and contemporary electronic music; dedicated festivals and all-night "dream concerts" present the music as a meditative, architectural experience.

Characteristics

Drone pieces often lack melody, beat and conventional structure; pitches may be tuned in just intonation to maximise audible overtones and combination tones. Performance volume and duration are integral: listeners perceive the room itself as part of the instrument, and slight changes in position alter the sound — a quality Young and Zazeela designed into their installations. Critics have linked the genre to ecstatic and contemplative traditions, comparing sustained listening to meditation.
