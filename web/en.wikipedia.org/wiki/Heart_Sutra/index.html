The Heart Sutra (Sanskrit: प्रज्ञापारमिताहृदय, Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya, "The Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom") is a popular sutra in Mahayana Buddhism. In Sanskrit it is called the "Twenty-Five-Verse Perfection of Wisdom Heart Sutra" in its longer form. Along with the Diamond Sutra, it is one of the most widely recited and studied texts of the Prajñāpāramitā (Perfection of Wisdom) literature.

Content

The sutra has no conventional narrative frame spoken by the Buddha. Instead, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, meditating on the perfection of wisdom, addresses the monk Śāriputra, declaring that the five aggregates (skandhas) — form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — are all empty of inherent existence (svabhāva): "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form." The text then negates the senses, the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, and even ignorance and enlightenment, concluding that because there is nothing to attain, bodhisattvas fearlessly realise nirvana. It ends with the famous mantra: gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā ("gone, gone, gone beyond, gone utterly beyond — awakening, hail!").

Origin and transmission

The sutra is generally dated to the early centuries CE. Scholar Jan Nattier argued controversially that it was composed in China, partly from a Chinese translation of the Large Prajñāpāramitā Sutra, and later back-translated into Sanskrit; other scholars defend an Indian origin. The best-known Chinese translation is by the pilgrim Xuanzang (649 CE). The text became the most recited sutra in East Asia and is chanted daily in Zen, Pure Land, and Tibetan traditions.

Influence

The Heart Sutra's pithy statement of emptiness (śūnyatā) influenced East Asian philosophy, calligraphy, and art; emperors and monks alike copied it as a meritorious practice, and it remains central to Japanese Zen and to the Shingon and Tendai schools, as well as to Tibetan Buddhism, where it is recited to avert obstacles.
