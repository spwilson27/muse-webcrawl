Pontic steppe

The Pontic steppe is the western portion of the great Eurasian Steppe, extending roughly from the Danube delta in the west to the Ural River in the east, and north of the Black Sea (the Pontus Euxinus of antiquity) and the Caspian Sea. It covers parts of modern Romania, Moldova, Ukraine, and southwestern Russia, and forms the western end of a grassland belt stretching to Manchuria.

Geography and ecology

The steppe is a temperate grassland of fertile chernozem (black earth) soils, among the most productive agricultural soils in the world. The climate is continental, with cold winters and hot, dry summers. Native vegetation is dominated by feather grasses and fescues; large herbivores historically included saiga antelope, wild horses (tarpans), and aurochs.

History

The Pontic steppe is central to the Kurgan hypothesis, which identifies it as the homeland of the Proto-Indo-Europeans, whose pastoralist cultures — including the Yamnaya — expanded across Europe and Asia from around 4000 BCE. In classical antiquity the steppe was home to Scythians, Sarmatians, and Goths; later it saw waves of Huns, Avars, Khazars, Pechenegs, Cumans, Mongols, and Tatars. In the early modern era Cossack hosts settled the frontier, and the region later became the "breadbasket" of the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union.
