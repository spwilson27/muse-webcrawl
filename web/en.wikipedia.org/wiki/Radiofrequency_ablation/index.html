Radiofrequency ablation

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is a medical procedure in which tissue is destroyed using heat generated by a high-frequency alternating current delivered through an electrode. The current causes ionic agitation and frictional heating in the surrounding tissue, producing a zone of coagulative necrosis. RFA is used to treat tumours, cardiac arrhythmias, chronic pain and other conditions.

Oncology

In cancer treatment, a needle-like probe is inserted into a tumour under ultrasound, CT or MRI guidance, and radiofrequency energy is applied to destroy the malignant tissue. RFA is an option for small tumours of the liver, kidney, lung and bone, particularly in patients who are poor candidates for surgery. Its main limitation is the size of the ablation zone and the "heat-sink" effect of nearby blood vessels, which can leave viable tumour at the margins.

Cardiology

Catheter ablation using radiofrequency energy is a standard treatment for many cardiac arrhythmias. An electrophysiologist maps the abnormal electrical pathways and applies lesions to interrupt them, curing conditions such as atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, accessory pathway tachycardias and, with pulmonary vein isolation, atrial fibrillation.

Pain management

Radiofrequency neurotomy applies the same principle to nerves transmitting pain — for example the medial branch nerves of the facet joints in chronic back pain — providing relief lasting months to years as the nerve slowly regenerates.
