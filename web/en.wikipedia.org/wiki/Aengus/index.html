Aengus (Old Irish: Óengus), also called Aengus Óg ("Aengus the Young"), Mac ind Óg and Mac Óg, is a god of Irish mythology, a member of the Tuatha Dé Danann associated with youth, love, beauty and poetic inspiration. He is the son of the Dagda — chief of the Tuatha Dé — and the river goddess Boann, and in the tales he outwits his father to gain possession of the great Neolithic mound of Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange), asking for it for "a day and a night", which in the logic of the story means for ever, since day and night together constitute all of time.

Myths

Aengus's conception is itself a trick: the Dagda sent Boann's husband Elcmar away on an errand and held the sun still so that a single day lasted nine months, allowing Aengus to be conceived and born within one day — hence "Mac Óg", the young son. His best-known tale is the Aislinge Óenguso ("The Dream of Aengus"), in which he falls in love with a girl seen in a dream, Caer Ibormeith, who spends alternate years as a woman and as a swan on a lake. With help from his father and from Bodb Derg, Aengus finds her, becomes a swan himself, and the two fly together to Brú na Bóinne, singing music that puts listeners to sleep for three days and nights. Aengus also acts as foster-father and protector of Diarmuid Ua Duibhne, and unsuccessfully tries to save him from the vengeance of Fionn mac Cumhaill in the Fenian tale of Diarmuid and Gráinne.

Attributes and legacy

Aengus is often accompanied by birds — four birds, said in some texts to be his kisses made visible, fly around his head — and he is linked in folklore with swans and with dreams. W. B. Yeats retold his story in the poem "The Song of Wandering Aengus" (1897), in which the god searches for a "glimmering girl" seen in a vision, one of the best-known lyrics of the Irish literary revival.
