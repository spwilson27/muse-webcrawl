Pleiotropy

One gene influencing multiple traits

Pleiotropy (from Greek pleion, "more", and tropos, "way") occurs when one gene influences two or more seemingly unrelated phenotypic traits. Consequently, a mutation in a pleiotropic gene may have effects on several characteristics simultaneously.

Mechanisms

Pleiotropy can arise because a gene product is used in many cell types or developmental processes, because a gene participates in a signalling pathway with multiple downstream targets, or because one gene encodes several products. It is distinguished from the effects of closely linked genes, which can mimic pleiotropy until separated by recombination.

Examples

In humans, mutations in the FBN1 gene in Marfan syndrome affect the skeleton, eyes, and cardiovascular system through defective fibrillin in connective tissue. Phenylketonuria results from a single enzyme deficiency that, untreated, affects both mental development and pigmentation. In chickens, the "frizzle" gene alters feathers but also affects metabolism and reproduction. Antagonistic pleiotropy – where a trait beneficial early in life is harmful later – was proposed by George C. Williams in 1957 as an evolutionary explanation for ageing (senescence).

Significance

Pleiotropy constrains evolution by natural selection, since improving one trait may degrade another, and it complicates genetic medicine and breeding: selecting for or against a single trait can drag correlated traits along. Genome-wide association studies have shown that a large fraction of gene variants associated with one disease or trait are also associated with others.
