Covenant theology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Covenant theology (also known as covenantalism, federal theology, or federalism) is a biblical-theological framework within Reformed theology that understands God's dealings with humanity in terms of covenants. Developed by Reformed theologians in the 16th and 17th centuries — including Heinrich Bullinger, Zacharias Ursinus, Caspar Olevianus and Johannes Cocceius — it reads the whole of Scripture through three overarching theological covenants: the covenant of redemption, the covenant of works, and the covenant of grace.

Federal theology organised Reformed doctrine around the biblical covenants.

The three covenants

Covenant of redemption

The covenant of redemption is an eternal agreement within the Trinity — between the Father and the Son — in which the Son undertakes to redeem the elect. It is not stated as a distinct covenant in the Westminster standards but is widely held in Reformed orthodoxy.

Covenant of works

In the covenant of works, God promised Adam eternal life on condition of perfect obedience, with death as the penalty for disobedience — symbolised by the prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Adam, as "federal head" of humanity, broke this covenant in the Fall, with consequences inherited by all his descendants.

Covenant of grace

In the covenant of grace, God promises salvation freely to sinners who believe in Christ, who as the second Adam and federal head fulfils the covenant of works on their behalf. This one covenant is administered differently across biblical history — under the patriarchs, under Moses (the old covenant), and in the New Testament church — but is one in substance. The Westminster Confession of Faith (ch. VII) gives the classical summary.

Distinctives and debates

Covenant theology grounds characteristic Reformed practices and doctrines, including infant baptism (by analogy with circumcision as the sign of the covenant in the Old Testament) and a high degree of continuity between Israel and the church. It is commonly contrasted with dispensationalism, which divides history into distinct dispensations and maintains a sharper Israel–church distinction. Debates within the tradition include the relationship of the Mosaic covenant to the covenant of grace (and whether the Mosaic covenant republished the covenant of works), the role of the law (antinomianism and theonomy controversies), and modern revisions such as Norman Shepherd's teachings and the "Federal Vision" movement.

Influence

Federal theology shaped the confessions of Reformed churches (Westminster Confession, Belgic Confession, Heidelberg Catechism, Second Helvetic Confession), Puritan thought in England and New England — including the political idea of covenanted communities, which influenced congregational polity and American federalism — and the theology of later figures such as Herman Bavinck, Louis Berkhof and Meredith Kline.
