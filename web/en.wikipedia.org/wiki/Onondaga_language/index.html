Onondaga language

The Onondaga homeland lies around Onondaga Lake in present-day New York.

The Onondaga language (Onondaga: Onǫda'gegá') is the Iroquoian language spoken by the Onondaga people, one of the original Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). It is spoken primarily at the Onondaga Nation territory south of Syracuse, New York, and at Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario, Canada.

Status

Onondaga is critically endangered. By the early twenty-first century only a small number of elderly first-language speakers remained, and the language is the subject of intensive revitalisation efforts, including immersion teaching and documentation of the ceremonial "Old Words" register used in condolence and council oratory.

Phonology

Onondaga has a relatively small consonant inventory and a system of seven oral vowels plus nasalised vowels, with contrastive vowel length and word accent. Like the other Northern Iroquoian languages, it is polysynthetic: whole sentences can be expressed in a single verb word built from many morphemes, including noun incorporation.

Grammar

The verb complex is the heart of the sentence, with pronominal prefixes marking agent and patient, aspect suffixes (habitual, punctual, stative), and prepronominal prefixes carrying modal and locative meanings. Word order is pragmatically driven rather than fixed.
