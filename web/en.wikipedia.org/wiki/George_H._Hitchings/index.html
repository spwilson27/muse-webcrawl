George Herbert Hitchings (18 April 1905 – 27 February 1998) was an American medical doctor and pharmacologist who shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Sir James W. Black and Gertrude B. Elion for their discoveries of important principles for drug treatment.

Career

Hitchings studied chemistry at the University of Washington and Harvard Medical School, where he earned his PhD in 1933. In 1942 he joined the Wellcome Research Laboratories (later Burroughs Wellcome) and began a lifelong scientific partnership with Gertrude Elion.

Rational drug design

Rather than relying on trial and error, Hitchings and Elion deliberately designed drugs that interfered with the nucleic acid metabolism of rapidly dividing cells, exploiting biochemical differences between normal human cells and pathogens or cancer cells. Their approach produced a string of landmark medicines: 6-mercaptopurine for leukaemia, azathioprine for organ transplantation, allopurinol for gout, pyrimethamine for malaria, trimethoprim for bacterial infections, and acyclovir for herpes — the foundation of modern antiviral therapy. Their methods transformed pharmacology and launched the era of rational drug design.
