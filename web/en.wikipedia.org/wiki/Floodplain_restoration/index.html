Floodplain restoration

Floodplain restoration is the process of re-establishing the natural hydrological and ecological connection between a river and its floodplain, typically by removing or setting back levees, reconnecting side channels and oxbow lakes, and restoring wetland vegetation. Across Europe and North America, centuries of embanking, channelisation and drainage disconnected most major rivers from their floodplains, eliminating habitat and increasing downstream flood peaks by confining water in the channel.

A restored floodplain provides storage during high flows.

Objectives

  • Flood risk reduction: reconnected floodplains store and slowly release floodwater, attenuating peaks downstream
  • Biodiversity: floodplain wetlands, meadows and backwaters support fish spawning, waterbirds, amphibians and specialised plant communities
  • Water quality: sedimentation and denitrification in inundated areas remove nutrients and pollutants
  • Groundwater recharge and climate adaptation through landscape-scale water retention

Methods

Techniques range from full levee removal to "controlled reconnection" through sluices, dike relocation (setting the dike further from the river to widen the floodplain), excavation of floodplain surfaces, and re-meandering of straightened channels. Notable programmes include the Dutch Room for the River projects, the Elbe dike relocations in Germany, and floodplain reconnection along the Sacramento and Missouri rivers in the United States. In England, projects on rivers such as the Aire and the Derwent have combined flood storage with habitat creation.

Challenges

Restoration competes with agriculture and settlement on fertile floodplain land, and can raise local water levels if poorly designed, so projects rely heavily on hydrodynamic modelling and land purchase or easements. Success is typically monitored over decades through hydrology, vegetation and fish-community indicators.
