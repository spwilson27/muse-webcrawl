The Russell family is a British aristocratic family, best known as holders of the dukedom of Bedford, one of the great landowning families of England since the Tudor period. The family's wealth was founded on grants of former monastic land following the Dissolution of the Monasteries, and for centuries its London estate in Bloomsbury — including Russell Square — and its seat at Woburn Abbey made it among the richest families in the kingdom.

History

The family's rise began with John Russell (c. 1485–1555), a courtier of Henry VIII who was created Earl of Bedford in 1550 and received extensive lands, including the abbey lands at Woburn. His descendant William Russell, 5th Earl of Bedford, was created Duke of Bedford in 1694. The family produced numerous soldiers, courtiers and politicians, and was closely associated with the Whig party from the seventeenth century onward; William Russell, Lord Russell, son of the 5th Earl, was executed in 1683 for his part in the Rye House Plot and was later celebrated as a Whig martyr.

Notable members

  • John Russell, 1st Earl of Bedford (c. 1485–1555) — founder of the family's fortunes.
  • William Russell, 1st Duke of Bedford (1616–1700) — Civil War commander, first holder of the dukedom.
  • John Russell, 4th Duke of Bedford (1710–1771) — statesman and Secretary of State.
  • Francis Russell, 5th Duke of Bedford (1765–1802) — agricultural improver and developer of Bloomsbury.
  • Lord John Russell (1792–1878), created Earl Russell — twice Prime Minister (1846–1852 and 1865–1866) and a leading Liberal reformer.
  • Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), 3rd Earl Russell — philosopher, logician, mathematician and Nobel laureate in Literature, grandson of Lord John Russell.
  • Conrad Russell, 5th Earl Russell (1937–2004) — historian of seventeenth-century England and Liberal Democrat peer.

Estates

The family's principal seat is Woburn Abbey in Bedfordshire, rebuilt in the eighteenth century and home to the Woburn Safari Park. Earlier and secondary seats included Chenies Manor House in Buckinghamshire. In London, the Bedford Estate covered much of Bloomsbury, whose garden squares — Bedford Square, Russell Square, Tavistock Square — preserve the family's names and development.
