Blanket bog or blanket mire is an area of peatland, forming where there is a climate of high rainfall and low evapotranspiration, allowing peat to develop not only in wet hollows but over large expanses of undulating ground. The covering of the ground with a blanket of peat gives the habitat its name. Blanket bogs are found worldwide, but are particularly extensive in the British Isles, where they are a globally rare habitat type.

Formation

Blanket bog develops under cool, oceanic climates with frequent rain. Peat-forming vegetation, dominated by Sphagnum mosses, grows faster than it decays in the waterlogged, acidic, nutrient-poor conditions. Unlike raised bogs, which are confined to basins, blanket bog spreads across slopes and plateaus, typically to a depth of 1–3 metres of peat. In Britain and Ireland most blanket bog began forming in the mid-Holocene, around 4,000–6,000 years ago, aided in places by Neolithic and Bronze Age woodland clearance, which reduced transpiration and increased waterlogging.

Ecology

Characteristic plants include bog cotton (Eriophorum species), heather (Calluna vulgaris), cross-leaved heath, deergrass (Trichophorum cespitosum), bog asphodel and carnivorous sundews. The surface is often patterned with pools and hummocks. Blanket bogs support breeding birds such as golden plover, dunlin, curlew and red grouse, and are important stores of carbon, holding a large fraction of the United Kingdom's terrestrial carbon stock.

Threats and conservation

Blanket bogs have been damaged by drainage for agriculture and forestry, peat extraction, overgrazing, rotational burning for grouse moor management, atmospheric nitrogen deposition and wildfire. Eroded peat releases carbon and discolours drinking water. Restoration, including blocking drainage ditches ("grip blocking") and revegetating bare peat, is a major conservation priority in the UK and Ireland, and many blanket bogs are protected as Special Areas of Conservation under the EU Habitats Directive.

Notable areas

Major blanket bogs include the Flow Country of Caithness and Sutherland in northern Scotland — recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024 — the Pennine bogs of northern England, the Bog of Allen and the blanket bogs of Connemara and County Mayo in Ireland, and extensive bogs on the Avalon Peninsula of Newfoundland.
