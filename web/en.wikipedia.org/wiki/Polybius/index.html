Polybius

Polybius
Born: c. 200 BC, Megalopolis, Achaea
Died: c. 118 BC
Occupation: Historian
Known for: The Histories

Polybius (Greek: Πολύβιος, c. 200 – c. 118 BC) was a Greek historian of the middle Hellenistic period. He is noted for his work The Histories, which covered the period 264–146 BC and the Punic Wars in detail, and for his analysis of the mixed constitution or the separation of powers in government.

Life

Polybius was born in Megalopolis, Arcadia, son of Lycortas, a prominent Achaean statesman. In 170–169 BC he served as hipparch (cavalry officer) of the Achaean League. After Rome's victory at Pydna in 168 BC, he was among 1,000 Achaean nobles deported to Rome as hostages, where he remained for 17 years. In Rome he became a mentor and friend of Scipio Aemilianus and witnessed the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC.

The Histories

Originally in 40 books, only the first five survive intact; the rest survive in fragments. The work explained how Rome came to dominate the Mediterranean in 53 years (220–167 BC). Polybius advocated pragmatic history based on first-hand inquiry, interviews with participants, and study of documents.

Political theory

Polybius described a cycle of constitutions (anacyclosis): kingship degenerates into tyranny, replaced by aristocracy, then oligarchy, democracy, ochlocracy, and back to monarchy. He praised Rome's mixed constitution balancing consuls (monarchical), Senate (aristocratic) and people (democratic) — an idea influential on Montesquieu, John Adams and the founders of the United States.
