Academia

Academia is the institutionally organized community of scholars, researchers, teachers, and students engaged in higher education and research. The term derives from the Akademeia, the grove outside Athens where Plato founded his school around 387 BC, named after the hero Akademos. Today "academia" broadly refers to the world of universities, learned societies, scholarly publishing, and the norms and culture that govern them.

History

The institutional roots of academia lie in Plato's Academy and Aristotle's Lyceum in classical Athens, in the medieval Islamic madrasas and centers of learning such as al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (founded 859) and al-Azhar in Cairo (970), and in the medieval European universities that emerged from the 11th century onward — Bologna (1088), Oxford, and Paris among them. These institutions developed the degree structure (bachelor, master, doctor) and the corporate guild model of masters and scholars that still shapes universities.

The 19th-century Humboldtian model, named for Wilhelm von Humboldt, united teaching and research in the modern research university, a model later combined with the American land-grant system and the rise of graduate schools. After the Second World War, government funding of research expanded dramatically, producing the large, publicly supported academic sector of the present day.

Structure

Academia is organized into academic departments and disciplines — the humanities, social sciences, natural sciences, formal sciences, and applied fields — each with its own journals, conferences, professional societies, and standards of evidence. Scholars progress through ranks such as lecturer, assistant professor, associate professor, and full professor; in many systems tenure provides long-term job security intended to protect academic freedom. Graduate students form a large part of the research workforce, supervised toward master's and doctoral degrees.

Culture and criticism

Academic culture is characterized by peer review, citation, conferences, and the pursuit of publication in scholarly journals. It has been criticized for insularity, the pressure to "publish or perish," precarious employment for early-career researchers, rising administrative costs, and replication problems in some fields. The Latin phrase ivory tower is often used to describe perceived academic detachment from practical concerns.
