Cultural evolution is an evolutionary theory of social change. It holds that human culture — defined as information capable of affecting behaviour, acquired from other members of a species through teaching, imitation and other forms of social transmission — changes over time through processes analogous to, but distinct from, biological evolution: variation, selection and inheritance. The modern field, which grew out of work in the 1970s and 1980s by Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza, Marcus Feldman, Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson, models culture as a second system of inheritance alongside genes, an approach known as dual inheritance theory or gene–culture coevolution.

History

Nineteenth-century anthropologists such as Edward Burnett Tylor and Lewis Henry Morgan proposed unilineal schemes in which societies progressed through fixed stages — an idea later rejected as ethnocentric. Darwin himself discussed the evolution of language and morality by natural selection. After a long period in which sociocultural anthropology emphasized description over evolutionary explanation, quantitative models of cultural transmission began to appear in the 1970s, culminating in Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman's Cultural Transmission and Evolution (1981) and Boyd and Richerson's Culture and the Evolutionary Process (1985).

Mechanisms

Cultural transmission can be vertical (parents to children), horizontal (between peers) or oblique (from unrelated older individuals). Learners apply decision rules or biases: content biases favour intrinsically attractive ideas; frequency-dependent biases include conformist transmission, which stabilizes group differences; and model-based biases such as prestige bias lead learners to copy successful or high-status individuals. Random copying produces cultural drift, while guided variation — individual learning filtered through transmission — allows rapid adaptation. Unlike genes, cultural traits can spread horizontally within a generation, be blended, and be modified intentionally, which is why researchers differ over how far the Darwinian analogy should be pushed. Richard Dawkins's concept of the meme (1976) popularized the idea of cultural replicators, though academic cultural evolution generally favours population-level models over meme-centred ones.

Applications

The framework has been used to explain the spread of farming, the evolution of cooperation and social norms, language change and phylogenetic relationships among languages, the demographic transition, and cumulative technological culture — the "ratchet effect" by which innovations are preserved and improved across generations, a capacity in which humans appear to exceed other animals.

Criticism

Critics argue that cultural change is often intentional and directed rather than blind, that cultural "traits" lack the discreteness of genes, and that evolutionary models may understate power, meaning and historical contingency. Proponents reply that the models are deliberately simplified tools whose predictions can be tested statistically.
