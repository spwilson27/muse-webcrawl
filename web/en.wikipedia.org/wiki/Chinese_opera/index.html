Chinese opera

Painted face (lianpu) of a jing role

Chinese opera (Chinese: 戲曲; pinyin: xìqǔ) is a popular form of drama and musical theatre in China with roots going back to the Tang dynasty (618–907). It combines music, singing, mime, dance, acrobatics and elaborate makeup into a highly stylized performance art; today more than 300 regional varieties exist, of which Peking opera is internationally the best known.

History

Early forms evolved from Tang court entertainments into the variety plays of the Song dynasty, and matured into the fully developed zaju theatre of the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368), when masterpieces by Guan Hanqing and others were written under Mongol rule. Kunqu, the refined "mother of Chinese operas" from the Ming dynasty, influenced most later forms; Peking opera crystallized in the Qing capital in the late 18th and 19th centuries.

Performance

Performers specialize in one of four role types: sheng (male), dan (female), jing (painted face), and chou (clown). Painted faces (lianpu) use colour codes to signal character: red for loyalty, black for integrity and impartiality, white for treachery, gold for gods and spirits. Costumes and gestures are governed by strict conventions; props are minimal and actions symbolic, so that mounting a horse or rowing a boat is conveyed by movement alone. Singing ranges from natural voice to falsetto, accompanied by an orchestra of strings, winds and percussion.

Major forms

Besides Peking opera and Kunqu, major forms include Cantonese opera (from Guangdong, sung in Cantonese), Yue opera, Sichuan opera (famous for face-changing, bian lian), Henan opera (Yu opera), Taiwanese opera (koa-á-hì) and Huangmei opera. Kunqu, Cantonese opera and Peking opera have all been inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage lists.
