Neurosyphilis

Neurosyphilis is the infection of the central nervous system by Treponema pallidum, the bacterium that causes syphilis. Neurosyphilis can occur at any stage of syphilis infection, though late forms were far more common in the pre-antibiotic era. Treponema pallidum invades the nervous system early in infection in many patients, but symptomatic disease may take years or decades to develop.

Treponema pallidum, the spirochete responsible for syphilis and neurosyphilis.

Forms and symptoms

Neurosyphilis is classified into several overlapping forms:

  • Asymptomatic neurosyphilis — cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities without symptoms.
  • Meningeal syphilis — meningitis, headache, and cranial nerve palsies, usually within the first years of infection.
  • Meningovascular syphilis — inflammation of cerebral and spinal arteries causing strokes, typically 5–12 years after infection.
  • General paresis (paresis of the insane) — a chronic meningoencephalitis arising 10–25 years after infection, causing progressive dementia, personality change, and psychiatric symptoms.
  • Tabes dorsalis — degeneration of the dorsal columns and roots of the spinal cord, causing lightning pains, ataxia, loss of proprioception, and the Argyll Robertson pupil, typically 15–30 years after infection.

Ocular syphilis and otosyphilis, affecting vision and hearing, can accompany neurosyphilis at any stage.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on serum treponemal and non-treponemal tests together with lumbar puncture; cerebrospinal fluid VDRL is specific but insensitive, and a negative result does not exclude neurosyphilis. Treatment is high-dose intravenous aqueous penicillin G for 10–14 days; ceftriaxone is an alternative in some cases. Historically, before penicillin, general paresis was treated with malaria therapy (fever therapy), for which Julius Wagner-Jauregg received the 1927 Nobel Prize.
