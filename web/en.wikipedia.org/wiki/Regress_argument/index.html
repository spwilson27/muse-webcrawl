Regress argument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
An infinite regress of justifications, each resting on the next

The regress argument (also the infinite regress argument) is a problem in epistemology concerning the justification of beliefs. According to the argument, any proposition requires justification, and that justification itself requires justification, generating a sequence of reasons that either never ends (infinite regress), circles back on itself (circularity), or stops at an unjustified assumption (dogmatism). Because none of these outcomes appears to yield genuine knowledge, the argument has been used since antiquity to motivate scepticism or to defend theories that explain how the regress can be acceptably terminated.

The Münchhausen trilemma

The argument is classically framed as the Münchhausen trilemma (also Agrippa's trilemma, after the Pyrrhonist Agrippa and the five modes reported by Sextus Empiricus). Hans Albert named it for Baron Münchhausen, who legendarily pulled himself from a swamp by his own hair. The three horns are: (1) an infinite regress of justifications; (2) circular reasoning, in which a proposition eventually justifies itself; and (3) an axiomatic stop, breaking the chain at a point treated as self-evident or given.

Responses

Foundationalism accepts the third horn in refined form: some beliefs — perceptual beliefs, self-evident truths — are "basic" and justified without inference, supporting the rest of the structure. Coherentism denies that justification is linear, holding instead that beliefs are justified by their mutual coherence within a web of belief, softening the circularity objection. Infinitism, defended by Peter Klein, argues that an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons is available in principle and is not vicious. Foundherentism (Susan Haack) combines experiential input with mutual support, by analogy with a crossword puzzle. Sceptics, from the Pyrrhonists to modern critics, conclude that the trilemma shows knowledge in the strong sense is unattainable.

Regress arguments also appear outside epistemology: in cosmology (what caused the first cause?), in ethics (the regress of "why?" questions about value), and in Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", which applies the regress to logical inference itself.
