Citron

Scientific name: Citrus medica
Family: Rutaceae
Origin: Northeast India / Southeast Asia

The citron (Citrus medica) is a large fragrant citrus fruit with a thick rind. It is said to resemble a 'huge, rough lemon'. It is one of the original citrus fruits from which all other citrus types developed through natural hybrid speciation or artificial hybridization. Though lemon is its most common English name, the citron known by other names in different languages is an ancestor primarily of the lemon and key lime cultivars. Its Latin name Citrus medica ('Median citrus') indicates its early route westward through Media (Persia).

Description

The citron tree is a slow-growing shrub or small tree reaching 8–15 ft (2–5 m) in height, with irregular straggling branches and stiff twigs. Its leaves are ovate-lanceolate; the flowers are white tinged with pink. The fruit is oblong to oval, 4–8 inches long, with a very thick, rough, fragrant rind and sparse, acidic pulp divided into few segments. Unlike lemons, most of the fruit's interior is pith (albedo) rather than juicy vesicles.

History and uses

The citron was the first citrus fruit to reach the Mediterranean, described by Theophrastus around 310 BC and cultivated in Persia and later in Italy, Greece, and the Levant. It is used in Jewish ritual as the etrog, one of the Four Species carried during the festival of Sukkot; unblemished, specially grown etrogim are prized. In cuisine, the rind is candied (succade) and used in fruitcakes and confectionery; in Corsica it flavours the liqueur cédratine. The fingered citron variety, known as Buddha's hand, is used as a fragrant offering and ornament in China and Japan.

Varieties

Well-known forms include the Florentine citron, the Diamante citron of Calabria, the Greek citron, the Moroccan citron, and the fingered citron (C. medica var. sarcodactylis), or Buddha's hand. The yuzu and some other East Asian citrus are hybrids partly descended from citron.
