Comprehensive sex education

Comprehensive sex education (CSE) is a curriculum-based approach to sex education that covers the cognitive, emotional, physical, and social aspects of sexuality. It aims to equip children and young people with knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values that will empower them to realise their health, well-being, and dignity; develop respectful social and sexual relationships; consider how their choices affect their own well-being and that of others; and understand and ensure the protection of their rights throughout their lives.

Content

Age-appropriate curricula typically progress through topics such as: families and relationships; respect, consent, and bodily autonomy; puberty and anatomy; contraception and pregnancy; sexually transmitted infections including HIV; online safety and media literacy; and gender equality and human rights. UNESCO's International Technical Guidance on Sexuality Education (2009, revised 2018), produced with other UN agencies, organises these into eight key concepts taught in a spiral curriculum from age five to eighteen and beyond.

Evidence

Reviews of evaluation studies by UNESCO, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and independent researchers generally find that comprehensive programmes do not hasten sexual debut, and that many programmes are associated with delayed initiation of intercourse, reduced numbers of partners, and increased use of condoms and contraception. Programmes focused exclusively on abstinence until marriage have not shown comparable effects in major reviews. CSE is also promoted as contributing to gender equality and to the prevention of gender-based violence, child marriage, and early pregnancy.

Debates and implementation

Implementation varies widely: CSE is integrated into national curricula in countries such as the Netherlands and Sweden, is mandated to varying degrees in many US states and in England (through Relationships and Sex Education), and faces opposition in other settings. Debates commonly concern the appropriate age for particular content, parental rights and opt-outs, and the treatment of sexual orientation, gender identity, and abortion. Supporters frame CSE as a health and rights measure; critics argue some content conflicts with local cultural or religious values.
