Taijitu

Taijitu
Chinese太極圖 (tàijítú)
RepresentsTaiji (the Supreme Ultimate) and the interplay of yin and yang
TraditionsDaoism, Neo-Confucianism, Chinese cosmology

A taijitu (Chinese: 太極圖; pinyin: tàijítú; Wade–Giles: t'ai chi t'u) is a symbol or diagram in Chinese philosophy representing Taiji (太極, "great pole" or "supreme ultimate") in both its monist and dualist aspects, particularly the well-known "yin-yang symbol": a circle divided by an S-shaped curve into black and white regions, each containing a small dot of the opposite color. The symbol encodes the idea that opposites — dark and light, passive and active, feminine and masculine — are interdependent, interpenetrating, and continually transforming into one another.

History

Diagrams of this family have roots in early Chinese cosmology. The concept of Taiji appears in the I Ching commentaries, where the supreme ultimate gives rise to the two modes (yin and yang), which generate the four images and the eight trigrams. During the Song dynasty (960–1279), philosophers such as Zhou Dunyi disseminated a famous Taijitu accompanied by an explanation (Taijitu shuo), though Zhou's own diagram was a stack of concentric circles rather than the modern swirl; the black-and-white double-spiral form took shape in the Ming dynasty and became the canonical image. Similar divided-circle motifs — sometimes called tomoe in Japan and appearing in earlier Roman and Celtic art — arose independently in other cultures.

Interpretation

The outer circle represents the undifferentiated whole of Taiji, or the universe. The S-curve shows the two forces in dynamic balance: neither half is static, and the swelling and narrowing of each side suggest constant motion and exchange. The small dots — black within the white, white within the black — express that each principle contains the seed of its opposite, so that at its extreme, yin gives birth to yang and vice versa. In Daoist and Neo-Confucian thought the diagram is a map of cosmic process rather than a picture of two substances: harmony arises from balance within change, not from the victory of one side.

Modern use

The taijitu is ubiquitous in modern visual culture: it appears in the flags and emblems associated with Daoism, in martial arts iconography (notably tai chi, whose full name, taijiquan, means "supreme ultimate fist"), in feng shui, acupuncture, and Chinese medicine contexts, and as a generic emblem of balance and harmony worldwide. A stylized relative, the taegeuk, forms the center of the national flag of South Korea in red and blue.
