Italianate architecture

A typical Italianate residence with bracketed eaves and a cupola.

Italianate architecture is a 19th-century phase of Classical architecture, drawing on the models of Italian Renaissance palazzi and picturesque rural villas. It was first developed in Britain in the early 1800s and became one of the most popular house styles in the United States between about 1840 and 1885, spreading widely in pattern books by designers such as Andrew Jackson Downing.

Characteristics

Italianate buildings are typically two or three storeys with low-pitched or nearly flat roofs, wide overhanging eaves supported by decorative brackets, and a prominent cornice. Windows are tall and narrow, often grouped in pairs with arched tops and elaborate hoods or surrounds. Many examples feature a square tower or cupola (belvedere), projecting bays, and loggias or porches with classical detailing. In Britain, Osborne House on the Isle of Wight, designed by Prince Albert with Thomas Cubitt, is a defining example; in America the style was used for everything from farmhouses to courthouses and railroad stations.

Decline

The style fell from favour in the United States after the financial panic of 1873 and was largely supplanted by the Queen Anne style, though it remained common in some regions into the 1890s. Large numbers of Italianate houses survive in the historic districts of cities such as Cincinnati, New Orleans, San Francisco, and Melbourne, Australia.
