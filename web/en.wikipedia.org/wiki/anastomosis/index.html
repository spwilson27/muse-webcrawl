Anastomosis

An anastomosis (from Ancient Greek ἀναστόμωσις, "opening") is a connection or opening between two things (especially cavities or passages) that are normally diverging or branching, such as between blood vessels, leaf veins, or streams. The connection may be normal (as in a fetal heart) or abnormal (as in a patent foramen ovale); it may be acquired (as in an arteriovenous fistula) or innate; and it may be natural or artificial (as in a surgical anastomosis).

In anatomy and biology

In the circulatory system, anastomoses between arteries provide collateral circulation: if one vessel is blocked, blood can reach the tissue through the alternative route. The circle of Willis at the base of the brain is a famous example of an arterial anastomotic ring. In mycology, anastomosis is the fusion of hyphae of the same or different fungal individuals; in botany, the veins of leaves and the roots of strangler figs form anastomosing networks. Rivers and streams also form anastomosing channels that divide and rejoin.

In surgery

A surgical anastomosis is the artificial connection of two hollow structures, performed when a segment of intestine, blood vessel, or other duct is removed or bypassed. Examples include the joining of bowel ends after a resection, the vascular connections created in coronary artery bypass grafting, and the ureteric and vascular anastomoses performed in organ transplantation. Anastomotic leak — failure of the join — is one of the most serious complications of gastrointestinal surgery.

Types

Anastomoses are described by the structures joined and the configuration of the join: end-to-end, end-to-side, and side-to-side. In vascular terminology, connections may be arterio-arterial, veno-venous, or arteriovenous.
