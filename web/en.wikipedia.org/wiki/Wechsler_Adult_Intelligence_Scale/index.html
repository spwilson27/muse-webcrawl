The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is an IQ test designed to measure intelligence and cognitive ability in adults and older adolescents. First published in February 1955 by David Wechsler as a revision of the Wechsler–Bellevue Intelligence Scale (1939), it is one of the most widely used intelligence tests in clinical and educational psychology. The current versions include the WAIS-IV (2008) and the WAIS-5 (2024).

History

David Wechsler developed his scales in reaction to the limitations of the Stanford–Binet test, particularly its reliance on a single composite score and verbal content. The WAIS introduced separate Verbal and Performance scales and expressed scores as deviation IQs with a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15.

Structure

The WAIS-IV comprises ten core subtests grouped into four index scores: Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed, which combine to yield a Full Scale IQ. Subtests include Vocabulary, Block Design, Digit Span, Coding, and Matrix Reasoning. Administration typically takes 60–90 minutes and must be conducted by a trained examiner.

Use and criticism

The WAIS is used in neuropsychological assessment, diagnosis of intellectual disability, and research. Criticisms have included cultural bias concerns, the Flynn effect requiring periodic renorming, and debate over whether the test measures a single general intelligence factor (g) or several distinct abilities.
