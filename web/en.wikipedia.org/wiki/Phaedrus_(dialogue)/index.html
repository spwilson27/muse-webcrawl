Phaedrus (dialogue)

The Phaedrus (Ancient Greek: Φαῖδρος) is a dialogue of Plato, written in the 4th century BCE. It is a conversation between Socrates and Phaedrus, an interlocutor who also appears in the Symposium. The dialogue covers the topics of love (eros), rhetoric, and the soul, and contains famous discussions of the divided soul (the charioteer myth) and of writing versus living speech. It is generally considered one of Plato's mature, middle-to-late dialogues.

Summary

Socrates meets Phaedrus outside the city walls of Athens, near the river Ilissus. Phaedrus has a speech by the orator Lysias arguing that a boy should grant favours to a non-lover rather than a lover, since the lover is mad and possessive. Socrates first delivers—with his head covered in shame—a rival speech in the same vein, then, warned by his divine sign, recants in a "palinode": a great speech in praise of madness and of the lover inspired by beauty.

The charioteer myth

In the palinode, Socrates compares the soul to a chariot drawn by two winged horses—a noble white horse (spirit) and an ignoble dark horse (appetite)—guided by a charioteer (reason). Before birth, souls travel in the train of the gods and glimpse the Forms; those that lose their wings fall to earth and are embodied. The sight of earthly beauty reawakens the memory of true Beauty, causing the soul's wings to sprout again: this is the experience of eros.

Rhetoric

The second half of the dialogue turns to rhetoric. True rhetoric, Socrates argues, must be based on knowledge of the truth and of the soul's types (psychagogy), dividing and collecting subjects according to their natural kinds—an early statement of dialectic. Speechwriting that aims merely at persuasion without knowledge is an artless routine.

The critique of writing

The dialogue ends with the myth of Theuth and Thamus: the Egyptian god Theuth offers writing to the king as a medicine for memory, but Thamus replies that it will produce forgetfulness and the appearance of wisdom without the reality. Written words, Socrates says, cannot answer questions or defend themselves; they are inferior to the "living, breathing speech" of dialectic planted in a suitable soul.
