In the early morning hours of March 18, 1990, two men dressed as police officers stole thirteen works of art from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston, Massachusetts. The theft is the largest single property theft in United States history and the most valuable art heist in the world: the stolen works are estimated to be worth around 500 million US dollars. None of the works has been recovered, and the case remains open.

The theft

At about 1:24 a.m., two men in police uniforms buzzed the museum's side entrance claiming to be responding to a disturbance call. Night security guard Richard Abath let them in, and the impostors handcuffed Abath and the second guard, Ryan B., in the basement, binding them with duct tape. Over the next 81 minutes the thieves made two trips through the museum, triggering motion sensors that mapped their route through the Dutch Room, the Short Gallery and other rooms, then left in a dark car with their loot.

Stolen works

The thirteen works taken were:

  • The Concert (c. 1664), Johannes Vermeer — the most valuable stolen painting in the world
  • The Storm on the Sea of Galilee (1633), Rembrandt van Rijn
  • A Lady and Gentleman in Black (1633), Rembrandt
  • Self-Portrait (c. 1634), Rembrandt (etching)
  • Landscape with an Obelisk (1638), Govert Flinck (once attributed to Rembrandt)
  • La Sortie de Pesage, Edgar Degas (and four other Degas drawings)
  • Cortege aux Environs de Florence, Degas
  • Three Mounted Jockeys, Degas
  • Chez Tortoni (c. 1875), Édouard Manet
  • A Chinese bronze beaker (gu) from the Shang dynasty, 12th–11th century BCE
  • A gilded bronze eagle finial from a Napoleonic battle flag

The selection has long puzzled investigators: the thieves ignored more famous and valuable works in the same rooms, including Titian's The Rape of Europa, and cut paintings crudely from their frames, suggesting opportunism rather than expertise.

The investigation

The FBI has investigated numerous suspects, including Boston organised crime figures associated with the Winter Hill Gang and the Merlino gang, and the IRA has periodically been mentioned in speculation, without conclusion. The statute of limitations for the theft expired in 1995, but recovery remains the stated goal. In 2013 the FBI announced it believed it knew the thieves' identities (both deceased) and that the works had been offered for sale in the Philadelphia area a decade earlier. A $10 million reward — the largest ever offered by a private institution for stolen property — remains in place.

Under the terms of Isabella Stewart Gardner's will, the collection must remain as she arranged it; the empty frames of the stolen paintings therefore still hang in their original places, awaiting the works' return.
