Daniel Chester French

Daniel Chester French (April 20, 1850 – October 7, 1931) was an American sculptor of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, regarded as one of the foremost American sculptors of his era. His best-known work is the monumental seated statue of Abraham Lincoln (1920) at the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, D.C.

Career

French received early training in anatomy and drawing, briefly attended MIT, and studied in Florence with Thomas Ball. His breakthrough came with The Minute Man (1874), commissioned for Concord, Massachusetts, and unveiled at the centennial of the Revolutionary War battles of Lexington and Concord. There followed a prolific career of public monuments, including the colossal Statue of the Republic for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago (a smaller bronze version still stands in Jackson Park), the John Harvard statue at Harvard University, the Alma Mater at Columbia University, and the Four Continents groups for the U.S. Custom House in New York.

Lincoln Memorial and later life

French's crowning commission was the seated Lincoln for Henry Bacon's Lincoln Memorial, carved by the Piccirilli brothers from Georgia marble and dedicated in 1922. His summer home and studio, Chesterwood in Stockbridge, Massachusetts, is now a museum and National Trust site. French was a co-founder of the American Academy in Rome and a leading figure in the American Renaissance movement in art and architecture.
