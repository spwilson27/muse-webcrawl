Albert Girard (11 October 1595 – 8 December 1632) was a French mathematician who worked in the Netherlands. He made early contributions to algebra, trigonometry and spherical geometry, and was among the first to use the abbreviations sin, cos and tan for the trigonometric functions.

Life

Born at Saint-Mihiel in Lorraine, Girard emigrated as a Protestant to the Dutch Republic, studying mathematics at Leiden and working as a teacher and military engineer. He edited and translated works of Simon Stevin and published books on fortification and music as well as mathematics.

Mathematics

In his Invention nouvelle en l'algèbre (1629), Girard gave an early statement of what became the fundamental theorem of algebra, asserting that every polynomial equation has as many solutions as its degree when imaginary roots are admitted, and he related the coefficients of an equation to sums and products of its roots. He also gave an inductive definition related to the Fibonacci numbers and a formula for the sums of powers of roots.

Girard's theorem

In spherical trigonometry, Girard's theorem states that the area of a spherical triangle equals R²(α + β + γ − π), where R is the sphere's radius and α, β, γ are the triangle's angles — the excess over π measuring the area. Girard published the result in 1629, and it remains the bearing of his name in geometry.
