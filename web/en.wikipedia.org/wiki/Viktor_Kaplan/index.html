Viktor Kaplan (27 November 1876 – 23 August 1934) was an Austrian engineer and the inventor of the Kaplan turbine, a propeller-type water turbine with adjustable blades ideally suited to low-head, high-flow hydroelectric sites such as large rivers.

Kaplan studied mechanical engineering at the Vienna University of Technology and, after military service and work on diesel engines, became a lecturer at the German Technical University in Brno, where he was made professor of water turbine theory in 1913. Working in a small laboratory, he developed the idea of a turbine whose runner blades could be pivoted — like a ship's propeller with adjustable pitch — so that high efficiency could be maintained at partial load, a key weakness of fixed-blade Francis turbines at low head. He filed his first patents in 1912–1913.

Struggle and success

Kaplan's patents were fiercely contested by established turbine manufacturers, and years of litigation, together with overwork, damaged his health; he suffered a breakdown in 1922. Nevertheless the design was vindicated in practice: the first commercial Kaplan turbine was installed in 1919 at a textile factory in Velm, Austria, and a large unit at Lilla Edet in Sweden (1925) demonstrated the type at scale. Kaplan turbines are now standard equipment in low-head hydroelectric plants worldwide. Kaplan died in 1934 and is commemorated on Austrian stamps and currency history; the Kaplan turbine remains his enduring legacy to renewable energy.
