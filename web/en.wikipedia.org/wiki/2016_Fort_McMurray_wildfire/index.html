2016 Fort McMurray wildfire

Wildfire details
Dates1 May – 5 July 2016 (declared under control)
LocationFort McMurray, Alberta, Canada

EvacuatedAbout 90,000 people

The 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire, nicknamed "the Beast", was a massive forest fire that swept through the Regional Municipality of Wood Buffalo in northern Alberta, Canada, in May 2016. It forced the evacuation of roughly 90,000 residents of Fort McMurray — the largest wildfire evacuation in Alberta's history — destroyed about 2,400 homes and buildings, and halted oil sands production. With total costs estimated at nearly C$10 billion, it remains the costliest natural disaster in Canadian history.

Course of the fire

The fire was first spotted on 1 May 2016 south-west of Fort McMurray, in remote forest; its cause was never conclusively determined, though human activity was considered likely. Hot, dry and windy weather — influenced by a strong El Niño — drove explosive growth. On 3 May the fire breached the city, prompting a mandatory evacuation of the entire community. Dramatic dashcam footage showed residents fleeing along Highway 63 through walls of flame and falling embers; two teenagers died in a vehicle collision during the evacuation. The fire destroyed the neighbourhoods of Beacon Hill, Abasand and Waterways almost entirely.

Response

Firefighters from across Canada and abroad fought the blaze, which spread into Saskatchewan before being declared under control on 5 July 2016; it was not fully extinguished until August 2017. The federal government matched donations to the Canadian Red Cross, whose appeal raised more than C$100 million for evacuees. Oil sands operations north of the city acted as firebreaks and shelters, though production shutdowns briefly removed about one million barrels per day from the market.

Aftermath

Residents began returning on 1 June 2016. Rebuilding took years, and studies of the fire prompted changes to Alberta's emergency management, building practices in wildland–urban interface areas, and community FireSmart programmes. The fire also fuelled national debate about the relationship between climate change and increasingly severe boreal wildfire seasons.
