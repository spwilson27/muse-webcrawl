Selective catalytic reduction

Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) is a means of converting nitrogen oxides, commonly written NOx, in exhaust gases into molecular nitrogen (N2) and water (H2O) using a reductant and a catalyst. The reductant, typically ammonia or a urea solution, is injected into the exhaust stream; over the catalyst the ammonia reacts selectively with NOx rather than with the abundant oxygen in the exhaust.

Chemistry

The principal reactions are:

  • 4 NO + 4 NH3 + O2 → 4 N2 + 6 H2O
  • NO + NO2 + 2 NH3 → 2 N2 + 3 H2O (the "fast SCR" reaction)
  • 6 NO2 + 8 NH3 → 7 N2 + 12 H2O

Vehicle systems use a 32.5% urea solution, sold as AdBlue or diesel exhaust fluid (DEF), which decomposes in the hot exhaust to release ammonia. Excess ammonia slip is controlled by metering and, in many systems, an ammonia slip catalyst.

Applications

SCR was first widely deployed on stationary sources — coal- and gas-fired power station boilers — from the 1970s and 1980s, particularly in Japan and Germany. It became standard on large marine diesel engines and, from the mid-2000s, on heavy-duty road vehicles in Europe and North America to meet Euro IV–VI and EPA emissions limits, and later on many passenger-car diesels. Catalysts are usually based on vanadium pentoxide and tungsten trioxide on titania for stationary and commercial vehicle use, or on metal-exchanged zeolites (such as copper or iron zeolites) where high-temperature durability is needed.

Performance and issues

Well-designed SCR systems reduce NOx emissions by 70–95%. Drawbacks include the cost and packaging of the dosing system, the need to refill the urea tank, urea freezing below about −11 °C, deposit formation at low temperatures, and the possibility of ammonia slip. The technology was at the centre of the diesel emissions controversies of the 2010s, in which some manufacturers used defeat devices to pass laboratory tests while real-world SCR dosing — and hence NOx control — was reduced on the road.
