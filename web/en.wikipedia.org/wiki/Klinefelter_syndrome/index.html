Klinefelter syndrome

Klinefelter syndrome (KS), also known as 47,XXY, is a chromosomal condition in which a male is born with an extra copy of the X chromosome. It is the most common sex-chromosome aneuploidy and one of the most common causes of primary testicular failure and male infertility.

Signs and symptoms

Features vary widely and many individuals are never diagnosed. Common findings include small, firm testes; reduced testosterone production (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism); infertility due to impaired sperm production; tall stature with long legs; gynaecomastia; reduced facial and body hair; and decreased muscle mass. Language-based learning difficulties, delayed speech, and psychosocial difficulties occur in a proportion of boys. Affected men have increased risks of breast cancer (relative to other men), osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, autoimmune disorders and venous thromboembolism.

Cause and diagnosis

The extra X chromosome arises from nondisjunction during meiosis in either parent; mosaic forms (46,XY/47,XXY) and higher-grade variants (48,XXXY, 49,XXXXY) also occur and tend to be more severe. Diagnosis is confirmed by karyotype analysis, often prompted by infertility investigation, developmental concerns, or prenatal testing, since most cases are not detected at birth.

Management

There is no cure, but testosterone replacement therapy from adolescence can improve virilisation, bone density, mood and energy. Speech and educational support, fertility treatment (including testicular sperm extraction in some men), breast screening awareness and psychological support are also important. The syndrome is named for American endocrinologist Harry Klinefelter, who described the condition with Edward Reifenstein and Fuller Albright in 1942; the chromosomal basis was identified in 1959 by Patricia Jacobs and John Strong.
