Permanent mold casting

Permanent mold casting is a metal casting process that employs reusable molds ("permanent molds"), usually made from metal such as cast iron or steel. The process is used most commonly for non-ferrous alloys, especially aluminium, magnesium, and copper alloys, though iron and steel can also be cast in graphite molds.

Process

The mold consists of two or more parts designed to open and close precisely. Before casting, the mold is preheated and its cavity coated with a refractory wash or release agent. Molten metal is poured into the cavity — in the basic gravity process it simply fills under its own weight — and allowed to solidify. Cores, sometimes metal and sometimes disposable sand, form internal features. After solidification the mold opens and the casting is ejected; the cycle can repeat thousands of times.

Variants

Related processes include low-pressure permanent mold casting, in which gas pressure pushes metal up into the mold; vacuum permanent mold casting; slush casting, used for hollow ornaments; and squeeze casting. Permanent mold casting sits between sand casting and die casting in cost, pressure, and production rate.

Advantages and limitations

Because the metal mold extracts heat quickly, castings have a finer grain structure, better mechanical properties, closer dimensional tolerances, and a smoother surface finish than sand castings, and the reusable mold gives good repeatability. The disadvantages are the high cost of tooling, which limits the process to higher production volumes, and restrictions on the size and complexity of parts, since the mold must open to release the casting.

Applications

Typical products include automotive pistons and wheels, gear housings, compressor parts, and aircraft components.
