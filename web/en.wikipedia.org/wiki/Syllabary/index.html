Syllabary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A syllabary is a set of written symbols that represent the syllables or (more frequently) moras which make up words. A symbol in a syllabary, called a syllabogram, typically represents an (optional) consonant sound followed by a vowel sound — that is, a CV or V syllable — but other phonographic mappings, such as CVC and CCV, are also found.

Types and examples

Syllabaries are best suited to languages with relatively simple syllable structure, such as Japanese, which uses two syllabaries — hiragana and katakana — whose characters correspond mostly to consonant–vowel moras. Other well-known syllabaries include:

  • Linear B, used to write Mycenaean Greek in the Bronze Age Aegean
  • The Cherokee syllabary, devised by Sequoyah in the early 19th century, with 85 characters
  • Vai and Yi syllabaries, used in Liberia and China respectively
  • The kana systems of Japanese (hiragana and katakana)

Comparison with other scripts

A syllabary differs from an alphabet, in which each symbol typically denotes a single phoneme (consonant or vowel), and from an abugida, in which consonant symbols carry an inherent vowel modified by diacritics. A true syllabary's symbols for syllables sharing a consonant or vowel need bear no visual resemblance to one another, though some systems, such as Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, show systematic shape patterns.
