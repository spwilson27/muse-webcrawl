RMS Lusitania was a British ocean liner built by John Brown & Company of Clydebank for the Cunard Line. Launched in 1906, she and her sister Mauretania were the largest and fastest ships in the world — the first liners driven by steam turbines — and held the Blue Riband for the fastest Atlantic crossing. Lusitania made 202 crossings before her loss.

Sinking

On 7 May 1915, during the First World War, Lusitania was on a voyage from New York to Liverpool when she was torpedoed by the German submarine SM U-20 off the Old Head of Kinsale, Ireland. A second, internal explosion followed the torpedo strike, and the liner sank in just 18 minutes. Of the 1,962 people aboard, 1,198 perished, including 128 Americans.

Germany defended the attack on the grounds that Lusitania was a legitimate target: she was listed as an auxiliary cruiser, Britain had declared the North Sea a war zone, the German embassy had published newspaper warnings to travellers, and her cargo included millions of rounds of rifle ammunition and other munitions. Britain and the United States protested the sinking of an unarmed passenger liner without warning; the incident severely strained German–American relations and turned American public opinion against Germany, though the United States did not enter the war until April 1917, after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram became public.

Wreck and legacy

The wreck lies in about 93 metres of water, badly collapsed and draped in fishing nets; it is a protected site under Irish and international law, and diving access is restricted. The sinking remains one of the most debated maritime events of the war — including questions about her cargo, her routing and speed, and the nature of the second explosion — and is commemorated by memorials at Cobh (Queenstown) and Kinsale, where many victims were buried.
