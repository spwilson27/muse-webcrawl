Johnson County War

Date: April 5–13, 1892
Location: Johnson County, Wyoming, U.S.
Parties: Wyoming Stock Growers Association "Invaders" vs. local settlers and small ranchers
Result: Siege lifted by U.S. Cavalry intervention; no prosecutions

The Johnson County War, also known as the War on Powder River and the Wyoming Range War, was a conflict between large cattle companies and smaller ranchers and settlers in Johnson County, Wyoming, in April 1892. The powerful Wyoming Stock Growers Association hired gunmen from Texas and elsewhere—known as the "Invaders"—to eliminate alleged cattle rustlers and to intimidate settlers the association accused of competing unfairly for range and stock.

Background

Tensions had built for years between the large cattle barons, who dominated Wyoming politics and the stock growers' association, and small homesteaders whom the big outfits accused of rustling and illegal branding. The association maintained a blacklist of alleged rustlers; several accused men, including Nate Champion and Nick Ray, had survived earlier attempts on their lives. Lynching of suspected rustlers such as Ella Watson and Jim Averell in 1889 deepened local resentment.

The invasion

In April 1892 a special train carried some fifty armed Invaders, including hired Texas gunmen, into the county with a death list of about seventy names. They first attacked the KC Ranch, where Nate Champion and Nick Ray were staying. Ray was killed and Champion, after a day-long lone defence during which he wrote a famous journal of the siege, was shot dead when the cabin was set alight. News reached Buffalo, where Sheriff Red Angus raised a posse of some 200 citizens who besieged the Invaders at the TA Ranch. Governor Amos Barber telegraphed President Benjamin Harrison, and troops from Fort McKinney intervened, taking the Invaders into custody.

Aftermath

Though charged with murder, the Invaders were never tried; the case collapsed amid legal manoeuvring and the county's inability to pay for a long trial. The war became an enduring symbol of conflict between corporate ranching interests and homesteaders, inspiring Owen Wister's novel The Virginian and the film Heaven's Gate (1980).
