Doric order

The Doric order is one of the three orders of ancient Greek and later Roman architecture; the other two canonical orders are the Ionic and the Corinthian. The Doric is recognised by its plain capital, its sturdy proportions, and the triglyph-and-metope frieze above the columns.

Greek Doric

Greek Doric columns stand directly on the stylobate without a base, have twenty shallow flutes meeting in sharp arrises, and taper with a slight convex curve (entasis). The capital consists of a rounded echinus and a square abacus. The frieze alternates triglyphs — panels with vertical grooves thought to imitate wooden beam-ends — with metopes, which were often sculpted, as on the Parthenon in Athens (447–432 BC).

Roman Doric and later use

Roman Doric, described by Vitruvius, typically adds a base and slenderer proportions. The order was revived in Renaissance and Neoclassical architecture and in the Greek Revival of the 19th century, where its severity suited banks, museums, and civic buildings.
