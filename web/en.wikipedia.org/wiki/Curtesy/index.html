Curtesy

Curtesy (also called tenancy by the curtesy of England) is a common-law doctrine under which a surviving husband acquires a life interest in the lands of his deceased wife. It is the counterpart of dower, which gave a widow a life interest in part of her husband's lands.

Requirements

At common law, a husband became tenant by the curtesy when:

  • The marriage was valid.
  • The wife was seised (in possession) of an estate of inheritance during the marriage.
  • A child of the marriage was born alive — the traditional requirement, sometimes said to be satisfied if the child was heard to cry within the four walls of the house.
  • The wife died before the husband.

The widower then held the whole of his wife's inheritable lands for his own life, after which they passed to her heirs. Unlike dower, which was limited to a one-third share, curtesy extended to all of the wife's lands.

History and abolition

The doctrine took shape in medieval England and was described by writers such as Bracton and Blackstone. It was carried into the legal systems of many former British colonies. Most jurisdictions have abolished or replaced curtesy and dower with statutory elective-share or homestead provisions that treat spouses equally; in England and Wales the Administration of Estates Act 1925 abolished both, and in the United States most states have done likewise, though vestiges survive in a few state codes, notably in modified form in Kentucky.
