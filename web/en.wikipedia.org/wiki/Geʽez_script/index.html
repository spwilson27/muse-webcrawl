Geʽez script

TypeAbugida
LanguagesGeʽez, Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, Blin, Meʼen

The Geʽez script (ግዕዝ Gəʽəz), also known as the Ethiopic script, is an abugida (alphasyllabary) used in Ethiopia and Eritrea. It is written left to right. Each of its basic characters represents a consonant together with a following vowel, and the shape of the character changes in a regular way to show which vowel is meant.

History and development

The script developed from the Ancient South Arabian alphabet used in the pre-Aksumite kingdom of Dʿmt. The earliest royal inscriptions of Aksum, from the 3rd and 4th centuries CE, were written in an unvocalised abjad strongly influenced by, and sometimes replaced by, Greek. The decisive innovation — systematic marking of vowels by modifying each consonant sign — appeared by the reign of King Ezana in the mid-4th century, making Geʽez one of the earliest writing systems in the world to indicate vowels consistently. The vocalised script then remained remarkably stable for over a millennium and a half.

The syllabary

There are 26 core consonants. Each is written in seven "orders": the first order carries the vowel ä (inherent in the base form), followed by u, i, a, e, ə, and o. The full chart therefore contains 182 primary characters, and languages other than Geʽez add further characters — for example, Amharic adds signs for sounds such as ʃ, tʃ, and ɲ, while several consonants also have labialised forms written with extra characters. Numerals in the traditional system, derived from Greek practice, are separate signs for 1–9, 10–90, 100, and 10,000, though Arabic numerals are now common.

Punctuation

Traditional punctuation includes ፡ (word separator, now usually replaced by a space), ። (full stop), ፣ (comma), and ፤ (semicolon). Modern Amharic and Tigrinya texts also freely use European punctuation.
