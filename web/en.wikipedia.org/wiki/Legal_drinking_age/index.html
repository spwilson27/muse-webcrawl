Legal drinking age

The legal drinking age is the minimum age at which a person can legally consume or purchase alcoholic beverages. Laws vary widely around the world and often differ between purchase and consumption, between types of beverage (beer and wine versus spirits), and between drinking in licensed premises, in public, or at home under parental supervision. Most countries set the age at 18; the United States, at 21, is a prominent exception among developed nations.

By region

Europe

Most European countries set the purchase age at 18. Germany and Austria allow beer and wine at 16 and spirits at 18; a German rule lets 14-year-olds drink beer or wine in public when accompanied by a parent or guardian. Some Nordic countries separate shop purchase (18 or, in Sweden's Systembolaget, 20) from bar service (18). A few countries, such as Iceland, set 20; others historically had no minimum for private consumption.

North America

In the United States, the National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984 effectively imposed a purchase age of 21 in all states by withholding highway funds from states with lower ages; consumption rules and exceptions (religious, medical, parental) vary by state. Canada sets the age provincially: 19 in most provinces and 18 in Alberta, Manitoba, and Quebec. Mexico's age is 18.

Asia and Oceania

Japan, South Korea, Thailand, and Indonesia use 20 or 21; China and Australia and New Zealand use 18. In India the age varies by state (commonly 21 or 25) and alcohol is prohibited entirely in some states. A number of Muslim-majority countries prohibit alcohol for Muslims regardless of age.

History and rationale

Minimum drinking ages are generally justified on public-health grounds: research links higher drinking ages to fewer alcohol-related traffic deaths and harms among adolescents, whose developing brains are considered vulnerable to alcohol. The US experience — states lowered ages to 18 in the 1970s when the voting age fell, then raised them again under pressure from groups such as Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD) — is the most studied case. Critics argue that an age of 21 encourages unsupervised binge drinking and note the anomaly of adults who can vote and serve in the military but not legally drink.
