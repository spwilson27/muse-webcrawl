KIT is a human gene encoding a receptor tyrosine kinase protein known as proto-oncogene c-KIT, or tyrosine-protein kinase Kit, or CD117 (cluster of differentiation 117). The KIT receptor sits in the cell membrane and is activated by its ligand, stem cell factor (SCF). It plays essential roles in the survival, proliferation and differentiation of several cell types, notably haematopoietic stem cells, mast cells, melanocytes, germ cells, and the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gut.

Discovery

The gene was first identified as the cellular homologue of the viral oncogene v-kit, carried by the Hardy–Zuckerman 4 feline sarcoma virus; the human gene was described in 1987 by Yarden and colleagues. KIT belongs to the type III receptor tyrosine kinase family, together with PDGFRA, PDGFRB and FLT3.

Function and signalling

Binding of stem cell factor causes KIT to dimerise and autophosphorylate, triggering downstream pathways including PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK and JAK/STAT, which govern cell survival, growth and migration.

Clinical significance

Activating mutations in KIT are the driving cause of most gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST), and KIT immunostaining (CD117) is a standard diagnostic test; the kinase inhibitor imatinib revolutionised GIST treatment, though secondary KIT mutations confer resistance. Gain-of-function mutations also occur in systemic mastocytosis (classically D816V), some acute myeloid leukaemias, seminomas and melanomas, notably acral and mucosal subtypes. Conversely, inherited loss-of-function mutations cause piebaldism, a condition of depigmented skin and hair patches. In mice, mutations at the equivalent W locus produce white spotting, anaemia and sterility.
