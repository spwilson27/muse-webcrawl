Evening gown

An evening gown, or simply gown, is a long dress worn by women to formal occasions, typically made of luxurious fabrics such as silk, satin, chiffon, velvet or lace and reaching to the floor. Evening gowns are the standard dress for white tie and black tie events such as state banquets, debutante balls, opera premieres, weddings (for guests and bridesmaids) and award ceremonies. Common silhouettes include the A-line, ball gown, mermaid, sheath and empire-line cuts, often with trains, beading or sequins.

History

Elaborate court dress of the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries set the pattern of the full-skirted ceremonial gown. In the early nineteenth century, high-waisted empire dresses gave way to the crinoline and bustle silhouettes of the Victorian era. The twentieth century freed the evening gown's shape: 1920s flapper dresses dropped the waist and shortened the hem; the 1930s introduced slinky bias-cut gowns; Christian Dior's "New Look" of 1947 restored cinched waists and full skirts; and by the later twentieth century designers produced a wide range of lengths and styles, though "evening gown" conventionally retains its full-length meaning, as distinct from the shorter cocktail dress.

Etiquette

Under traditional Western dress codes, a floor-length gown is required for white tie and customary for black tie, where shorter formal dresses may also be acceptable. Gowns are coordinated with evening gloves, jewellery, formal shoes and, at the most formal events, tiaras. Beauty pageants, notably evening-gown competitions in contests such as Miss Universe, have made the garment a focus of design and television attention.
