Eyewitness testimony

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eyewitness testimony is the account a bystander or victim gives in court of an event they personally experienced — most consequentially, the identification of a suspect as the person who committed a crime. Persuasive to juries out of proportion to its reliability, eyewitness identification is the single largest contributor to proven wrongful convictions: it figures in the great majority of the DNA exoneration cases catalogued since 1989 (about 70 per cent in the Innocence Project's long-cited figure).

Memory science (Elizabeth Loftus's misinformation studies and the estimator/system-variable framework of Gary Wells) shows identification to be vulnerable to weapon focus, cross-racial impairment, stress, post-event suggestion and feedback effects that inflate witness confidence without improving accuracy. Reforms follow the science: double-blind sequential lineups, standardised instructions, confidence statements at first identification, and expert testimony or judicial directions on memory's fallibility (New Jersey's Henderson framework, 2011; Oregon's Lawson, 2012). Courts weigh identifications under the Manson v. Brathwaite (1977) reliability factors, a test psychologists largely regard as inadequate — a standing friction between doctrine and evidence central to wrongful-conviction scholarship.
