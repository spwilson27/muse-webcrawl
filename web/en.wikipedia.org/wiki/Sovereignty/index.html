Sovereignty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sovereignty is the defining authority of a state: supreme, self-contained legitimate authority within a territory, and external independence from other states. In modern political theory, sovereignty is the full right and power of a governing body over itself, without interference from outside sources or bodies. Debates distinguish the location of sovereignty (monarch, people, parliament, constitution) and its limits (natural law, rights, international obligations).

History of the idea

Jean Bodin's Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576) gave the first systematic theory — sovereignty as absolute and perpetual power, though bound by divine and natural law. Thomas Hobbes grounded the sovereign's authority in a social contract escaping the state of nature; John Locke and Rousseau relocated it in the people (popular sovereignty), legitimising revolution and democracy. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) is traditionally taken to found the system of sovereign states, though historians question the neatness of that story.

Contemporary issues

Sovereignty is pooled or limited in practice by treaties, trade rules, human-rights law and membership of bodies like the European Union (a standing theme in Brexit-era politics). International law asserts sovereign equality of states while the "responsibility to protect" doctrine qualifies non-intervention for mass atrocities. Indigenous sovereignty, digital sovereignty and monetary sovereignty (currency unions) extend the concept's modern debates.
