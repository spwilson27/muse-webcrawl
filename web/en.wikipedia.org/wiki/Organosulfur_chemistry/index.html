Organosulfur chemistry

Representative organosulfur compounds: a thiol, a sulfide, and a sulfoxide.

Organosulfur chemistry is the study of the properties and synthesis of organosulfur compounds, which are organic compounds that contain sulfur. They are often associated with foul odours, but many of the sweetest compounds known are organosulfur derivatives, such as saccharin. Nature abounds with organosulfur compounds—sulfur is essential for life. Of the 20 common amino acids, two (cysteine and methionine) are organosulfur compounds, and the antibiotics penicillin and sulfa drugs both contain sulfur. While sulfur-containing antibiotics save many lives, sulfur mustard is a deadly chemical warfare agent. Fossil fuels, coal, petroleum, and natural gas, which are derived from ancient organisms, necessarily contain organosulfur compounds, the removal of which is a major focus of oil refineries.

Sulfur shares the chalcogen group with oxygen and selenium, and it is expected that organosulfur compounds have similarities with carbon–oxygen and carbon–selenium bonds. The classical chemical test for the presence of sulfur compounds is the Carius halogen method.

Classes of organosulfur compounds

Thiols and thioethers

Thiols (R–SH), also called mercaptans, are the sulfur analogues of alcohols. They are strong nucleophiles and are easily oxidised to disulfides (R–S–S–R), a linkage of central importance in biochemistry, where it stabilises protein structures such as that of insulin. Thioethers (R–S–R), also called sulfides, include the amino acid methionine and the cofactor biotin. Oxidation of thioethers gives sulfoxides and then sulfones.

Sulfoxides and sulfones

Sulfoxides (R–S(=O)–R) include dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), a widely used polar aprotic solvent. Sulfones (R–S(=O)2–R) are chemically robust and appear in several pharmaceuticals. Sulfonic acids, such as methanesulfonic acid and p-toluenesulfonic acid, are strong acids used as catalysts, and their salts, the sulfonates, include important detergents and dyes.

Thiocarbonyl compounds

Thioketones and thioaldehydes contain C=S double bonds and are generally less stable than their carbonyl counterparts. Thioamides, thioureas, and dithiocarbamates are widely used in synthesis and in the vulcanization of rubber.

Sulfur heterocycles

Thiophene is a five-membered aromatic heterocycle found in petroleum and in many pharmaceuticals. Other important sulfur heterocycles include thiazoles (as in vitamin B1, thiamine), thiiranes, and thietanes. The bicyclic systems in penicillins and cephalosporins combine a β-lactam ring with a sulfur-containing ring.

Biological significance

Cysteine residues form disulfide bridges that shape protein tertiary structure. Iron–sulfur clusters are ancient cofactors in electron-transfer proteins. S-Adenosylmethionine is a principal methyl-group donor in metabolism, and coenzyme A carries acyl groups as thioesters. The characteristic smells of garlic, onions, and skunk spray all arise from volatile organosulfur compounds such as allicin and various thiols.

Industrial applications

Vulcanization cross-links rubber chains with sulfur bridges. Hydrodesulfurization removes sulfur from fossil fuels to reduce sulfur dioxide emissions. Organosulfur compounds also serve as pesticides, dyes, solvents, and flotation agents in mining.
