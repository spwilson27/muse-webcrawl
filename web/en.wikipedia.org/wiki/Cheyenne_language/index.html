Cheyenne language

Native to: United States (Montana, Oklahoma)
Language family: Algic → Algonquian → Cheyenne
Native name: Tsêhésenêstsestôtse

The Cheyenne language (Tsêhésenêstsestôtse) is a Native American language spoken by the Cheyenne people, predominantly in present-day Montana and Oklahoma in the United States. It is part of the Algonquian language family.

Status

Cheyenne is an endangered language. The number of fluent speakers is small and declining, with most living on the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation in Montana and among the Southern Cheyenne in Oklahoma. Language revitalization efforts include immersion schools, dictionaries, and teaching programs. UNESCO classifies the language as severely endangered.

Phonology

Cheyenne has a pitch-accent system in which vowel pitch is contrastive. Its consonant inventory is relatively small, and its vowels can be voiceless (whispered), a characteristic feature marked in writing with a dot above the vowel. The language is known among linguists for its complex phonological processes and for the distance between its surface forms and underlying forms.

Grammar

Like other Algonquian languages, Cheyenne is polysynthetic, with verbs carrying a great deal of information through prefixes and suffixes. Nouns are classified as animate or inanimate, and the language distinguishes proximate and obviative third persons.
