Multi-messenger astronomy is the coordinated observation of an astronomical event using different types of signals — "messengers" — that carry complementary information: electromagnetic radiation (light, radio, gamma rays), gravitational waves, neutrinos and cosmic rays. Because each messenger is produced by different processes and interacts differently with intervening matter, combining them yields a far fuller picture of energetic cosmic phenomena than any one alone.

History

An early example was supernova SN 1987A, observed across the electromagnetic spectrum and via a burst of neutrinos detected hours before the light arrived. The field transformed in the 2010s with gravitational-wave detectors. On 17 August 2017 the LIGO–Virgo network detected GW170817, the merger of two neutron stars; a short gamma-ray burst followed 1.7 seconds later, and over the following days and weeks some 70 observatories tracked the resulting kilonova in galaxy NGC 4993 across the whole spectrum — the first event observed in both gravitational waves and light.

Weeks later, in September 2017, the IceCube detector recorded a high-energy neutrino, IC170922A, traced back to the flaring blazar TXS 0506+056, the first likely identification of an extragalactic source of high-energy neutrinos and cosmic rays. Cosmic rays themselves, being deflected by magnetic fields, point poorly, which makes the neutral messengers — photons, neutrinos and gravitational waves — especially valuable.

Methods and goals

Multi-messenger campaigns rely on real-time alert networks (such as NASA's GCN) that broadcast detections within seconds so other telescopes can slew to the source. Goals include pinpointing the origins of cosmic rays and neutrinos, measuring the expansion rate of the universe with gravitational-wave "standard sirens," testing general relativity, and understanding the engines of gamma-ray bursts, supernovae and merging compact objects.
