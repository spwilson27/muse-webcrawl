Mantis shrimp

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassMalacostraca
OrderStomatopoda

Mantis shrimps, or stomatopods, are carnivorous marine crustaceans of the order Stomatopoda. They are not true shrimps, nor are they related to mantises; the name combines their resemblance to both the praying mantis and shrimp. Around 450 species are known, ranging in size from a few centimetres to more than 30 centimetres in the largest species.

Anatomy and strike

Stomatopods are specialised predators whose second pair of thoracic appendages is greatly enlarged into raptorial claws. Species are often divided into "spearers", which snatch soft prey with spined claws, and "smashers", which club hard-shelled prey with a heavily calcified hammer-like club. The smasher's strike is among the fastest movements in the animal kingdom: the club accelerates comparably to a small-calibre bullet and generates cavitation bubbles whose collapse produces a second shock wave, heat and even brief flashes of light (sonoluminescence).

Eyes and vision

Mantis shrimps possess some of the most complex eyes of any animal. Each compound eye, mounted on a mobile stalk, contains up to 12 types of photoreceptors (some species more), including receptors for ultraviolet light and for the polarisation of light. Each eye can move independently and provides trinocular vision on its own. How the stomatopod brain processes this wealth of visual information remains an active research question.

Ecology

Most species live in burrows in shallow tropical and subtropical waters, including coral reefs. Many are territorial and some, such as the peacock mantis shrimp, form long-term monogamous pairs. Mantis shrimps are popular in the marine aquarium trade but are also notorious for preying on tankmates and, in the case of large smashers, potentially cracking aquarium glass.
