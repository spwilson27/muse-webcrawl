Gokaidō (Five Routes)
PeriodEdo period (1603–1868)
HubNihonbashi, Edo
RoutesTōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū Kaidō, Ōshū Kaidō, Nikkō Kaidō

The Gokaidō (五街道, "five routes") were the five major roads radiating from Nihonbashi in Edo (modern Tokyo) during Japan's Edo period, officially maintained by the Tokugawa shogunate. They were used by daimyō travelling to and from Edo under the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance, by imperial and shogunal messengers, merchants and pilgrims.

The five routes

  • Tōkaidō — the great coastal road to Kyoto, with 53 post stations, immortalised in Hiroshige's print series The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō.
  • Nakasendō (Kiso Kaidō) — the inland mountain road to Kyoto via the Kiso Valley, with 69 stations.
  • Kōshū Kaidō — to Kai Province (modern Yamanashi) and on to join the Nakasendō.
  • Ōshū Kaidō — north to Mutsu Province (modern Tōhoku).
  • Nikkō Kaidō — to the shrines of Nikkō, site of Tokugawa Ieyasu's mausoleum.

Administration and travel

The shogunate's road magistrates maintained milestones (ichirizuka), post stations with relay horses and porters (honjin and hatago inns for official and common travellers), and checkpoints (sekisho) that monitored the movement of women and firearms. Travel along the routes generated a rich culture of guidebooks, woodblock prints and comic literature such as the novel Tōkaidōchū Hizakurige ("Shank's Mare").

Legacy

Modern national highways and the Tōkaidō Shinkansen broadly follow the old roads, and many post-station towns preserve historic streetscapes.
