Women's suffrage
First country: New Zealand (1893)
Key movements: Suffragists; suffragettes

Women's suffrage is the right of women to vote in elections. Movements campaigning for it arose in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, achieving landmark victories with New Zealand becoming the first self-governing country to grant women the vote in 1893, followed by Australia (1902), Finland (1906) and Norway (1913).

History

In the United States the movement from the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 culminated in the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. In Britain, the suffragists of the NUWSS and the militant suffragettes of the WSPU pressed Parliament until partial enfranchisement in 1918 and equal terms in 1928. France granted women's suffrage in 1944, and Switzerland at federal level in 1971. Many states expanded or confirmed women's voting rights after the Second World War and through decolonisation.

Legacy

Women's suffrage is now recognised as a fundamental democratic right, though disparities in political representation persist worldwide. See also Women's suffrage in the United Kingdom.
