Nyquist stability criterion

The Nyquist stability criterion is a graphical technique in control theory for determining the stability of a feedback system from the frequency response of its open-loop transfer function. It was proposed by the Swedish-American engineer Harry Nyquist in 1932 while he was working on feedback amplifiers at Bell Telephone Laboratories.

Principle

One plots the open-loop transfer function G(s)H(s) as the complex variable s traverses a contour enclosing the right half of the complex plane (the Nyquist contour). By the argument principle of complex analysis, the number of times the resulting Nyquist plot encircles the critical point (−1, 0) reveals how many poles the closed-loop system has in the right half-plane.

The criterion

Let P be the number of unstable (right half-plane) poles of the open-loop transfer function, and let N be the number of clockwise encirclements of the point −1. Then the number of unstable closed-loop poles is

Z = N + P

The closed-loop system is stable if and only if Z = 0, i.e. when the number of counterclockwise encirclements of −1 equals the number of unstable open-loop poles. For the common case of an open-loop stable plant (P = 0), the closed loop is stable precisely when the Nyquist plot does not encircle −1 at all.

Advantages

The criterion remains fundamental because it works directly with measured frequency-response data, handles systems with time delays and non-minimum-phase behaviour, and yields quantitative robustness measures — gain margin and phase margin — read straight from the plot. Together with Bode plots and the Routh–Hurwitz criterion, it forms the classical core of feedback design, applied today in everything from process control to flight-control systems.
