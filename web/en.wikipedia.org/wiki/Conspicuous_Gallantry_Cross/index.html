The Conspicuous Gallantry Cross (CGC) is a British military decoration awarded for conspicuous gallantry during active operations against the enemy. It is the second-highest award for gallantry in the face of the enemy in the British honours system, after the Victoria Cross, and is open to all ranks of all services. It is the nearest British equivalent to the United States Navy Cross or Distinguished Service Cross.

History

The CGC was instituted in 1993 as part of a review of the British honours system. It replaced the Distinguished Conduct Medal, the Conspicuous Gallantry Medal, and the Distinguished Service Medal — three decorations that had distinguished between commissioned officers and other ranks and between the services. The CGC is rank-neutral: any member of the Armed Forces, and since 1999 civilians serving under military command, may receive it.

Design

The cross is a circular wreath of laurel enclosing a representation of St Edward's Crown on a central shield. It is suspended from a white, blue and white striped ribbon with a central red stripe. Post-nominal letters: CGC. A bar may be awarded for further acts of conspicuous gallantry; the bar carries the post-nominal "CGC and Bar".

Notable recipients

The CGC has been awarded for actions in Northern Ireland, Bosnia, Kosovo, Sierra Leone, Iraq and Afghanistan. Recipients include Corporal Terry Thomson, awarded the medal for his actions in Afghanistan in 2006, and numerous members of special forces and line regiments for gallantry under fire.
