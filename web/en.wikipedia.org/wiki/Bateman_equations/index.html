Bateman equations

In physics, the Bateman equations are a set of first-order differential equations describing the abundances of nuclides in a radioactive decay chain as functions of time. The model was formulated by Ernest Rutherford in 1905 for simple cases and solved in general by the English mathematician Harry Bateman in 1910.

Formulation

For a chain of n nuclides in which each member decays only into the next, the rate of change of the number of atoms of member i is governed by its own decay constant and the production rate from its parent: dNi/dt = −λiNi + λi−1Ni−1. Bateman's solution expresses each abundance as a sum of exponentials weighted by products of the decay constants.

Applications

The equations are fundamental in nuclear engineering and health physics: computing the activity of spent nuclear fuel, the growth and decay of fission products, decay heat after reactor shutdown, and the behaviour of radioactive tracers and generator systems such as the molybdenum-99/technetium-99m generator used in medical imaging.

Numerical considerations

When decay constants are nearly equal, Bateman's closed-form solution suffers catastrophic cancellation, so modern burnup codes often solve the system numerically or use matrix-exponential and linear-chain methods.
