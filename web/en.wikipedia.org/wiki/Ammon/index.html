Ammon is the Greek and Latin form of the name of the ancient Egyptian god Amun (also rendered Amon, Amen), a major deity of the Egyptian pantheon. From his cult centre at Thebes, Amun rose during the Middle and New Kingdoms to become the pre-eminent god of the Egyptian empire, fused with the sun god as Amun-Ra, "King of the Gods".

Cult and oracle

Amun's great temple at Karnak was among the largest religious complexes of the ancient world, and his priesthood became immensely wealthy and powerful. As Zeus Ammon he was worshipped by the Greeks, and his oracle at the Siwa Oasis in the Libyan desert achieved international fame. In 331 BC Alexander the Great made a celebrated pilgrimage to Siwa, where the oracle reportedly greeted him as the son of Zeus Ammon — a title that bolstered his claim to rule Egypt and the wider world.

Iconography

Amun is typically depicted as a man wearing a crown with two tall plumes, or with the head of a ram, his sacred animal, bearing curved horns. The fossil cephalopods known as ammonites were named in the early modern period for their resemblance to the coiled ram's horns of Ammon, "horns of Ammon".
