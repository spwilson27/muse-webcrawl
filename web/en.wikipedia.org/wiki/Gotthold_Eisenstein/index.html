Gotthold Eisenstein

Ferdinand Gotthold Max Eisenstein (16 April 1823 – 11 October 1852) was a German mathematician. He specialized in number theory and analysis, and proved several results that eluded even Carl Friedrich Gauss. Like Galois and Abel before him, Eisenstein died before the age of 30. He was praised by Gauss in superlative terms: according to a famous remark attributed to Gauss, there had been only three epoch-making mathematicians — Archimedes, Newton, and Eisenstein.

Life

Eisenstein was born in Berlin, the son of Johann Konstantin Eisenstein and Helene Pollack. His early life was marked by poor health — he survived meningitis as a child — and by the deaths of all five of his siblings. Despite this, he showed an early aptitude for mathematics and music. He attended the Friedrich Wilhelm Gymnasium and the Friedrich Werder Gymnasium in Berlin, and at 14 was already studying the works of Euler and Lagrange on his own.

In 1843 Eisenstein entered the University of Berlin, where he attended lectures of Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet. That same year, on a visit to see his family in Ireland, he met William Rowan Hamilton, who gave him a copy of his work on Abel's proof of the impossibility of solving the general quintic — an encounter that stimulated Eisenstein's own research. Alexander von Humboldt became his patron and tirelessly sought stipends and positions for him. In 1844, at age 21, Eisenstein published extensively in Crelle's Journal, including his first proof of the law of cubic reciprocity, attracting the attention and support of Gauss. He received an honorary doctorate from the University of Breslau in 1845.

During the revolutions of 1848 Eisenstein was arrested for republican sympathies. Chronic illness, poverty and overwork worsened his condition; he died of tuberculosis in Berlin on 11 October 1852, aged 29. Humboldt, then 83, accompanied his coffin to the cemetery.

Contributions

In a career of barely eight years, Eisenstein made fundamental contributions to several areas:

  • Eisenstein integers: the ring of complex numbers of the form a + bω, where ω is a primitive cube root of unity — a foundational example in algebraic number theory.
  • Eisenstein's criterion: a widely used sufficient condition for a polynomial to be irreducible over the rationals.
  • Reciprocity laws: proofs of cubic and quartic reciprocity and the general "Eisenstein reciprocity" law for power residues in cyclotomic fields, extending Gauss's quadratic reciprocity.
  • Elliptic functions and theta functions: new approaches, including the series that now bear his name (Eisenstein series), which later became central to the theory of modular forms.
  • Eisenstein primes and results on the arithmetic of quadratic forms.

His collected works were published posthumously, and several of his notebook manuscripts were only fully explored much later; André Weil's writings drew renewed attention to the depth of Eisenstein's ideas on elliptic functions.
