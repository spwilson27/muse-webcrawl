Color index

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Colour index measures a star's temperature from its brightness in two filters.

In astronomy, the color index is a simple numerical expression that determines the color of an object, which in the case of a star gives its temperature. The color index is measured by comparing the magnitudes of the object observed through two different filters, such as U (ultraviolet), B (blue), and V (yellow-green, roughly visual).

Definition

The most commonly used index is B − V, the difference between the blue and visual magnitudes. Because magnitudes are logarithmic and decrease with brightness, a hot, blue star (which shines relatively more brightly in the B filter) has a small or negative B − V, while a cool, red star has a large positive value. The Sun has a B − V of about +0.65. Other standard indices include U − B, V − R, and V − I.

Relation to temperature

For main-sequence stars, color index correlates closely with spectral type and effective temperature, by virtue of blackbody radiation: hotter stars emit proportionally more short-wavelength light. The relation is the basis of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, whose horizontal axis is often plotted as B − V. Interstellar dust reddens starlight (making measured indices larger than intrinsic values); this "color excess" must be corrected when determining a star's true temperature and distance.

Other meanings

The name is shared with the Colour Index International, a reference database of dyes and pigments maintained by the Society of Dyers and Colourists and the American Association of Textile Chemists and Colorists, which assigns generic names and constitution numbers to colourants.
