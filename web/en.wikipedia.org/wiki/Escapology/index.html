Escapology

Escapology is the practice of escaping from restraints and confinements as a performance art, made famous by Harry Houdini.

Escapology is the art, practice and study of escaping from physical restraints and confinement, performed as entertainment by an escape artist. Escapes may be secured with handcuffs, chains, ropes, straitjackets, locked boxes, barrels, tanks of water, mail bags, prison cells or purpose-built apparatus, and are typically performed under time pressure and apparent danger.

History

Escape acts grew out of nineteenth-century spiritualist stage shows, in which mediums such as the Davenport Brothers escaped from tied cabinets to suggest supernatural agency, and from variety performers who challenged police and locksmiths. The art was transformed by Harry Houdini (born Erik Weisz, 1874–1926), whose career from the 1890s made him world famous. Houdini escaped from handcuffs of every design, from jails after being searched and locked in naked, from a packing crate submerged in the East River, from the "Chinese Water Torture Cell" — in fact an upside-down water tank whose method he patented in dummy form to protect its secret — and from a straitjacket while suspended by his ankles from cranes above city crowds. His milk-can escape and buried-alive stunts added suffocation suspense. Houdini insisted his feats were accomplished by skill, strength and hidden methods rather than supernatural power, and he spent his later years debunking fraudulent mediums.

Methods and performance

Techniques are closely guarded but include picking or shimming locks, concealing keys and tools, exploiting slack while restraints are applied, dislocating shoulders to slip straitjackets, regurgitating swallowed keys (a genuine and risky skill), and building gimmicked apparatus. Performers distinguish between escapes done in full view, those behind a curtain or screen, and "exhibition escapes" against clocks and apparently lethal conditions. Modern successors include Dorothy Dietrich, billed as the "female Houdini", Dean Gunnarson, who survived escapes from shark cages and alligators, and David Blaine, who blended escapology with endurance stunts. The danger is real: a number of performers have died attempting water and burial escapes.
