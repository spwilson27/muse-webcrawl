Medicinal plants

Medicinal plants, also called medicinal herbs, have been discovered and used in traditional medicine practices since prehistoric times. Plants synthesize hundreds of chemical compounds for functions including defense against insects, fungi, diseases, and herbivorous mammals; many of these compounds have effects in humans and form the basis of modern drugs.

History

The earliest written evidence comes from Sumerian tablets some 5,000 years old listing plant remedies, and the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) records hundreds of herbal medicines. Greek and Roman authors such as Dioscorides, whose De Materia Medica remained authoritative for over 1,500 years, systematized herbal knowledge. Chinese and Indian traditions — traditional Chinese medicine and Ayurveda — developed extensive materia medica independently.

Plants as drug sources

Numerous modern pharmaceuticals derive from plants or from plant compounds:

  • Aspirin, from salicin in willow bark and meadowsweet.
  • Morphine and codeine, from the opium poppy.
  • Quinine, from the bark of the cinchona tree, long used against malaria.
  • Digoxin, from foxglove.
  • Artemisinin, from sweet wormwood, now a frontline antimalarial.
  • Paclitaxel (Taxol), originally from the Pacific yew, used in chemotherapy.

Modern use and concerns

The World Health Organization estimates that a large share of the world's population relies on traditional herbal medicine for primary care. However, herbal products raise issues of variable potency, contamination, adulteration, and interactions with conventional drugs; in many countries they are regulated as supplements rather than medicines. Pharmacognosy, the study of drugs from natural sources, continues to screen plants for new leads.
