Parrotfish

Parrotfish are a group of about 95 species of marine fish traditionally forming the family Scaridae, now often placed within the wrasse family Labridae. They live in shallow tropical and subtropical waters worldwide, especially on coral reefs. Their name comes from their beak-like fused teeth, resembling a parrot's beak.

Description

Parrotfish are typically brightly coloured, and many species change colour and pattern — and sex — during their lives, passing through juvenile, initial and terminal phases. Most are herbivores that scrape algae from coral and rock, grinding the ingested material with pharyngeal teeth; the resulting fine sand is excreted, making parrotfish major producers of tropical beach sand. Larger species such as the humphead parrotfish can exceed 1 metre in length.

Ecology

By grazing algae, parrotfish help prevent algae from smothering coral, making them keystone species on reefs. Some species secrete a mucus cocoon at night, thought to mask their scent from predators. Parrotfish are fished for food in many regions, and protecting them is a reef-conservation priority in the Caribbean.
