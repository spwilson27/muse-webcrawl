Hardy–Weinberg principle

Hardy–Weinberg principle
Named afterG. H. Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg (1908)
FieldPopulation genetics
Equationp² + 2pq + q² = 1

The Hardy–Weinberg principle, also known as the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, model, theorem, or law, states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of other evolutionary influences.

Conditions

The principle assumes an infinitely large population with random mating and no mutation, migration, or natural selection. Under these conditions, if the frequencies of two alleles A and a are p and q, then the genotype frequencies in the next generation are given by p² (AA), 2pq (Aa), and q² (aa), with p + q = 1.

Derivation

Because mating is random, the frequency of each genotype is the product of the probabilities of the alleles contributed by each parent, which follows from expanding the binomial (p + q)². The result generalises to multiple alleles and polyploid cases.

Applications

The Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium provides a null hypothesis for detecting evolution in real populations: departures from the expected genotype frequencies indicate that one or more of the assumptions, such as absence of selection or random mating, has been violated. It is also used to estimate carrier frequencies of recessive genetic disorders.
