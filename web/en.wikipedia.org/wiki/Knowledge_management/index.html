Knowledge management

[Image placeholder: diagram of knowledge creation, storage, sharing, and application]

Knowledge management (KM) is the process by which an organisation creates, shares, uses, and manages its knowledge and information. It is an interdisciplinary field drawing on business administration, information systems, management science, and library science, and it emerged as a distinct discipline in the early 1990s.

Concepts

A central distinction is between explicit knowledge, which can be written down and transferred in documents and databases, and tacit knowledge, the know-how embedded in people's experience. Ikujiro Nonaka's SECI model describes knowledge creation as a cycle of socialisation, externalisation, combination, and internalisation. Common KM practices include lessons-learned reviews, communities of practice, expertise directories, wikis, and structured repositories.

History and practice

Early KM initiatives were driven by information technology — intranets, document management, and groupware — and consultants such as Peter Drucker, who emphasised the rise of the "knowledge worker". Later work stressed that knowledge is embedded in people and culture, so successful KM combines technology with organisational processes and incentives. KM supports innovation, decision-making, and the retention of expertise when employees leave, and it underpins approaches such as organisational learning.
