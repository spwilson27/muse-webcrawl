Nylon 66

Nylon 66 (loosely written nylon 6-6, nylon 6/6 or nylon 6,6) is a type of polyamide or nylon. It, and nylon 6, are the two most common for textile and plastic industries. Nylon 66 is made of two monomers each containing 6 carbon atoms, hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid, which give nylon 66 its name.

Synthesis

Hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid are combined in water to form "nylon salt", which is then polymerised in a polycondensation reaction, releasing water as the amide bonds form. The polymer can be extruded and granulated or directly spun into fibres. Nylon 66 was developed at DuPont following the work of Wallace Carothers, whose team produced the polymer in 1935; it was commercialised in the late 1930s, initially famous for women's stockings.

Properties and uses

Nylon 66 has a higher melting point (about 265 °C) and is somewhat stronger and more abrasion-resistant than nylon 6, owing to its more ordered hydrogen bonding. It is used in fibres for textiles and carpets, and in engineering plastics for gears, bearings, bushings, automotive parts and cable ties, where its toughness and resistance to heat and chemicals are valued.
