In mathematics, the axis–angle representation of a rotation in three-dimensional space parameterises the rotation by two quantities: a unit vector e indicating the direction of the rotation axis, and an angle θ describing the magnitude of the rotation about that axis. The equivalence of every rotation to a single axis–angle pair is guaranteed by Euler's rotation theorem. Only two of the three components of e are independent, since its length is fixed at one, so a rotation is described by three degrees of freedom in total.

Rotation vector

Multiplying the unit axis by the angle gives the rotation vector θe, whose direction is the rotation axis and whose magnitude is the angle. Rotation vectors are widely used in computer vision, robotics and physics because of this compactness, though they are singular and multi-valued: adding 2π to θ, or negating both axis and angle, yields the same rotation.

Rodrigues' rotation formula

Given a vector v, the rotated vector can be computed directly by Rodrigues' rotation formula, named after Olinde Rodrigues (1840):

vrot = v cos θ + (e × v) sin θ + e(e · v)(1 − cos θ).

The same formula generates the corresponding rotation matrix R = I cos θ + (sin θ)[e]× + (1 − cos θ)eeT, where [e]× is the cross-product matrix of the axis.

Relation to other representations

The axis–angle pair converts directly to a unit quaternion via q = (cos(θ/2), e sin(θ/2)), and to Euler angles through the rotation matrix. The matrix exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix built from the rotation vector gives the rotation (via the exponential map of the Lie group SO(3), whose Lie algebra so(3) consists of rotation vectors); conversely the matrix logarithm recovers the axis and angle, with θ = arccos((trace(R) − 1)/2).

Applications

Axis–angle parameters appear in rigid-body dynamics and attitude control of spacecraft, in crystallography, in robotics for specifying end-effector orientation, and in computer graphics as an alternative to Euler angles that avoids gimbal lock in interpolation tasks, though quaternions are generally preferred for composing rotations.
