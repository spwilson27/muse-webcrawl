Woodblock printing or block printing is a technique for printing text, images, or patterns in which a design is carved in relief into a block of wood, the raised surface inked, and paper or fabric pressed against it. Originating in China, it spread throughout East Asia and to Europe, and was for centuries the principal method of reproducing books and images before and alongside movable type.

History in East Asia

Printing on cloth with blocks existed in China by the Han dynasty, and by the Tang dynasty the method was applied to paper. The earliest surviving dated printed book is the Diamond Sutra of 868, a complete scroll with an elaborate frontispiece. In the Song dynasty, woodblock printing underpinned a flourishing book trade. The technique spread to Korea and Japan, where it reached an artistic summit in the polychrome ukiyo-e prints of the Edo period, produced from multiple registered blocks, one per colour.

Technique

A block maker pastes a reversed drawing or text onto a flat plank (traditionally cherry, pear, or jujube wood), and carvers cut away the non-printing areas with knives and chisels. Ink — water-based in East Asia, oil-based in Europe — is applied to the relief surface; paper is laid on top and rubbed with a baren (in Japan) or pressed in a press (in Europe) to transfer the image. A separate block is carved for each page and each colour.

Europe and later use

In Europe, woodblock printing appeared in the late 14th century for playing cards, devotional images, and "block books". Woodcut became a major fine-art medium through artists such as Albrecht Dürer, and wood engraving later served book and newspaper illustration. Although letterpress and lithography displaced block printing commercially, it survives as an artistic printmaking technique worldwide.
