Interferon

Interferons (IFNs) are a group of signalling proteins (cytokines) made and released by host cells in response to the presence of viruses, bacteria, parasites and tumour cells. They belong to the large class of proteins known as cytokines and are named for their ability to "interfere" with viral replication. Interferons trigger protective defences of the immune system — in particular, they induce an antiviral state in neighbouring cells, increase antigen presentation, activate natural killer cells and macrophages, and modulate the adaptive immune response.

Discovery and types

Interferon was discovered in 1957 by Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann at the National Institute for Medical Research in London, who found that influenza-infected cells released a factor that protected other cells from infection. Three types are now recognised. Type I interferons, including IFN-α (many subtypes) and IFN-β, are produced by most cells in response to viral patterns detected by receptors such as RIG-I, MDA5 and Toll-like receptors, and signal through the IFNAR receptor. Type II interferon consists of IFN-γ, produced mainly by T cells and natural killer cells, which is primarily immunomodulatory and macrophage-activating. Type III interferons (IFN-λ family), discovered in 2003, act especially at epithelial surfaces.

Mechanism

Interferon binding activates the JAK-STAT signalling pathway, leading to expression of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes. Their products include protein kinase R (which halts protein synthesis), oligoadenylate synthetase and RNase L (which degrade viral RNA), and Mx proteins that block viral replication. Viruses have in turn evolved numerous interferon-antagonist proteins, reflecting an evolutionary arms race.

Medical uses

Recombinant and pegylated interferons have been used therapeutically: IFN-α for hepatitis B and C (largely superseded by direct-acting antivirals) and some cancers; IFN-β as a disease-modifying therapy for relapsing multiple sclerosis; and IFN-γ for chronic granulomatous disease. Treatment is often limited by flu-like side effects, fatigue, cytopenias and depression. Inherited or acquired defects in interferon immunity — including autoantibodies against type I interferons — predispose to severe viral disease, a finding that gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic.
