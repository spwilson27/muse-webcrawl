Vocoder

A vocoder (a portmanteau of voice and encoder) is a category of speech coding that analyses and synthesises the human voice signal for audio data compression, multiplexing, voice encryption or voice transformation. Originally developed in the 1930s as a speech coder for telecommunications, it became famous as a musical effect that makes a voice sound robotic by imposing the spectral character of the voice on a synthesiser sound.

History

The vocoder was developed at Bell Telephone Laboratories by Homer Dudley, who patented it in the late 1930s, as a way to reduce the bandwidth needed to transmit speech. It analysed speech into slowly varying control signals describing the spectral envelope and pitch, which could be sent over a narrow channel and used to resynthesise intelligible speech. Its most famous early application was SIGSALY, the secure voice system used for encrypted transatlantic communication between Allied leaders during the Second World War. Dudley's related Voder, demonstrated at the 1939 New York World's Fair, was operated manually by keyboard.

How it works

In a channel vocoder, the input signal passes through a bank of band-pass filters; the envelope of each band is measured and transmitted. At the receiver, the envelopes control matching filters excited by a buzz (for voiced sounds) or hiss (for unvoiced sounds). Modern descendants include linear predictive coding (LPC), used in digital mobile telephony and in the Speak & Spell toy. Related but distinct is phase vocoding, used for time-stretching audio.

Musical use

In music, the vocoder's modulator is typically a voice (or drums) and the carrier a synthesiser. Wendy Carlos and Robert Moog built a vocoder used on the soundtrack of A Clockwork Orange (1971). Kraftwerk made the robotic vocoder voice a signature of electronic music; it appears in works by Herbie Hancock, Electric Light Orchestra ("Mr. Blue Sky"), Afrika Bambaataa, and Daft Punk. The vocoder is often confused with the talk box and with pitch-correction effects such as Auto-Tune, which work differently.
