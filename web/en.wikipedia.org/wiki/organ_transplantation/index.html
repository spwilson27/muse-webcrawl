Organ transplantation

FieldTransplant surgery
Common organsKidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas, intestine
First successKidney transplant, 1954

Organ transplantation is a medical procedure in which an organ is removed from one body and placed in the body of a recipient, to replace a damaged or missing organ. The donor and recipient may be at the same location, or organs may be transported from a donor site to another location.

History

The first successful organ transplant was a kidney transplant between identical twins performed by Joseph Murray and colleagues in Boston in 1954. The first successful heart transplant was carried out by Christiaan Barnard in Cape Town in 1967. The introduction of ciclosporin in the 1980s greatly improved graft survival by suppressing rejection.

Types

Transplants include autografts (within the same person), allografts (between genetically different individuals of the same species), and xenografts (between species). Kidneys are the most commonly transplanted organ, followed by livers and hearts.

Rejection and immunosuppression

The recipient's immune system may attack the graft; recipients therefore take immunosuppressive drugs, often for life, balanced against the risk of infection and other side effects. Tissue typing and cross-matching reduce the risk of hyperacute rejection.

Ethics and allocation

Because demand far exceeds supply, allocation systems prioritize medical urgency, compatibility, and waiting time. Ethical debates cover organ markets, presumed consent, and donation after circulatory death.
