Statehood movement in the District of Columbia

The "Taxation Without Representation" license plate, adopted in 2000.

The District of Columbia statehood movement is a political movement that advocates making the District of Columbia a U.S. state, in order to provide the residents of the District with voting representation in Congress and complete control over local affairs. The District's roughly 700,000 residents — more than the populations of Wyoming and Vermont — pay federal taxes but elect only a non-voting delegate to the House of Representatives and no senators.

Background

Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution grants Congress authority over a federal district "not exceeding ten Miles square" that serves as the seat of government. The District of Columbia was formed in 1790 from land ceded by Maryland and Virginia (the Virginia portion was retroceded in 1846). Residents lost their congressional voting rights in 1801. The Twenty-third Amendment (1961) granted the District electoral votes for president, and the District of Columbia Home Rule Act (1973) established an elected mayor and council, though Congress retains the power to review and overturn local legislation.

The movement

Supporters argue that statehood is a civil rights issue: "taxation without representation" was a rallying cry of the American Revolution, and District license plates have carried the phrase since 2000. In 1982, District voters ratified a constitution for a proposed state to be called New Columbia; the D.C. Statehood Party, founded by Julius Hobson in 1970, long championed the cause. In a 2016 referendum, 85.7% of District voters supported statehood.

Recent proposals would admit the residential and commercial areas of the District as the 51st state — under the name Washington, Douglass Commonwealth — while shrinking the federal district to a core containing the Capitol, White House, and National Mall. The Washington, D.C. Admission Act passed the U.S. House of Representatives in 2020 and again in 2021, the first times a statehood bill had passed either chamber, but stalled in the Senate.

Arguments against

Opponents argue that the Constitution requires a constitutional amendment rather than ordinary legislation, that the Founders intended the capital to be independent of any state, and that admission would be an unconstitutional power grab or unfair partisan advantage. Some propose retroceding the residential parts of the District to Maryland as an alternative.
