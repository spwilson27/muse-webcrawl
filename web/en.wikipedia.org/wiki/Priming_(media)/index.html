Priming (media)

Priming is a theory in media effects and political communication research which holds that the mass media influence audiences by suggesting which issues, traits, or considerations should be used as the standards by which to evaluate public figures, policies, and events. When the media devote heavy coverage to an issue — for example, foreign policy or the economy — audiences tend to judge a president's overall performance primarily on that issue, even without changing their underlying opinions.

[Image: Media priming diagram]

Origins

The concept was developed from psychological research on priming — the process by which recently or frequently activated mental concepts become more accessible and more likely to be used in interpreting new information. Political scientists Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder applied the idea in their 1987 book News That Matters, using experiments showing that television news coverage altered the criteria by which viewers evaluated the President of the United States.

Relationship to other media effects

Priming is closely related to agenda-setting, which concerns the media's ability to influence which issues the public considers important. Agenda-setting is often described as telling people "what to think about", while priming concerns the downstream consequence: those salient issues become the benchmarks for evaluating political actors. Framing research, by contrast, examines how the presentation of an issue affects the interpretation of that issue itself.

Applications and findings

Priming effects have been demonstrated in studies of presidential approval, voting behaviour, and evaluations of economic and foreign policy. The effect depends on the volume and recency of coverage and tends to decay over time. Critics note that effects measured in laboratory settings may be weaker or shorter-lived in the real media environment, where audiences encounter competing messages.
