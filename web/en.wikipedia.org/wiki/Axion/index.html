The axion is a hypothetical elementary particle postulated to solve the strong CP problem in quantum chromodynamics and regarded as one of the leading candidates for dark matter. The Peccei–Quinn theory of 1977 introduced a new symmetry whose breaking would dynamically cancel the CP-violating term of the strong interaction; Steven Weinberg and Frank Wilczek independently noted in 1978 that the theory implied a new, very light particle. Wilczek named it the "axion" after a brand of laundry detergent, because it "cleaned up" a problem.

Properties and dark matter

Axions are expected to be electrically neutral, extremely light (possibly in the micro-electronvolt range), and very weakly interacting. In the early universe they would have been produced non-thermally, in enormous numbers, and would still pervade the cosmos as a cold, coherent field — behaving gravitationally like dark matter while almost never interacting otherwise.

Searches

The principal search strategy exploits the predicted axion–photon coupling: in a strong magnetic field, a dark-matter axion can convert into a microwave photon. The ADMX experiment at the University of Washington uses a resonant cavity (a haloscope) inside a large magnet — an instrumental philosophy reminiscent of other great underground and precision detectors such as Super-Kamiokande — and has progressively excluded swathes of the favoured mass range. Complementary helioscopes (CAST, and the planned IAXO) look for axions produced in the Sun, and light-shining-through-a-wall experiments probe axion-like particles in the laboratory. No confirmed detection has been made.
