Herringbone pattern

The herringbone pattern is an arrangement of rectangles used for floor tilings and road paving; the name derives from its fancied resemblance to the skeleton of a herring. The blocks are laid at right angles to one another, each rectangle's end abutting the side of its neighbour, producing a zigzag, broken pattern.

Geometry

A perfect herringbone requires rectangles whose length is an integer multiple of their width (commonly 2:1 or 3:1) so that blocks meet exactly without gaps. The pattern has glide-reflection and two-fold rotational symmetry and can be extended indefinitely across a plane.

Uses

Herringbone paving was used in Roman roads (opus spicatum) and is still prized because interlocking blocks resist creep under traffic. In wood flooring, herringbone parquet creates a classic zigzag distinct from the straight chevron pattern, in which planks are cut at an angle to meet point-to-point. The pattern also appears in brickwork bonds and in tweed and twill textiles, where herringbone cloth is a staple of suiting.

Herringbone gear

In engineering, a herringbone gear combines two opposed helical gears to cancel axial thrust; the French carmaker Citroën adopted the double-chevron logo from the herringbone gears its founder manufactured.
