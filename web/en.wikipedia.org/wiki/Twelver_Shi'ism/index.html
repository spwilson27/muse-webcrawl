Twelver Shi'ism (Arabic: ٱثْنَا عَشَرِيَّة, Ithnā ʿAshariyya), also known as Imamiyyah, is the largest branch of Shia Islam, comprising roughly 85 percent of all Shia Muslims. Its name refers to its belief in the Twelve Imams, divinely guided descendants of the Prophet Muhammad through his cousin and son-in-law Ali ibn Abi Talib. Twelvers constitute the majority of the population in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, and Bahrain, with significant communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India, and elsewhere.

The Twelve Imams

The line of Imams begins with Ali and passes through his sons Hasan and Husayn, then through Husayn's descendants, ending with Muhammad al-Mahdi, the twelfth Imam. Twelvers believe the Mahdi went into occultation (ghayba) in 874 CE — first the Minor Occultation, mediated by four deputies, then the Major Occultation, which continues today — and will return as the Mahdi to establish justice before the Day of Judgment.

Beliefs and practice

Twelver theology (Usul al-Din) rests on five principles: divine unity (tawhid), prophethood (nubuwwa), divine justice (adl), leadership (imama), and resurrection (ma'ad). Religious law is the Ja'fari school; most Twelvers follow a living marja' al-taqlid (source of emulation), a practice developed in the Usuli school. Central observances include the mourning of Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala during Muharram (Ashura) and Arba'in, and shrine pilgrimages to Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad, and Qom.
