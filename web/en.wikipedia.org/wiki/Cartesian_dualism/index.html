Cartesian dualism is the form of mind–body dualism defended by the French philosopher René Descartes (1596–1650). It holds that reality consists of two fundamentally different kinds of substance: mind (res cogitans, a "thinking thing", unextended and indivisible) and matter (res extensa, an "extended thing", divisible and governed by mechanical laws), and that in human beings these two substances interact.

Arguments

In the Meditations on First Philosophy Descartes argued from the indubitability of his own existence as a thinking thing (cogito ergo sum) and from his clear and distinct idea of mind as non-extended and of body as extended: since he could conceive of mind existing without body, and God could bring about whatever is clearly conceived, mind and body must be really distinct substances. From his physics he concluded that non-human animals, lacking minds, are mere automata, while the human body is a machine directed by an immaterial soul.

The interaction problem

The most famous objection, pressed on Descartes by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in 1643, is how an unextended, immaterial mind can causally move a material body. Descartes proposed that interaction occurred in the pineal gland, a small unpaired structure in the brain, but critics from his own century to Gilbert Ryle — who mocked the view as the "ghost in the machine" — have regarded the proposal as unsatisfying. Cartesian dualism nevertheless set the terms of the modern mind–body problem and remains the standard example of substance dualism against which physicalist theories define themselves.
