Anacyclosis (Ancient Greek: ἀνακύκλωσις, "recycling" or "cycle of revolution") is a cyclical theory of political evolution developed by the Greek historian Polybius in Book VI of his Histories. The theory proposes that forms of government follow a natural sequence of growth and decay, each degenerate form giving way to the next in a recurring cycle.

The cycle

According to Polybius, primitive monarchy — rule by a single just leader — corrupts into tyranny. The tyrant is overthrown by the noblest citizens, establishing an aristocracy, which decays into oligarchy, rule by a wealthy few in their own interest. The people then revolt and establish democracy, which in time degenerates into ochlocracy, mob rule. From the chaos of ochlocracy a single strong leader eventually emerges, and the cycle begins again. Polybius argued that Rome's mixed constitution, combining consuls (monarchical), Senate (aristocratic) and assemblies (democratic), checked this cycle and explained Roman stability.

Influence

The theory influenced later political thinkers including Cicero, Machiavelli, and, through the classical tradition, the framers of modern republican constitutions who sought institutional balances against decay. It remains a reference point in discussions of democratic backsliding.
