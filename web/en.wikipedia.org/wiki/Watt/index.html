The watt (symbol: W) is the unit of power or radiant flux in the International System of Units (SI), equal to 1 joule per second or 1 kg⋅m²⋅s⁻³. It is used to quantify the rate of energy transfer. The watt is named in honour of James Watt (1736–1819), the Scottish inventor and mechanical engineer whose improvements to the steam engine drove the Industrial Revolution.

Definition

In terms of electromagnetism, one watt is the rate at which electrical work is performed when a current of one ampere flows across an electrical potential difference of one volt: W = V × A. Multiples in common use include the kilowatt (10³ W), megawatt (10⁶ W) and gigawatt (10⁹ W); for very small signals the milliwatt and microwatt are used.

Examples

A typical household light bulb draws 5–100 watts; a human being at rest radiates roughly 100 watts of heat; a large power station generates thousands of megawatts. The watt should not be confused with the watt-hour, a unit of energy equal to one watt sustained for one hour.
