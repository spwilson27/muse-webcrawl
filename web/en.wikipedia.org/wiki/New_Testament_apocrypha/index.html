New Testament apocrypha

The New Testament apocrypha (singular: apocryphon) are a number of writings by early Christians that give accounts of Jesus, his disciples and their teaching, but were not accepted into the orthodox canon of the New Testament. Some of these works were produced by orthodox communities to fill in gaps in the canonical accounts; others come from groups later considered heretical, including Gnostic Christians. The term derives from the Greek apokrypha, "hidden things".

Categories

Gospels

Apocryphal gospels include infancy gospels, such as the Infancy Gospel of Thomas and the Protoevangelium of James — the latter a major source for traditions about Mary's life and perpetual virginity; Jewish-Christian gospels; passion and resurrection accounts such as the Gospel of Peter and the Gospel of Nicodemus (with its account of the Harrowing of Hell); and Gnostic gospels such as the Gospel of Thomas, a collection of 114 sayings of Jesus found complete at Nag Hammadi in 1945, and the Gospel of Mary and Gospel of Judas.

Acts

The apocryphal Acts tell romanticised adventures of the apostles, often containing encratite (anti-marriage) teaching: the Acts of Paul (including the story of Thecla), Acts of Peter, Acts of John, Acts of Andrew and Acts of Thomas — the last preserving the Hymn of the Pearl and the earliest traditions of Thomas's mission to India.

Epistles and apocalypses

Pseudepigraphal letters include the correspondence between Paul and Seneca and the Epistle to the Laodiceans. Apocalyptic works include the Apocalypse of Peter, once nearly canonical and containing vivid tours of heaven and hell, and the Shepherd of Hermas, a manual of repentance highly regarded in the second century and included in Codex Sinaiticus.

History of study

The church fathers discussed many of these works while distinguishing genuine from spurious writings. Most apocrypha survived only in fragments or in translation until modern discoveries, above all the Nag Hammadi library (1945), transformed knowledge of the diversity of early Christianity. Standard modern collections include the editions by Wilhelm Schneemelcher and the English translation edited by J. K. Elliott, The Apocryphal New Testament (1993).
