MPEG-1

MPEG-1 is a standard for lossy compression of video and audio. It is designed to compress VHS-quality raw digital video and CD audio down to about 1.5 Mbit/s (26:1 and 6:1 compression ratios respectively) without excessive quality loss, making Video CDs, digital cable/satellite TV and digital audio broadcasting (DAB) practical.

It was developed by the Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG) and published as the international standard ISO/IEC 11172 in 1993. Today, MPEG-1 is the most widely compatible lossy audio/video format in the world, and is used in a large number of products and technologies. Perhaps the best-known part of the MPEG-1 standard is the MP3 audio format it introduced.

Parts

  • Part 1 (Systems) — defines how video and audio are multiplexed into a single program stream.
  • Part 2 (Video) — the video compression format, based on block motion compensation and the discrete cosine transform (DCT), using I-frames, P-frames and B-frames. It was designed for source input format (SIF) resolutions of 352×240 (NTSC) or 352×288 (PAL).
  • Part 3 (Audio) — defines Layer I, Layer II and Layer III (MP3) audio coding.
  • Part 4 (Conformance testing) and Part 5 (Reference software).

History

Work on the standard began in 1988, building on the H.261 video codec and the JPEG still-image standard. MPEG-1 video was optimised for bitrates of roughly 1.5 Mbit/s so that it would fit the data rate of a single-speed CD-ROM and the Video CD format launched in 1993.

Successors

MPEG-1 was followed by MPEG-2, used for DVDs and broadcast television, and later by MPEG-4 and its successors. See MPEG-4 Part 14 for the popular MP4 container format.
