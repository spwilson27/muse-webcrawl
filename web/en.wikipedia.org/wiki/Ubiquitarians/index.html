Ubiquitarians

The Ubiquitarians, also called Ubiquists, were a Lutheran theological party in the 16th century who held the doctrine of the ubiquity of Christ's human body: that the body of Jesus Christ is present everywhere, including in the Eucharist at every celebration, rather than being confined to a single place in heaven. The name derives from the Latin ubique, "everywhere".

The Lutheran doctrine of Christ's real presence in the Eucharist lay at the centre of the ubiquitarian controversy.

Doctrine and origins

The doctrine was developed in support of the Lutheran teaching of the real presence: because Christ's human nature is personally united with his divine nature (the communicatio idiomatum), it participates in the divine attribute of omnipresence. Johannes Brenz and Martin Chemnitz were among its leading proponents. The teaching allowed Lutherans to affirm that Christ's body is truly present "in, with, and under" the elements of the sacrament wherever it is celebrated.

Controversy

Ubiquity was fiercely opposed by Reformed theologians, who argued that a true human body must be locally circumscribed, and Christ's body is in heaven. Within Lutheranism the question also divided parties, contributing to the intra-Lutheran controversies of the period after Luther's death. The Formula of Concord (1577), a foundational Lutheran confession, endorsed the doctrine in measured terms, affirming that Christ is present in the sacrament by virtue of the personal union without teaching a crude local inclusion of his body in the bread.
