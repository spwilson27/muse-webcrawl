Evolutionary developmental biology

Evolutionary developmental biology, widely known as evo-devo, is a field of biology that compares the developmental processes of different organisms to infer their evolutionary relationships and to understand how development itself evolves. Its central discovery, arising in the 1980s and 1990s, is that a relatively small "toolkit" of highly conserved genes — especially Hox and other transcription-factor genes — patterns the bodies of animals as different as fruit flies and humans.

Key concepts

Research on the homeobox (Hox) gene clusters showed that homologous genes control corresponding body regions across bilaterians ("deep homology"): the same gene, Pax6/eyeless, helps initiate eye development in flies, mice, and squid, whose eyes evolved independently. Evo-devo explains much evolutionary change as changes in the timing (heterochrony), place (heterotopy), and regulatory wiring of these toolkit genes, rather than the invention of wholly new genes. Cis-regulatory changes — mutations in the switches controlling where and when a gene is expressed — allow evolution to tinker with one body part without breaking others.

History and significance

The field builds on nineteenth-century comparative embryology (von Baer's laws; Haeckel's contested "biogenetic law") and on the embryological interests partly sidelined by the Modern Synthesis. Popularized by works such as Sean B. Carroll's Endless Forms Most Beautiful (2005), evo-devo is a pillar of the "extended evolutionary synthesis" debate and informs research on the origin of animal body plans in the Cambrian explosion.
