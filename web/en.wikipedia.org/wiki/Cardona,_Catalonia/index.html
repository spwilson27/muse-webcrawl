Cardona, Catalonia

Cardona is a town and municipality in the comarca of Bages, province of Barcelona, in Catalonia, Spain. It is famous for two landmarks: the great medieval Castle of Cardona, seat of the powerful Dukes of Cardona, and the Muntanya de Sal, a mountain of rock salt and potash that has been mined since antiquity. The town lies on the river Cardener, a tributary of the Llobregat.

History

Salt from Cardona's diapiric salt mountain was prized from Roman times and made the local lords rich: the viscounts, then counts, then dukes of Cardona — the Folch de Cardona family — became one of the richest and most powerful houses of the Crown of Aragon, known as the "uncrowned kings" of Catalonia. The castle, begun in the 9th century, houses the Romanesque church of Sant Vicenç (consecrated 1040), a jewel of Catalan Lombard Romanesque architecture. During the War of the Spanish Succession, Cardona was the last Catalan stronghold to surrender to Bourbon forces, holding out until 18 September 1714, a week after Barcelona fell, and its defence is commemorated in Catalan national memory. In 1714 the castle also withstood siege; it later served as a military garrison and prison.

The salt mountain

The Muntanya de Sal is a salt diapir outcropping at the surface, composed of halite, sylvite and carnallite. Industrial potash mining in the 20th century (from the 1930s) transformed the local economy, leaving terraced spoil heaps and vast underground galleries. The mine closed in 1990 and the mountain is now a cultural park, where visitors tour the old galleries and the open-air salt formations, which weather into fantastic shapes.

Sights

  • Castle of Cardona — now a parador (state hotel); includes the church of Sant Vicenç, the Minyona tower and 11th-century ducal palace.
  • Salt Mountain Cultural Park — guided visits into the former mine.
  • Old town — the Gothic church of Sant Miquel and the Portal de Graells, a surviving town gate.
