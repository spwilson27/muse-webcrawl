Hibiscus is a large genus of flowering plants in the mallow family, Malvaceae, comprising several hundred species of herbs, shrubs and small trees native to warm temperate, subtropical and tropical regions worldwide. The genus is famous for its large, showy, trumpet-shaped flowers, each typically lasting only a day, with five or more petals and a characteristic long central column of fused stamens surrounding the style. Hibiscus flowers are widely grown as ornamentals and several species have culinary, medicinal and fibre uses.

Notable species

  • Hibiscus rosa-sinensis — Chinese hibiscus, the classic tropical garden hibiscus and a parent of thousands of cultivars; the national flower of Malaysia (bunga raya) and a symbol of Haiti.
  • Hibiscus syriacus — rose of Sharon, a hardy deciduous shrub and the national flower of South Korea (mugunghwa).
  • Hibiscus sabdariffa — roselle, whose red calyces are used for drinks such as hibiscus tea, sorrel and agua de jamaica, rich in vitamin C and anthocyanins.
  • Hibiscus cannabinus — kenaf, grown for bast fibre; H. sabdariffa is also a fibre source.
  • Hibiscus tiliaceus — sea hibiscus, a coastal tree of tropical shores.

Cultivation and uses

Tropical species require warmth and are grown outdoors in frost-free climates or under glass elsewhere; hardy species such as H. syriacus and H. moscheutos suit temperate gardens. Beyond ornament, hibiscus is used in herbal teas and traditional medicine, as a natural food colouring, in cosmetics and hair care in South Asia, and its fibres in rope and paper. The flower is the state flower of Hawaii (the native yellow H. brackenridgei) and a widely recognised emblem of the tropics.
