Log cabin

A log cabin is a small log house, especially a less finished or architecturally sophisticated structure. Log cabins have an ancient history in Europe, and in America are often associated with first-generation home building by settlers. Construction with logs was used in Rome by Vitruvius, and remains common in heavily wooded regions of Scandinavia, Russia, Eastern Europe, and North America.

Log cabin
Materials: Horizontal logs, interlocked at corners with notches
Regions: North America, Scandinavia, Eastern Europe
Sym­bolism: Humility and frontier origins in US politics

Construction

Logs are stacked horizontally and interlocked at the corners with notches (such as saddle or dovetail notches); the gaps between logs were traditionally filled with chinking of stones, wood, or moss, and later with daubing of clay or mortar. Roofs were typically gabled and covered with shingles or sod; early cabins had packed-earth or puncheon (split-log) floors.

History in North America

Swedish settlers in New Sweden (from 1638) brought log construction to the Delaware Valley; German and Scots-Irish settlers spread the form westward. Log cabins were the archetypal frontier dwelling of American settlement, and Abraham Lincoln's birth in a Kentucky log cabin cemented its place in American political iconography — the 1840 presidential campaign of William Henry Harrison popularised the "log cabin and hard cider" image, and seven US presidents were born in log cabins, more than in any other kind of residence.

Modern use

Today log cabins are built as rustic second homes, resort lodging, and park structures — notably by the Civilian Conservation Corps in US national parks in the 1930s — using both handcrafted full-scribe methods and milled-log kits.
