Mordecai

Mordecai (Hebrew: מָרְדֳּכַי, Mordokhai) is one of the main personalities in the Book of Esther in the Hebrew Bible. He is the cousin and adoptive guardian of Esther, the Jewish queen of the Persian king Ahasuerus (usually identified with Xerxes I). His name is generally thought to derive from Marduk, the chief god of Babylon, and his lineage is given as a Benjaminite, son of Jair, of the family of Kish — connecting him to the house of King Saul.

In the Book of Esther

Mordecai sits at the king's gate in Susa, where he overhears two chamberlains plotting to assassinate the king and reports the plot through Esther — a deed recorded in the royal chronicles and later the cause of his reward. When the king's chief minister Haman is elevated, all bow to him except Mordecai, whose refusal — tied in the narrative to the ancient enmity between Israel and Agag the Amalekite, Haman's ancestor — provokes Haman to plot the destruction of all the Jews of the empire on a date chosen by lot (pur, hence Purim).

Mordecai urges Esther to intercede, telling her she may have come to her royal position "for such a time as this". After Esther's banquets expose Haman, the minister is hanged on the gallows he had built for Mordecai. Mordecai is then led through the city in royal apparel on the king's horse — the honour Haman had hoped for himself — and is raised to Haman's office. With Esther, he issues the counter-edict allowing the Jews to defend themselves, and institutes the festival of Purim "for the Jews of his time" and their descendants. The book closes with Mordecai "great among the Jews" and seeking the good of his people.

Traditions and historicity

Jewish tradition (the Talmud and midrash) magnifies Mordecai's role: he is counted among the prophets and members of the Great Assembly, and the Fast of Esther and the reading of the Megillah are attached to his story. Scholars debate the historicity of the Esther narrative; a cuneiform attestation of an official named Marduka at the Persian court in Susa has sometimes been associated with him, and the name Mordecai was common among Babylonian Jews.
