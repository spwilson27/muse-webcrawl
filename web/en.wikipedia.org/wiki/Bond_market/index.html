Bond market

The bond market (also called the debt market or credit market) is a financial market in which participants issue new debt, in the primary market, or buy and sell existing debt securities, in the secondary market. It is dominated by government bonds, such as United States Treasuries, and also includes corporate bonds, municipal bonds, mortgage-backed securities, and other instruments. With total global outstandings well over US$100 trillion, the bond market is larger than the global stock market.

Structure

In the primary market, issuers sell new bonds directly to investors, often through underwriting syndicates or auctions, as with Treasury securities. Most secondary trading takes place over the counter through networks of dealers rather than on centralized exchanges. Prices move inversely to yields: when interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall, and vice versa. The yield curve—the plot of yields against maturity—is a key indicator watched for signals about economic growth, inflation, and recession risk.

Participants and risk

Participants include governments, corporations, institutional investors such as pension funds and insurers, central banks, and bond funds. Credit rating agencies grade issuers by default risk; high-yield ("junk") bonds offer higher returns in exchange for greater risk. Bonds are generally considered less volatile than equities, though episodes such as the 1994 bond market crisis, the 2013 "taper tantrum," and the 2022 global bond rout show they can fall sharply when interest rates rise quickly.
