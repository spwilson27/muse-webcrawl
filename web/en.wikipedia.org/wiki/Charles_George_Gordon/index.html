Charles George Gordon

Major-General Charles George Gordon CB (28 January 1833 – 26 January 1885), also known as Chinese Gordon, Gordon Pasha, and Gordon of Khartoum, was a British Army officer and administrator. He saw action in the Crimean War and the Second Opium War, commanded the Ever Victorious Army during the Taiping Rebellion in China, and served as Governor-General of the Sudan, where he was killed at the fall of Khartoum to Mahdist forces.

Early career

Gordon was commissioned into the Royal Engineers in 1852 and served in the Crimean War, taking part in the Siege of Sevastopol. In 1860 he went to China during the Second Opium War and was present at the occupation of Beijing and the burning of the Old Summer Palace. In 1863 he took command of the Ever Victorious Army, a Chinese force officered by Europeans, and led it in a series of victories that contributed decisively to the suppression of the Taiping Rebellion; the Emperor rewarded him with the rank of tidu and the imperial yellow jacket.

Sudan

Gordon first served in Sudan as governor of Equatoria (1873–1876) and then as Governor-General of the whole Sudan (1877–1879) under the Khedive of Egypt, campaigning against the slave trade. In 1884, as the Mahdist revolt spread, the British government sent Gordon to Khartoum to evacuate Egyptian garrisons. Instead he organized the city's defence through a siege of 317 days. Khartoum fell on 26 January 1885 and Gordon was killed; a British relief expedition arrived two days later. His death caused a public outcry in Britain and damaged the government of W. E. Gladstone.

Legacy

Deeply religious and famously eccentric, Gordon became a Victorian hero and martyr; statues were erected to him in London and elsewhere. Modern historians debate his responsibility for the disaster and his role in imperial expansion, but he remains one of the most celebrated figures of the Victorian army.
