Posse Comitatus Act

EnactedJune 18, 1878
Codified18 U.S.C. § 1385
Applies toU.S. Army and U.S. Air Force (extended by policy to Navy and Marine Corps)
PurposeLimit use of federal military for domestic law enforcement

The Posse Comitatus Act is a United States federal law (18 U.S.C. § 1385) signed on June 18, 1878, by President Rutherford B. Hayes that limits the powers of the federal government in the use of federal military personnel to enforce domestic policies within the United States. The Act provides that whoever, except in cases expressly authorized by the Constitution or an Act of Congress, willfully uses any part of the Army or the Air Force as a posse comitatus or otherwise to execute the laws shall be fined or imprisoned for not more than two years.

History

The law was passed as part of the end of Reconstruction. During Reconstruction, federal troops had been stationed throughout the South to protect freedmen and enforce federal law, and had been used by marshals as a posse comitatus — literally, the "power of the county", the traditional authority of a sheriff to summon able-bodied men to assist in keeping the peace. Southern Democrats, and many Northern Democrats, objected to this military enforcement; after the disputed election of 1876 and the withdrawal of troops under the Compromise of 1877, Congress attached the Act as a rider to an army appropriations bill, criminalizing the practice. The original statute named only the Army; the Air Force was added in 1956, and the Navy and Marine Corps are bound by parallel regulations. The Space Force was added by amendment in 2021 legislation, and Department of Defense policy extends restrictions across the armed services.

Scope and exceptions

The Act does not apply to the National Guard when under state control (Title 32 or state active duty), nor to the Coast Guard, which by statute has law-enforcement authority. Congress has created statutory exceptions, notably the Insurrection Act, which authorizes the President to use federal troops domestically in specified circumstances, and laws permitting the military to provide equipment, training, intelligence and other support to civilian law enforcement, especially in counterdrug operations. Courts have generally held that indirect military support that does not pervade civilian enforcement does not violate the Act, and there have been virtually no successful prosecutions under it; its main force is as a policy constraint observed by the Department of Defense.
