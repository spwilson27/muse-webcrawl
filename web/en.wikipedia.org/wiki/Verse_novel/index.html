Verse novel

GenreNarrative poetry / novel
Notable examplesEugene Onegin, The Golden Gate, Out of the Dust

A verse novel is a book-length work of narrative fiction told in verse rather than prose. Although related to the epic poem and the medieval verse romance, the verse novel is distinguished by its use of novelistic conventions — character development, dialogue and plotted scenes — within a poetic form such as blank verse, sonnets or free verse.

History

The most famous early example is Alexander Pushkin's Eugene Onegin (1833), written in a distinctive fourteen-line stanza. Nineteenth-century examples include Elizabeth Barrett Browning's Aurora Leigh (1856) and Robert Browning's The Ring and the Book (1868–69). The modern verse novel revived in the late 20th century with works such as Vikram Seth's The Golden Gate (1986), written in Onegin sonnets, and Anne Carson's Autobiography of Red (1998).

Young-adult verse novels

Since the 1990s the form has flourished in young-adult literature, where short free-verse poems present scenes from a protagonist's point of view. Karen Hesse's Out of the Dust (1997) won the Newbery Medal, and authors such as Ellen Hopkins, Kwame Alexander and Jason Reynolds have made the YA verse novel a major commercial category. Critics note that the white space and pace of verse can make long books feel fast and intimate for reluctant readers.
