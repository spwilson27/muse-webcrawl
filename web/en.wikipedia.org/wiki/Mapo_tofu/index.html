Mapo tofu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of mapo tofu in red chilli oil with minced beef and scallions]

Mapo tofu is a Sichuan dish of soft tofu set in a fiery, oily, mouth-numbing sauce of chilli-bean paste (doubanjiang), fermented black beans, minced beef, garlic and chilli, finished with a snow of ground Sichuan pepper. It was created in Chengdu in the 1860s–70s at a small restaurant by the Wanfu Bridge run by a woman surnamed Chen whose face bore smallpox scars — mápó, "pockmarked grandmother", became the dish's name and the restaurant's fame. Porters and labourers brought oil and tofu; she did the rest; the dish outlived the bridge, the dynasty and the century, becoming the world's introduction to Sichuan cooking.

1. Character and spread

Character and spread

The dish is a study in contrasts the Sichuan tradition prizes: silken tofu against granular beef, chilli heat against the pepper's tingle, a sauce that should be — in the classic description — numbing, spicy, hot (in temperature), fresh, tender and aromatic all at once. In Japan, the Sichuan chef Chen Kenmin (and his son Kenichi) naturalised a milder, sweeter mapo tofu that is now a home staple; vegetarian versions worldwide swap the beef for mushrooms. The Chen Mapo restaurant in Chengdu still operates under the founding name, and "mapo" has escaped its dish — mapo-flavoured everything, from noodles to crisps, now trades on those three syllables of heat.
