Paradox of the plankton

The paradox of the plankton is the observation that many different species of plankton coexist in a seemingly uniform body of water, apparently contradicting the competitive exclusion principle — the idea that two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot stably coexist, because the superior competitor should drive the other to extinction. The paradox was articulated by limnologist G. Evelyn Hutchinson in 1961.

The problem

Phytoplankton in a mixed water column all require essentially the same few resources: light, nitrogen, phosphorus, silicon, and a handful of trace elements. There are far fewer of these resources than there are plankton species, yet dozens or hundreds of species persist together in the same waters, season after season.

Proposed resolutions

Several explanations have been proposed. The environment may never reach equilibrium: changing weather, seasons and mixing continually alter conditions before exclusion can finish. Water is also not truly homogeneous — there are gradients of light and nutrients, patches and micro-niches. Other mechanisms include differences in grazing susceptibility, viral infection (the "kill the winner" hypothesis), storage effects, chaotic population dynamics, and trade-offs between competitive ability and other traits, which can permit coexistence on few resources. The paradox is a special case of the wider ecological question of how biodiversity is maintained.
