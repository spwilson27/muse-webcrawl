Derivative (finance)

Derivative
TypeFinancial contract
Common formsFutures, options, swaps, forwards
UsesHedging, speculation, arbitrage

In finance, a derivative is a contract that derives its value from the performance of an underlying entity. This underlying entity can be an asset, an index, or an interest rate, and is often simply called the underlying. Derivatives can be used for a number of purposes, including insuring against price movements (hedging), increasing exposure to price movements for speculation, or getting access to otherwise hard-to-trade assets or markets.

Some of the more common derivatives include forwards, futures, options, swaps, and variations of these such as synthetic collateralized debt obligations and credit default swaps. Most derivatives are traded over-the-counter (off-exchange) or on an exchange such as the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, while most insurance contracts have developed into a separate industry.

Basics

Derivatives are one of the three main categories of financial instruments, the other two being equity (stocks) and debt (bonds and mortgages). The oldest example of a derivative in history is thought to be a contract transaction of olives, entered into by the ancient Greek philosopher Thales, attested by Aristotle. In Mesopotamia, clay tablets from about 1750 BCE describe contracts resembling futures for agricultural goods.

Size of the market

The derivatives market is enormous, commonly estimated at over $1 quadrillion on a notional basis. Economists and commenters note that notional amounts greatly overstate market value and credit risk, because many contracts offset one another; gross market values are typically a few percent of notional principal.

Usage

Hedging

A farmer expecting to sell a harvest at a future date may sell a futures contract to lock in a price, transferring price risk to a buyer willing to bear it. Airlines hedge fuel costs and exporters hedge currency movements in similar ways.

Speculation and arbitrage

Because derivatives offer leverage — a small margin controls a large underlying exposure — they are widely used for speculation. Arbitrageurs exploit small price differences between a derivative and its underlying.

Criticism

Derivatives played a central role in the 2008 financial crisis, particularly mortgage-backed securities and credit default swaps, prompting reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and increased central clearing of standardised contracts.
