Static electricity

Static electricity is an imbalance of electric charges within or on the surface of a material, or between materials. The charge remains until it is able to move away by means of an electric current or electrical discharge. The word "static" distinguishes it from current electricity, where charges flow through a conductor. Static electricity is named in contrast with current electricity and many everyday phenomena — the shock from a doorknob, hair standing after removing a hat, lightning on a grand scale, clinging laundry — result from the build-up and sudden release of such charges.

Causes

The most familiar cause is the triboelectric effect: when two different materials touch and separate, electrons transfer from one to the other, leaving one positively and the other negatively charged. Rubbing increases the contact area and hence the charge. Materials are ranked in a triboelectric series — human hair, glass and nylon tend to charge positively against rubber, polyester and PVC. Charge can also be separated by induction (a nearby charged object polarises a conductor), by heating (pyroelectricity) or by pressure (piezoelectricity). Dry air favours accumulation because surface moisture that would leak charge away is absent.

Discharge and effects

When the electric field around a charged object exceeds the breakdown strength of air (about 3 million volts per metre), the charge escapes as a spark — an electrostatic discharge. A person walking across a carpet can accumulate 10,000 volts or more, yet the energy is tiny and the shock merely startling. The same phenomenon scaled up in thunderclouds is lightning. Uncontrolled, ESD can ignite flammable vapours (hence bonding and grounding when refuelling or handling solvents) and can destroy semiconductor devices at voltages far below human perception, which is why electronics factories use conductive flooring, wrist straps and ionisers. Conversely, static is exploited in photocopiers and laser printers, electrostatic precipitators, powder coating and paint spraying.

History

Theophrastus and Thales of Miletus noted that rubbed amber (Greek elektron) attracts light objects; William Gilbert systematised such "electrics" around 1600; 18th-century experimenters such as Stephen Gray, Charles du Fay and Benjamin Franklin built electrostatic generators and the Leyden jar and established the two-charge (positive/negative) picture; and Michael Faraday and others later unified statics with current electricity in classical electromagnetism, the science of which is called electrostatics.
