Possibilism (geography)

Possibilism in geography is the theory that the environment sets certain constraints or limitations, but culture is otherwise determined by social conditions. It holds that the natural environment offers a set of possibilities from which human societies choose according to their culture, technology, and values. The concept was developed primarily by French geographers as a reaction against environmental determinism.

Origins

The French historian Lucien Febvre is credited with formulating the doctrine, summarizing it in the dictum that there are "no necessities, but everywhere possibilities". The geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache, founder of the French school of geography, argued that landscapes reflect the genres de vie (ways of life) of their inhabitants, and that nature provides opportunities whose use depends on human choice. In the United States, Carl Sauer and the Berkeley School advanced related cultural-landscape ideas.

Contrast with determinism

Environmental determinism, associated with figures such as Friedrich Ratzel and Ellen Churchill Semple, held that climate and terrain directly shape human character and society. Possibilists countered that similar environments can support very different societies, and that human agency, not nature, is the primary driver of cultural landscapes. Later approaches, including probabilism and cultural ecology, sought middle positions between the two schools.
