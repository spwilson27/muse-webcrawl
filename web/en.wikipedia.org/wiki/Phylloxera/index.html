Phylloxera (Daktulosphaira vitifoliae, commonly the grape phylloxera) is a tiny aphid-like insect, native to eastern North America, that feeds on the roots and leaves of grapevines. In the late 19th century it devastated the vineyards of Europe in the catastrophe known as the Great French Wine Blight, permanently transforming the world's wine industry.

The blight

American vine species had evolved resistance to the insect, but the European wine grape Vitis vinifera had none: root-feeding phylloxera created galls and open wounds through which secondary infections entered, killing vines within a few years. Introduced probably on imported American vines in the early 1860s, the pest was identified in the southern Rhône in 1868 and spread across France and then to Spain, Italy, Germany and beyond. By the 1880s a large majority of Europe's vineyards were destroyed or dying; wine production collapsed and rural economies were ruined.

Solution: grafting

After years of failed remedies — flooding, sand planting, carbon disulfide injection — the durable solution, proposed by French botanists including Jules-Émile Planchon and championed by growers such as Gustave Foëx, was to graft vinifera scions onto rootstocks of resistant American species (V. riparia, V. rupestris, V. berlandieri and their hybrids). Virtually all wine grapes in the world are still grown this way. A few regions escaped the pest — notably Chile, protected by deserts and the Andes, and some sandy or phylloxera-free pockets — and their ungrafted vines are prized.

Legacy

The crisis prompted landmark plant-quarantine laws, reshaped the geography of European wine, and caused lasting changes in appellations and varieties. It remains the classic example of an invasive pest transforming an industry.
