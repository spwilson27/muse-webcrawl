The New Town is a central area of Edinburgh, Scotland, built in stages between 1767 and about 1850. It is considered a masterpiece of Georgian town planning and, together with the medieval Old Town, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995. Its wide streets, squares, and neoclassical terraces stand in deliberate contrast to the cramped, organic layout of the Old Town across the Nor Loch valley.

History

By the mid-18th century Edinburgh's Old Town was severely overcrowded. Lord Provost George Drummond championed expansion northward across the drained Nor Loch. A design competition held in 1766 was won by the 26-year-old architect James Craig, whose plan laid out a simple, elegant grid: three parallel streets — Princes Street, George Street, and Queen Street — linked by cross streets, with St Andrew Square at the east end and Charlotte Square at the west.

Charlotte Square, designed by Robert Adam in 1791 and built from 1820, is regarded as the finest Georgian square in Britain. Later phases — the "Second New Town" of the early 19th century and William Henry Playfair's Moray Estate of the 1820s — extended the development north toward Stockbridge and the Water of Leith.

Today

The New Town is Edinburgh's principal shopping and commercial district: Princes Street is lined with shops facing Edinburgh Castle, George Street hosts offices, hotels, and restaurants, and the area remains one of the most desirable residential districts in Scotland, much of it protected as a conservation area.
