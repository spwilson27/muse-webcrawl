Diesel particulate filter

A diesel particulate filter (DPF) is a device designed to remove diesel particulate matter or soot from the exhaust gas of a diesel engine. Particulate filters have been in use on non-road machines since 1980, and in automobiles since 1996. Most engines equipped with a DPF also use other systems to reduce harmful emissions.

Principle of operation

Wall-flow diesel particulate filters usually remove 85% or more of the soot, and under certain conditions can attain soot removal efficiencies approaching 100%. Some filters are single-use (disposable), intended for replacement with a new one when full of accumulated ash. Others are designed to burn off the accumulated particulate either passively through the use of a catalyst or by active means such as a fuel burner which heats the filter to soot combustion temperatures. This is known as filter regeneration. Engine ECU programming runs the regeneration cycle periodically, usually by injecting extra fuel to raise exhaust temperature.

Filter materials

The most common filter material is cordierite, a ceramic material also used as catalytic converter cores. Cordierite filters provide excellent filtration efficiency, are (relatively) inexpensive, and have thermal properties that make packaging them for installation in the vehicle simple. Silicon carbide is another popular material, with greater thermal conductivity and a higher melting point, though it is more expensive and heavier. Sintered metal fibres and powder-metallurgy meshes have also been used.

Regeneration

As soot accumulates in the filter, back pressure in the exhaust system rises. The pressure loss across the DPF must periodically be reset by burning the accumulated soot. Passive regeneration occurs when exhaust temperatures are high enough during normal driving, often assisted by a catalyst coating. Active regeneration, triggered by the engine control unit using pressure and temperature sensors, raises the exhaust temperature to roughly 550–650 °C to oxidise the soot. Ash — the incombustible residue from lubricating oil additives — cannot be burned off and gradually accumulates over the filter's service life.

Regulation

DPFs became effectively mandatory on new diesel cars in the European Union with the Euro 5 emission standard in 2009, and in the United States emissions standards for heavy-duty trucks from 2007 onward drove widespread adoption. Removing or tampering with a DPF is illegal in many jurisdictions.
