Surface plasmon

Surface plasmons (SPs) are coherent, delocalised oscillations of electrons at the interface between a metal and a dielectric (such as air or glass), coupled to an electromagnetic field. When the field and the electron oscillation are coupled as a travelling wave bound to the interface, the excitation is called a surface plasmon polariton (SPP). Because their wavelength can be much shorter than that of free-space light, surface plasmons are a foundation of the field of plasmonics.

Properties

Surface plasmon polaritons are transverse-magnetic waves whose fields decay exponentially away from the interface on both sides. Their dispersion relation lies to the right of the light line, meaning an SPP at a flat interface cannot be excited directly by light striking the surface from the dielectric side; momentum must be supplied by a prism coupler (the Kretschmann or Otto configurations), a grating, or a nearby defect or nanoparticle. Ohmic losses in the metal limit propagation distances, typically to tens of micrometres in the visible range for silver and gold.

Localised surface plasmons

In metal nanoparticles much smaller than the wavelength of light, the oscillation is confined and non-propagating, giving a localised surface plasmon resonance. The resonance frequency depends on the particle's size, shape, and surroundings, producing the vivid colours of gold and silver colloids — and of medieval stained glass and the Lycurgus Cup — and making nanoparticles exquisitely sensitive refractive-index sensors.

Applications

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) instruments monitor binding of biomolecules to a metal film in real time and are widely used in drug discovery. Plasmonic effects also underpin surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), in which molecules near roughened metal surfaces show Raman signals enhanced by many orders of magnitude, as well as plasmonic waveguides, metamaterials, and proposed sub-wavelength optical circuits.
