Drive-through


A car collecting an order at a drive-through window.

A drive-through or drive-thru is a type of take-out service provided by a business that allows customers to purchase products without leaving their cars. Customers drive into a lane, place an order at a menu board and speaker (or a first window), then proceed to a service window to pay and collect their goods.

History

The format grew out of American car culture of the 1930s and 1940s. Drive-in restaurants with carhop service preceded it; the first true drive-through bank window is often credited to a St. Louis bank in the 1930s, and Red's Giant Hamburg in Springfield, Missouri, is frequently cited as an early drive-through restaurant (1947). The concept spread widely with fast-food chains: In-N-Out Burger adopted a two-way speaker system in 1948, Jack in the Box built its model around the drive-through in the 1950s, and McDonald's opened its first drive-through in 1975 in Sierra Vista, Arizona, initially to serve soldiers from nearby Fort Huachuca who were not permitted to leave their cars in uniform. Wendy's followed with its pick-up window the same era.

Formats

Drive-throughs are most associated with fast-food restaurants, but the model extends to banks, pharmacies, coffee shops, liquor stores, dry cleaners, COVID-19 testing and vaccination sites, and even wedding chapels and funeral homes in parts of the United States. Modern lanes may include dual ordering lanes, digital menus, licence-plate or app-based ordering, and timers measuring service speed, since a large share of fast-food revenue passes through the window.

Criticism

Idling queues generate local air pollution and noise, and drive-throughs encourage car-dependent urban design; some cities have restricted new drive-throughs in pedestrian-oriented districts. Accessibility for pedestrians, cyclists and people without cars is a further concern.
