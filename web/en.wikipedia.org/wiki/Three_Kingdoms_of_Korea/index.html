Three Kingdoms of Korea

The Three Kingdoms of Korea (Korean: 삼국시대, Samguk sidae) refers to the period in Korean history when the Korean Peninsula and parts of Manchuria were ruled by three rival kingdoms: Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla. The period is traditionally dated from 57 BCE, the legendary founding of Silla, to 668 CE, when Silla, allied with Tang China, conquered Goguryeo and unified most of the peninsula.

The kingdoms

  • Goguryeo (37 BCE – 668 CE), the largest and most powerful, occupied the northern peninsula and much of Manchuria. Under kings such as Gwanggaeto the Great it expanded dramatically and repeatedly repelled invasions by Sui and Tang China.
  • Baekje (18 BCE – 660 CE) controlled the south-west of the peninsula. A maritime trading power, it transmitted Buddhism and continental culture to Japan and maintained close ties with the Yamato court.
  • Silla (57 BCE – 935 CE), in the south-east, developed latest but ultimately prevailed, conquering Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668 with Tang assistance, then expelling Tang forces to unify the peninsula south of the Taedong River.

Culture and religion

Buddhism was adopted by all three kingdoms — entering Goguryeo in 372, Baekje in 384 and Silla formally in 527 — and coexisted with shamanic traditions and Confucian statecraft. The period produced notable art and architecture, including the astronomical observatory Cheomseongdae in Silla's capital Gyeongju and the mural-painted tombs of Goguryeo, now UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The later Gaya confederacy in the south is sometimes counted alongside the three kingdoms in broader accounts of the era.

Sources

The principal native sources are the 12th-century Samguk sagi and the 13th-century Samguk yusa, supplemented by Chinese dynastic histories and Japanese chronicles such as the Nihon Shoki.
