Exploration of Mars

The exploration of Mars has been a goal of national space programmes for decades and a focus of serious scientific study since the 19th century. Dozens of robotic spacecraft — orbiters, landers and rovers — have been sent to Mars since the 1960s, mapping its surface, analysing its geology and atmosphere, and searching for evidence of past or present life.

Early missions

The Soviet Union made the first attempts in 1960; NASA's Mariner 4 returned the first close-up photographs in 1965, revealing a cratered, seemingly barren world. Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet in 1971, and the Viking landers of 1976 conducted the first successful surface missions and the first direct (and inconclusive) search for life.

The rover era

After a series of failures in the 1990s, Mars Pathfinder and its Sojourner rover landed in 1997. The twin rovers Spirit and Opportunity (2004) found strong evidence that liquid water once flowed on the surface. Curiosity (2012) demonstrated that Gale Crater once held a habitable freshwater lake, and Perseverance (2021) is caching samples for a possible return to Earth while testing oxygen production from the CO2 atmosphere. China's Tianwen-1 mission landed the Zhurong rover in 2021, making China the second nation to operate a rover on Mars.

Orbiters and science goals

Orbiters including Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey, Mars Express, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, MAVEN and the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter have mapped mineralogy, subsurface ice, and atmospheric escape. Key goals remain: characterising past habitability, searching for biosignatures, and preparing for eventual crewed missions, which agencies and private companies have proposed for the 2030s and beyond.
