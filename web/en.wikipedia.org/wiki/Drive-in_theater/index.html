A drive-in theater or drive-in cinema is a form of cinema structure consisting of a large outdoor movie screen, a projection booth, a concessions stand, and a large parking area for automobiles. Within this enclosed area, customers can view movies from the privacy and comfort of their own cars. The screen can be as simple as a wall painted white or a steel truss structure with a complex finish.

History

Richard M. Hollingshead Jr., a sales manager for his father's auto-parts company in Camden, New Jersey, experimented with a projector mounted on his car hood and a screen nailed to trees in his backyard, motivated in part by finding a comfortable way for his mother to watch films. He received a patent in 1933 and opened the first Park-In Theater on June 6, 1933, on the Admiral Wilson Boulevard in Camden, charging 25 cents per car and 25 cents per person. The concept spread slowly before World War II and boomed after it, riding postwar car culture and suburbanization: by the late 1950s the United States had roughly 4,000 drive-ins, around a quarter of all its screens.

Design

Rows of parking spaces are arranged on ramps or slightly graded tiers so each car has an unobstructed view. Original sound came from large speakers near the screen, then from individual speakers mounted on poles that patrons clipped to a partially rolled-down window; later venues broadcast an FM (or AM) radio signal to car stereos. A single screen typically holds 200–500 cars; large venues added playgrounds, miniature golf, and even laundry facilities. Concessions—popcorn, hot dogs, soft drinks—provide most of the profit.

Decline and revival

Rising land values, the spread of indoor multiplexes, daylight saving time (which pushed showtimes later in summer), home video, and the cost of converting to digital projection drove a long decline; many sites became flea markets, churches, or retail developments. A few hundred drive-ins survive in the United States—the Bengies Drive-In in Maryland boasts one of the largest screens—sustained by nostalgia programming and double features. The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 produced a brief revival, as drive-ins offered socially distanced entertainment when indoor cinemas closed.

In popular culture

The drive-in became an icon of 1950s American youth culture, associated with car culture and dating, and features in films such as American Graffiti (1973) and Grease (1978). Its B-movie and exploitation programming also gave rise to the term "drive-in movie" for low-budget genre fare.
