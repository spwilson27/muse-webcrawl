Anaclitic depression

Anaclitic depression is a syndrome of severe psychological and physical deterioration observed in infants separated from their caregivers for prolonged periods. The term was introduced by the psychoanalyst René Spitz in the 1940s, based on his studies of institutionalized infants in nurseries and foundling homes. The word "anaclitic" derives from the Greek anaklitos, meaning "leaning upon," reflecting the infant's total dependence on the caregiver.

History

In 1945, Spitz published a landmark study comparing infants raised in a foundling home, where they received adequate physical care but minimal human contact, with infants raised in a prison nursery alongside their mothers. Despite comparable nutrition and hygiene, the foundling-home infants showed dramatically worse developmental outcomes. Spitz documented the progression of the syndrome through stages of protest, despair, and detachment.

Symptoms and progression

Spitz described a characteristic sequence. During the first month of separation, infants became weepy, demanding, and clingy toward any available caregiver. In the second month, crying often gave way to screaming, weight loss began, and developmental progress halted. By the third month, infants became withdrawn and rigid-faced, lying prone in their cots for long periods, with developmental quotients dropping sharply. If separation continued beyond three to five months without restoration of the maternal figure, the condition could merge into the more severe syndrome of hospitalism, marked by profound retardation and increased mortality.

Relationship to attachment theory

Spitz's findings, along with the work of John Bowlby and Harry Harlow, were foundational for attachment theory. The studies demonstrated that emotional nourishment is as essential to infant survival and development as physical care. Anaclitic depression is related to, but distinct from, the concept of reactive attachment disorder in modern psychiatric classification.
