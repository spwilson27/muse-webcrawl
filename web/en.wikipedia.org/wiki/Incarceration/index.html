Incarceration

Incarceration is the detention of a person in prison or jail, generally as punishment for a crime or while awaiting trial. It is one of the principal forms of penalty in modern criminal justice systems, alongside fines, probation and community service. Incarceration may also be used for civil contempt, immigration detention, military discipline, and the detention of prisoners of war, and its extent, conditions and purposes vary widely between countries.

History

Although confinement was used in antiquity mainly to hold accused persons before trial or execution, long-term imprisonment as a punishment in itself developed in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, influenced by Enlightenment reformers such as Cesare Beccaria and John Howard. The penitentiary movement produced rival models: the Pennsylvania system of solitary confinement with labour, exemplified by Eastern State Penitentiary (opened 1829), and the Auburn system of congregate silent labour. Twentieth-century practice shifted among retributive, rehabilitative and incapacitative rationales, with parole and indeterminate sentencing dominant in many countries before a punitive turn in the 1970s and 1980s.

The United States

The United States has historically recorded the world's largest incarcerated population and one of its highest incarceration rates, with roughly 1.8 to 2 million people held in state and federal prisons, local jails, juvenile facilities and other institutions in recent years. The prison population grew roughly fivefold between the early 1970s and its peak around 2008–2009, a phenomenon widely termed mass incarceration, driven by longer sentences, mandatory minimums, drug enforcement and restrictions on parole. Rates have declined modestly since the peak. Federal sentencing is governed in part by the advisory Federal Sentencing Guidelines.

Imprisonment in the U.S. falls disproportionately on racial minorities and people with low incomes, and carries extensive collateral consequences, including disenfranchisement, employment and housing barriers, and family disruption. Recidivism rates are high: Bureau of Justice Statistics studies have found that a majority of released state prisoners are rearrested within a few years of release.

Alternatives and reform

Alternatives to incarceration include probation, parole, fines, restorative justice programmes, electronic monitoring, drug courts and diversion for people with mental illness or substance-use disorders. Reform debates concern sentence lengths, private prisons, prison conditions, solitary confinement, the incarceration of juveniles, and the comparative experience of countries — notably in Scandinavia — that combine much lower incarceration rates with rehabilitative regimes.
