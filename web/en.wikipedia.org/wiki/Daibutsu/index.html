Daibutsu

Daibutsu (Japanese: 大仏, "Great Buddha") is a Japanese term used to describe large statues of the Buddha. Japan's oldest and most famous daibutsu is the bronze statue of Vairocana at Tōdai-ji in Nara, completed in the 8th century, while the best-known outdoors example is the Great Buddha of Kamakura at Kōtoku-in.

Notable examples

The Tōdai-ji Daibutsu, dedicated in 752 under Emperor Shōmu, stands about 15 metres tall and sits within the Daibutsuden, among the largest wooden buildings in the world. Its casting consumed enormous quantities of bronze and much of Japan's available gold for gilding. The Kamakura Daibutsu, cast around 1252, depicts Amida Buddha, is about 13 metres tall including its base, and has stood in the open air since a tsunami destroyed its enclosing hall in the late 15th century. Other notable examples include the Daibutsu at Gifu, the Nihon-ji Daibutsu carved into a cliff face in Chiba, and the modern Ushiku Daibutsu in Ibaraki, completed in 1993 and about 120 metres tall including its pedestal — among the tallest statues in the world.

Significance

Daibutsu were often commissioned by rulers as acts of devotion and statecraft, intended to protect the nation and demonstrate its wealth and piety. The great statues became enduring pilgrimage sites and symbols of Japanese Buddhism, and several are designated National Treasures.
