In chemistry, a solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances: a solute dissolved in a solvent, uniformly down to the molecular scale. Water is the commonest solvent ('aqueous solutions'), but solutions may involve any phases — air is a gaseous solution, brass a solid one.

Concentration is expressed as molarity (mol/dm³), molality, mass percent or parts per million. A solution saturates when no more solute dissolves at that temperature, with solid in dynamic equilibrium with dissolved particles.

[Image: Blue copper sulfate solution being poured, showing uniform colour]

Why things dissolve

'Like dissolves like': polar solvents such as water dissolve ionic and polar substances (hydrating the ions), while non-polar solvents dissolve oils and greases. Dissolving may release or absorb heat — ammonium nitrate cold packs exploit the latter.

Colligative properties

Dissolved particles lower vapour pressure and freezing point and raise boiling point in proportion to their number, not identity — the physics behind antifreeze, salted ice and osmotic pressure.
