Daniel Coit Gilman (6 July 1831 – 13 October 1908) was an American educator and academic administrator. As the founding president of Johns Hopkins University (1876–1901), he established the first American university devoted primarily to graduate research on the German model, reshaping higher education in the United States.

Early life and career

Gilman graduated from Yale in 1852, studied in Berlin, and served Yale as librarian and professor of geography, helping build the Sheffield Scientific School. He was the third president of the University of California (1872–1875), overseeing its early development at Berkeley, before accepting the presidency of the newly endowed Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore.

Johns Hopkins

Inaugurated in 1876, Johns Hopkins under Gilman emphasised advanced scholarship: graduate fellowships, research seminars, scholarly journals (such as the American Journal of Mathematics) and a university press — the oldest continuously operating university press in the United States. Gilman recruited a distinguished young faculty and later helped found the Johns Hopkins Hospital (1889) and its School of Medicine (1893), which set new standards for medical education.

Later work

After retiring in 1901, Gilman became the first president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington (1902–1904) and served numerous civic and philanthropic bodies, including the Peabody and Slater funds for southern education. Gilman Hall at Johns Hopkins and the town of Gilman, Illinois, are among the places named for him.
