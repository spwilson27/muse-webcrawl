Revenue equivalence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Revenue equivalence is a concept in auction theory stating that, under certain standard conditions, all standard auction formats — first-price sealed-bid, second-price (Vickrey), English, and Dutch auctions — yield the same expected revenue to the seller and the same expected payment by each bidder type. The result, known as the revenue equivalence theorem, derives from William Vickrey's 1961 analysis of auctions and counter-speculation.

Assumptions

The theorem holds when:

  • Bidders are risk-neutral.
  • Each bidder's valuation is drawn independently from a common probability distribution (independent private values).
  • The bidders are symmetric — their values come from the same distribution.
  • The good is awarded to the bidder with the highest value, and a bidder with the lowest possible value earns zero surplus.

Intuition and proof sketch

By the revelation principle, attention can be restricted to direct mechanisms in which truthful reporting is optimal. A bidder's expected payment is pinned down by how likely each of his possible reports is to win: two mechanisms that produce the same allocation rule must produce the same expected payments, up to a constant. Because the constant is zero when the lowest type earns nothing, expected revenue is identical across formats.

Expected payment of type v = v · Q(v) − ∫ Q(t) dt + constant

where Q(v) is the probability that a bidder of value v wins the item.

Violations

Revenue equivalence breaks when its assumptions fail. With risk-averse bidders, first-price auctions raise more revenue than second-price auctions, because bidders bid more aggressively to reduce the risk of losing. With affiliated or common values, open (English) auctions tend to outperform sealed formats by revealing information and mitigating the winner's curse. Asymmetry between bidders can also destroy equivalence.
