The Eleatic school was a school of pre-Socratic philosophy founded by Parmenides in the early fifth century BC in the ancient Greek colony of Elea in southern Italy. Its best-known members besides Parmenides were his student Zeno of Elea and Melissus of Samos. Xenophanes is sometimes included as a precursor. The Eleatics are noted for their uncompromising monism and for pioneering rigorous deductive argument in Western philosophy.

Doctrine

Parmenides' poem On Nature argues that reality — "what is" — must be ungenerated, imperishable, whole, uniform, motionless and perfect, like a well-rounded sphere. Change, motion, plurality and empty space are impossible, because they would require "what is not" to exist. The world presented by the senses is therefore a deceptive "way of opinion" opposed to the "way of truth" reached by reason alone.

Zeno's paradoxes

Zeno defended Parmenides indirectly, by reducing the opposing view — that motion and plurality are real — to absurdity. His famous paradoxes, preserved mainly through Aristotle, include Achilles and the tortoise, the Dichotomy, the Arrow, and the Stadium. They challenged philosophers for over two millennia and were only fully addressed with the development of the mathematics of infinite series and the continuum.

Melissus and influence

Melissus, a Samian general, restated Parmenidean doctrine in prose and modified it: being is spatially infinite, and because it is one it can feel neither pain nor grief. Later pluralists — Empedocles, Anaxagoras and the atomists Leucippus and Democritus — accepted the Eleatic constraints that being cannot come from nothing while reintroducing plurality and motion through unchanging elements or atoms moving in the void. Plato engaged deeply with Parmenides in his dialogue Parmenides and the Sophist, treating him as a towering, if partly mistaken, predecessor.
