Choice reaction time

Choice reaction time (CRT) is the time taken to respond to a stimulus when the response must be selected from two or more alternatives, each associated with a different stimulus. It contrasts with simple reaction time, in which there is only one possible response to a single stimulus. CRT tasks are a foundational tool of experimental psychology and mental chronometry, used to study the speed and organization of information processing in the brain.

Hick's law

In 1952 W. E. Hick found that choice reaction time increases as a logarithmic function of the number of stimulus–response alternatives: RT = a + b·log2(n + 1), where n is the number of choices. This relationship, known as Hick's law (or the Hick–Hyman law, after Ray Hyman's 1953 confirmation), expresses reaction time in terms of the information (in bits) that must be processed to select a response. The law holds approximately for compatible, well-practised stimulus–response mappings.

Factors affecting CRT

Choice reaction time is lengthened by stimulus–response incompatibility, low stimulus intensity, divided attention, ageing, fatigue, and various neurological conditions; it is shortened by practice and by warning signals that allow preparation. The influential Fitts's law (1954) similarly relates movement time to the information content of the target acquisition task, and together these laws shaped the design of human–machine interfaces.

Applications

CRT measures are used in cognitive assessment and neuropsychology (to detect slowing in dementia, traumatic brain injury, or ADHD), in intelligence research — where CRT correlates modestly with IQ — and in applied settings such as driver testing, sport science, and human factors engineering. Computer-based batteries typically record both mean reaction time and its variability, the latter proving a sensitive marker of cognitive impairment.
