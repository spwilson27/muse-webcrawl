Âşık Veysel (born Veysel Şatıroğlu, 25 October 1894 – 21 March 1973) was a Turkish Alevi folk poet and ashik (wandering minstrel) who is regarded as one of the most beloved figures of Turkish folk literature. Blind from childhood, he composed and sang hundreds of poems accompanying himself on the bağlama, expressing themes of life, death, love, unity, and the human relationship with the earth.

Life

Veysel was born in the village of Sivrialan in the Şarkışla district of Sivas Province. Smallpox — which had already killed two of his sisters — left him blind in one eye at age seven; an accident soon took the sight of the other. His father bought him a bağlama to console him, and he learned from local minstrels and from the poet Çamışıhlı Ali. He spent much of his youth in his village, married, and endured personal tragedies, including his wife's departure and the deaths of children. In the 1930s the folklorist Ahmet Kutsi Tecer "discovered" him at a folk-poets' festival in Sivas and brought his work to national attention.

During the early Republic he travelled widely, taught bağlama and folk singing at Village Institutes, and performed on Turkish Radio. The Grand National Assembly granted him a pension in 1965 in recognition of his services to Turkish language and national culture. He died in his home village in 1973; his house is now a museum.

Works

His best-known songs include "Uzun İnce Bir Yoldayım" ("I walk a long and narrow road"), a meditation on life as a journey between two doors; "Kara Toprak" ("Black Earth"), affirming that the soil is man's only faithful beloved; "Güzelliğin On Para Etmez" ("Your beauty is worth nothing [without my love]"); and "Dostlar Beni Hatırlasın" ("May my friends remember me"). Written in a clear Anatolian Turkish within the syllabic hece metre of folk poetry, his verse combines Alevi-Bektashi spirituality, humanism, and praise of Atatürk's reforms. His poems entered school textbooks and the repertoire of generations of Turkish musicians.
