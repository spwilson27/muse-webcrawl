Feminist philosophy is philosophy done from a feminist perspective: it re-examines the concepts, methods and canon of philosophy through the lens of gender, asks how philosophical traditions have encoded male experience as universal, and develops alternatives across every branch of the discipline — ethics, epistemology, metaphysics, philosophy of language, political philosophy and aesthetics. It overlaps with, but is not identical to, the philosophy of Feminism: feminist philosophy includes both criticism of the tradition and constructive work that need not take feminism's history as its subject.

History

Early landmarks include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869), and Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949), with its famous thesis that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman". From the 1970s, as women entered academic philosophy in growing numbers, feminist work reorganised the field's questions: anthologies and journals professionalised it, and canonical figures — Plato's guardians, Aristotle's biology, Rousseau's Sophie, Kant's and Hegel's accounts of women — were read against their exclusions.

Major areas

Ethics: Carol Gilligan's In a Different Voice (1982) challenged Kohlberg's justice-centred model of moral development and launched the ethics of care, developed by Nel Noddings, Virginia Held and others into a major alternative framework. Epistemology: standpoint theory (Nancy Hartsock, Sandra Harding) argues that knowledge is socially situated and that marginalised standpoints can be epistemically advantaged; Donna Haraway's "situated knowledges" is a landmark statement; Miranda Fricker's Epistemic Injustice (2007) named testimonial and hermeneutical injustice. Metaphysics and language: debates over the sex/gender distinction, social construction, and the metaphysics of gender (Sally Haslanger's ameliorative analyses; Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity in Gender Trouble, 1990) reshaped philosophy of sex and gender. Political philosophy: Susan Moller Okin's Justice, Gender, and the Family (1989) pressed liberal theories of justice on the family; intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw) transformed analyses of oppression; feminist philosophy of disability, ecofeminism and global feminisms extend the field's scope.

Debates

Feminist philosophers disagree among themselves — liberal, radical, Marxist/socialist, psychoanalytic, poststructuralist and decolonial feminisms diverge on the nature of gender, the state, and the goals of liberation — and the field debates its own category ("woman"), its relation to queer and trans theory, and the persistent under-representation of women in academic philosophy, which feminist philosophers have documented and worked to change.
