Quantified self

The quantified self is both a cultural phenomenon and a community organised around "self-knowledge through numbers": the practice of tracking data about one's own life — physical activity, sleep, diet, mood, productivity, physiology and more — using wearable devices, smartphone apps and manual logging, in order to gain insight and change behaviour.

Wearable sensors made continuous self-tracking practical for millions.

History

The term was proposed in 2007 by Wired editors Gary Wolf and Kevin Kelly, who founded the Quantified Self community and its conferences, where members present personal experiments in "show and tell" talks. The idea has older roots in life-logging and in the self-experimentation of scientists such as Seth Roberts, but it spread widely after 2010 with the mass adoption of smartphones, activity trackers such as Fitbit, and later smartwatches and continuous glucose monitors.

Practices and technologies

Common self-tracking targets include steps and exercise, heart rate and heart-rate variability, sleep stages, blood glucose, weight, caffeine and alcohol intake, work focus and location. Enthusiasts often run informal n-of-1 experiments, correlating variables to find what affects their own health or productivity. The resulting data have also entered medicine, where patient-generated data may inform chronic disease management.

Criticism

Commentators have raised concerns about privacy and the commercial exploitation of intimate data, the accuracy of consumer devices, obsessive tracking and orthosomnia (sleep anxiety caused by tracker data), reductionism about health, and the shifting of responsibility for well-being from institutions to individuals. Sociologists describe the phenomenon under labels such as the "datafied self" and digital self-tracking culture.
