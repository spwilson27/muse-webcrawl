Commonwealth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commonwealth is a traditional English term for a political community founded for the common good. Historically, it has sometimes been synonymous with "republic". The noun "commonwealth", meaning "public welfare; general good or advantage", dates from the 15th century. Originally a phrase (the common-wealth or the common wealth – echoed in the modern synonym "public wealth") it comes from the old meaning of "wealth", which is "well-being", and is itself a loose translation of the Latin res publica (from which "republic" derives). The term literally meant "common well-being".

Historical usage

In the 17th century, the definition of "commonwealth" expanded from its original sense of the public good to mean a state in which the supreme power is vested in the people — a republic. The term was given literary expression by Thomas Hobbes, whose Leviathan (1651) analysed the "Common-wealth" as the artificial person created by covenant among individuals.

Between 1649 and 1660, following the execution of Charles I, England, Scotland, and Ireland were ruled as a republic known as the Commonwealth of England, under the leadership first of the Rump Parliament and then of Oliver Cromwell as Lord Protector (the period 1653–1659 is usually called the Protectorate). The word thus acquired a specifically republican association in British history.

Modern usages

The Commonwealth of Nations

The best-known modern usage is the Commonwealth of Nations, a voluntary association of 56 independent and equal countries, most of them former territories of the British Empire. It originated in the Imperial Conferences of the late 19th and early 20th centuries and took modern shape with the London Declaration of 1949, which allowed republics such as India to remain members while recognising the British monarch as symbolic Head of the Commonwealth. Member states cooperate on the basis of shared values set out in the Commonwealth Charter, including democracy, human rights, and the rule of law. Rwanda and Mozambique, and later Gabon and Togo, joined despite having little or no historical connection to the British Empire.

United States

Four of the constituent states of the United States officially designate themselves "commonwealths": Kentucky, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, and Virginia. The word appears in Massachusetts's constitution of 1780 and carries no legal distinction from other states; the title emphasises government based on the common consent of the people. Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands are also organised as commonwealths in free association with the United States.

Australia and other uses

The federal government of Australia is officially the Commonwealth of Australia, named at federation in 1901. The Bahamas uses the style "Commonwealth of The Bahamas", and Dominica is officially the Commonwealth of Dominica. Iceland's medieval free state (c. 930–1262) is known in English as the Icelandic Commonwealth. In political science, the term is also used for the former Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the dual monarchy of Poland and Lithuania (1569–1795), which, though a monarchy, styled itself Rzeczpospolita — a rendering of res publica.
