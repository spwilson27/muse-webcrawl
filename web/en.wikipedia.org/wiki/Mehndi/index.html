Mehndi

Mehndi (also spelled mehendi or henna) is a form of temporary body art in which intricate designs are applied to the skin using a paste made from the powdered leaves of the henna plant (Lawsonia inermis). The paste stains the skin a reddish-brown colour that fades over one to three weeks. Mehndi is a central tradition in South Asian, Middle Eastern and North African cultures, most famously at weddings.

History

Henna has been used for skin decoration, hair dye and textile dyeing for thousands of years, with evidence from ancient Egypt, where it was used to dye hair and nails, and from the Middle East and South Asia. The art reached a high degree of sophistication in Mughal India, where elaborate bridal mehndi became a courtly and popular tradition.

Application and styles

Fresh henna leaves are dried, ground and mixed with water, lemon juice and essential oils (traditionally eucalyptus) into a paste, which is applied through a cone or syringe in fine lines. As the paste dries and is left on the skin — traditionally overnight — the dye (lawsone) binds to keratin, and the stain darkens over the following day. Major styles include:

  • Indian/Pakistani — dense, intricate patterns covering hands and forearms, including paisleys, flowers and figurative motifs, often telling a story on bridal designs.
  • Arabic — bolder, more open floral and vine designs with larger spaces of bare skin.
  • Moroccan/African — geometric, angular patterns with symbolic meanings.

Wedding tradition

At South Asian weddings, the bride's mehndi ceremony (the mehndi ki raat) is a major pre-wedding celebration with music and dance. Tradition holds that the darker the bride's stain, the deeper her husband's love — or, in another version, the better she will be treated by her mother-in-law. The groom's name is often hidden in the design for him to find on the wedding night.

Safety

Natural henna is generally safe, but "black henna" — henna adulterated with paraphenylenediamine (PPD) to produce a dark, fast stain — can cause severe allergic reactions and permanent scarring, and is discouraged by dermatologists.
