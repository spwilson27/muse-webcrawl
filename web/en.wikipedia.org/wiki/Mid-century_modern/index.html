Mid-century modern

Mid-century modern (MCM) is an American design movement in architecture, interior design, product design, and graphic design that flourished from roughly 1945 to the late 1960s, with roots in the 1930s. The term, popularized by Cara Green's 1984 book Mid-Century Modern: Furniture of the 1950s, describes work characterized by clean lines, organic and geometric forms, integration with nature, and the use of new materials and production methods developed during and after the Second World War.

Architecture

Mid-century residential architecture favoured flat or low-pitched planes, large glass walls dissolving the boundary between indoors and outdoors, open floor plans, and post-and-beam construction. California's Case Study Houses, sponsored by Arts & Architecture magazine, and Joseph Eichler's mass-produced tract homes brought the style to the middle class; Palm Springs became a celebrated centre of "desert modernism". Architects such as Richard Neutra, John Lautner, and Craig Ellwood exemplified the idiom.

Furniture and design

Furniture designers exploited moulded plywood, fibreglass, plastics, and aluminium. Iconic pieces include the Eames lounge chair and moulded plastic shell chairs, Saarinen's tulip table and womb chair, George Nelson's platform bench and bubble lamps, Isamu Noguchi's coffee table, and Arne Jacobsen's egg and swan chairs in Denmark. Scandinavian modern design, with its warm woods and craftsmanship, developed in parallel and heavily cross-fertilized the American movement.

Legacy

The style fell from fashion in the 1970s but has enjoyed a sustained revival since the 1990s, driven by collectors, reproduction manufacturers, and popular culture such as the television series Mad Men. Original pieces by the major designers command high prices, and "mid-century modern" has become a pervasive marketing term in real estate and home furnishings — sometimes applied loosely to almost any design of the postwar decades.
