The Universalis Cosmographia is a wall map of the world drawn by the German cartographer Martin Waldseemüller and published at Saint-Dié-des-Vosges in Lorraine in April 1507. It is famous as the first map to use the name "America" for the newly encountered lands in the western Atlantic, and the first to depict those lands as a continent separated from Asia by a great ocean — before Europeans had actually seen the Pacific from the New World. The Library of Congress, which acquired the sole surviving copy in 2003, calls it "America's birth certificate".

Content

The map was printed from woodblocks on twelve sheets forming an image about 1.3 by 2.3 meters, in a heart-shaped (cordiform) modification of Ptolemy's projection, and was issued with the explanatory booklet Cosmographiae Introductio and a set of globe gores. Perhaps a thousand copies were printed. Waldseemüller drew on Ptolemy for the Old World and on the latest Portuguese and Spanish voyage reports — and on the letters attributed to Amerigo Vespucci — for the new discoveries. Portraits of Ptolemy and Vespucci flank the top of the map. The southern continent is labeled AMERICA across what is now Brazil; a strait is suggested through the continent far to the south; and the landmass is drawn with sea on both sides, a bold conjecture, since Balboa first saw the Pacific in 1513 and Magellan's fleet first crossed it in 1520–21. The map also shows Cuba as an island (labeled Isabella) and depicts the Caribbean from Columbus's voyages.

Survival and acquisition

All copies of the 1507 wall map were thought lost until 1901, when the Jesuit historian Joseph Fischer rediscovered one bound in a portfolio of maps at Schloss Wolfegg in Germany, in the library of the princes of Waldburg-Wolfegg. It remained there, largely unknown, for a century. In 2001 the Library of Congress agreed to purchase the map for ten million dollars — the largest sum it had ever paid for a single item — and the sale, after export approval, was completed in 2003. The map was formally handed over in 2007, at a ceremony attended by Chancellor Angela Merkel marking the 500th anniversary of the naming of America, and is on permanent display in the Library's Thomas Jefferson Building.
