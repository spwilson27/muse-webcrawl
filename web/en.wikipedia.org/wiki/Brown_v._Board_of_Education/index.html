Brown v. Board of Education

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that ruled state laws establishing racial segregation in public schools unconstitutional — because "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal". The unanimous opinion by Chief Justice Earl Warren overturned the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) for public education, holding that segregation violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Context and aftermath

The case consolidated challenges from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware and the District of Columbia (decided companionably for DC on Fifth Amendment grounds), litigated by the NAACP Legal Defense Fund under Thurgood Marshall. The Court's short, non-accusatory opinion famously relied in part on social-science evidence of segregation's psychological harms (footnote 11, long debated). Brown II (1955) remanded implementation to local courts "with all deliberate speed" — a formula under which massive resistance stalled desegregation for a decade until Congress (Civil Rights Act 1964) and later decisions (Green, 1968; Swann, 1971) compelled it. Brown's moral authority made it the archetype of judicial rights-protection; debates continue over its rationale (anti-subordination versus colour-blindness), its limits (de facto segregation, school-funding inequality) and its method (originalist defences and critiques).
