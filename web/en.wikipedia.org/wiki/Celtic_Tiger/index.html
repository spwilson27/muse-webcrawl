Celtic Tiger

Celtic Tiger (Irish: An Tíogar Ceilteach) is a nickname for the Irish economy during the boom years of rapid economic growth between the mid-1990s and the late 2000s. The term, coined by Morgan Stanley analyst Kevin Gardiner in 1994 by analogy with the East Asian Tiger economies, is generally applied to two phases: the export-led growth of 1995–2000, and the credit- and construction-fuelled boom of 2001–2007.

Causes

The first phase combined foreign direct investment — especially from US technology and pharmaceutical firms attracted by Ireland's low 12.5% corporation tax, English-speaking educated workforce, and EU membership — with social partnership wage agreements, EU structural funds, demographic growth, and rising female labour-force participation. GDP grew at rates averaging around 9–10% per year in the late 1990s, unemployment fell below 4%, and Ireland changed from a country of emigration to one of net immigration.

The second phase and the crash

After 2001, growth increasingly depended on domestic credit and a property bubble. House prices roughly quadrupled between 1996 and 2007. When the global financial crisis struck, the bubble burst; the state guaranteed the banking system in September 2008, property prices ultimately fell by more than half, unemployment rose above 15%, and in November 2010 Ireland entered an EU–IMF bailout programme. The subsequent decade of recovery, emigration reversal, and renewed multinational-led growth is sometimes called the "Celtic Phoenix."

Legacy

The Tiger era permanently transformed Ireland's economy and society — motorways, the Luas tram system, and a greatly expanded university sector date from the period — but also left legacies of ghost estates, banking debt, and a lasting political debate about the sustainability of the low-tax, FDI-led model.
