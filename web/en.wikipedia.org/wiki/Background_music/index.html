Background music

Background music is ubiquitous in retail environments
Related termsElevator music, Muzak, incidental music
Common settingsRetail, restaurants, hotels, elevators, hold systems

Background music is music performed or played back in a setting where it is not the primary focus of listeners, intended to enhance the atmosphere of a space rather than to be actively attended to. It is ubiquitous in shops, restaurants, hotels, elevators, airports, and telephone hold systems, and research in consumer psychology suggests it can influence mood, perceived waiting time, pace of movement, and purchasing behaviour.

History

The concept grew out of functional uses of music such as the French composer Erik Satie's musique d'ameublement ("furniture music") of 1917. In the 1930s the Muzak Corporation began piping specially recorded instrumental music into commercial premises, developing "Stimulus Progression" programming that varied tempo and intensity through the working day. Muzak became a generic term for the bland instrumental background style often called elevator music. By the late 20th century most providers had shifted to foreground music services using original-artist recordings.

Psychology and commerce

Studies have found that tempo, volume, genre, and familiarity of background music can affect how long customers stay in a store, how quickly they eat, and how much they spend. Slow music tends to slow shopper movement and increase sales volume in some settings, while music congruent with a product's image — for example, classical music in a wine shop — can steer buyers toward more expensive choices. Retailers and hospitality brands therefore curate playlists as part of their brand identity.

In media

In film, television, and video games, background music (the score or soundtrack) supports mood and narrative without being the focus of a scene. Adaptive game music changes dynamically with gameplay. Production music libraries also supply background tracks for broadcasts and advertising.

Criticism

Forced background music in workplaces and public spaces has drawn criticism from campaign groups such as Pipedown, which argue it constitutes noise pollution and can distress some listeners, including people with hearing impairments or certain neurological conditions.
