Probit model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The probit model maps a linear predictor to a probability using the standard normal CDF, Φ.

In statistics, a probit model is a type of regression where the dependent variable can take only two values, for example married or not married. The word is a portmanteau, coming from probability + unit. The purpose of the model is to estimate the probability that an observation with particular characteristics will fall into a specific one of the categories; moreover, classifying observations based on their predicted probabilities is a type of binary classification model.

Specification

A probit model expresses the probability of the outcome as Pr(Y = 1 | X) = Φ(XTβ), where Φ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution and β is a vector of parameters to be estimated, usually by maximum likelihood. Equivalently, the model can be written with an unobserved latent variable Y* = XTβ + ε, with ε normally distributed; the observed outcome is 1 when Y* exceeds zero. The standard normal distribution of the error term distinguishes probit from its close relative the logit model, which uses the logistic distribution instead; in practice the two give very similar fitted probabilities after rescaling.

History

The probit method was developed in biological assay: Chester Ittner Bliss introduced the term in 1934 while studying dose–response relationships, building on work by J. H. Gaddum and others on transforming sigmoid dose–response curves into straight lines. David Finney's Probit Analysis (1947) codified the technique. Its econometric use as a model of discrete choice grew after the 1970s, alongside the random utility framework developed by Daniel McFadden.

Applications and extensions

Probit models are used to analyse binary outcomes in economics, political science, epidemiology, and marketing—for example labour-force participation, voting, or product adoption. Extensions include the bivariate probit for two correlated binary outcomes, the ordered probit for ordinal responses, and the multinomial probit, which allows flexible correlation among choices in discrete-choice analysis at the cost of more demanding simulation-based estimation.
