Good Samaritan paradox

FieldDeontic logic; philosophical logic
Named forThe parable of the Good Samaritan
RelatedRoss's paradox; paradoxes of deontic logic

The Good Samaritan paradox is a paradox of deontic logic, the formal study of obligation and permission. It arises from an apparently harmless inference: if it ought to be the case that a Good Samaritan helps a man who has been robbed, and helping a robbed man logically implies that the man has been robbed, then by the rule that obligation is closed under logical implication, it ought to be the case that the man has been robbed — an absurd conclusion, since the robbery is precisely what ought not to have happened.

Formulation

In standard deontic logic, if A logically implies B and A ought to be the case, then B ought to be the case. Letting A be "the Samaritan helps the victim and the victim has been robbed", the conclusion follows mechanically. Yet our intuitions reject the idea that anyone's robbery falls under an obligation. The paradox therefore shows that the simple closure principle cannot be right as stated, and it helped motivate more refined systems — including dyadic deontic logics, which treat obligations as conditional ("given that the man has been robbed, the Samaritan ought to help him"), and analyses that resist treating "helps a robbed man" as a single proposition whose parts inherit the obligation.

Significance

Alongside Ross's paradox and the paradox of the gentle murderer, the Good Samaritan paradox is one of the classic puzzles that shaped twentieth-century work on the logic of norms, forcing logicians to distinguish between obligations about states of affairs, the conditions under which they arise, and the acts that fulfil them.
