Njáls saga

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A page from a vellum manuscript of Njáls saga

Njáls saga (Old Norse: Njáls saga, also Brennu-Njáls saga, "The Story of Burnt Njáll") is the longest, most famous, and generally considered the greatest of the Icelandic sagas of Icelanders. Written in the late 13th century (c. 1270–1290) by an unknown author, it spans events from about 960 to 1020, from the settlement generation to the aftermath of the Battle of Clontarf, and is set mainly in southern Iceland.

Plot summary

The saga turns on the friendship between the wise, peace-loving lawyer Njáll Þorgeirsson of Bergþórshvoll and the heroic marksman Gunnar Hámundarson of Hlíðarendi, and on the feuds driven by their formidable wives, Bergþóra and Hallgerðr. Gunnar, trapped by legal manoeuvring and his own honour after killing men in self-defence, is outlawed; refusing to leave Iceland, he is killed defending his home. In the second half, Njáll's sons become entangled in escalating feuds that culminate in the saga's central catastrophe: Flosi Þórðarson and a hundred men burn Njáll, Bergþóra, and their household inside their farmhouse (c. 1011). The avenger Kári Sölmundarson pursues the burners as far as Orkney, Wales, and Rome, before a final reconciliation, sealed by marriage and pilgrimage, ends the feud. Interwoven is the official conversion of Iceland to Christianity in 999/1000.

Themes and style

Njáls saga is celebrated for its intricate plotting, dozens of vividly drawn characters, laconic dialogue, and deep engagement with law: lawsuits at the Althing drive the action, and the saga contrasts vengeance with legal settlement and Christian forgiveness. Its author clearly knew the law and earlier sagas intimately; debate continues over how much of the narrative reflects genuine oral tradition versus literary invention. The work survives in numerous vellum and paper manuscripts — more than any other saga — testifying to its enduring popularity in Iceland, where it remains a cornerstone of national literature, known in English through translations by George Webbe Dasent (1861), Lee M. Hollander, and Robert Cook.
