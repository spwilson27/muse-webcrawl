Mu'allaqat

The Mu'allaqāt (Arabic: المعلقات, [ʔalmuʕallaqaːt]) are a collection of pre-Islamic Arabic qasidas (odes) regarded as the finest works of early Arabic poetry. The name literally means "the suspended (poems)," traditionally explained by the legend that the winning poems of a poetry contest were written in gold and hung on the walls of the Kaaba in Mecca — an account modern scholars generally treat as doubtful.

Contents

The canonical collection contains seven poems, although some versions give nine or ten. The seven poets are usually listed as Imru' al-Qais, Tarafa, Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, Labid, 'Antara ibn Shaddad, 'Amr ibn Kulthum and al-Harith ibn Hilliza. Later additions sometimes include al-Nabigha, al-A'sha and Abid ibn al-Abras.

Form and themes

Each poem follows the conventions of the qasida: it typically opens with the nasib, a nostalgic halt at the abandoned campsite of the beloved, moves to the rahil, the desert journey in which the poet describes his camel or horse in lavish detail, and concludes with the poem's main purpose — praise, boast, satire or moral reflection. The odes display a highly developed metre and rhyme, a rich desert imagery and a tribal ethos of honour, generosity and fatalism.

Transmission

The poems were transmitted orally by professional reciters (rawis) and collected in the 8th century, most famously by the scholar Hammad al-Rawiya. They became foundational texts for Arabic grammar, lexicography and literary criticism. Sir William Jones published an English translation in 1782, introducing the Mu'allaqat to European readers.
