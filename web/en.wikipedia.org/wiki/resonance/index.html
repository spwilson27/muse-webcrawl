Resonance

Amplitude of a driven oscillator peaks at its resonant frequency.

Resonance is the phenomenon in which a physical system responds with larger amplitude to a periodic driving force whose frequency matches (or nearly matches) one of the system's natural frequencies. Resonant systems can be used to generate vibrations of a specific frequency (for example, quartz clocks and musical instruments) or pick out specific frequencies from a complex vibration containing many frequencies (for example, filters in radio receivers).

The term originates from the Latin resonantia ("echo") and was first applied to acoustics, where sympathetic strings vibrate in response to sound at their own pitch. Every mechanical, electrical, and acoustic system has resonant frequencies; the sharpness of the response is described by the Q factor.

Examples and applications

Resonance is exploited in magnetic resonance imaging (nuclear magnetic resonance), microwave cavity resonators, laser optical cavities, RLC circuits in radio tuning, and the design of musical instruments. It can also be destructive: wind-induced resonance and aeroelastic effects contributed to the famous 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge (though the mechanism is usually described as flutter rather than simple forced resonance), and engineers design bridges, buildings, and machinery to avoid dangerous resonant excitation, sometimes installing tuned mass dampers.
