Howard H. Aiken

Howard H. Aiken
Born: March 8, 1900, Hoboken, New Jersey
Died: March 14, 1973, St. Louis, Missouri
Alma mater: University of Wisconsin–Madison; Harvard University
Known for: Harvard Mark I computer

Howard Hathaway Aiken (March 8, 1900 – March 14, 1973) was an American physicist and a pioneer in computing, being the original conceptual designer behind IBM's Harvard Mark I computer. Aiken conceived in 1937 an electromechanical machine to automate the solution of complex numerical problems, reviving ideas of Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine.

Harvard Mark series

Built by IBM and installed at Harvard in 1944, the Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator (Harvard Mark I) was a 51-foot-long electromechanical computer of switches, relays, and rotating shafts driven by punched paper tape; Grace Hopper was among its programmers. Aiken led the design of the successor Mark II, the all-relay Mark III, and the electronic Mark IV, built for the U.S. Navy and academic use.

Later career

Aiken founded Harvard's Computation Laboratory and in 1947 established one of the first master's programs in computer science. He is attributed with the (likely apocryphal in wording) 1947 remark that only six electronic digital computers would be needed to satisfy the computing needs of the entire United States. He received the Harry H. Goode Memorial Award (1964) and the IEEE Edison Medal (1970).
