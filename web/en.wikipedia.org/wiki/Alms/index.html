Alms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alms (from Greek eleēmosynē, "pity, mercy") are money, food, or other material goods donated to people living in poverty. Providing alms is regarded as an act of charity and virtue in many religions and cultures, and in several traditions it is a formal religious obligation rather than a purely voluntary kindness.

In the Abrahamic religions

In Judaism, tzedakah — charitable giving rooted in the concept of justice — is a commandment; Maimonides famously ranked forms of giving, placing anonymous aid that enables self-sufficiency highest. In Christianity, almsgiving is one of the traditional works of mercy and, together with prayer and fasting, one of the three traditional practices of Lent; medieval and monastic institutions distributed alms at church and abbey gates, and the office of almoner arose to administer royal charity. In Islam, zakāt — obligatory almsgiving calculated as a proportion of wealth — is one of the Five Pillars, while ṣadaqah denotes additional voluntary charity.

In the Dharmic religions

In Hinduism, dāna (giving) is a central virtue and is practiced through feeding the poor, gifts to temples and teachers, and endowment of wells and rest houses. In Buddhism, almsgiving is the first of the perfections (pāramitā) for laypeople and the daily material basis of the monastic Sangha: monks and nuns in the Theravada tradition make morning alms rounds (piṇḍapāta), receiving food in their bowls, and lay donors earn merit (puñña) by giving. Jain and Sikh traditions likewise institutionalize charity — Sikh langar kitchens feed all visitors without charge.

Social history

Direct almsgiving long served as the principal form of poor relief. From the early modern period, European states increasingly regulated begging and shifted relief toward poor laws, workhouses, and municipal institutions, while reformers debated whether indiscriminate alms encouraged dependency. Modern welfare systems, philanthropy, and humanitarian aid have taken over much of this function, but almsgiving remains a living religious practice worldwide, from Ramadan food distribution to temple offerings.
