Fuel poverty

Fuel poverty is a condition in which a household cannot afford to keep its home adequately heated (and, in broader definitions, to meet other domestic energy needs) at a reasonable cost. The concept was developed in the United Kingdom from the 1970s, with the researcher Brenda Boardman giving it influential form: under the definition long used in England, a household was fuel poor if it would need to spend more than 10 percent of its income on fuel to maintain a satisfactory heating regime.

Definitions and measurement

Definitions differ between the nations of the UK and internationally. England adopted the "Low Income High Costs" (LIHC) definition following the 2012 Hills Review: a household is fuel poor if its required fuel costs are above the national median and, after paying them, its residual income falls below the poverty line. In 2021 England moved to the "Low Income Low Energy Efficiency" (LILEE) measure, based on low income combined with a home rated below band C in energy efficiency. The wider term "energy poverty" is used internationally for lack of access to modern energy services, a major issue in the developing world.

Causes and effects

Fuel poverty arises from the interaction of low household income, high energy prices and poor energy efficiency of housing — the "leaky" homes that lose heat quickly. Consequences include cold and damp homes associated with respiratory and cardiovascular illness, mental health effects, and contributions to excess winter deaths; households may ration heating, accumulate debt, or choose between "heating and eating".

Responses

Policy responses include energy-efficiency programmes such as insulation and boiler replacement schemes, income support such as the UK's Winter Fuel Payment and Warm Home Discount, price regulation, and minimum energy efficiency standards for rented housing. Fuel poverty rose sharply in many countries during the energy price crisis of 2021–2023.
