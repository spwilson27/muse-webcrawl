Masonic Temple

A Masonic Temple or Masonic Hall is a building used by a Masonic lodge for its meetings and ceremonies. The term "temple" in Freemasonry is used in an allegorical sense, evoking King Solomon's Temple, and does not denote a place of worship; many lodges avoid the term and simply call their buildings Masonic Halls or Masonic Centres.

The lodge room

The heart of a Masonic Temple is the lodge room, laid out symbolically as an oblong square oriented east and west. It typically contains an altar bearing the Volume of the Sacred Law, three great lights (the square, compasses, and the sacred book), chairs for the Worshipful Master in the east and other officers, and a mosaic or checkered pavement. Lodge rooms are decorated with the pillars, globes, and working tools of the craft degrees, and many historic temples feature elaborate murals, pipe organs, and stained glass.

History and notable examples

Purpose-built Masonic halls spread in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries as Freemasonry grew. Famous examples include Freemasons' Hall in London, the headquarters of the United Grand Lodge of England; the House of the Temple in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the Scottish Rite's Southern Jurisdiction; the Masonic Temple in Detroit, the largest such building in the world; and the monumental Masonic Temple in Philadelphia. Besides lodge rooms, larger temples often contain libraries, museums, banqueting halls, theatres, and offices, and many are rented for public events.
