A lawn is an area of soil-covered land planted with grasses and other durable, low-growing plants such as clover, maintained at a short height with a lawn mower and used for aesthetic and recreational purposes. Lawns are a defining feature of suburban landscapes in North America, Australia, and parts of Europe, and also form the playing surfaces of sports such as football, golf, cricket, and bowls.

History

The word "lawn" derives from the Middle English launde, a glade or clearing. Closely cropped grass appeared in the formal gardens of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century France and England, maintained expensively by scythe and grazing animals. The invention of the mechanical lawn mower by Edwin Budding in 1830 made lawns affordable to the middle classes, and the suburban expansion of the twentieth century — especially in the United States — made the uniform front lawn a social norm backed in places by ordinance and homeowners' associations.

Grasses and care

Cool-season grasses (bluegrass, ryegrass, fescue) dominate temperate lawns; warm-season grasses (Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine) suit hot climates. Establishment is by seed, sod, or sprigs. Care involves regular mowing, irrigation, fertilisation, and control of weeds, pests, and diseases, though low-input approaches using clover, fine fescues, and meadow plantings have grown in popularity.

Environmental debate

Lawns are criticised for water consumption, fertiliser and pesticide runoff, mower emissions, and low habitat value compared with more diverse plantings. Defenders note benefits for recreation, cooling, erosion control, and carbon storage in soil. Movements such as "No Mow May" and xeriscaping promote alternatives in suitable climates.
