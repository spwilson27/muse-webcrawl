Ataraxia (Greek: ἀταραξία, "unperturbedness", "tranquillity") is a state of serene calmness and freedom from emotional disturbance and anxiety. It was a central goal of several Hellenistic schools of philosophy, though each understood and pursued it differently.

In the schools

For Epicureanism, ataraxia — together with aponia, the absence of bodily pain — constitutes the highest pleasure and the aim of the philosophical life, achieved by limiting desires, withdrawing from politics and cultivating friendship. For the Pyrrhonism sceptics, ataraxia is the result of suspending judgement (epoché) about all non-evident matters: Sextus Empiricus famously compared it to a shadow following the body, arriving when one stops seeking to resolve dogmatic questions. The Stoics valued a related state, though for them tranquillity follows from living according to reason and nature rather than being the goal itself, and they more often speak of freedom from the destructive passions (pathe).

Related concepts

Ataraxia is often paired with autarkeia (self-sufficiency) in Epicurean texts. The concept influenced later discussions of tranquillity of mind in Roman philosophy, including Seneca's De tranquillitate animi, and has been compared — with caution — to ideals of equanimity in Buddhist and other traditions.
