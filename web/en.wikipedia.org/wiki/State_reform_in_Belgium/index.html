State reform in Belgium

State reform in Belgium is the ongoing process of constitutional revision that has transformed Belgium from a unitary state into a federal state, in response to tensions between its Dutch-speaking Flemish population and its French-speaking Walloon population. Six major reforms have been completed since 1970.

The six reforms

The first state reform (1970) recognized three cultural communities and laid the groundwork for regional autonomy. The second (1980) created the Flemish and Walloon Regions and the Flemish and French Communities with their own parliaments and governments. The third (1988–89) devolved education to the communities and created the Brussels-Capital Region. The fourth (1993) formally declared Belgium a federal state in Article 1 of the Constitution and introduced direct election of regional parliaments. The fifth (2001, the Lambermont and Lombard agreements) expanded regional fiscal autonomy and agricultural and foreign-trade powers.

The sixth reform

After the record-breaking government formation crisis of 2010–2011 (541 days), the sixth state reform (2012–2014) under the Di Rupo government transferred further powers — including family allowances, aspects of health care, and employment policy — to the communities and regions, reformed the Senate into a chamber of the federated entities, and split the Brussels-Halle-Vilvoorde electoral arrondissement. Belgium's federal structure now comprises three regions (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels) and three language communities (Flemish, French, German-speaking), whose overlapping institutions make it one of the most complex federal systems in the world. Debate continues over a possible seventh reform, with Flemish nationalist parties advocating confederalism.
