Abseiling

Abseiling (from German abseilen, "to rope down"), also known as rappelling in North America, is the controlled descent of a steep slope, such as a rock face, by moving down a rope. Climbers use this technique when a cliff or slope is too steep or dangerous to descend without protection. The technique is also used in caving, canyoning, rescue operations and military applications.

Technique

The descender is anchored to a secure point at the top, clips the rope through a friction device such as a figure-eight or tubular belay device attached to their harness, and walks or slides down the face while controlling the rate of descent with the brake hand. Speed is regulated by the friction in the device and by the angle of the rope in the brake hand.

History

The technique is often credited to Jean Charlet-Straton, a Chamonix guide who devised a method of descending by rope during a failed attempt on the Petit Dru in 1876. Early climbers used the body rappel (the "Dülfersitz"), in which the rope passed around the body; modern practice uses mechanical descenders and harnesses.

Safety

Abseiling is statistically one of the more hazardous parts of climbing, because the climber relies entirely on the anchor and equipment. Common safeguards include using a prusik backup knot, tying stopper knots in the rope ends and checking anchors and harness connections before descent.
