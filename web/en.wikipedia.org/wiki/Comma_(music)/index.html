In music theory, a comma is a very small interval, the difference between two kinds of tuning systems or between two pitches that are nominally the same note. Commas arise because the pure intervals of just intonation cannot all be combined consistently: stacking perfect fifths never quite returns to a stack of octaves, and just major thirds disagree with fifths. Temperaments exist largely in order to distribute — "temper out" — these commas.

[Image: comma as the gap between two nearly identical pitches]

Pythagorean comma

The Pythagorean comma is the difference between twelve perfect fifths (3/2)12 and seven octaves (2/1)7, the ratio 531441/524288, about 23.46 cents — roughly a quarter of a semitone. A circle of twelve pure fifths overshoots the octave by this amount, so some fifth in any closed Pythagorean system must be narrowed (the "wolf" interval) or the comma must be spread across the circle.

Syntonic comma

The syntonic comma, ratio 81/80 or about 21.51 cents, is the difference between a major third built from four perfect fifths (81/64) and the pure major third 5/4 (80/64). It is also the difference between the two whole tones of just intonation, 9/8 and 10/9. Quarter-comma meantone, the dominant tuning of the Renaissance and Baroque eras, tempers each fifth flat by one quarter of the syntonic comma so that major thirds are pure.

Other commas

Many other commas are defined in tuning theory, including the lesser diesis (128/125), the septimal comma (64/63), and the schisma (32805/32768, about 1.95 cents) — the difference between the Pythagorean and syntonic commas. In twelve-tone equal temperament, both the Pythagorean and syntonic commas are tempered out, which is why enharmonic notes coincide on a modern piano.
