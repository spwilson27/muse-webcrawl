Pre-Code Hollywood

Pre-Code Hollywood refers to the period in the American film industry between the widespread adoption of sound in 1929 and the enforcement of the Motion Picture Production Code (the "Hays Code") in mid-1934. Although the Code was adopted in 1930, it was largely unenforced during these years, and films of the era frequently depicted subjects that would soon be forbidden: sexual frankness, violence, drug use, interracial relationships, and sympathetic criminals.

Background

Scandals of the 1920s and fear of federal censorship led the studios to create the Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America under Will H. Hays, which issued a list of "Don'ts and Be Carefuls" in 1927 and adopted a formal Production Code in 1930. With the Great Depression pressuring box office receipts, studios competed with sensational content and routinely ignored the Code. Films such as Baby Face (1933), Scarface (1932), Red-Headed Woman (1932) and The Public Enemy (1931) exemplified the era's boldness.

Enforcement and legacy

Pressure from the Catholic Legion of Decency and state censorship boards forced the industry in July 1934 to require a seal of approval from the Production Code Administration under Joseph Breen for every film. Scripts were vetted in advance and content rules were enforced for the next three decades, until the Code was replaced by the MPAA rating system in 1968. The pre-Code films, long difficult to see, were rediscovered from the 1990s onward and are now celebrated for their frankness and strong roles for actresses such as Barbara Stanwyck, Joan Blondell and Miriam Hopkins.
