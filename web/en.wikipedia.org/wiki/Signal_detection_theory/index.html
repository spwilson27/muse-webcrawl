Signal detection theory

Signal detection theory (SDT) is a framework for measuring the ability to differentiate between information-bearing patterns (called a "stimulus" in living organisms or a "signal" in machines) and random patterns that distract from the information (called noise). Originally developed during World War II for radar operators deciding whether a blip represented an enemy aircraft, and formalized by Wilson P. Tanner and John A. Swets in the 1950s, the theory has been applied throughout psychology, medical diagnosis, quality control, and machine learning.

Basic model

On each trial the observer decides whether a signal is present. Four outcomes are possible: a hit (signal present, reported present), a miss (signal present, reported absent), a false alarm (signal absent, reported present), and a correct rejection. The theory assumes the observer's evidence on each trial is drawn from one of two overlapping distributions — noise alone, or signal plus noise. Sensitivity is measured by d′ (d-prime), the standardized separation between the two distributions, which is independent of the observer's decision bias. The placement of the decision criterion — how much evidence is required to say "yes" — reflects bias or response strategy, influenced by payoffs and expectations.

ROC curves

Varying the criterion traces a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which plots hit rate against false-alarm rate; the area under the curve (AUC) summarizes overall discriminability and is widely used to evaluate diagnostic tests and classifiers.

Applications

SDT underlies the analysis of recognition memory experiments, vigilance and eyewitness identification research, radiology (detecting tumors in scans), lie detection critiques, and the evaluation of screening tests, where it separates a test's true sensitivity from the threshold chosen for calling a result positive.
