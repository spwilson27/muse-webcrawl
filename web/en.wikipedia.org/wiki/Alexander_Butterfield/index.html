Alexander Porter Butterfield (born April 6, 1926) is a retired American Air Force officer and government official who served as deputy assistant to President Richard Nixon. On 16 July 1973, testifying before the Senate Watergate Committee chaired by Sam Ervin, he revealed the existence of the secret White House taping system that recorded nearly all of Nixon's conversations — the disclosure that led directly to Nixon's downfall.

Early life and White House service

Butterfield was born in Pensacola, Florida, into a Navy family and became a career Air Force pilot, serving in Vietnam. A college friend of Nixon's chief of staff H. R. Haldeman, he joined the White House in 1969 as Haldeman's deputy, supervising the President's daily schedule and the flow of paper to the Oval Office. In that administrative capacity he was made the official in charge of the secret taping system installed by the Secret Service from 1971 at Nixon's order.

The revelation

In a private interview with committee staff investigator Scott Armstrong on 13 July 1973, Butterfield — asked a question staff had posed to many witnesses as a matter of completeness — confirmed that a taping system existed and had recorded the President's conversations. Three days later, questioned by minority counsel Fred Thompson in public session, he confirmed it on live television: "Everything was taped… as long as the President was in attendance." The subpoena battle over the tapes that followed consumed the Nixon presidency.

Later life

Butterfield had already left the White House in March 1973 to head the Federal Aviation Administration under Nixon, serving until 1975; he later worked in business in California. In 2015, Bob Woodward's book The Last of the President's Men drew on extensive interviews with Butterfield, who had kept copies of many White House documents.
