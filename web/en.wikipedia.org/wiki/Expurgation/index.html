Expurgation

Expurgation, also known as bowdlerization, is a form of censorship that involves removing or altering portions of a work — typically a text — that are considered objectionable, vulgar, blasphemous, or otherwise unsuitable for an intended audience. The resulting edition is said to be expurgated. The term "bowdlerize" derives from Thomas Bowdler, whose 1818 Family Shakespeare omitted passages he deemed unfit for women and children to read aloud.

History

The practice is ancient: Roman and early Christian authorities produced corrected editions of classical works, and in the sixteenth century the Roman Catholic Church issued the Index Expurgatorius, listing specific passages to be deleted from otherwise permitted books. In the nineteenth century, expurgated editions of Shakespeare, the Bible, and classical literature were marketed for family and school use.

Notable examples

  • The Family Shakespeare (1807, by Henrietta Bowdler; expanded 1818 by Thomas Bowdler) removed sexual innuendo and profanity from the plays.
  • Expurgated school editions of authors such as Chaucer, Swift, and Mark Twain altered or deleted profanity and coarse episodes.
  • In 2011, a NewSouth Edition of Adventures of Huckleberry Finn replaced racial slurs with the word "slave," provoking wide debate about altering classic texts.
  • Posthumous revisions of Roald Dahl's children's books in 2023 by Puffin Books, softening language about weight, gender, and appearance, drew criticism and led the publisher to also keep the original texts in print.

Expurgation versus censorship

Expurgation differs from outright banning: the work remains available, but in altered form. Defenders argue that expurgation makes classic works accessible to younger readers or aligns older material with current sensitivities; critics respond that silent alteration distorts an author's intent and the historical record, and that readers are denied the chance to encounter and judge the original. The debate commonly resurfaces when publishers revise works by deceased authors without their consent.

Other media

The term is also applied to edited versions of films (for example, airline or television edits that remove profanity and nudity), music with explicit lyrics replaced by "clean" radio edits, and translations that omit passages offensive in the target culture.
