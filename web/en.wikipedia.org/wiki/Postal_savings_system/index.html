Postal savings system

Postal savings systems provide depositors who do not have access to banks a safe and convenient method to save money and to facilitate savings among the poor. Post offices, with their wide geographic reach, accept small deposits and typically invest the funds in government securities, with the state guaranteeing the deposits.

History

The first modern postal savings system was established in the United Kingdom in 1861 as the Post Office Savings Bank, intended to encourage thrift among working people and to provide the government with a source of finance. The model spread widely: New Zealand (1867), Canada (1868), and many European and colonial administrations followed. Japan Post Bank, founded in 1875, grew into one of the largest deposit-taking institutions in the world. The United States Postal Savings System operated from 1911 to 1967.

Operation

Customers open accounts at post office counters, deposit and withdraw cash, and earn modest interest. Because post offices exist in rural and low-income areas where commercial banks may not, postal savings are a major instrument of financial inclusion. In some countries the systems have evolved into full postal banks offering loans, insurance, and payment services.

Modern role

Postal savings remain significant in countries such as Japan, France (La Banque Postale), India (India Post Payments Bank and post office savings schemes), and China. Debates continue over whether postal banks complement or compete unfairly with private banks, and periodic proposals to revive postal banking in the United States draw on this history.
