Grands ensembles

Grands ensembles ("large estates" or "large housing projects") are large-scale social housing developments built in France between the mid-1950s and the mid-1970s, typically on the outskirts of major cities. Characterised by repetitive modernist slabs (barres) and towers (tours) built with industrialised methods, they were constructed to address the severe housing shortage of the post-war period — the Trente Glorieuses — amid rapid urbanisation and the arrival of repatriates and immigrants.

History

The term was defined administratively in the 1958 creation of the zones à urbaniser en priorité (ZUP), which enabled developments of hundreds or thousands of dwellings at once. Early estates such as Sarcelles, north of Paris, were initially welcomed for their modern comforts — running water, central heating, bathrooms — lacking in older housing. Famous examples include Toulouse-Le Mirail, designed by Candilis, Josic and Woods, and the estates of the Parisian banlieues.

Criticism and renewal

From the late 1960s the grands ensembles were criticised for monotony, poor sound insulation, lack of services, and social isolation — "sarcellite" became a byword for anomie in Sarcelles. Construction of very large estates was halted by government circular in 1973. Many estates later became concentrations of unemployment and immigrant populations, and some were focal points of the 2005 French riots. Since the 2000s the national urban renewal programme (ANRU) has demolished and rebuilt substantial parts of the stock, replacing some towers with smaller-scale housing.
