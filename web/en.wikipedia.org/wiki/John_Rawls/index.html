John Rawls (1921–2002) was an American moral and political philosopher, widely regarded as the most important political philosopher of the 20th century in the English-speaking world. He taught for most of his career at Harvard University.

A Theory of Justice

His 1971 book revived systematic normative Political philosophy by proposing "justice as fairness": principles of justice are those that free and equal persons would choose in an "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance" that hides their place in society, talents and conception of the good. Rawls argues such parties would choose, first, a scheme of equal basic liberties, and second, social and economic inequalities arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the difference principle) and attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity.

Later work and criticism

In Political Liberalism (1993) Rawls recast the theory as a political, not metaphysical, doctrine that citizens with incompatible religious and philosophical doctrines could endorse by an "overlapping consensus". The Law of Peoples (1999) extended his approach to international justice. Major critics include Robert Nozick's libertarian Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), communitarians such as Michael Sandel, and feminist and critical race theorists who questioned the theory's abstractions.
