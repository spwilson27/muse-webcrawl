Jewish outreach

Jewish outreach, known in Hebrew as kiruv (קירוב, "bringing close"), describes the efforts of religious Jews and organisations to draw secular or less observant Jews closer to Orthodox Jewish practice. Those who become newly observant through outreach are called baalei teshuva ("masters of return/repentance"). Outreach is distinguished from Jewish proselytism directed at non-Jews, which mainstream Judaism has historically not practised actively.

History

Although informal efforts are centuries old, organised outreach grew sharply after the Second World War and especially after the 1960s, in response to rising assimilation and intermarriage. The Lubavitcher Rebbe, Menachem Mendel Schneerson, launched "mitzvah campaigns" encouraging Jews to perform individual commandments, and his Chabad-Lubavitch movement built a global network of emissaries (shluchim) and Chabad Houses, often on university campuses.

Organisations and methods

Major outreach bodies include Chabad, Aish HaTorah (founded in Jerusalem in 1974 by Rabbi Noah Weinberg), Ohr Somayach, and campus organisations such as Hillel and MEOR. Methods range from free Shabbat hospitality, classes, study partnerships (chavruta) and yeshivas for beginners, to one-to-one "mitzvah tanks", holiday campaigns and online content. Critics within Judaism debate the movement's emphasis on observance over other denominations' approaches, while supporters credit kiruv with reconnecting thousands of Jews to their heritage.
