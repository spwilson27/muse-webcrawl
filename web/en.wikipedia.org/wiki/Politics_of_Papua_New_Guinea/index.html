Politics of Papua New Guinea

Polity type: Unitary parliamentary constitutional monarchy

Monarch: King of Papua New Guinea

Representative: Governor-General

Head of government: Prime Minister

The politics of Papua New Guinea take place within a Westminster-style parliamentary democracy under a constitutional monarchy. Papua New Guinea is a Commonwealth realm: the monarch of Papua New Guinea is head of state, represented in the country by a Governor-General, while executive power rests with the Prime Minister and cabinet.

Constitution

The Constitution of Papua New Guinea entered into force at independence from Australia on 16 September 1975. It establishes the National Parliament, an independent judiciary headed by the Supreme Court and National Court, and protects fundamental rights.

Executive branch

The Governor-General is appointed by the monarch on the advice of the National Parliament, following a vote. The Prime Minister is elected by parliament after a general election or when the office falls vacant, and appoints the National Executive Council (cabinet). Governments are formed from shifting coalitions of parties and independents, and votes of no confidence are a recurring feature of parliamentary life, moderated by constitutional grace periods that bar such motions in the first 18 months of a parliament and in the final year before an election.

Legislature

The National Parliament is unicameral. Members are elected from open (provincial and district) electorates by limited preferential voting, reintroduced for the 2007 election to reduce the very high vote shares once needed under first-past-the-post in a fragmented field.

Political parties

Political parties in Papua New Guinea tend to be weakly institutionalised and organised around personalities rather than platforms. Historically significant parties include Pangu Party, the People's Progress Party, the National Alliance Party and the People's National Congress. The Organic Law on the Integrity of Political Parties and Candidates was enacted to strengthen party discipline, though parts were struck down by the Supreme Court in 2010.
