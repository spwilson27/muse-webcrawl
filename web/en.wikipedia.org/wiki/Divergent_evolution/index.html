Divergent evolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Darwin's finches illustrate divergent evolution

Divergent evolution or divergent selection is the accumulation of differences between closely related populations within a species, sometimes leading to speciation. Divergent evolution is typically exhibited when two populations become separated by a geographic barrier (such as in allopatric or peripatric speciation) or experience different selective pressures, driving adaptations to different environments.

After many generations and continual evolution, the populations become less able to interbreed with one another. Structures arising from divergent evolution in related species are called homologous structures: they share a common origin but may perform different functions, as with the pentadactyl limb of vertebrates adapted for walking, flying, and swimming.

Examples

Charles Darwin's finches on the Galapagos Islands famously diverged from a common ancestor into species with different beak shapes suited to different foods, an example of adaptive radiation. The divergent evolution of wolves and domestic dogs from a common ancestor, and the variety of dog breeds produced by artificial selection, is another familiar case.

Contrast with convergent evolution

Divergent evolution contrasts with convergent evolution, in which unrelated species independently evolve similar traits (analogous structures) in response to similar environments, such as the streamlined bodies of sharks, dolphins, and ichthyosaurs.
