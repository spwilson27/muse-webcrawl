Catallactics

Catallactics is a theory of how a free price system reaches its results, developed by the Austrian School of economics. The term was coined by the English theologian and philosopher Richard Whately in his Introductory Lectures on Political Economy (1831), who proposed "catallactics" (from the Greek katallattein, "to exchange", also "to reconcile" or "to admit into a community") as a more accurate name than "political economy" for the science of exchanges. The concept was later elaborated by Ludwig von Mises, who made catallactics the branch of praxeology concerned with market phenomena — prices, exchange ratios, interest, profit and loss — arising from voluntary exchange.

Development

Whately argued that economics studies not wealth itself but the exchanges through which people cooperate. The idea was largely dormant until Mises adopted it in works such as Human Action (1949), defining catallactics as the analysis of the market economy: the determination of money prices for goods and services of all orders through supply and demand. Friedrich Hayek later distinguished a spontaneously ordered catallaxy — the network of relationships produced by market exchange — from a deliberately organised economy, arguing that a catallaxy has no single hierarchy of ends.
