Horses in World War I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Horses hauling artillery on the Western Front

The use of horses in World War I marked the last era in which horses were deployed in vast numbers in war, even as mechanised warfare — machine guns, tanks, and motor transport — was rendering cavalry obsolete on the battlefield. All major belligerents relied on horses for cavalry, artillery traction, supply wagons, and ambulances; some 8 million horses, donkeys, and mules are estimated to have died during the war.

Roles

At the war's opening in 1914, cavalry was still expected to exploit breakthroughs, and cavalry divisions manoeuvred in the war of movement. On the static Western Front, horses shifted overwhelmingly to logistics: a single British division required thousands of horses to move guns, ammunition, and fodder. On the Eastern Front and in the Middle East, mounted troops remained decisive — as in the Australian Light Horse charge at Beersheba in 1917. Horses suffered terribly from shellfire, gas, mud, disease, exhaustion, and shortages of fodder; veterinary hospitals, horse hospitals and evacuation stations treated millions of animals, and the British Army Veterinary Corps returned a high proportion of casualties to service.

Supply

Britain requisitioned horses at home and imported over a million from North America; the United States and other belligerents similarly drew on civilian horse populations. Remount depots purchased, conditioned, and shipped animals by sea, where losses to U-boats and disease were significant. At the Armistice, surplus horses were sold locally, put to work in reconstruction, or in many cases slaughtered; the fate of British horses sold in Egypt became a public scandal that led Dorothy Brooke to found the Old War Horse Memorial Hospital in Cairo in 1934.

Legacy

The Animals in War Memorial in London (2004) commemorates the horses and other animals that served. Cultural works such as Michael Morpurgo's novel War Horse (1982) and its stage and film adaptations have kept the subject prominent.
