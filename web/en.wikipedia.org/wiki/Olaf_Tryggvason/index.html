Olaf Tryggvason (c. 968 – 9 September 1000) was King of Norway from 995 to 1000. He was the son of Tryggve Olafsson, a king of Viken, and a great-grandson of Harald Fairhair, the first king of Norway. Olaf is remembered in the sagas as a vigorous champion of Christianity who spread the faith, often by force, through Norway and the Norse settlements of the North Atlantic.

Early life

According to the sagas, after his father's death Olaf was taken by his mother Astrid into exile, fleeing the sons of Gunnhild. Tradition held that he was captured by Estonian Vikings and spent part of his childhood in slavery before being ransomed by a relative. He later served in the retinue of Vladimir the Great in Kievan Rus', then became a Viking raider in the Baltic and around the British Isles.

Olaf took part in raids on England in the 990s, and according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle he was confirmed in the Christian faith with King Æthelred the Unready as his sponsor after the Treaty of Andover in 994.

Reign

In 995 Olaf returned to Norway, where the unpopular rule of Haakon Sigurdsson had collapsed. He was acclaimed king and set about consolidating royal power and converting the country. The sagas credit him with founding the town of Nidaros (modern Trondheim) and with compelling the conversion of Iceland, Greenland, the Faroe Islands, and Orkney. The missionary Leif Erikson, son of Erik the Red, was among those converted at Olaf's court.

Death

Olaf's aggressive policies made enemies of the kings of Denmark and Sweden and of the jarls of Lade. On 9 September 1000 he was ambushed at the Battle of Svolder while sailing home from Wendland aboard his great longship, the Long Serpent. Outnumbered, he is said in the sagas to have leapt into the sea rather than surrender; his body was never recovered, which gave rise to legends that he had survived.

Legacy

Olaf's life was recorded in Latin histories by Oddr Snorrason and Gunnlaugr Leifsson and later in Heimskringla by Snorri Sturluson. Though his reign was brief, his missionary work made him one of the two great "missionary kings" of Norway alongside Olaf Haraldsson (St Olaf). A monument to him, the Olaf Tryggvason Monument, stands in Trondheim.
