Vampire bats, members of the subfamily Desmodontinae, are leaf-nosed bats currently found in Central and South America. Their food source is the blood of other animals, a dietary trait called hematophagy. Three extant bat species feed solely on blood: the common vampire bat (Desmodus rotundus), the hairy-legged vampire bat (Diphylla ecaudata), and the white-winged vampire bat (Diaemus youngi).

Unlike some other species of bats, vampire bats can walk on the ground using a unique, bounding gait, in which the forelimbs are used instead of the hindlimbs to propel the animal forward, possibly as an adaptation for feeding on quadrupedal hosts on the ground. Vampire bats hunt only when it is fully dark. Like fruit-eating bats, and unlike insectivorous and fish-eating bats, they emit only low-energy sound pulses. The common vampire bat feeds mostly on the blood of mammals (occasionally including humans), whereas both the hairy-legged vampire bat and white-winged vampire bat feed on the blood of birds.

Taxonomy

The three known species of vampire bats all seem more similar to one another than to any other species of leaf-nosed bat. This suggests that hematophagy evolved only once among bats, and the three species share this common ancestor. The subfamily Desmodontinae includes extinct species such as Desmodus draculae, a giant vampire bat that lived during the Pleistocene and Holocene epochs of the Americas.

Anatomy

Vampire bats have short muzzles and lack a nose leaf, instead having naked pads with U-shaped grooves at the tip. Their front teeth are specialized for cutting, and their back teeth are reduced. The common vampire bat has a wingspan of about 35–40 cm (14–16 in) and weighs 25–40 grams (0.9–1.4 oz). A vampire bat can detect the signature of a prey animal's breathing and remember it across nights, allowing it to return to the same host.

Feeding

Vampire bats feed primarily on the blood of their prey. After locating a host, a vampire bat lands and approaches on the ground. It uses heat sensors in its nose to find a suitable spot where the blood flows close to the skin. It then makes a small incision with its sharp incisor teeth and laps up the blood. The bat's saliva contains anticoagulants, such as draculin, that prevent the blood from clotting. A typical feeding session lasts 20 to 30 minutes, during which the bat may consume up to 20 ml of blood.

Social behavior

Vampire bats form strong social bonds. They live in colonies of up to a few hundred individuals, though colonies of several thousand have been reported. Females form stable associations and groom one another; bats that fail to find a meal may receive regurgitated blood from a successful roostmate, a behavior studied as an example of reciprocal altruism in nature.

Relation to humans

Vampire bats transmit diseases to humans and livestock. In Latin America, the common vampire bat is a major vector of rabies to cattle, causing significant economic losses; estimates suggest tens of thousands of cattle deaths per year in some regions. Human rabies cases caused by vampire bats are rare but documented, particularly in remote areas of the Amazon basin.
