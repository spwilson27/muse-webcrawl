Eunuch

Image: A court eunuch (placeholder)

A eunuch is a man who has been castrated. Throughout history, castration often served a specific social function: eunuchs were employed as guards, servants, courtiers, and administrators, particularly in the imperial courts of China, the Byzantine Empire, the Ottoman Empire, Persia, and various ancient Near Eastern states.

History

Because eunuchs could not father children, rulers considered them free of dynastic ambition and therefore trustworthy in sensitive posts — guarding harems, carrying confidential messages, and managing treasuries. In imperial China, eunuchs such as Zheng He, the admiral who led the Ming treasure voyages, sometimes amassed enormous power. In the Ottoman Empire, the chief black eunuch (Kızlar Ağası) was among the most powerful figures at court.

Religious and cultural roles

Some boys were castrated for musical purposes: the castrati of Italian opera retained high singing voices into adulthood and were celebrated performers in the 17th and 18th centuries; Alessandro Moreschi, the last castrato of the Sistine Chapel, was recorded in the early 20th century. Eunuchs are mentioned frequently in the Bible and in ancient literature. In India, hijras — a community that historically included some castrated men — hold a recognised social and religious role.

Effects

Castration before puberty prevents the deepening of the voice and normal masculine development of muscle and bone; performed after puberty the effects are less pronounced. The practice of creating eunuchs died out in the 20th century; the last imperial Chinese eunuch, Sun Yaoting, died in 1996.
