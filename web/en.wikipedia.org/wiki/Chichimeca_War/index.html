Chichimeca War

The Chichimeca War (1550–1590) was a military conflict between the Spanish colonizers and their Native allies against the Chichimeca peoples, a confederation of nomadic and semi-nomadic groups inhabiting the region of northern New Spain known as La Gran Chichimeca, in present-day northern Mexico and the Bajío.

Background

The discovery of rich silver deposits around Zacatecas in the 1540s drew Spanish settlers and miners northward along routes that the Chichimeca — including the Zacatecos, Guachichiles, Pames, and Guamares — attacked repeatedly. Raids on silver caravans and settlements provoked a prolonged war of attrition, notable for being the longest and most expensive conflict of the Spanish colonial era in the region.

Conduct and resolution

Initial Spanish policy of enslavement and violent retaliation intensified resistance. By the 1580s, under Viceroy Álvaro Manrique de Zúñiga and his successor Luis de Velasco II, Spain adopted a "peace by purchase" strategy: providing food, clothing, tools, and land to Chichimeca groups in exchange for settling in mission and frontier towns, assisted by Christianized Tlaxcalan and other allied settlers. The conflict wound down by about 1590.
