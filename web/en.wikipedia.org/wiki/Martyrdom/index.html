Martyrdom

Martyrdom is the suffering of death on account of adherence to a religious faith. A martyr (Greek: μάρτυς, mártys, "witness") is a person who is killed because of their testimony of faith, or who refuses to renounce that faith under threat of death. The term has been extended beyond religion to those who die for a political or ideological cause.

The martyrdom of Saint Sebastian, a frequently depicted subject in Christian art.

Etymology

The word derives from the Greek martys, meaning "witness". In early Christianity the term came to denote those whose witness to Christ was sealed by death, beginning with Saint Stephen, traditionally regarded as the first Christian martyr (the "protomartyr"), stoned in Jerusalem as recorded in the Acts of the Apostles.

In Christianity

Persecution under the Roman Empire produced numerous martyrs, venerated as saints; their feast days mark the dates of their deaths, understood as their "birthday" into heaven. Theologians distinguished "red martyrdom" (death), "white martyrdom" (a life of ascetic renunciation) and "green martyrdom" (penitential exile). In Catholic theology, martyrdom is held to remit all sin as a "baptism of blood" for the unbaptised. Martyrs from the Reformation era on both sides were commemorated in works such as Foxe's Book of Martyrs (1563).

In other religions

In Judaism, martyrdom is expressed as Kiddush Hashem — the sanctification of God's name — exemplified by the Maccabean martyrs and by Rabbi Akiva. In Islam, the shahid is one who dies in the path of God; see Martyrdom in Islam. In Sikhism, the martyrdoms (shahidi) of Guru Arjan (1606) and Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) are central events. The Baháʼí Faith commemorates the execution of the Báb in 1850 and thousands of persecuted followers. Hindu and Buddhist traditions also honour those who died defending the dharma.

Secular usage

The concept extends to political martyrs whose deaths galvanised movements. Scholars have debated the psychology and social function of voluntary martyrdom, including modern phenomena such as suicide attacks, which most religious authorities distinguish from classical martyrdom because they involve killing others.
