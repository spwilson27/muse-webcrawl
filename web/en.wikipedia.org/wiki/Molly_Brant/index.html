Molly Brant

Mary Brant, also known as Molly Brant (c. 1736 – 16 April 1796), was a Mohawk leader influential in New York and Canada in the era of the American Revolution. She lived most of her life in the Mohawk Valley of New York, where she met and became the common-law wife of Sir William Johnson, British Superintendent of Indian Affairs. Her brother, Joseph Brant, became an important Mohawk military leader.

Early life

Mary Brant was born about 1736, possibly in the Mohawk village of Canajoharie or in the Ohio Country. She was the daughter of Peter, a Mohawk sachem, and Margaret. Her family moved to Canajoharie, in the Mohawk Valley, where they lived in a substantial house and were prominent in the community. Baptised in the Church of England, she was given the Christian name Mary, but was commonly known as Molly.

Consort of Sir William Johnson

By 1759 she had become the consort of Sir William Johnson, the influential British Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the northern colonies. Johnson had come to the Mohawk Valley in the 1730s as a trader and had established a close alliance with the Mohawk. Brant lived with Johnson at Johnson Hall in Johnstown, New York, where she managed the household and entertained guests, acting as hostess for the many Native and colonial leaders who visited. The couple had nine children together. Brant was a prominent figure in her own right, exerting considerable influence among the Mohawk and within the Iroquois Confederacy, particularly among the matrons who held power in the matrilineal society.

American Revolution

After Johnson's death in 1774, Brant returned to Canajoharie. During the Revolutionary War she was a committed Loyalist, providing food, shelter, and intelligence to British forces. In 1777 she warned the British of American plans before the Siege of Fort Stanwix, information that helped the British and their Native allies ambush American reinforcements at the Battle of Oriskany. After her home was looted and she was threatened, she fled with her family to Canada, settling at Carleton Island and later Kingston, where the British provided her a house and pension.

Later life and legacy

Brant spent her remaining years in Kingston, Upper Canada, remaining an influential figure among Loyalist Mohawk who resettled there and at the Bay of Quinte. She died in Kingston on 16 April 1796. The Government of Canada recognised her as a Person of National Historic Significance in 1994.
