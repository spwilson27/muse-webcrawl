Heteronormativity


Heteronormativity diagram

Heteronormativity is the concept that heterosexuality is the preferred or normal mode of sexual orientation. It assumes the sexual binary of male/female and that sexual and marital relations are most fitting between people of opposite sexes. A heteronormative view therefore involves alignment of biological sex, sexuality, gender identity, and gender roles.

The term was popularized by queer theorist Michael Warner in 1991, in one of the earliest major works of queer theory. The concept builds on earlier ideas, including Adrienne Rich's notion of "compulsory heterosexuality" and Gayle Rubin's analysis of the "sex/gender system," as well as Michel Foucault's writings on the history of sexuality.

Origins and terminology

Michael Warner coined the term in his introduction to Fear of a Queer Planet (1991). Cathy J. Cohen has argued that heteronormativity can also be used to analyze the intersections of race, class, gender, and sexuality, noting that not all heterosexual people benefit equally from heteronormative structures. Lauren Berlant and Warner further developed the concept in their essay "Sex in Public" (1998).

Criticism and analysis

Critics of heteronormativity argue that it stigmatizes marginalized and stigmatized sexual practices and identities, placing heterosexual relationships at the top of a hierarchy of acceptable relationships. It is closely related to the concept of compulsory heterosexuality, and is contrasted with homonormativity, which describes the assimilation of heteronormative ideals into LGBTQ culture.

Scholars have applied the concept to areas including education, law, media representation, and family structures, examining how institutions presume heterosexuality as the default and render other orientations invisible or deviant.
