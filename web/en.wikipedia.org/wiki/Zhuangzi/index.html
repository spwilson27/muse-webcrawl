Zhuangzi (c. 369 – c. 286 BC), also known as Zhuang Zhou, was an influential Chinese philosopher of the Warring States period and, with Laozi, a founder of Taoism. The book named after him — part philosophy, part fantastical anecdote — is among the most playful and profound in world literature: Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, and on waking could not tell whether he was a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it is a man.

Thought

Zhuangzi relativises human distinctions — life and death, big and small, this and that — urging free wandering (xiaoyao you) in accord with the transformations of the Tao. He satirised Confucian moralising and the ambition of rulers, refused office ('I would rather drag my tail in the mud'), and praised uselessness: the gnarled tree survives because no one can use it. His sceptical, perspectival writing influenced Chinese Buddhism and all later East Asian aesthetics.
