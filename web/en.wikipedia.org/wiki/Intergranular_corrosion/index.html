Intergranular corrosion

Intergranular corrosion (IGC), also known as intergranular attack (IGA), is a form of corrosion where the boundaries of crystallites of the material are more susceptible to corrosion than their insides. This situation can occur in otherwise corrosion-resistant alloys, when a deleterious agent is present that makes the grain boundaries anodic to the material in the interior of the grains.

Causes

In austenitic stainless steels, intergranular corrosion is classically caused by "sensitization": holding the alloy in the temperature range of roughly 450–850 °C allows chromium-rich carbides (such as Cr23C6) to precipitate at grain boundaries, depleting the adjacent metal of chromium and leaving it unable to form a protective passive film. Welding heat-affected zones are particularly vulnerable, a phenomenon historically called "weld decay".

Examples

Aluminium-based alloys may be sensitized by segregation of copper- or magnesium-rich phases at grain boundaries. In nickel alloys, segregation of sulfur or other impurities can play a similar role. Intergranular attack can lead to exfoliation corrosion, in which corrosion products force apart layers of the metal.

Prevention and testing

Sensitization is avoided by using low-carbon grades (such as 304L and 316L), stabilized grades containing titanium or niobium (321, 347), or by solution annealing and rapid quenching after welding. Susceptibility is assessed by tests such as ASTM A262 practices, including the Strauss and Huey tests.
