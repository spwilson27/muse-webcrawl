Calanus is a genus of marine copepods in the family Calanidae, found throughout the world's oceans and especially abundant in northern seas. Its species are among the most important zooplankton on Earth, converting phytoplankton production into lipid-rich food for fish, seabirds and whales, and they are sometimes described as the most numerous multicellular animals on the planet by biomass.

Biology

Calanus species are relatively large copepods, 2–6 mm long (the Arctic C. hyperboreus reaches about 10 mm). They feed by filtering phytoplankton. Their defining adaptation is the accumulation of enormous reserves of wax esters in an oil sac — lipids may make up more than half of dry body weight — which fuels a remarkable life cycle: after spring-bloom feeding near the surface, late-stage juveniles descend hundreds of metres (in the Arctic, over 2,000 m) to overwinter in a dormant state called diapause, ascending in spring to mature and reproduce. The reddish colour imparted by astaxanthin gives surface swarms a visible tint, historically noted by whalers as "red feed."

Ecological and economic significance

Calanus finmarchicus dominates zooplankton biomass in the North Atlantic and is the key food of larval cod, herring and other commercial fish, of North Atlantic right whales, and of seabirds; its abundance is tied to the success of entire fisheries and is shifting poleward with ocean warming. Because of its omega-3-rich oil, C. finmarchicus is harvested at modest scale in Norway for nutritional oil, and it is a model organism for studying lipid metabolism and the biological carbon pump — its sinking carcasses and faecal pellets transport carbon to the deep ocean.
