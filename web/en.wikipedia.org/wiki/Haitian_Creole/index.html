Haitian Creole (Haitian Creole: Kreyòl ayisyen, pronounced [kɣejɔl ajisjɛ̃]; French: créole haïtien), commonly referred to as simply Creole, is a French-based creole language spoken by 10–12 million people worldwide, principally in Haiti, where it is the first language of the great majority of the population. It is the most widely spoken creole language in the world.

History

Haitian Creole emerged in the French colony of Saint-Domingue during the 17th and 18th centuries, in the contact between French colonists and enslaved West Africans working on sugar plantations. Its vocabulary is overwhelmingly derived from 18th-century French, but its grammar was shaped by West African languages, particularly the Gbe languages such as Fon and Ewe, as well as by other influences including Spanish, English, Portuguese, Taíno and Arabic loanwords.

For most of Haiti's history, French was the language of government, law and education, while Creole was spoken by everyone but long stigmatised as a "broken" form of French. A standardised phonetic orthography for Creole was developed in the mid-20th century and officially adopted in 1979. The 1987 Constitution of Haiti recognised Haitian Creole and French as the country's two official languages, and Creole has since expanded into education, media, literature and public life.

Grammar

Unlike French, Haitian Creole does not conjugate verbs for person or tense. Instead, tense, aspect and mood are marked by preverbal particles: te (past/anterior), ap (progressive or prospective), a/va (future/irrealis) and fèk (recent past), which can combine, as in te ap ("was doing"). Nouns do not have grammatical gender, and the plural is marked by the postposed particle yo, which also serves as the third-person plural pronoun and a definite plural article. The definite article follows the noun and varies in form (la, a, an, lan, nan) according to the final sound of the word it follows.

Pronouns include mwen (I), ou (you), li (he/she/it), nou (we/you plural) and yo (they). Word order is basically subject–verb–object. The copula is generally absent in the present tense: Li doktè — "He/she is a doctor."

Vocabulary

Roughly 90 percent of the vocabulary comes from French, but many words have shifted in meaning. For example, kreyòl itself derives from French créole. African-derived words survive especially in the domains of Vodou religion, food, music and everyday life. Scientific and technical vocabulary continues to be drawn largely from French.

Status and use

All Haitians speak Creole, while only a minority — often estimated at under ten percent — are fully fluent in French. This diglossic situation has long had consequences for education and access to justice and public services, and reformers have campaigned for wider use of Creole in schools. Notable Creole literature includes the works of Frankétienne, who wrote the first major novel entirely in Haitian Creole, Dézafi (1975). Large diaspora communities in the United States, Canada, France, the Dominican Republic and elsewhere sustain Creole-language media, and the language has a growing presence online.
