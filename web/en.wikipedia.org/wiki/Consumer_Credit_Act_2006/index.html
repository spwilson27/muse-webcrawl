Consumer Credit Act 2006

The Consumer Credit Act 2006 (c 14) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom intended to increase consumer protection when borrowing money. It amended the Consumer Credit Act 1974, the statute that had governed most consumer lending and the licensing of credit businesses since the 1970s. The Act received royal assent on 30 March 2006 and its provisions were brought into force in stages during 2007 and 2008.

Main provisions

Unfair relationships

The Act replaced the narrow "extortionate credit bargain" test with a broader unfair relationships test (sections 140A–140B of the 1974 Act, inserted by the 2006 Act). A court may reopen a credit agreement where the relationship between creditor and debtor is unfair to the debtor because of the terms of the agreement, the way the creditor has exercised or enforced its rights, or any other thing done (or not done) by the creditor before or after the agreement was made. If the debtor alleges unfairness, the burden of proof lies on the creditor to show otherwise. The test was considered by the Supreme Court in Plevin v Paragon Personal Finance Ltd [2014], concerning undisclosed commission on payment protection insurance.

Other reforms

The Act extended the coverage of the 1974 Act to most previously exempt business lending up to £25,000, empowered consumers to challenge unfair credit agreements, strengthened the licensing regime administered by the Office of Fair Trading (requiring fitness and competence and allowing fines), gave debtors rights to regular statements and notices of sums in arrears and default sums, and brought consumer credit disputes within the remit of the Financial Ombudsman Service as an alternative to court action. It also removed the financial limit for many regulated agreements, so that high-value agreements (except those secured on land or for business purposes) became regulated regardless of amount.

Later developments

From 1 April 2014, regulation of consumer credit passed from the Office of Fair Trading to the Financial Conduct Authority under the Financial Services Act 2012, and much of the detailed rule-making moved into the FCA Handbook, though the 1974 Act as amended by the 2006 Act remains the statutory foundation of the regime.
