Sturmabteilung

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sturmabteilung (SA; literally "Storm Division" or "Storm Battalion"), commonly known as the Brownshirts for their brown uniforms, was the original paramilitary wing of the Nazi Party in Germany. It played a significant role in Adolf Hitler's rise to power during the 1920s and early 1930s, providing protection for Nazi rallies, disrupting opponents' meetings, and intimidating political adversaries, trade unionists, and Jews.

History

The SA emerged in 1921 from groups of party strong-arm men used to guard Nazi meetings in Munich beer halls. After the failed Beer Hall Putsch of 1923, the SA was briefly banned but soon re-formed. Under Ernst Röhm, who became its chief of staff in 1931, the SA expanded enormously, reaching some three million members by 1934 — far outnumbering the regular army, which was restricted to 100,000 men by the Treaty of Versailles.

Röhm envisioned the SA as the core of a future revolutionary "people's army" and pressed for a "second revolution" against conservative elites, alarming both the army leadership and Hitler, who sought the support of Germany's industrialists and generals. On 30 June 1934, in the purge known as the Night of the Long Knives, Röhm and other SA leaders were arrested and murdered by the SS and Gestapo. Thereafter the SA declined sharply in power and prestige, eclipsed by the SS, though it continued to exist until 1945 and participated in violence including the Kristallnacht pogrom of November 1938.

Organisation and uniforms

The SA's brown uniform derived in part from surplus tropical uniforms originally intended for German colonial troops. Its ranks and insignia were later adapted by other Nazi organisations. Membership provided many unemployed men with food, lodging, and a sense of purpose during the Great Depression, contributing to the SA's rapid growth.
