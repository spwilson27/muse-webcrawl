Reed's law

Reed's law is the assertion by David P. Reed that the utility of large networks, particularly social networks, can scale exponentially with the size of the network. The reason is that the number of possible sub-groups of network participants grows as 2N, where N is the number of participants — far faster than the network's size itself.

Context and comparison

Reed's law completes a commonly cited trio of network scaling laws:

  • Sarnoff's law: the value of a broadcast network grows linearly with its audience, N.
  • Metcalfe's law: the value of a communications network grows with the number of possible pairwise connections, roughly N2.
  • Reed's law: the value of a group-forming network grows with the number of possible groups, 2N.

Reed developed the idea in the 1990s while analysing group-forming networks (GFNs) such as online communities, arguing — initially in his essay "The Law of the Pack" in the Harvard Business Review (2001, with his fuller paper "That Sneaky Exponential") — that networks that let users form their own groups (chat groups, marketplaces, collaborative communities) create value that dwarfs one-to-one or broadcast networks once the community exceeds a certain scale.

Criticism

Critics including Bob Briscoe, Andrew Odlyzko and Benjamin Tilly have argued that Reed's and Metcalfe's laws overstate value: not all possible connections or subgroups are equally valuable, and the number of actively used groups in real networks grows far more slowly. Reed himself acknowledged the law describes an upper-bound potential rather than realised value.
