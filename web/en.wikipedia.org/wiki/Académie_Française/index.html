Académie Française

Formation22 February 1635
FounderCardinal Richelieu
TypeLearned society; language regulator
HeadquartersInstitut de France, Paris
Membership40 "Immortels"

The Académie Française is France's principal learned society for matters of the French language. Founded in 1635 by Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister to King Louis XIII, it is the oldest of the five academies of the Institut de France. Its forty members, known as les immortels ("the Immortals"), are elected for life and wear the famous green habit (habit vert) with a ceremonial sword.

Role

The Academy's official task is to give "definite rules to the language" and maintain its purity and eloquence. Its principal instrument is the Dictionnaire de l'Académie française, first published in 1694; the ninth edition has been in preparation since the 1980s. The Academy issues rulings on grammar, spelling and neologisms — famously resisting Anglicisms and proposing French equivalents — although its recommendations are advisory rather than legally binding for the general public.

History

Abolished during the French Revolution in 1793, the Academy was restored within the Institut de France in 1803. Over the centuries its seats have been held by figures such as Racine, Voltaire, Victor Hugo and Alexandre Dumas. Valéry Giscard d'Estaing and Hélène Carrère d'Encausse were among its recent members; Carrère d'Encausse served as perpetual secretary from 1999 until her death in 2023, the first woman to hold that post.
