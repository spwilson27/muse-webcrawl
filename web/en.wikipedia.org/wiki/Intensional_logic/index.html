Intensional logic

Intensional logic is a branch of formal logic that studies reasoning in contexts where the principle of substitutivity of identicals fails — that is, where replacing an expression with a co-referring one can change truth value. Such contexts include modal statements ("necessarily"), belief reports ("Alice believes that…"), temporal statements and quotation. The name reflects the distinction, going back to Gottlob Frege's sense/reference distinction and Rudolf Carnap's terminology, between an expression's extension (what it picks out) and its intension (the way it picks it out, modelled as a function from possible worlds — or, more generally, indices — to extensions).

Development

Carnap's Meaning and Necessity (1947) gave an early formal treatment using state descriptions. Saul Kripke's possible-worlds semantics for modal logic in the late 1950s and early 1960s supplied the model theory that Richard Montague then adapted in the 1960s–70s into a systematic intensional logic for the semantics of natural language ("English as a Formal Language"; "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English"). Alonzo Church's logic of sense and denotation offered an alternative, more finely individuated treatment.

Framework

In a typical intensional type logic, expressions are evaluated relative to an index, usually a possible world and time. The intension of a sentence is a proposition — a set of worlds (or a function from worlds to truth values); the intension of a predicate is a function from worlds to sets of individuals. Operators such as modal and tense operators shift the index of evaluation, which explains why co-extensional expressions are not interchangeable within their scope: "the morning star" and "the evening star" have the same extension at the actual world but different intensions.

Applications

Intensional logic underlies Montague grammar in formal semantics, provides tools for analysing propositional attitudes, counterfactuals and conditionals, and is used in computer science and artificial intelligence for knowledge representation and reasoning about time, knowledge and belief (epistemic and temporal logics).
