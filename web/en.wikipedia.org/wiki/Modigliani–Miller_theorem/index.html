Modigliani–Miller theorem

FieldFinancial economics
AuthorsFranco Modigliani, Merton Miller
First published1958 ("The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance and the Theory of Investment")
SubjectCapital structure irrelevance

The Modigliani–Miller theorem (often called MM) is a foundational result in modern corporate finance, developed by Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller in papers beginning in 1958. In its best-known form it states that, under a set of idealised conditions — no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no transaction costs, symmetric information and efficient markets — the market value of a firm is independent of its capital structure: how the firm finances itself, through debt or equity, does not affect its total value.

Propositions

Proposition I (without taxes): the value of a levered firm equals the value of an otherwise identical unlevered firm, VL = VU. The proof rests on arbitrage: investors can replicate ("home-made") leverage on personal account, so they will not pay a premium for leverage supplied by the firm.

Proposition II (without taxes): the expected return on a firm's equity rises linearly with the debt–equity ratio, exactly offsetting the benefit of cheaper debt, so the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) stays constant. The extra return compensates shareholders for the additional financial risk.

With corporate taxes (1963 correction): because interest is tax-deductible, value increases with debt: VL = VU + TC·D, where TC is the corporate tax rate and D the value of debt; and the WACC declines as leverage rises.

Assumptions and limitations

The theorem's assumptions are deliberately unrealistic; its importance lies in showing why capital structure might matter in the real world — only through violations such as taxes, costs of financial distress, agency costs and information asymmetry. Later theories, including the trade-off theory and pecking-order theory, build directly on this analysis. Modigliani (1985) and Miller (1990) each received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, in part for this work.

Related results

The authors also proved a dividend irrelevance proposition (1961): under the same ideal conditions, a firm's dividend policy does not affect its value. Extensions include the treatment of risky debt, growth, and personal taxes (Miller, 1977), where the tax advantage of debt is partly offset by the higher personal tax rate on interest income.
