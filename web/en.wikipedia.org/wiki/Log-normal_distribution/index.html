Log-normal distribution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In probability theory, a log-normal (or lognormal) distribution is a continuous probability distribution of a random variable whose logarithm is normally distributed. Thus if the random variable X is log-normally distributed, then Y = ln(X) has a normal distribution. A variable can be modelled as log-normal if it arises as the product of many small independent random factors—a multiplicative form of the central limit theorem—which is why the distribution appears so often in the natural and social sciences.

Definitions

A positive random variable X is log-normal with parameters μ (the mean of ln X) and σ > 0 (its standard deviation) if its probability density function is

f(x) = 1 / (x σ √(2π)) · exp( − (ln x − μ)² / (2σ²) ), for x > 0.

The distribution has median eμ, mode eμ−σ², and mean eμ+σ²/2; because the mean exceeds the median, the distribution is right-skewed with a long upper tail, and the skew grows with σ. The log-normal is closely related to the work of Francis Galton and is sometimes called the Galton distribution; Donald McAlister gave the first full mathematical treatment in 1879.

Applications

Log-normal distributions describe many quantities that are positive and multiplicatively generated: the distribution of income and of city sizes, the size of particles produced by crushing and grinding, the latency and incubation periods of infectious diseases, file sizes and comment lengths on the internet, the abundance of species in ecology, and the concentration of elements in the Earth's crust. In finance, the Black–Scholes model assumes that asset prices follow geometric Brownian motion, which makes prices log-normally distributed over any fixed horizon.
