Somatic symptom disorder

Somatic symptom disorder (SSD) is a mental disorder characterised by one or more distressing or disruptive somatic (physical) symptoms accompanied by excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviours related to the symptoms — such as disproportionate and persistent concern about their seriousness, persistently high health anxiety, or excessive time and energy devoted to them. The diagnosis, introduced in the DSM-5 in 2013, does not require that the symptoms be medically unexplained; instead it emphasises the maladaptive psychological response to them. Symptoms must typically persist for more than six months.

History

SSD replaced and consolidated several earlier DSM-IV categories, including somatization disorder, undifferentiated somatoform disorder, and in many cases hypochondriasis (most of whose former diagnoses are now classified either as SSD or as illness anxiety disorder). The older concepts of hysteria and Briquet's syndrome are historical antecedents. The change was controversial: critics worried that people with genuine but poorly understood diseases might be inappropriately labelled, while supporters argued the new criteria end the unreliable requirement of proving symptoms "medically unexplained".

Symptoms and diagnosis

Common symptoms include pain at various sites, fatigue, gastrointestinal complaints, and cardiopulmonary sensations. Severity is graded by how many of the characteristic cognitive and behavioural features are present. The condition frequently co-occurs with anxiety and depressive disorders, and overlaps with functional disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome and fibromyalgia.

Treatment

Management centres on a stable, collaborative relationship with a single primary-care clinician, regular scheduled visits, avoidance of unnecessary investigations, and treatment of comorbid conditions. Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence among psychological treatments; certain antidepressants may help both mood symptoms and pain.
