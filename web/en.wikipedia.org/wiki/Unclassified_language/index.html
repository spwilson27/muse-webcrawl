Unclassified language

An unclassified language is a language whose genetic affiliation to other languages has not been established. Such languages may lack sufficient documentation for comparative analysis, may be extinct and known only from small wordlists, or may have been studied without any convincing relationship emerging. Unclassified languages are distinguished from language isolates, which have been studied thoroughly and demonstrably have no known relatives.

Causes

Languages remain unclassified for several reasons. Some are attested only by a few dozen words recorded by travellers, insufficient for classification. Others, such as the little-known languages of the Amazon or New Guinea, await adequate field documentation. In still other cases a language may be so heavily influenced by contact, or so aberrant in structure, that its ancestry is obscured. Unclassified extinct languages of antiquity, typically known from inscriptions or names, are sometimes grouped geographically (for example the "Paleosiberian" languages) without implying genetic unity.

Examples

Frequently cited unclassified languages include Meroitic, the language of ancient Kush, written in its own script but still not securely related to any known family; the Iberian language of pre-Roman Spain; various extinct languages of South America known only from colonial lists; and a number of poorly documented languages of New Guinea and the Solomon Islands. Catalogues such as Glottolog list dozens of unclassified languages in every macro-area. Some languages once written off as unclassifiable have later been assigned to families as documentation improved, while others have been re-examined and deemed isolates.

Classification practice

In reference catalogues, "unclassified" is a working label rather than a claim about a language's true history: every natural language presumably descends from older languages, but the evidence may be irrecoverable. Families proposed for unclassified languages — often on slender evidence — are treated with caution by most linguists until supported by regular sound correspondences and shared morphology.
