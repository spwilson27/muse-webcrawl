Haploid

Symboln
ContrastDiploid (2n), polyploid
Produced byMeiosis

Haploid describes a cell or organism that has a single set of chromosomes. In sexually reproducing organisms, the gametes — sperm and egg cells — are haploid, carrying one copy of each chromosome (denoted n); when two gametes fuse at fertilisation, the resulting zygote is diploid (2n), with two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. The halving of chromosome number that produces haploid cells occurs in the specialised cell division called meiosis.

Life cycles

Animals are predominantly diploid, with haploidy restricted to the gametes. Plants and many algae alternate between haploid and diploid generations: in mosses the conspicuous plant is haploid (the gametophyte), while in flowering plants the haploid generation is reduced to the pollen grain and embryo sac. Fungi and many protists spend most of their lives haploid, becoming diploid only briefly before meiosis. In social Hymenoptera (ants, bees, wasps), males develop from unfertilised eggs and are haploid throughout life — a system called haplodiploidy.

Significance

Haploidy exposes every gene to selection, since there is no second copy to mask recessive mutations; this makes haploid organisms such as yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (which can be grown in either state) valuable laboratory models for genetics. Haploid cell lines derived from human tumours or engineered from stem cells are used in genetic screens, including genome-wide CRISPR knockout studies. In plant breeding, doubled haploids — haploid plants whose chromosomes are artificially doubled to yield completely homozygous lines — greatly accelerate the production of new varieties.

Terminology

The term was coined from the Greek haploos, "single". Related terms include monoploid (a single basic chromosome set, which in some organisms differs from the gametic number), euploidy and aneuploidy (gain or loss of individual chromosomes).
