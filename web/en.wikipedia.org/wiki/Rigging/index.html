Rigging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rigging comprises the system of ropes, cables and chains, which support a sailing ship's masts and, in part, control its sails. The term derives from the Old English wrigan or Dutch riggen, "to fit out". More broadly, rigging also denotes the ropes, slings, chains and hoists used in lifting and moving heavy loads in construction, entertainment and industry.

On sailing vessels

Shipboard rigging is divided into two classes:

  • Standing rigging — fixed lines that support the masts, including shrouds, stays and backstays. Once made of natural fibre rope and later of galvanized or stainless steel wire, it is set up taut and adjusted only occasionally.
  • Running rigging — movable lines used to hoist, trim and control the sails, including halyards, sheets, braces and lifts. It must run freely through blocks and is handled constantly while under way.

A vessel's rig — sloop, cutter, ketch, schooner, brig, full-rigged ship and so on — is defined by the arrangement of its masts and sails. The sailor responsible for rigging was traditionally the rigger or the boatswain's crew; on square-rigged ships, crew "worked aloft" in the rigging to loose and furl sails.

Rigging for lifting

In construction and industry, rigging is the work of assembling slings, shackles, spreader bars and hoists to lift loads with cranes or other equipment. Certified riggers calculate load weights and centres of gravity, select rated hardware and observe strict safety rules, since rigging failure is a major cause of lifting accidents.

Theatrical and entertainment rigging

Theatres and concert venues employ riggers to hang scenery, lighting trusses, speakers and — in aerial rigging — performers. Arena riggers work at height from roof steel, installing chain motors and bridles to support suspended production equipment.
