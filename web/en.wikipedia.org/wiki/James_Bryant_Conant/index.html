James Bryant Conant

Born: March 26, 1893, Dorchester, Massachusetts, U.S.

Died: February 11, 1978 (aged 84), Hanover, New Hampshire, U.S.

Offices: President of Harvard University (1933–1953); U.S. High Commissioner for Germany (1953–1955)

James Bryant Conant (March 26, 1893 – February 11, 1978) was an American chemist, university president, and government official. He served as president of Harvard University from 1933 to 1953, helped lead the Manhattan Project's administrative structure, and became the first U.S. Ambassador to West Germany.

Scientific career

A distinguished organic chemist, Conant earned his PhD from Harvard in 1916 and became a leading authority on the chemistry of hemoglobin and chlorophyll. During World War I he worked on chemical weapons research, and in World War II he chaired the National Defense Research Committee, overseeing American scientific research for the war effort and playing a key role in the administration of the Manhattan Project.

Harvard presidency

As Harvard's president, Conant reshaped the university's admissions policy, emphasizing merit over social background, expanded its graduate and research programs, and strengthened the sciences. He championed the use of standardized testing in college admissions and helped found the Educational Testing Service.

Later career

In 1953 Conant became the U.S. High Commissioner for Germany, later the first ambassador to the Federal Republic of Germany. After retiring, he wrote influential studies of American secondary education, including The American High School Today (1959).
