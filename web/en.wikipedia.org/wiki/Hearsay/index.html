Hearsay

Hearsay is testimony from a witness under oath who is reciting an out-of-court statement, the content of which is being offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. In most courts, hearsay evidence is inadmissible (the "hearsay rule") unless an exception to the hearsay rule applies.

[Image: Counsel rising to object on hearsay grounds during a trial]

Rationale

The rule against hearsay rests on the unreliability of second-hand statements: the original declarant was not under oath, their demeanour could not be observed by the fact-finder, and they cannot be cross-examined on the statement. Historically, the rule developed in English common law alongside jury trial.

Exceptions

Numerous exceptions permit hearsay where circumstances suggest reliability or necessity. Under the United States Federal Rules of Evidence these include present sense impressions, excited utterances, statements of then-existing state of mind, statements made for medical diagnosis or treatment, recorded recollections, business records, public records, and dying declarations. Statements by an opposing party are treated as not hearsay. The Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment separately limits testimonial hearsay against criminal defendants, as interpreted in Crawford v. Washington (2004).

Civil proceedings

In England and Wales, hearsay evidence is generally admissible in civil proceedings under the Civil Evidence Act 1995, subject to notice requirements, with the court weighing its reliability when assessing the evidence.
