Forfaiting

In trade finance, forfaiting is a service providing medium-term financial support for export/import of capital goods. The forfaiter (the party providing forfaiting) provides medium-term credit, typically from 180 days up to seven years, to an importer while purchasing, without recourse, a negotiable instrument issued by the importer and guaranteed by the importer's bank. The forfaiter thus assumes the political, transfer and commercial risk of the transaction while the exporter receives payment in full at the time of delivery.

The word forfaiting derives from the French à forfait, meaning "at a flat rate" or "without recourse" — the forfaiter "forfeits" the right of recourse against the exporter if the importer defaults.

Characteristics

  • Without recourse: the exporter is released from all risks and responsibilities of the export receivable once the documents have been accepted.
  • Fixed rate: financing is usually provided at a fixed rate of interest for the whole credit period, insulating the parties from interest-rate movements.
  • Guarantee: the payment obligation is typically guaranteed (avalised) by a bank in the importer's country.
  • Tradability: the note or bill can be traded on the secondary market, where many forfaiters sell on their holdings.

History and market

Forfaiting developed in Switzerland during the 1960s to finance East–West trade, particularly grain imports by the Soviet bloc, and was centred in Zurich. It grew substantially with trade in capital goods to emerging markets. The International Trade and Forfaiting Association (ITFA), founded in 1999, represents the industry and publishes standard documentation and market practice.

Comparison with factoring

Forfaiting is often compared with factoring, but differs in that it usually deals with single, larger transactions of medium-term maturity, commonly 100% of the invoice value and always without recourse, whereas factoring typically involves the bulk purchase of shorter-term receivables, sometimes with recourse.
