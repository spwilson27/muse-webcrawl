Golden age of Hollywood

The Golden Age of Hollywood, also called Classical Hollywood cinema, is the period from the advent of sound films in the late 1920s (or, in stricter accounts, the early 1930s) through the 1960s, during which the American film industry dominated world cinema through a factory-like studio system. The major studios controlled production, distribution, and exhibition, kept stars and craftspeople under long-term contract, and turned out hundreds of genre films a year — Westerns, musicals, gangster films, screwball comedies, and films noir — to a weekly audience that peaked at some 90 million Americans in 1946.

The studio system

The "Big Five" studios — MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox, and RKO — owned theatre chains and practised block booking, while the "Little Three" (Universal, Columbia, United Artists) distributed films without theatres. Studios cultivated distinctive house styles and manufactured star personas through publicity departments. Content was policed by the Motion Picture Production Code (the Hays Code), enforced from 1934, which shaped everything from the restrained plotting of The Seven Year Itch to the disguised violence of film noir.

Stars and styles

The era produced the defining stars of American film — Humphrey Bogart, Katharine Hepburn, James Stewart, Judy Garland, and, in its final phase, Marilyn Monroe, whose vehicles from Niagara and Gentlemen Prefer Blondes to The Misfits spanned the period's genres. Technicolor musicals, widescreen epics of the 1950s, and the method-acting revolution associated with the Actors Studio and Lee Strasberg all emerged within the system even as it weakened.

Decline

The system's decline came from converging blows: the 1948 Supreme Court decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures forced studios to sell their theatres; television conquered American living rooms in the 1950s; stars won freelance freedom; and audiences fragmented. By the 1960s the old studio order had given way to independent production and, eventually, the New Hollywood of the late 1960s and 1970s. The period's films and stars nonetheless remain central cultural icons, endlessly revived and referenced.
