Mass surveillance

Mass surveillance is the intricate surveillance of an entire population or a substantial fraction of it in order to monitor that group of citizens. It is usually carried out by governments or governmental organisations, but may also be conducted by corporations, either on behalf of governments or on their own initiative. Unlike targeted surveillance, mass surveillance is typically indiscriminate, collecting data on people regardless of individual suspicion.

Methods

Modern mass surveillance draws on communications interception (telephone and internet traffic, including metadata), closed-circuit television networks with facial recognition, automatic number-plate recognition, financial transaction records, aerial surveillance, and the bulk analysis of data held by technology companies. The advent of digital communications allowed collection at a scale impossible in earlier eras.

History

Twentieth-century states such as the Soviet Union and East Germany maintained extensive informant and file systems; East Germany's Stasi kept files on millions of citizens. In the digital era, the 2013 disclosures by Edward Snowden revealed programmes such as the NSA's PRISM and upstream collection, and GCHQ's Tempora, prompting a global debate about privacy, security, and oversight. Several countries operate extensive camera networks and national data-retention regimes, while China's surveillance apparatus and social-credit experiments are frequently cited examples of state-led mass monitoring.

Debate

Supporters argue that bulk collection is necessary for counterterrorism and crime prevention. Critics argue that it chills free expression, is prone to abuse and mission creep, and that its security benefits are unproven relative to targeted methods. Courts in several jurisdictions have ruled aspects of bulk retention unlawful or disproportionate.
