Taihō Code

The Taihō Code (大宝律令, Taihō ritsuryō) was a legal code promulgated in Japan in 701–702, during the Taihō era of the Asuka period. It was one of the foundational codes of the ritsuryō system, the imperial administrative and penal legal framework modelled on the codes of Tang dynasty China. The code organised the central government and the provinces, and confirmed earlier reforms, including elements of the Taika Reforms.

Contents

Like other ritsuryō codes, the Taihō Code had two parts: the ritsu, penal law, and the ryō, administrative law. It defined the structure of the imperial bureaucracy under the Council of State (Daijō-kan) and the ministries, established the provincial and district administration, regulated the census and the allotment of rice land (the handen system), taxation in rice, cloth and labour, and the military conscription system. It also formalised court ranks and offices and set out the legal status of classes of the population.

History and survival

The code was compiled by a commission of officials and scholars under Prince Osakabe and Fujiwara no Fuhito, drawing on earlier Japanese law, notably the Ōmi Code and the Asuka Kiyomihara Code, and on Tang models. It was slightly revised and reissued as the Yōrō Code in 718 (promulgated in 757). The text of the Taihō Code itself is lost, but much of its content can be reconstructed from the Yōrō Code, which differed only slightly, and from later commentaries such as the Ryō no Gige and Ryō no Shūge, which quote both codes extensively.

Significance

The Taihō Code marks the consolidation of Japan as a centralised bureaucratic state under the emperor, and its institutions — provinces, ministries, census registration and land allotment — shaped Japanese government for centuries, even as real power later passed to regents, retired emperors and eventually military governments.
