Faust

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Faust in his Gothic study conjuring Mephistopheles, in a nineteenth-century lithograph after the drama's opening scenes]

Faust is a tragic play in two parts by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, the supreme work of German literature. Part One, published in 1808, dramatises the scholar Faust's pact with the devil Mephistopheles and the destruction of the girl Gretchen; Part Two, completed in 1831 and published after Goethe's death in 1832, expands into a vast allegorical pageant of court, classical myth, land reclamation and redemption. Goethe worked on the material for some sixty years, from the Urfaust of the 1770s to the sealed manuscript of his last summer.

The legend — from the historical Dr Faustus through the 1587 chapbook and Marlowe's Doctor Faustus — becomes in Goethe a drama of striving: Faust wagers not his soul for pleasure but for a moment he would wish to linger, and his restless striving, even in error and guilt, is finally what redeems him. Gretchen's tragedy in Part One — seduced, disgraced, her child drowned, awaiting execution and refusing rescue as madness takes her — remains the emotional core most performed on stage.

Part One in outline

In Heaven, Mephistopheles wagers with the Lord for Faust's soul. Faust, despairing of knowledge, summons the Earth Spirit, is mocked, and signs the pact in blood: the devil will serve him, and if Faust ever bids the moment stay — 'Verweile doch! Du bist so schön!' ('Stay, thou art so fair', as the line is usually rendered) — he is the devil's. Rejuvenated, Faust seduces Gretchen with the devil's help; her mother dies of a sleeping potion, her brother Valentin dies at Faust's sword, and Gretchen, imprisoned for infanticide, refuses escape and is left to God's judgement — a voice declares her saved.

Part Two and legacy

Part Two abandons plot for vision: imperial finance and paper money, the Helena act conjuring classical beauty, the engineer Faust reclaiming land from the sea and dying, blind, mistaking the digging of his grave for his workers — yet speaking the fatal words over an imagined free people, and so losing and winning the wager at once, his soul carried off by angels past a cheated devil. Gounod's opera, Berlioz's Damnation, Liszt's and Mahler's settings, and countless stage versions have made Faust the German myth, the symbol of Western striving itself.
