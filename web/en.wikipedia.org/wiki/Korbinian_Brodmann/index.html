Korbinian Brodmann

Korbinian Brodmann (17 November 1868 – 22 August 1918) was a German neuroanatomist and psychiatrist best known for dividing the cerebral cortex into 52 distinct regions based on their microscopic cellular structure — the Brodmann areas, which remain a standard reference framework in neuroscience and neuroimaging more than a century later.

Life

Brodmann was born in Liggersdorf, in the Hohenzollern lands of southern Germany. He studied medicine at Munich, Würzburg, Berlin, and Freiburg, taking his degree in 1895. After illness (diphtheria) interrupted his early career, he retrained in neurology and psychiatry, working with Alois Alzheimer in Frankfurt and from 1901 with Oskar Vogt at the private Neurobiological Institute in Berlin. He later held posts at Tübingen, Halle, and Munich, and died suddenly in 1918, aged 49, probably of sepsis following pneumonia.

The cortical map

Working with the then-new Nissl staining method, Brodmann compared the arrangement of cell bodies — cytoarchitecture — across the cortex in humans and other mammals. In his 1909 monograph Vergleichende Lokalisationslehre der Großhirnrinde ("Comparative Localization Studies in the Brain Cortex") he published his famous map: in humans, 43 numbered regions (areas 1–52, with some numbers shared or subdivided), each hypothesised to correspond to a distinct function. Many of his boundaries were later vindicated by physiology: area 17 is the primary visual cortex, areas 44 and 45 form much of Broca's language area, and areas 3, 1, and 2 form the primary somatosensory cortex.

Although modern neuroscience recognises finer subdivisions and networks that cross his borders, "Brodmann area" numbering remains the everyday shorthand used in textbooks, functional MRI reports, and clinical neurology.
