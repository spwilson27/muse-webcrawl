The cyclostomes (superclass Cyclostomata, from Greek for "round mouths") are the living jawless fishes: the lampreys and the hagfishes. They are the only surviving members of the once-diverse jawless vertebrates (traditionally the "Agnatha"), and molecular evidence supports their forming a clade, sister to the jawed vertebrates (Gnathostomata).

Characteristics

Cyclostomes lack jaws, paired fins (in most), and scales, and have cartilaginous skeletons and a persistent notochord. Lampreys possess a round oral disc armed with horny teeth used to attach to prey and rasp flesh; hagfish have barbels and slime glands and feed on carrion and invertebrates on the sea floor. Both groups have eel-like bodies. Cyclostomes have a single median nostril and distinctive pouched gills.

Evolution

Fossil cyclostome relatives appear in Cambrian and Ordovician deposits, and lampreys remarkably similar to living forms are known from the Devonian and Carboniferous. Whether hagfish and lampreys share a jawless common ancestor or whether hagfish are secondarily simplified was long debated; genomic studies now favor cyclostome monophyly, implying features such as vertebrae were elaborated along their lineage.
