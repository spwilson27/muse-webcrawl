Prior probability

In Bayesian inference, prior beliefs are updated by data to give a posterior distribution.

In Bayesian statistical inference, a prior probability distribution, often simply called the prior, of an uncertain quantity is the probability distribution that expresses one's beliefs about this quantity before some evidence is taken into account. The prior is combined with the likelihood of the observed data through Bayes' theorem to produce the posterior probability distribution.

Informative and uninformative priors

An informative prior expresses specific, definite information about a variable, for example knowledge gained from previous studies. An uninformative prior, also called a diffuse or objective prior, expresses vague or general information, intended to let the data speak for themselves. Examples of uninformative priors include uniform distributions over a parameter's range and the Jeffreys prior, which is invariant under reparameterisation.

Conjugate priors

A prior is called a conjugate prior if the resulting posterior distribution belongs to the same probability distribution family as the prior. Conjugate priors are algebraically convenient: for example, a beta prior combined with binomial data yields a beta posterior, and a gamma prior combined with Poisson data yields a gamma posterior.

Improper priors

An improper prior is a prior whose integral (or sum) diverges — it does not integrate to 1 and so is not a genuine probability distribution. Improper priors can still yield proper posteriors when combined with data, and they are used in practice, for instance a uniform prior over the whole real line for a location parameter.

Philosophy

The choice of prior is one of the most debated aspects of Bayesian statistics. Subjective Bayesians regard the prior as a legitimate expression of personal belief, while objective Bayesians seek reference priors that are determined by the structure of the model rather than by opinion. Critics from the frequentist school have historically objected to priors as introducing subjectivity into statistical inference.
