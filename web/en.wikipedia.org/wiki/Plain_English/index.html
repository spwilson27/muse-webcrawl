Plain English

Plain English (or plain language) is a style of writing and communication designed to be understood by its intended audience on first reading. It favours short sentences, everyday words, the active voice, clear structure and headings, and direct address to the reader, and avoids jargon, legalism, and unnecessary complexity.

History

Calls for plain official language date back centuries — Geoffrey Chaucer and later reformers complained of obscurantist prose — but the modern movement grew in the 1970s. In Britain, the Plain English Campaign, founded by Chrissie Maher in 1979, campaigned against "gobbledygook" in government and business documents and awards its Crystal Mark to publications meeting its clarity standards. In the United States, President Jimmy Carter's 1978 executive order requiring regulations to be "written in plain English" began a series of reforms culminating in the Plain Writing Act of 2010, which obliges federal agencies to write public documents clearly.

Principles

Plain-language guidance typically recommends: planning around the reader's needs; sentences averaging 15–20 words; preferring common words ("use" over "utilise", "before" over "prior to"); using the active voice and personal pronouns; organising material with informative headings and lists; and testing documents with real readers. Advocates argue plain language saves money, reduces errors and complaints, and is a democratic necessity: citizens cannot comply with rules they cannot understand. Critics occasionally object that simplification can lose legal precision, but plain-language specialists reply that precision and clarity are compatible, and that much obscure drafting is habit rather than necessity.
