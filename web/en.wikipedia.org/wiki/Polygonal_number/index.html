Polygonal number

In mathematics, a polygonal number is a number represented as dots or pebbles arranged in the shape of a regular polygon. The dots are thought of as units. These are one kind of two-dimensional figurate numbers, studied since the time of the ancient Greeks, in particular by the Pythagoreans and by Hypsicles, who gave a definition in terms of the side length of the polygon.

Examples

The number 10, for example, is a triangular number because it can be arranged as a triangle of rows of 1, 2, 3 and 4 dots. The number 16 is a square number. The first several sequences include:

Formula

If s is the number of sides of the polygon and n is the number of dots on each side (n ≥ 1), then the n-th s-gonal number P(s, n) is given by:

P(s, n) = ((s − 2)n2 − (s − 4)n) / 2

Equivalently, each polygonal number decomposes into copies of triangular numbers: P(s, n) = (s − 2)·Tn−1 + n, where Tn−1 is the (n−1)-th triangular number.

Properties

For a given polygonal number with s sides, one can extend the polygon by adding a layer (a gnomon) of new dots along two adjacent sides. The difference between consecutive s-gonal numbers is P(s, n+1) − P(s, n) = (s − 2)n + 1.

Every hexagonal number is also a triangular number. Fermat's polygonal number theorem, conjectured by Pierre de Fermat and proved by Cauchy in 1813, states that every positive integer is a sum of at most s s-gonal numbers (with 1 counted as a polygonal number and repetitions allowed). Famous special cases include Lagrange's four-square theorem and the theorem that every natural number is the sum of at most three triangular numbers, proved by Gauss, who recorded the discovery in his diary with the word "ΕΥΡΗΚΑ!"

Generalizations

Higher-dimensional analogues include pyramidal numbers (dots stacked in three-dimensional pyramids), centered polygonal numbers (with a central dot surrounded by polygonal layers), and multiply perfect combinations studied in recreational mathematics.
