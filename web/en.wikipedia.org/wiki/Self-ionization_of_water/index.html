Self-ionization of water

The self-ionization of water (also called autoionization or autoprotolysis) is the chemical reaction in which two water molecules react to produce a hydronium ion (H3O+) and a hydroxide ion (OH−). It is the basis of the pH scale and explains why pure water, though a poor conductor, does conduct electricity slightly.

2 H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + OH− (aq)

Equilibrium constant

The equilibrium constant for self-ionization, denoted Kw, is about 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C. In pure water the concentrations of hydronium and hydroxide are equal, each about 1.0 × 10−7 mol/L, which corresponds to a neutral pH of 7.0 at that temperature. Kw increases with temperature, so the neutral pH falls slightly as water is heated, even though the water remains neutral.

Mechanism

Self-ionization occurs when a proton transfers between two neighbouring water molecules linked by a hydrogen bond. The process is extremely rapid and reversible; at any instant only about one water molecule in roughly 550 million is ionized. Proton transfer through a chain of hydrogen bonds, known as the Grotthuss mechanism, accounts for the unusually high mobility of hydrogen and hydroxide ions in water.

Heavy water

Heavy water (D2O) undergoes an analogous self-ionization but with a smaller equilibrium constant, reflecting the stronger deuterium–oxygen bond.
