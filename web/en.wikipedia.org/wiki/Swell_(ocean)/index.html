Swell (ocean)

In oceanography, a swell is a series of waves generated by wind blowing over a distant stretch of ocean (the fetch) that have travelled out of their generating area. Swell waves are distinguished from locally generated "wind sea" by their longer wavelengths, smoother and more regular crests, and consistent direction and period.

Formation and travel

Storm winds transfer energy to the sea surface, creating a chaotic mixture of wave lengths. As waves leave the storm area they sort themselves by speed — longer, faster waves outrun shorter ones (dispersion) — so a storm's energy may arrive at a distant coast as several distinct, orderly swells with periods of 10–20 seconds. Swells can cross entire ocean basins with little energy loss; swells generated near Antarctica routinely reach California.

Significance

Surfers prize groundswell for its power and regularity, and surf forecasting is largely the science of tracking distant storms and swell windows. For shipping and coastal engineering, swell matters because its long period can set up harbour resonance, drive beach erosion, and interact with local seas to produce dangerous conditions far from any storm.
