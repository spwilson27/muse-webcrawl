Eblaite is an extinct Semitic language spoken in the third millennium BC in and around the ancient city of Ebla, at modern Tell Mardikh in northern Syria. It is known almost entirely from the roughly 20,000 cuneiform texts of the Ebla palace archives, dated mainly to c. 2500–2250 BC. Eblaite is generally classified as an East Semitic language, closely related to Akkadian, though some scholars have argued for affinities with later Northwest Semitic languages.

Decipherment

The language became accessible largely through the archive's own bilingual lexical lists, which equate Sumerian words and logograms with their Eblaite equivalents. Because many tablets are written with a heavy use of Sumerian logograms, reconstructing the actual pronunciation of Eblaite words remains difficult; only a few hundred words written syllabically are directly attested.

Features

Like Akkadian, Eblaite had largely lost several Proto-Semitic consonants, and its grammar includes a dual number, a case system with nominative, accusative and genitive endings, and verbal prefixes similar to those of Akkadian. Names of kings, officials and deities in the tablets provided much of the earliest evidence for the language's vocabulary and for the religious life of early Syria, including deities such as Dagan, Kura and Nidakul.
