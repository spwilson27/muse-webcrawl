Dimensional analysis

Dimensional analysis
FieldPhysics, engineering, applied mathematics
Key resultsBuckingham π theorem; Rayleigh's method
Base dimensionsLength, mass, time, current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity

Dimensional analysis is the analysis of the relationships between different physical quantities by identifying their base quantities (such as length, mass, time, and electric current) and units of measure (such as metres and grams) and tracking these dimensions as calculations or comparisons are performed. The term was coined by Joseph Fourier, who laid foundations of the method in his 1822 work Théorie analytique de la chaleur.

Principle of homogeneity

The fundamental principle is that only quantities of the same dimension may be compared, added, subtracted, or equated: an equation that purports to describe a physical process must be dimensionally homogeneous. Checking dimensions is a powerful first test of the correctness of derived formulas.

Buckingham π theorem

The Buckingham π theorem, formalized by Edgar Buckingham in 1914, states that a physically meaningful equation involving n variables expressed in terms of k independent fundamental dimensions can be rewritten in terms of p = n − k dimensionless parameters (the π groups). This underlies the use of dimensionless numbers such as the Reynolds number in fluid mechanics, allowing experiments on scale models to predict full-scale behaviour.

Applications

Dimensional analysis is used to derive scaling laws, check units in computations, convert between unit systems, and design experiments. A celebrated example is G. I. Taylor's estimate of the energy of the first atomic bomb explosion from published photographs of the expanding fireball.
