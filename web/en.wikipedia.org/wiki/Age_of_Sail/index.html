The Age of Sail is the period in which international trade and naval warfare were dominated by sailing ships. It is conventionally dated from the late 16th century — often the Battle of Lepanto (1571), the last great battle of galleys — to the mid-19th century, when steamships took over the main trade routes; a common closing marker is the 1862 battle between the steam-powered ironclads Monitor and Virginia. The period saw European maritime empires span the globe, carried on the full-rigged ship, the carrack and galleon of the early centuries, the ship of the line as the capital ship of battle fleets, and finally the clipper, the fastest sailing merchantmen ever built.

Development

Oceanic sailing was made possible by combining the European square rig with lateen sails, the sternpost rudder, and advances in navigation — the compass, charts, the chronometer (for longitude), and the sextant. Dutch fluyts cut cargo costs in the 17th century; in the 18th, copper sheathing protected hulls from shipworm and fouling. Navies organised around the line of battle: fleets of two- and three-decked ships of the line, rated by their guns, fighting broadside-to-broadside, supported by frigates for scouting and commerce raiding. The era's climactic conflicts — the Anglo-Dutch Wars, the Seven Years' War, and the Napoleonic Wars ending at Trafalgar (1805) — were decided substantially at sea.

Trade and decline

Sailing ships carried the Atlantic slave trade, the East India Companies' tea and spice trades, emigrants, and bulk cargoes such as grain, timber, guano and wool. From the 1820s steamers took over mail and passenger routes; iron and steel hulls, compound engines and the screw propeller eroded sail's last advantages. Clippers of the 1850s–60s still beat steam on long tea and wool routes, but the opening of the Suez Canal (1869), useless to most sailing ships, and falling coal costs ended large-scale commercial sail by the early 20th century, though windjammers carried bulk cargo into the 1930s.
