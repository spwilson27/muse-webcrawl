Lexan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lexan is a registered trademark of SABIC Innovative Plastics (formerly General Electric Plastics) for a family of polycarbonate resins. The material was developed in 1953, when General Electric chemist Daniel W. Fox, working on a synthetic wire-coating resin, found that a sample in his flask had hardened into a tough, unbreakable plastic; independently, Hermann Schnell at Bayer in Germany developed the equivalent material, sold as Makrolon, at nearly the same time. GE introduced Lexan commercially in 1958.

Properties

Lexan is a transparent thermoplastic noted for combining high impact resistance—often described as virtually unbreakable in sheet form—with good clarity, heat resistance, and dimensional stability. It can be cold-formed, thermoformed, and machined, and it withstands temperatures up to about 130 °C (266 °F) in service, though it scratches more readily than glass and can yellow and become brittle under prolonged ultraviolet exposure unless coated.

Applications

  • Bullet-resistant and security glazing, riot shields, and machine guards
  • Greenhouse panels and architectural glazing
  • Automotive headlamp lenses and aircraft canopies
  • Compact discs and DVDs, whose substrate is polycarbonate
  • Reusable water bottles, eyewear lenses, and electronic device housings

Manufacture

Polycarbonate resins are produced from bisphenol A and a carbonyl source (historically phosgene, in an interfacial process) or by transesterification with diphenyl carbonate in a melt process. Concern over bisphenol A leaching has led some manufacturers to offer BPA-free alternatives for food-contact uses.
