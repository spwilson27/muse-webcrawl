History of Tobago

The history of Tobago is the history of the smaller of the two principal islands of the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago in the southern Caribbean. Tobago is famous in colonial history as the island that changed hands more often than any other in the Caribbean — some thirty times among Spanish, English, Dutch, French, Courlander and other claimants — before being definitively ceded to Britain in 1814.

Indigenous and early colonial eras

Tobago was inhabited by Amerindian peoples, including Saladoid cultures and later Kalinago (Island Caribs), who resisted European settlement. Columbus sighted the island in 1498. Through the 17th century the Dutch (who called it Nieuw Walcheren), English, French and settlers from the Duchy of Courland (present-day Latvia) repeatedly founded and lost colonies, while Carib resistance and disease destroyed several settlements. Britain declared Tobago neutral in the 1730s, leaving it largely to its indigenous inhabitants.

Plantation era

The Treaty of Paris (1763) ceded Tobago to Britain, and surveyors laid out plantations in cotton, sugar and indigo worked by enslaved Africans. France captured the island in 1781 and held it under the 1783 peace; Britain retook it in 1793, France again in 1801, and Britain finally in 1803, with the cession confirmed in 1814. The enslaved population resisted repeatedly, notably in the revolts of 1770–71 and 1807. Emancipation came in 1834, followed by apprenticeship and the arrival of some indentured labour.

Union with Trinidad and self-government

Sugar's decline led Britain to unite Tobago administratively with Trinidad in 1889, initially as a ward; Tobagonians long resented subordination to Port of Spain. Trinidad and Tobago became independent in 1962. The Tobago House of Assembly was created in 1980, giving the island a measure of internal self-government, and demands for greater autonomy — including a Tobago autonomy bill debated in recent years — remain central to the island's politics. The economy, once plantation-based, now depends heavily on tourism drawn by reefs such as Buccoo Reef and the Main Ridge Forest Reserve, among the oldest protected rainforests in the Western Hemisphere (1776).
