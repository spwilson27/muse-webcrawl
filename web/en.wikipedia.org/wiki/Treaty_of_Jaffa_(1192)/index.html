The Treaty of Jaffa, signed on 2 September 1192, was an agreement between the Muslim ruler Saladin and Richard I of England that effectively ended the Third Crusade. It followed the Battle of Jaffa, the last major engagement of the crusade, and restored a coastal strip of the Levant to the Crusader states while leaving Jerusalem in Muslim hands.

Background

After the Crusader victory at Arsuf (1191) and the recapture of Jaffa, Richard twice advanced toward Jerusalem but declined to besiege the city, judging that he could not hold it once his army dispersed. Negotiations ran intermittently alongside the fighting; at one point Richard even proposed a marriage alliance between his sister Joan and Saladin's brother al-Adil. When Saladin attacked a weakened Jaffa in July 1192, Richard relieved it in a dramatic counterattack, including the famous Battle of Jaffa in early August.

Terms

The treaty established a truce of three years and eight months (in some accounts, three years). Its main provisions were:

• The Crusaders retained the coastline from Tyre to Jaffa, including the recovered ports.
• Jerusalem remained under Muslim control, but Christian pilgrims were granted free access to the holy places.
• Ascalon's fortifications, recently rebuilt by Richard, were to be demolished.
• Both sides were free to move through each other's territory for trade and pilgrimage.

Aftermath

Richard departed the Holy Land on 9 October 1192 and was captured in Austria on his way home. The treaty preserved a rump Kingdom of Jerusalem—often called the "Kingdom of Acre"—which survived for another century until the fall of Acre in 1291. A later agreement, the Treaty of Jaffa of 1229 between Frederick II and al-Kamil, temporarily restored Jerusalem itself to Christian rule by negotiation.
