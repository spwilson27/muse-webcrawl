Manuel Azaña Díaz (10 January 1880 – 3 November 1940) was a Spanish politician and writer who served as Prime Minister of the Second Spanish Republic and later as its last President. A leading figure of the republican left, he was the principal architect of the Republic's early reform programme and its wartime head of state during the Spanish Civil War.

Early life and career

Azaña was born in Alcalá de Henares into a prosperous family, was educated in law, and worked as a civil servant in Madrid while building a literary reputation as an essayist, translator, and critic; his biography of Juan Valera won the National Literature Prize in 1926. During the First World War he was an outspoken supporter of the Allies. He founded the party Acción Republicana in 1925 and, after the fall of the monarchy, became Minister of War in the provisional government of April 1931.

Prime Minister

As Minister of War, Azaña carried through a controversial reform of the oversized officer corps. In October 1931 he became Prime Minister, heading coalition governments that enacted a new constitution, Catalan autonomy, agrarian reform, and measures separating church and state. His famous declaration in the Cortes that "Spain has ceased to be Catholic" symbolised the anticlerical thrust of the government. The coalition lost the 1933 elections, and Azaña was briefly imprisoned after the failed revolution of October 1934.

President and Civil War

Following the Popular Front victory of February 1936, Azaña again became Prime Minister and, in May, was elected President of the Republic. When the military uprising of July 1936 triggered civil war, he remained head of state but real power rested with successive governments. Disheartened by the Republic's dependence on the Soviet Union and the failure of his appeals for international mediation, he crossed into France in February 1939 and resigned the presidency. He died in exile at Montauban on 3 November 1940, under the shadow of possible extradition to Franco's Spain.

Writings

Azaña's diaries and memoirs, including El jardín de los frailes and the posthumous La velada en Benicarló — a dialogue on the war written in 1937 — are regarded as classics of twentieth-century Spanish political literature.
