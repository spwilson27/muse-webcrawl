Lagrangian mechanics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lagrangian mechanics is a formulation of classical mechanics introduced by the Italian-French mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1788 in his Mécanique analytique. Instead of the forces and vectors of Newtonian mechanics, it describes a system by a single scalar function, the Lagrangian, usually defined as L = T − V, the difference between kinetic and potential energy, expressed in generalised coordinates.

Euler–Lagrange equations

The equations of motion follow from the principle of stationary action (Hamilton's principle): the path a system takes between two states makes the action — the time integral of the Lagrangian — stationary. This yields the Euler–Lagrange equations:

d/dt (∂L/∂q̇i) − ∂L/∂qi = 0

where qi are generalised coordinates. Constraint forces need not be computed explicitly, and any convenient coordinates can be used, which makes the method especially powerful for systems with constraints, such as pendulums and rigid bodies.

Symmetries and conservation laws

If the Lagrangian does not depend on a coordinate, the corresponding generalised momentum is conserved. Emmy Noether's theorem (1915) generalised this: every continuous symmetry of the action implies a conservation law — time-translation symmetry gives conservation of energy, spatial translation gives momentum, and rotation gives angular momentum.

Extensions

Lagrangian methods extend naturally to fields, relativity and quantum mechanics: modern theories such as the Standard Model are specified by writing down a Lagrangian density. The alternative Hamiltonian formulation is closely related via the Legendre transform.
