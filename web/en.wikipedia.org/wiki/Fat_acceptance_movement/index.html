Fat acceptance movement

The fat acceptance movement (also known as size acceptance, fat pride, fat empowerment, and fat liberation) is a social movement seeking to eliminate the social stigma of obesity. The movement originated in the 1960s in the United States and advocates for civil rights and cultural acceptance for fat people, challenging anti-fat bias in employment, healthcare, media, and public life.

History

The movement is often dated to 1967, when a "fat-in" was held in New York's Central Park. In 1969 the National Association to Aid Fat Americans (now the National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance, NAAFA) was founded. In the 1970s, the radical feminist Fat Underground collective in Los Angeles issued the "Fat Liberation Manifesto" (1973), framing fat oppression in political terms.

Strands and debates

Fat acceptance encompasses a range of positions, from campaigns against weight-based discrimination and for accessible public spaces, to body positivity's broader promotion of self-acceptance. The Health at Every Size (HAES) approach, which emphasizes health behaviours rather than weight loss, has been influential within parts of the movement. Critics, including many medical professionals, argue that the movement understates health risks associated with obesity, while supporters respond that weight stigma itself harms health and that diets rarely produce lasting weight loss.

Within the movement there are debates over terminology ("fat" reclaimed as a neutral descriptor versus terms such as "overweight" or "plus-size") and over its relationship with commercial body positivity.
