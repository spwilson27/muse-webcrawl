Colloblast

Colloblasts are specialised adhesive cells found on the tentacles of ctenophores (comb jellies), used to capture prey. They are the defining functional counterpart — though not the evolutionary homologue — of the stinging cnidocytes of cnidarians: instead of injecting venom, a colloblast discharges a sticky secretion that glues small planktonic animals to the tentacle, which is then drawn to the mouth.

Structure

A colloblast consists of a dome-shaped apical cap studded with adhesive granules, supported by a coiled spiral filament anchored in the tentacle's mesoglea and surrounded by a straight axial filament connected to the cell nucleus. On contact with prey, the granules rupture and release adhesive; the spiral filament acts as a shock absorber, stretching so that the struggling prey does not tear the cell free. Colloblasts are concentrated along the tentilla (side branches) of the two feeding tentacles in most cydippid ctenophores.

Distribution

Almost all ctenophores possess colloblasts; the notable exception is the class Nuda (beroids), which lack tentacles entirely and engulf prey with large mouths, and species of the genus Haeckelia, which instead sequester functional cnidocytes from the jellyfish they eat.
