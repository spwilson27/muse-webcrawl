Religious denomination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Branches within religious traditions

A religious denomination is a subgroup within a religion that operates under a common name and tradition, among other identifying factors. The term refers to the various Christian denominations, but is also used to describe the four major branches of Judaism, the denominations of Islam and Hinduism, and the various denominations found in other religions.

Christian denominations

Christianity is divided into denominations such as Roman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, and the many traditions of Protestantism, including Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal and Adventist churches. Estimates of the number of distinct Christian denominations worldwide run into the tens of thousands, although counts vary widely depending on how groups are defined.

Islamic denominations

The major denominations of Islam are Sunni Islam, Shia Islam and Kharijite Islam (today represented mainly by the Ibadi). Within these are numerous schools of jurisprudence (madhhab) and theological traditions, as well as Sufi orders (tariqa).

Jewish denominations

Jewish religious movements include Orthodox Judaism (including Haredi and Modern Orthodox Judaism), Conservative Judaism, Reform Judaism, Reconstructionist Judaism and Humanistic Judaism, which differ in their approaches to Jewish law and tradition.

Formation and denominationalism

Denominations form when groups within a religion develop distinct interpretations of doctrine, practice or church governance, sometimes leading to schism. Denominationalism is the belief that some or all Christian groups are legitimate churches despite differences in doctrine. It contrasts with sectarianism, which denies legitimacy to other groups. Ecumenism seeks increased cooperation and unity among denominations.
