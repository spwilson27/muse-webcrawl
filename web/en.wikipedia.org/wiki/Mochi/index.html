Mochi

TypeRice cake
Place of originJapan
Main ingredientsGlutinous rice (mochigome), water
VariationsDaifuku, kagami mochi, kirimochi, warabimochi

Mochi (Japanese: 餅) is a Japanese rice cake made of mochigome, a short-grain japonica glutinous rice, and sometimes other ingredients such as water, sugar and cornstarch. The rice is steamed, then pounded and mashed into a sticky, elastic paste which is moulded into shapes and eaten fresh, or dried and cooked later. Mochi is a traditional food for the Japanese New Year and is commonly eaten all year round.

History

Rice cultivation reached Japan from mainland Asia in the Yayoi period, and pounded rice cakes appear in early Japanese ritual and court culture. Mochi was offered to the kami (deities) at Shinto shrines and eaten at festivals and seasonal turning points. By the Heian period it was an established part of New Year celebrations, including the kagami mochi offering of two stacked round cakes topped with a bitter orange. The communal rice-pounding ceremony, mochitsuki, remains a popular New Year event.

Production

Traditional mochi is made in the labor-intensive process of mochitsuki: soaked glutinous rice is steamed, placed in a large mortar (usu), and pounded with wooden mallets (kine) — one worker pounding rhythmically while another turns and wets the mass by hand. The resulting dough is shaped, cut or formed into cakes. Modern production uses automated machines, and many Japanese households own electric mochi makers or bread machines with a mochi function. Mochi can also be made from mochiko, a sweet rice flour, mixed with water and steamed or microwaved.

Uses

Plain mochi is grilled or boiled and eaten with soy sauce and nori, dusted with kinako (roasted soybean flour), or added to soups such as ozōni, the New Year soup. Sweetened and filled mochi confections include daifuku (mochi stuffed with sweet red bean paste or other fillings), ichigo daifuku (with a whole strawberry), kusa mochi (flavoured with mugwort) and sakuramochi (wrapped in a pickled cherry leaf). Outside Japan, mochi is widely known as a topping for frozen yogurt and in the form of mochi ice cream, a ball of ice cream wrapped in a thin mochi skin, commercialised in the 1990s. Similar pounded glutinous rice cakes exist elsewhere in Asia, such as Korean tteok and Chinese niangao.

Safety

Because of its extreme stickiness and elasticity, mochi causes a number of choking deaths in Japan each year, most often among elderly people at New Year. Japanese authorities issue annual warnings to cut mochi into small pieces and chew thoroughly.
