Deuterocanonical books

The deuterocanonical books (from the Greek words for "second canon" or "second list") are books and passages considered by the Catholic Church and several other Christian traditions to be canonical books of the Old Testament, but which are considered non-canonical by most Protestant traditions and are absent from the Hebrew Bible. They were written largely during the Second Temple period, mostly in Greek, and are preserved in the Septuagint.

The Book of Tobit is among the best-known deuterocanonical writings.

The books

The seven books recognised as deuterocanonical by the Catholic Church (canon affirmed at the Council of Trent, 1546) are: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch (including the Letter of Jeremiah), and 1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees — plus additions to Esther and Daniel (including Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon). Eastern Orthodox churches accept these and a few additional texts; Oriental Orthodox canons are broader still.

History and status

The books circulated in the Greek-speaking Jewish diaspora and were widely used by the early Church. Jerome distinguished them from the Hebrew canon when translating the Vulgate, though he included them. At the Reformation, Protestants followed the Hebrew canon and relegated these writings to a separate "Apocrypha" section (or excluded them), while Catholics formally canonised them in reaction at Trent. Anglican tradition historically printed them for "example of life and instruction of manners" but not to establish doctrine.
