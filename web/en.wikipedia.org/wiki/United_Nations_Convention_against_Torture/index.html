The United Nations Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (commonly the Convention against Torture, CAT or UNCAT) is an international human rights treaty, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 10 December 1984 and in force since 26 June 1987 — a date now marked as the International Day in Support of Victims of Torture. Building on Article 5 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the UN's 1975 Declaration on the Protection of All Persons from Torture, the Convention requires states to prohibit torture absolutely, to criminalise it, to prevent it in territory under their jurisdiction, and never to return a person to a state where they face a substantial risk of torture.

Key provisions

Article 1 defines torture as any act by which severe pain or suffering, physical or mental, is intentionally inflicted for purposes such as obtaining information or a confession, punishment, intimidation, or discrimination, when inflicted by or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official. Lawful sanctions are excluded. Article 2 makes the prohibition absolute: "No exceptional circumstances whatsoever, whether a state of war or a threat of war, internal political instability or any other public emergency, may be invoked as a justification of torture", and superior orders are no defence. Article 3 enshrines non-refoulement: no state may expel, return, or extradite a person to a state where there are substantial grounds for believing they would be in danger of torture.

Articles 4–9 oblige states to make torture a criminal offence, to establish jurisdiction — including universal jurisdiction over offenders found in their territory — and to prosecute or extradite (aut dedere aut judicare). Articles 10–13 require training of officials, review of interrogation rules, prompt and impartial investigation of allegations, and a right of complaint for victims; Article 14 guarantees victims redress and rehabilitation; Article 15 excludes evidence obtained by torture from any proceedings.

Monitoring

The Committee Against Torture, a body of ten independent experts, examines periodic reports from states parties (Articles 17–24) and may consider individual complaints and inter-state complaints where states have accepted those procedures, and conduct confidential inquiries into systematic torture. The Optional Protocol of 2002 (OPCAT), in force since 2006, created the Subcommittee on Prevention of Torture and requires states to designate independent National Preventive Mechanisms empowered to visit all places of detention — an inspection model borrowed from the European Convention for the Prevention of Torture.

Practice and significance

The Convention underpinned the landmark extradition proceedings against former Chilean dictator Augusto Pinochet in London (1998–99), in which the House of Lords held that a former head of state could not claim immunity for torture committed after the relevant dates. The treaty's non-refoulement rule overlaps with refugee law and the European Convention on Human Rights, and its definition and obligations have been tested in litigation over extraordinary rendition, "enhanced interrogation" after 2001, and diplomatic assurances. Despite near-universal ratification, the Committee and the UN Special Rapporteur on torture continue to document widespread non-compliance, and several states — including major powers — have entered reservations, notably on the definition of cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment under Article 16.
