A qadi (Arabic: قاضٍ, qāḍī; also spelled cadi, kadi, or kazi) is a judge in Islamic law (Sharia) who presides over a Sharia court. Historically, the qadi was responsible for the administration of justice in Muslim communities, hearing civil and criminal cases and applying the jurisprudence (fiqh) of a recognised school of law.

History

The office emerged under the Umayyad caliphs in the late 7th and early 8th centuries, as deputies of provincial governors were appointed to settle disputes. Under the Abbasids, the office of qadi al-qudat (chief qadi) was created in Baghdad, first held by Abu Yusuf (d. 798), a founder of the Hanafi school. In the Ottoman Empire, qadis (Turkish: kadı) formed a central part of the provincial administration, combining judicial work with notarial and administrative duties, and their registers (sicil) are a major source for Ottoman social history.

Functions and qualifications

A qadi's judgement was based on the Quran, the Sunna, consensus, and analogical reasoning, as systematised by the legal schools. Classical jurists required the qadi to be an adult Muslim of sound mind, upright character, and (in most schools) trained in law; the qadi's role was distinct from that of the mufti, who issued non-binding legal opinions (fatwas), and from executive authorities, who enforced judgements. Qadis also supervised markets in practice, oversaw endowments (waqf), and registered marriages, sales, and inheritances.

Modern usage

In many modern Muslim-majority states, Sharia courts survive with limited jurisdiction, typically over family law—marriage, divorce, and inheritance—while general civil and criminal law is handled by secular courts. In a few countries, such as Saudi Arabia and Iran, qadi-style religious judges retain broader authority.
