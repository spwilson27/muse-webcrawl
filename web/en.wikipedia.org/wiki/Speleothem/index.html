A speleothem (from Greek spēlaion, "cave", and thema, "deposit") is a geological formation by mineral deposits that accumulate over time in natural caves. Speleothems most commonly form in limestone or dolomite solutional caves, where water seeping through rock dissolves calcium carbonate and re-deposits it as calcite when the water enters the cave air and loses carbon dioxide.

Types

  • Stalactites hang from the cave ceiling; thin hollow "soda straws" are their earliest form.
  • Stalagmites grow upward from the floor beneath dripping water; where a stalactite and stalagmite meet, they form a column.
  • Flowstone is sheet-like deposits formed by flowing water, including draperies or "curtains" and rimstone dams.
  • Helictites are twisted, gravity-defying growths driven by capillary forces.
  • Cave pearls are small spherical concretions formed around a nucleus in shallow pools.
  • Anthodites are radiating clusters of needle-like crystals, usually of aragonite.

Palaeoclimate value

Because speleothems grow in annual or seasonal layers over tens or hundreds of thousands of years, and can be dated precisely by uranium–thorium dating, their oxygen and carbon isotope ratios are a major archive of past climate. Stalagmite records from caves such as Hulu Cave in China have been used to date the variations of the Asian monsoon through the last glacial cycle.
