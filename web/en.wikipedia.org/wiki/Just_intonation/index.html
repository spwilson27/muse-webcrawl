Just intonation is a musical tuning system in which the frequencies of notes are related by ratios of small whole numbers. Any interval tuned in this way is described as a just interval. Just intervals are heard as particularly consonant because the waveforms of the two notes align at regular intervals and their overtones coincide. The two most common just intervals are the octave (2:1) and the perfect fifth (3:2).

Principles

In five-limit just intonation, the most common form, intervals are built from the prime numbers 2, 3 and 5, giving the major scale ratios 1:1, 9:8, 5:4, 4:3, 3:2, 5:3, 15:8 and 2:1. The just major third (5:4, about 386 cents) is noticeably flatter and sweeter than the third of twelve-tone equal temperament (400 cents), while the just fifth (3:2, about 702 cents) is very close to the tempered fifth.

History

Just intonation descends from the tuning theories of antiquity: Ptolemy described five-limit scales in the 2nd century AD. In the Renaissance, theorists such as Gioseffo Zarlino systematised the "syntonon" of just ratios. As modulation between distant keys became central to Western music, the inconsistencies of just intonation in fixed-pitch instruments (the syntonic comma problem) encouraged the adoption of temperaments, culminating in the near-universal use of twelve-tone equal temperament.

Modern practice

Voices and unfretted instruments naturally drift toward just intervals; barbershop quartets and a cappella choirs are celebrated for the ringing "just" sound. In the 20th century composers such as Harry Partch (who built a 43-tone just scale and custom instruments), Lou Harrison, and Ben Johnston revived just intonation as a compositional resource, and electronic instruments have made arbitrary just tunings easy to realise.
