Aeolian processes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wind transporting sand over dunes (image placeholder)

Aeolian processes, also spelled eolian, pertain to wind activity in the study of geology and weather and specifically to the wind's ability to shape the surface of the Earth (or other planets). Winds may erode, transport, and deposit materials and are effective agents in regions with sparse vegetation, a lack of soil moisture and a large supply of unconsolidated sediments. The term derives from Aeolus, the Greek god of the winds.

Erosion

Wind erodes surfaces by deflation, the removal of loose fine-grained particles, and by abrasion, the sandblasting action of wind-borne grains against rock surfaces. Abrasion produces ventifacts — stones faceted by wind — and in bedrock can carve streamlined yardangs. Deflation can lower a surface until it reaches the water table, forming deflation hollows and desert pavement.

Transport

Particles are moved by three mechanisms: suspension of fine dust, which can travel thousands of kilometres; saltation, the bouncing of sand grains in low trajectories that accounts for most sand movement; and surface creep, the rolling or sliding of coarser grains pushed by saltating impacts. The physicist Ralph Alger Bagnold established the physics of blown sand in his 1941 work The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes.

Deposition

Where wind speed falls, sediment accumulates. Sand builds dunes of many forms — barchan, transverse, linear, and star dunes — while fine silt settles as widespread blankets of loess, which form fertile soils in parts of China, Europe, and North America.

Extraterrestrial aeolian processes

Aeolian features are prominent on Mars, where thin-atmosphere winds move extensive dune fields, and on Saturn's moon Titan, where dunes of organic sand girdle the equator. Wind streaks and yardangs have also been identified on Venus.
