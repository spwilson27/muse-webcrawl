Island Caribs

The Island Caribs, also known as the Kalinago, are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean. Their name was given to the Caribbean Sea. According to their oral traditions, the Kalinago originated in the Orinoco River area of South America and migrated to the Caribbean islands, where — in the traditional account — Carib men conquered and absorbed the earlier Igneri (Arawakan) inhabitants.

History

By the time of Columbus's arrival in 1493, the Kalinago dominated much of the Lesser Antilles, including Dominica, Martinique, Saint Vincent, and Grenada. They fiercely resisted European colonization longer than many other Indigenous groups; Dominica's mountainous terrain sheltered them, and a Kalinago Territory (reserve) was formally established there in 1903.

On Saint Vincent, intermixing between Kalinago and Africans produced the Garifuna ("Black Caribs"), who resisted the British in the Second Carib War (1795–1797) under chief Joseph Chatoyer. After their defeat, thousands of Garifuna were exiled to Roatán Island off Honduras, from where they spread along the Central American coast.

Culture and language

The Island Carib language was an Arawakan language, notable because men and women historically spoke distinct registers — reflecting, according to tradition, the union of Carib men with Arawak women. The men's language was closely related to mainland Galibi (Kali'na). The language ceased to be spoken in the Antilles in the 20th century, though Garifuna — a descendant language — survives in Central America and has been recognized by UNESCO.

Kalinago society was organized in villages under chiefs (ouboutou), with men living in communal men's houses (taboui), skilled canoe-building and seafaring, and cultivation of cassava, sweet potatoes, and other crops.

Today

About 3,000 Kalinago live in the Kalinago Territory on Dominica's east coast, governed by an elected Kalinago Council and Chief. Revival efforts promote Kalinago culture, crafts, and language heritage.
