Prolactin (PRL), also known as lactotropin, is a protein hormone produced and secreted mainly by lactotroph cells of the anterior pituitary gland. It is best known for its role in enabling mammals to produce milk (lactation), but in humans and other vertebrates it has hundreds of additional functions relating to reproduction, metabolism, immune regulation, fluid balance and parental behaviour.

Structure and secretion

Human prolactin is a 199-amino-acid single-chain polypeptide with a molecular mass of about 23 kilodaltons, structurally related to growth hormone and placental lactogen. Uniquely among pituitary hormones, prolactin secretion is under predominantly inhibitory control: dopamine released from the hypothalamus tonically suppresses its release. Secretion is stimulated by thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), oestrogen, and the suckling stimulus, and follows a sleep-entrained daily rhythm. Prolactin is also produced locally in many extrapituitary tissues, including the breast, uterus, prostate and immune cells.

Functions

In pregnancy, rising prolactin (together with oestrogen and progesterone) drives development of the mammary glands and, after delivery, maintains milk synthesis and secretion; each episode of suckling triggers a prolactin surge. Prolactin contributes to maternal behaviour, suppresses the reproductive cycle during lactation (lactational amenorrhoea), and plays roles in osmoregulation and immune function. In males it influences reproductive physiology, including testosterone production and libido.

Clinical significance

Excess prolactin (hyperprolactinaemia) most commonly results from a prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma (prolactinoma), or from dopamine-blocking drugs such as many antipsychotics. It causes galactorrhoea, menstrual disturbance and infertility in women, and reduced libido and erectile dysfunction in men. It is usually treated with dopamine agonists such as cabergoline or bromocriptine. Abnormally low prolactin (hypoprolactinaemia) can cause failure of lactation after childbirth.
