Ideomotor phenomenon

The ideomotor phenomenon is a psychological phenomenon in which a subject makes motions unconsciously. It occurs when a person performs actions seemingly dictated by thoughts, expectations, or suggestions without conscious awareness of doing so. The term was coined by William Benjamin Carpenter in 1852 in his explanation of how table-turning, dowsing rods, and pendulums appear to move with a will of their own; Michael Faraday's experiments on table-turning in 1853 demonstrated that the sitters' own involuntary muscular movements were responsible.

Examples

The ideomotor effect is the accepted scientific explanation for a range of phenomena:

  • Ouija boards — the planchette moves because participants unconsciously push it toward answers they expect.
  • Dowsing — rods and pendulums respond to the dowser's expectations; controlled double-blind tests show performance at chance when the dowser is ignorant of the target's location.
  • Automatic writing and table-turning in séances.
  • Facilitated communication, in which a facilitator unconsciously guides a non-speaking person's hand; controlled studies showed the facilitator, not the patient, authored the messages, leading professional bodies to classify the technique as pseudoscientific.
  • Applied kinesiology muscle testing and the movements of "Chevreul's pendulum," described by the French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul in the 1830s–1850s.

Mechanism and research

Ideomotor actions arise when anticipating an action's sensory consequences primes the motor system to produce small movements which, amplified by compliant instruments such as pendulums or planchettes, become visible. Because the movements are not experienced as voluntary, participants attribute them to external agents — spirits, water, or another person's communication. Experiments by Daniel Wegner and others on the illusion of conscious will explored how this misattribution arises. The related observer expectancy effect and cases such as Clever Hans, the horse that appeared to calculate, are considered special cases of ideomotor cueing.
