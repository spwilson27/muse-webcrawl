A sultanate is a state or country governed by a sultan, a ruler in the Islamic world bearing the title of sultan (Arabic: سلطان, sulṭān, "authority" or "power"). The term originally denoted the office or authority of the sultan himself, and by extension the territory under his rule. Sultanates emerged across the Middle East, North and East Africa, Central Asia, South Asia and Southeast Asia from the 11th century onward, as de facto independent rulers within or beyond the nominal framework of the Abbasid Caliphate asserted their own authority.

History

The title of sultan was first formally assumed by Mahmud of Ghazni in the early 11th century, and it was later borne by the Seljuk Turks, whose Great Seljuk Empire received recognition from the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad. Unlike the caliph, whose claim rested on religious succession to the Prophet Muhammad, the sultan's authority was understood to be temporal and military, often legitimised by caliphal investiture.

Over subsequent centuries numerous sultanates arose, including the Ayyubid and Mamluk sultanates of Egypt, the Sultanate of Rum in Anatolia, the Delhi Sultanate in India, the sultanates of the Maghreb and the Horn of Africa, and the Malay sultanates of maritime Southeast Asia. The Ottoman sultanate, founded around 1299, became the most powerful of them, lasting until its abolition in 1922.

Modern sultanates

Today, the Sultanate of Oman is the only sovereign state whose ruler bears the title of sultan as head of state. Brunei's ruler is styled Sultan and Yang Di-Pertuan, though Brunei is usually classified as an absolute monarchy rather than a "sultanate" in its official name. Within federal Malaysia, seven of the nine Malay rulers are sultans of their constituent states. Sultanates also persist as subnational or traditional entities in Indonesia, the Philippines (notably Sulu and Maguindanao) and parts of Africa.
