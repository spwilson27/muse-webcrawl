Wallachia

Wallachia or Walachia (Romanian: Țara Românească, literally "The Romanian Land") is a historical and geographical region of Romania. It is situated north of the Lower Danube and south of the Southern Carpathians. Wallachia was traditionally divided into two sections, Muntenia (Greater Wallachia) and Oltenia (Lesser Wallachia). The region is sometimes referred to as Muntenia through identification with its larger eastern section.

History

Wallachia was founded as a principality in the early 14th century by Basarab I, following a rebellion against Charles I of Hungary. Its traditional founding date of c. 1330 is marked by Basarab's victory over the Hungarian king at the Battle of Posada. The principality was bounded by Moldavia to the east, the Ottoman Empire across the Danube to the south, and Transylvania and Hungary to the northwest.

Under Mircea the Elder (reigned 1386–1418), Wallachia reached its greatest territorial extent and resisted Ottoman expansion, though it gradually became a tributary vassal state of the Ottoman Empire. The principality is perhaps best known internationally for Vlad III, nicknamed Vlad the Impaler (Drăculea), who ruled intermittently in the mid-15th century and became the inspiration for Bram Stoker's Count Dracula.

Michael the Brave briefly united Wallachia, Transylvania, and Moldavia in 1600, an achievement later celebrated as a precursor of modern Romania. From the early 18th century, the Ottomans appointed Greek Phanariote rulers. In 1859 Wallachia united with Moldavia under Alexandru Ioan Cuza, forming the United Principalities that became the modern Romanian state.

Geography

Wallachia stretches from the Danube in the south to the Carpathian Mountains in the north. The Danube plain occupies the southern portion, while the Subcarpathian hills and the Getic Plateau rise toward the mountains. The Olt River traditionally divides Muntenia in the east from Oltenia in the west. Major cities include Bucharest (the capital of Romania), Craiova, Ploiești, Pitești, Târgoviște, and Brăila.
