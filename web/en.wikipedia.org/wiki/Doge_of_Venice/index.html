The Doge of Venice (Venetian: Doxe de Venexia; Italian: Doge di Venezia) was the chief magistrate and leader of the Republic of Venice between 697 and 1797. The word doge derives from the Latin dux, "leader." Doges were elected for life by the city's aristocracy through an extraordinarily complex electoral procedure designed to prevent factional manipulation, involving alternating rounds of sortition and voting by committees of electors.

Powers and restrictions

Although the doge was the ceremonial head of state, represented Venice to foreign powers, and led its fleets and armies in earlier centuries, his actual power was carefully circumscribed. A sworn oath, the promissione ducale, restricted each doge's conduct; he could not open foreign correspondence alone, accept gifts, leave the city without permission, or allow his family to exploit his office. The doge reigned, surrounded by pomp — most famously in the annual Marriage of the Sea ceremony — but increasingly did not rule, as authority passed to councils such as the Great Council, the Senate and the feared Council of Ten.

Notable doges

Notable holders include Enrico Dandolo, the blind octogenarian who diverted the Fourth Crusade to Constantinople in 1204; Francesco Foscari, deposed in 1457 after a long reign; and Marino Faliero, executed for treason in 1355 after plotting a coup. The office ended in 1797 when the last doge, Ludovico Manin, abdicated before Napoleon's forces, ending over a thousand years of the Venetian Republic.
