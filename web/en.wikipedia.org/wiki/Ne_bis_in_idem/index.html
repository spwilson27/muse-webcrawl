Ne bis in idem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ne bis in idem — Latin for "not twice for the same [thing]" — is a legal principle according to which a person cannot be tried or punished twice for the same offence or facts. It is the civilian-law expression of the protection known in common-law systems as double jeopardy. The principle appears in Article 14(7) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 4 of Protocol No. 7 to the European Convention on Human Rights, and Article 50 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights.

Scope

The principle raises two recurring questions: what counts as the "same" — the same legal offence (idem crimen) or the same underlying facts (idem factum), with the European Court of Human Rights favouring the factual approach in Zolotukhin v. Russia (2009) — and what counts as a final determination triggering the bar. In the European Union, the principle also operates transnationally: a person finally tried in one member state may not be prosecuted in another for the same acts.

Exceptions and limits

Many systems allow reopening where new and compelling evidence emerges or where the first proceedings were fundamentally defective; England and Wales, which long treated the rule as absolute, introduced a statutory exception in the Criminal Justice Act 2003 permitting retrial for serious offences on new and compelling evidence — first used in the case of William Dunlop. Within federations, the "separate sovereigns" doctrine may allow state and federal prosecutions for the same act, a persistent source of controversy.
