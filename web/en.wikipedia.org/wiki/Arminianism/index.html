Arminianism

[Image: Arminianism — Portrait of Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609), founder of the Arminian school of theology]

Arminianism is a movement within Protestant theology, originated in the Dutch Reformed tradition and developed in reaction to the Reformed (Calvinist) doctrine of predestination. It is based on the theological ideas of Dutch pastor and professor of theology Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609), and maintained by the Remonstrants. Arminianism centres on the conditional, grace-enabled free will of humanity: God predestines on the basis of foreseen faith, Christ's atonement is universal in intent, and the elect are those who persevere in faith — each point framed as an alternative to the corresponding Calvinist doctrine.

The Five Articles of Remonstrance

After Arminius's death, his followers presented the Five Articles of Remonstrance (1610) to the Dutch States-General. In summary, they taught: (1) conditional election based on God's foreknowledge of faith; (2) universal atonement — Christ died for all, though only believers benefit; (3) total depravity — humans cannot believe without prevenient (preceding) grace; (4) resistible grace — that grace can be rejected; and (5) the possibility of falling from grace, a point the early Remonstrants treated cautiously.

Synod of Dort and later history

The international Synod of Dort (1618–19) condemned the Remonstrant articles and formulated the Canons of Dort, the "five points of Calvinism" (often summarised by the acronym TULIP) written in direct response. Remonstrant leaders were banished, though the movement survived and the Remonstrant Brotherhood persists in the Netherlands. Arminian theology later shaped John Wesley and Methodism, much of the Holiness and Pentecostal movements, General Baptists, and large parts of evangelicalism, making it one of the two dominant soteriological systems in Protestantism alongside Calvinism.
