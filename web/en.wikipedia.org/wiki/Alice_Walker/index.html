Alice Malsenior Tallulah-Kate Walker (born February 9, 1944) is an American novelist, short story writer, poet, and social activist. In 1982 she became the first African-American woman to win the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, for her novel The Color Purple, which was later adapted into a film by Steven Spielberg and a Tony Award-winning Broadway musical.

Early life

Walker was born in Eatonton, Georgia, the youngest of eight children of sharecroppers. At age eight she was accidentally shot in the eye by a brother's BB gun, an injury that left her partially blind and deeply marked her childhood. She attended Spelman College and Sarah Lawrence College, graduating in 1965, and worked in voter registration and civil rights organizing in Mississippi.

Career

Her first book of poetry, Once, appeared in 1968, followed by the novels The Third Life of Grange Copeland (1970) and Meridian (1976). The Color Purple (1982), an epistolary novel about Celie, an abused Black woman in the rural South, won both the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award. Walker also recovered the work of Zora Neale Hurston, editing a 1979 collection of Hurston's writings and helping restore her to the literary canon. Walker coined the term "womanism" in her 1983 collection In Search of Our Mothers' Gardens.
