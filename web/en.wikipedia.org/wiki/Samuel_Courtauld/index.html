Samuel Courtauld (7 May 1876 – 1 December 1947) was an English industrialist and art collector who chaired the family's textile giant Courtaulds and became one of the most important British patrons of modern French painting. His gift founded the Courtauld Institute of Art in London, and his personal collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces forms the core of the Courtauld Gallery.

Business career

Courtauld was a member of the Huguenot-descended Courtauld family, whose silk firm dated to the 18th century. After an unusually cosmopolitan education — he travelled widely and studied in Germany and France, considering an academic life before entering the business — he rose through the company and became chairman in 1921. Under his leadership Courtaulds became a world leader in the new artificial silk industry, acquiring the British rights to viscose rayon production and absorbing rivals, notably through the purchase of the Viscose Company in the United States.

Art collecting and philanthropy

Inspired by exhibitions at Roger Fry's circle and a transformative visit to a collection of French modern art, Courtauld began in the 1920s to buy works by Manet, Monet, Renoir, Degas, Cézanne, Seurat, Gauguin, Van Gogh, Matisse, and Modigliani, assembling one of the finest private collections of its kind — including Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère and Seurat's A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte studies. Believing such art should be publicly available and scholarly studied, in 1932 he co-founded, with Lord Lee of Fareham and Sir Robert Witt, the Courtauld Institute of Art, the first institution in Britain devoted to the academic study of art history. He endowed a £50,000 fund for the Tate Gallery to buy modern French works and left his house, Home House in Portman Square, and his collection to the Institute; after his wife Elizabeth's death in 1931 he had already given much of the collection in her memory. The Courtauld Gallery is now housed at Somerset House.
