Third Estate

ContextEstates of the realm in Ancien Régime France
IncludedAll commoners: bourgeoisie, professionals, artisans, workers and peasants
Contrasted withFirst Estate (clergy) and Second Estate (nobility)
Key textAbbé Sieyès, What Is the Third Estate? (1789)

The Third Estate (French: Tiers État) was, in the estate system of Ancien Régime France, the order comprising everyone who belonged neither to the clergy (First Estate) nor to the nobility (Second Estate). It therefore included the vast majority of the population, from wealthy bankers and lawyers to shopkeepers, artisans, labourers and peasants. The category was legally unified by commoner status but socially divided by enormous differences of wealth, education and interest.

The estates system

French society was imagined as a hierarchy of orders with distinct privileges and functions: clergy prayed and maintained religion, nobles served and held honour, and the third produced and paid. In practice privilege meant exemptions and advantages—especially partial relief from the taille and other taxes—while commoners bore much of the fiscal load and faced seigneurial dues, guild rules and legal disabilities. Some commoners purchased offices that could ennoble families over generations, blurring the boundary without abolishing it.

The Estates-General, summoned rarely and last before the Revolution in 1614, represented the three orders separately. Representation by order meant that the First and Second Estates could combine to outvote the Third even though the Third represented nearly the whole nation.

1789 and Sieyès

When Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General for 1789, the Third Estate’s deputies—mostly prosperous lawyers, officials and men of letters rather than peasants—demanded voting by head and a doubling of their number. The abbé Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès gave the demand a revolutionary theory in What Is the Third Estate?: “What is the Third Estate? Everything. What has it been hitherto in the political order? Nothing. What does it desire to be? Something.” Sieyès argued that the Third Estate alone constituted the nation because it performed the productive and public functions necessary to society, while privileged orders were a burden outside the common law.

National Assembly

Deadlock over credentials and voting led the Third Estate’s deputies, joined by some clergy and nobles, to declare themselves the National Assembly on 17 June 1789. After the Tennis Court Oath they vowed not to disperse until France had a constitution. Royal recognition followed, and in August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The legal order of estates was destroyed, though social inequality and conflict over who spoke for “the nation” continued through the Revolution.

Legacy

“Third Estate” remains a shorthand for the politically excluded majority whose labour sustains a privileged order. Later uses, including analogies to the press or to colonized peoples, borrow the drama of 1789: a category defined as nothing claims to be everything. Historians caution that the Third Estate was not a single class and that its deputies’ interests were not identical with those of peasants or the urban poor, but the transformation of an estate into a nation remains a founding gesture of modern democratic politics.
