Edvard Beneš, second president of Czechoslovakia

Edvard Beneš (Czech: [ˈɛdvard ˈbɛnɛʃ]; 28 May 1884 – 3 September 1948) was a Czech politician and statesman who served as the president of Czechoslovakia from 1935 to 1938 and again from 1945 to 1948. With Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk and Milan Rastislav Štefánik he was a principal founder of independent Czechoslovakia in 1918, and as its foreign minister for seventeen years he built the Little Entente alliance system and became a leading figure of the League of Nations.

Early life and independence movement

Beneš was born the youngest of ten children of a peasant family in Kožlany, Bohemia. He studied in Prague, Paris and Dijon, earning a doctorate in law in 1908 and lecturing in sociology at Charles University. During the First World War he organised the Czech resistance abroad (the "Maffie"), co-founding the Czechoslovak National Council in Paris in 1916, negotiating international recognition of a future Czechoslovak state, and organising the Czechoslovak Legions. At the Paris Peace Conference he presented Czechoslovakia's claims, and the new republic was proclaimed on 28 October 1918 with Beneš as foreign minister — an office he held continuously until 1935.

Foreign minister and president

As foreign minister Beneš created the Little Entente (Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Romania, 1920–21) against Hungarian revisionism, served as president of the League of Nations Assembly and rapporteur in numerous disputes, and aligned his country with France and, from 1935, the Soviet Union. He succeeded Masaryk as president in December 1935. In the Munich crisis of September 1938, confronted by the Munich Agreement — by which Britain and France accepted the German annexation of the Sudetenland "in the name of peace" — Beneš chose not to fight Germany alone and resigned on 5 October 1938, going into exile in London and then the United States, where he taught at the University of Chicago.

Exile and return

After the outbreak of the Second World War Beneš led the Czechoslovak government-in-exile in London, recognised by the Allies in 1941; his government repudiated the Munich Agreement (which Britain and France formally renounced in 1942), maintained links with the home resistance, and in December 1943 signed a treaty of friendship with the USSR in Moscow. Returning in 1945 as president of the restored republic, he issued the controversial Beneš decrees, which stripped citizenship and property from most ethnic Germans and Hungarians, underpinning the expulsion of some three million Sudeten Germans — decrees that remain politically sensitive in Czech-German relations. In the February 1948 communist coup, Beneš accepted the Gottwald government's resignation demands rather than call elections or risk civil war; he resigned the presidency on 7 June 1948 and died three months later at Sezimovo Ústí.

Legacy

Beneš is a contested figure: honoured as a founder of Czechoslovak democracy and a prophet of appeasement's folly, criticised for the 1938 capitulation without resistance and for the postwar expulsions. A Czech law of 2004 declared that "Edvard Beneš contributed to the state". His statue stands outside the Czech foreign ministry in Prague.
