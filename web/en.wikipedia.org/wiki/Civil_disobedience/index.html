Civil disobedience

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil disobedience is the active, professed refusal of a citizen to obey certain laws, demands, orders or commands of a government (or any other authority) as a moral or political protest — characteristically nonviolent, public, conscientious and accepting of the legal consequences, distinguishing it from ordinary crime, riot and revolution. The term was popularised by Henry David Thoreau's 1849 essay "Resistance to Civil Government" (published posthumously as "Civil Disobedience"), written after a night in jail for refusing a poll tax in protest against slavery and the Mexican–American War.

Practice and theory

The method's masters made it a strategy: Gandhi's salt march (1930) and the Indian independence movement; the American civil rights movement's sit-ins, freedom rides and Birmingham campaign (King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail", 1963, the tradition's classic apologia — one who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly and with willingness to accept the penalty); anti-apartheid, anti-nuclear, suffragette and climate movements (from Greenpeace to Extinction Rebellion's mass arrests). Philosophers systematise the conditions of justification — Rawls: a public, nonviolent, conscientious act contrary to law, addressed to the majority's sense of justice within a nearly just society; critics and later theories relax or contest each condition (covert, evasive, and even disruptive-violence-adjacent variants defended by some contemporary theorists). Its legal treatment is uniformly unwelcoming (motivation rarely a defence; occasional jury nullification and sentence mitigation the exceptions) — the tension being the point: civil disobedience appeals over the law's head to the community's professed principles.
