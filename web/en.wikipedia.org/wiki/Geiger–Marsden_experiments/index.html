The Geiger–Marsden experiments (also called the Rutherford gold foil experiments) were a landmark series of experiments by which scientists learned that every atom has a nucleus where all of its positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated. They deduced this after measuring how an alpha particle beam is scattered when it strikes a thin metal foil.

Background

The prevailing model of the atom in 1909 was J. J. Thomson's "plum pudding model", in which electrons were embedded in a diffuse sphere of positive charge. Such a structure could only deflect alpha particles by tiny angles. At Manchester, Ernest Rutherford's assistants Hans Geiger and the undergraduate Ernest Marsden were set to study alpha scattering from metal foils using a scintillation screen.

The experiments

Most alpha particles passed through gold foil almost undeflected, as expected — but about 1 in 8,000 was scattered through more than 90 degrees, some bouncing almost straight back. Rutherford later recalled: "It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it rebounded and hit you."

Interpretation

In 1911 Rutherford published his analysis: the large deflections required the atom's positive charge and most of its mass to be concentrated in a minute central nucleus, with electrons orbiting a mostly empty atom. Geiger and Marsden's 1913 measurements of the angular distribution confirmed Rutherford's scattering formula. The resulting Rutherford model was soon refined by Niels Bohr into the Bohr model, founding modern atomic physics.
