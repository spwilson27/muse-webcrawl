Gravitational microlensing

A characteristic microlensing light curve: a smooth, symmetric brightening.

Gravitational microlensing is an astronomical phenomenon in which the light from a distant star or quasar is bent and magnified by the gravity of an intermediate object — a star, planet, or compact dark object — passing almost directly in front of it. It is a special case of gravitational lensing predicted by Einstein's theory of general relativity, in which the separation between the multiple images produced is too small to be resolved by telescopes, so only a temporary brightening of the source is observed.

How it works

As the lensing object crosses the line of sight to the background source, the source's apparent brightness rises and falls in a smooth, symmetric, achromatic light curve whose timescale — typically days to months — depends on the lens's mass, distance and relative velocity. The characteristic scale is the Einstein radius of the lens. If the lens is a star hosting a planet, the planet can produce a brief additional spike or dip in the light curve, revealing its presence even though the planet itself emits almost no light.

Applications

Microlensing surveys such as OGLE, MOA and KMTNet monitor hundreds of millions of stars toward the Galactic bulge. The technique is one of the principal methods for detecting exoplanets that are cold, distant from their host stars, or free-floating, and it is sensitive to planets at distances and masses inaccessible to the transit and radial-velocity methods. Microlensing has also been used to search for dark matter in the form of massive compact halo objects (MACHOs); surveys in the 1990s showed such objects cannot make up the bulk of the Galactic dark matter halo. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is expected to conduct a large microlensing survey that will complete the statistical census of exoplanets begun by Kepler.
