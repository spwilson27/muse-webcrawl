Track gauge

Multi-gauge track allowing trains of two gauges to share a line.

In rail transport, track gauge (also called rail gauge) is the distance between the inner sides of the two load-bearing rails of a railway track. It determines which rolling stock can use a line and is a fundamental parameter of railway design.

Standard gauge

The most common gauge worldwide is standard gauge, 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in), used on about 55–60% of the world's railways, including nearly all high-speed lines. It derives from the gauge used by George Stephenson on the early British railways.

Other gauges

Broad gauges include Russian gauge (1,520 mm), Iberian gauge (1,668 mm), and Indian gauge (1,676 mm), the widest in widespread use. Narrow gauges include Cape gauge (1,067 mm), common in Japan and southern Africa, and metre gauge (1,000 mm). Where networks of different gauges meet, a break of gauge forces transshipment, bogie exchange, or the use of variable-gauge axles or multi-gauge track.

History

Early railways used many local gauges; Britain's Gauge Act of 1846 standardised 1,435 mm in Great Britain, while other countries standardised later or retained distinct gauges for strategic or geographic reasons. See also standard-gauge railway and break of gauge.
