Cursive script (East Asia)

The cursive script (simplified Chinese: 草书; traditional Chinese: 草書; pinyin: cǎoshū), often translated as "grass script", is a style of Chinese calligraphy and handwriting notable for its speed, abbreviation, and flowing strokes. Characters are written with strokes merged, omitted, or radically simplified, making the script fast to write but difficult to read for the untrained.

History

Cursive forms began as rapid, informal simplifications of the clerical script during the Han dynasty. By the Tang dynasty, masters such as Zhang Xu and Huaisu developed "wild cursive" (狂草, kuángcǎo), an expressive style prized as high art, in which characters dissolve into sweeping, gestural lines. Alongside the fully cursive style, a semi-cursive or "running script" (行書, xíngshū) balanced legibility with speed and became the standard style of everyday handwriting.

Features

  • Strokes are linked and the brush rarely leaves the paper within a character.
  • Complex components are replaced by simple conventional abbreviations.
  • Character shapes vary with the writer's hand, following established cursive conventions.

Influence

Cursive Chinese forms influenced the development of Japanese kana: hiragana derives from cursive writing of whole characters, and katakana from character fragments. Simplified Chinese characters of the twentieth century adopted many long-established cursive abbreviations. In Japan the cursive style is called sōsho, and in Korea choseo.
