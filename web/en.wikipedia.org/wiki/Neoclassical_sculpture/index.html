Neoclassical sculpture

Neoclassical sculpture was the dominant style of Western sculpture between about 1750 and 1830, part of the broader Neoclassical movement inspired by the rediscovery of ancient Greek and Roman art and by Enlightenment ideals of reason, clarity and civic virtue.

Characteristics

Reacting against the drama and ornament of the Baroque and Rococo, neoclassical sculptors favoured smooth white marble, restrained emotion, balanced compositions and subjects drawn from classical mythology and history. The excavations at Pompeii and Herculaneum and the writings of Johann Joachim Winckelmann fuelled enthusiasm for antiquity.

Major sculptors

The leading figure was the Italian Antonio Canova (1757–1822), famed for works such as Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss, The Three Graces and his tomb monuments. Others included the Dane Bertel Thorvaldsen, Canova's great rival in Rome; Jean-Antoine Houdon, celebrated for portraits of Voltaire and Washington; John Flaxman in England; and, in the United States, Hiram Powers and Thomas Crawford. Neoclassical taste shaped public monuments and funerary sculpture across Europe and the Americas well into the 19th century.
