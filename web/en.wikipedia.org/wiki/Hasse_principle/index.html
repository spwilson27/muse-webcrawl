Hasse principle

In mathematics, the Hasse principle, also called the local–global principle, states that an equation has a solution in integers or rational numbers if and only if it has solutions in the real numbers and in the p-adic numbers for every prime p. The principle is named after Helmut Hasse, who proved it for quadratic forms; it holds for some classes of equations but fails in general, and its failures are a central subject of modern arithmetic geometry.

Formulation

For a Diophantine equation over the rationals, solvability over the rationals can often be tested locally: the equation must be soluble over the real numbers and over the p-adic numbers Qp for all primes p. The Hasse principle holds for a given class of equations when this local test is also sufficient.

Where it holds

The Hasse–Minkowski theorem states that the principle holds for quadratic forms over number fields: a quadratic form represents zero over a number field if and only if it does so over every completion. The principle also holds for conics (genus 0 curves), and more generally for forms of low degree in many variables, by the Hasse bound and related results.

Counterexamples

The principle fails already for cubic curves. The best-known counterexample, due to Selmer, is the curve 3x4 + 4y4 + 5z4 = 0, which has nontrivial points everywhere locally but no nontrivial rational point. Failures for elliptic curves are measured by the Tate–Shafarevich group, and the general obstruction is captured by the Brauer–Manin obstruction.
