The analytic–synthetic distinction is a distinction in philosophy between two kinds of propositions. Analytic propositions are true solely by virtue of the meanings of their terms — for example, "All bachelors are unmarried", where the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject. Synthetic propositions are true by how their meaning relates to the world — for example, "All bachelors are unhappy", which can only be verified by experience.

History

The distinction received its classic statement in Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781/1787), where Kant combined it with the a priori/a posteriori distinction and provocatively argued that mathematics and the foundations of natural science contain synthetic a priori judgements. The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle later equated the analytic with the a priori and treated all meaningful synthetic claims as empirical.

Quine's critique

In "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), W. V. O. Quine attacked the distinction, arguing that attempts to define analyticity in terms of synonymy, definition or semantic rules are circular, and that confirmation applies to our whole web of belief rather than to individual sentences. The paper was one of the most influential in twentieth-century philosophy; defenders of the distinction, such as Paul Grice and Peter Strawson, replied that Quine's standards for a legitimate distinction were unreasonably high.
