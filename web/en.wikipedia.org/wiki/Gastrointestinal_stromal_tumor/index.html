Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract. GISTs arise in the interstitial cells of Cajal (the gut's pacemaker cells) or their precursors, and are defined in most cases by activating mutations in the KIT gene (encoding the CD117 receptor tyrosine kinase) or, less often, PDGFRA. They occur most often in the stomach and small intestine, in adults typically aged 50–70, with an incidence of roughly 10–15 cases per million people per year.

Presentation and diagnosis

Many GISTs are found incidentally; symptoms, when present, include gastrointestinal bleeding, anaemia, abdominal pain, a palpable mass, or obstruction. Diagnosis rests on imaging (CT, endoscopy/endoscopic ultrasound) and biopsy with immunohistochemistry — almost all GISTs stain positive for CD117 (KIT) and DOG1. Risk of malignant behaviour is stratified by tumour size, mitotic rate and site: even small tumours have some malignant potential, so GISTs are not classified as simply benign or malignant.

Treatment

Localised disease is treated by complete surgical resection. The great advance came with targeted therapy: the tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib (approved for GIST in 2002) transformed a chemo-resistant cancer with poor prognosis into a controllable disease for most patients, and is used both for metastatic disease and as adjuvant therapy after surgery in higher-risk cases. Later-line agents include sunitinib, regorafenib, ripretinib and avapritinib (the latter especially for PDGFRA D842V-mutant tumours, which resist imatinib). A minority of GISTs are "wild-type", including SDH-deficient tumours associated with Carney triad and neurofibromatosis type 1.

History

GISTs were once lumped with smooth-muscle tumours (leiomyosarcomas). The entity was delineated in the 1980s–90s; the discovery of KIT mutations by Hirota and colleagues in 1998 and the dramatic responses to imatinib made GIST a paradigm of molecularly targeted cancer therapy.
