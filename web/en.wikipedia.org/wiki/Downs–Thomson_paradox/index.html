Downs–Thomson paradox

The Downs–Thomson paradox (also known as the Pigou–Knight–Downs paradox) states that the equilibrium speed of car traffic on a road network is determined by the average door-to-door speed of equivalent journeys taken by public transport. It follows that increasing road capacity can actually make traffic congestion worse, because as road travel becomes faster, travellers shift from public transport to cars; falling transit ridership reduces service frequency or revenue, making transit slower or worse, until a new, worse equilibrium is reached. The result was derived independently by Anthony Downs (1962) and J. M. Thomson (1977), building on the work of A. C. Pigou and Frank Knight.

Explanation

Under a user equilibrium, commuters distribute themselves between modes so that no one can improve their journey time by switching. If transit service quality depends on ridership — through fare revenue and frequency — then road expansion drains transit passengers, degrading transit until the two modes again equalise, possibly at a slower overall speed than before. Conversely, the paradox implies that improving public transport, or constraining road capacity, can raise equilibrium speeds for all travellers; it is closely related to Braess's paradox and to induced demand.

Implications

The paradox is cited in debates over road building, congestion charging, and transit investment: evidence from cities such as London suggests that road-space reallocation need not worsen car travel times when accompanied by transit improvements.
