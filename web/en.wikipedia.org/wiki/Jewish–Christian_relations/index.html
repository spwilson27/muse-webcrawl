Jewish–Christian relations

Jewish–Christian relations describe the interaction between Judaism and Christianity from the first century to the present. Christianity emerged from Second Temple Judaism, and the two faiths share scriptures, figures and ethical traditions, but their relationship has been marked by centuries of theological dispute, persecution and, in the modern era, sustained efforts at dialogue and reconciliation.

Judaism and Christianity share roots in the Hebrew Bible, but developed into distinct traditions.

Origins and separation

Early Christianity grew out of a Jewish movement centred on Jesus of Nazareth. The two communities gradually separated over questions such as the identity of the Messiah, observance of Jewish law, and the inclusion of Gentiles. By the second century, Christian writers such as Justin Martyr were arguing that the Church had superseded Israel, a theology later known as supersessionism, while rabbinic Judaism consolidated around the Mishnah and Talmud.

Medieval and early modern periods

In medieval Europe, Jews lived under Christian rule as a protected but restricted minority. They faced expulsions, forced disputations, blood libel accusations and periodic violence, notably during the Crusades. At the same time, Jewish and Christian scholars interacted in philosophy, medicine and biblical translation, and in the Islamic world both communities lived under Muslim rule.

Modern dialogue

The Holocaust prompted a profound reassessment. The Catholic Church's 1965 declaration Nostra aetate repudiated the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the death of Jesus and condemned antisemitism, and similar statements followed from Protestant bodies. Since then, Jewish–Christian dialogue has expanded through interfaith organisations, shared study of scripture, and joint social action, though theological differences over Jesus, covenant and mission remain.
