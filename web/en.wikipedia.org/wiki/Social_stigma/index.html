Social stigma

Social stigma is the disapproval of, or discrimination against, an individual or group based on perceived characteristics that distinguish them from other members of a society. The modern sociological concept derives from Erving Goffman's Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (1963), which analysed how people with an "undesired differentness" — physical deformity, illness, addiction, criminal records, mental illness or membership of a devalued group — manage information about themselves in everyday interaction. The word comes from the Greek for a mark branded or cut into the skin of slaves and criminals.

Theory

Goffman distinguished the discredited, whose stigma is visible or known, from the discreditable, whose stigma can be concealed and who must manage disclosure. Later theorists, notably Link and Phelan (2001), modelled stigma as the convergence of labelling, stereotyping, separation ("us" vs "them"), status loss and discrimination, occurring in a situation where power allows these processes to unfold. Researchers also distinguish public stigma (society's reaction), self-stigma or internalised stigma (the target's acceptance of devaluation, reducing self-esteem and help-seeking), structural stigma (laws and institutional policies that disadvantage a group), and courtesy stigma affecting associates of the stigmatised person.

Effects and reduction

Stigma is a major barrier in public health: it deters testing and treatment for HIV, mental illness, tuberculosis, epilepsy and substance use disorders. Anti-stigma strategies include protest, education to replace myths with facts, and interpersonal contact with members of the stigmatised group — contact-based approaches generally showing the strongest evidence of effect.
