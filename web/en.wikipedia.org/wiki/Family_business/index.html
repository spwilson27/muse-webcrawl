Family business
Type: Form of business ownership
Definition: Commercial organisation in which decision-making is influenced by multiple generations of a family
Examples: Walmart (Walton family), Samsung (Lee family), Ford Motor Company (Ford family)
Members of a founding family outside their multi-generational family firm, among the most common form of business organisation worldwide.

A family business is a commercial organisation in which decision-making is influenced by multiple generations of a family, related by blood, marriage, or adoption, who have both the ability to influence the vision of the business and the willingness to use that ability to pursue distinctive goals. Family businesses are the oldest and most common model of economic organisation: by most estimates they account for the majority of enterprises worldwide and a large share of global employment and GDP.

Characteristics

Family firms range from small local businesses to some of the world's largest corporations, including Walmart, Samsung, Volkswagen, and Ford. They are often characterised by long time horizons, strong organisational identity and values, concentrated ownership, and the overlap of family, ownership, and management roles — an overlap modelled in the academic literature as the "three-circle model" of family business systems. This overlap can be a source of trust, commitment, and patient capital, but also of conflict when family and business interests diverge.

Succession

The transfer of leadership and ownership between generations is the central challenge of family business. Commonly cited research suggests that only about 30 per cent of family businesses survive into the second generation, around 12 per cent into the third, and roughly 3 per cent into the fourth — a pattern captured in the saying "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations". Formal governance mechanisms — family constitutions, councils, shareholders' agreements, and professional boards — are used to manage succession and the employment of family members, and to guard against nepotism and sibling rivalry undermining the firm.
