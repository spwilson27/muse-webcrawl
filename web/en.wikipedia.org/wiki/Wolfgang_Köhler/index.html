Wolfgang Köhler (21 January 1887 – 11 June 1967) was a German psychologist and phenomenologist who, like Max Wertheimer and Kurt Koffka, contributed to the creation of Gestalt psychology. He is best known for his research on insight learning in chimpanzees, reported in The Mentality of Apes (1917/1925), and for his defence of Gestalt theory against behaviourism and introspectionism.

Biography

Köhler was born in Reval (Tallinn) in the Russian Empire to a German family and raised in Germany. He studied at the universities of Tübingen, Bonn and Berlin, earning his PhD in 1909 under Carl Stumpf with a dissertation on psychoacoustics. From 1913 to 1920 he directed the Prussian Academy of Sciences' anthropoid research station on Tenerife in the Canary Islands; stranded there during the First World War, he conducted his famous experiments on problem-solving in chimpanzees.

In 1922 Köhler became professor of psychology and director of the Psychological Institute at the University of Berlin. He co-founded the journal Psychologische Forschung and published the systematic statement of Gestalt theory in Gestalt Psychology (1929). In 1933 he wrote a newspaper article openly criticising the Nazi regime's dismissal of Jewish professors — often described as the last anti-Nazi article published in a German newspaper — and after repeated conflicts with the authorities he emigrated to the United States in 1935.

In America Köhler taught at Swarthmore College, where he remained until his retirement in 1958. He served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1959 and continued to write on the philosophy of mind, including his concept of psychophysical isomorphism and his late work The Task of Gestalt Psychology (published posthumously, 1969).

The Mentality of Apes

On Tenerife, Köhler presented chimpanzees with problems such as obtaining bananas placed out of reach. His star subject, Sultan, solved problems by stacking boxes to climb to the fruit and by joining two sticks together to rake in food. Köhler argued that the animals did not learn by blind trial and error, as behaviourists claimed, but experienced sudden restructuring of the problem — insight (German: Einsicht) — in which the elements of the situation were perceived as a coherent whole, or Gestalt. The work became a classic of comparative psychology and a key challenge to associationist accounts of learning.

Legacy

Köhler's ideas on insight influenced later research on problem-solving and creativity. The "Köhler effect" in social psychology (motivation gains in groups) is named after a different researcher, Otto Köhler. Wolfgang Köhler is also remembered for the bouba/kiki effect: his 1929 observation that people across cultures match rounded and spiky shapes to particular nonsense words, evidence for cross-modal correspondences in perception.
