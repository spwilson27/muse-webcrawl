Lactic acid bacteria


Lactobacilli, a major group of lactic acid bacteria

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are a group of Gram-positive, low-GC, acid-tolerant, generally nonsporulating bacteria that produce lactic acid as the major or sole product of carbohydrate fermentation. They include the genera Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, Streptococcus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus, Enterococcus and others, traditionally grouped in the order Lactobacillales. Homofermentative species convert sugars almost entirely to lactic acid, while heterofermentative species also produce carbon dioxide, ethanol and acetic acid.

Human uses

Lactic acid bacteria have been used for millennia in food fermentation and remain essential to the production of yogurt, cheese, sourdough bread, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, cured sausages and many other foods, where the acid they generate preserves the product and develops flavour. Some strains are marketed as probiotics, and LAB are important members of the healthy human gut and vaginal microbiota, where their acidity suppresses pathogens.

Biology

LAB are aerotolerant anaerobes with limited biosynthetic abilities, so they thrive in nutrient-rich environments such as milk, plant material and the animal gut. Many produce bacteriocins — antimicrobial peptides such as nisin — that are exploited as food preservatives. A few species, notably some streptococci and enterococci, can act as opportunistic pathogens.
