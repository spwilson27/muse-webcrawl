Compounding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Compounding is a term used in several fields, most prominently in finance, where it describes the process by which the value of an investment or debt grows because the earnings on the principal — interest or returns — also earn interest over time. This produces exponential growth rather than linear growth, and is often summarised by the expression "interest on interest." Compounding also refers to practices in pharmacy, where licensed pharmacists combine or alter ingredients to create a medication tailored to the needs of an individual patient.

Compounding in finance

In finance, compound interest is the addition of interest to the principal sum of a loan or deposit, so that interest in subsequent periods is earned on both the original principal and the accumulated interest. The effect was famously praised by Albert Einstein in a widely repeated — though probably apocryphal — quotation calling compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world." The future value of an amount compounded annually is given by FV = P(1 + r)n, where P is the principal, r the interest rate per period, and n the number of periods. More frequent compounding — monthly, daily, or continuous — produces a slightly higher effective annual rate for the same nominal rate.

Compounding works against borrowers in the same way it works for investors: credit card debt, for example, can grow quickly when monthly interest is charged on a balance that itself includes previously accrued interest. The "rule of 72" is a common approximation: dividing 72 by the annual percentage rate gives the approximate number of years required for an investment to double under annual compounding.

Compounding in pharmacy

Pharmaceutical compounding is the creation of a particular pharmaceutical product to fit the unique needs of a patient when a commercially available product does not meet those needs. A physician may prescribe a compounded drug for a patient who needs a specific dosage strength not manufactured commercially, a different dosage form (such as a liquid instead of a tablet), or a medication without a particular allergenic ingredient such as a dye, preservative, or lactose. In the United States, compounding pharmacies are regulated primarily by state boards of pharmacy, with the Food and Drug Administration exercising oversight under sections 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the latter covering "outsourcing facilities" that compound in bulk.
