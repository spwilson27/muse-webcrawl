Visual release hallucinations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Complex visual hallucinations may occur following vision loss.

Visual release hallucinations, also known as Charles Bonnet syndrome, are the experience of complex visual hallucinations in a person with partial or severe blindness. First described by the Swiss naturalist Charles Bonnet in 1760, the condition is characterised by vivid, elaborate hallucinations — often of people, faces, animals, patterns or landscapes — in people who are mentally healthy and typically retain insight that the images are not real.

Causes

The hallucinations are thought to be "release phenomena": when visual input to the brain is reduced or lost through eye disease, the visual cortex becomes spontaneously active, producing images. The most common causes of the underlying vision loss include age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataracts and diabetic retinopathy.

Characteristics

Hallucinations may consist of simple patterns, grids or lattices, or of complex scenes with costumed figures, children or animals. They are purely visual, are not accompanied by sounds, and typically disappear when the person blinks, moves their eyes or looks directly at them. Episodes may last from seconds to much of the day, and can recur over months or years.

Prognosis and management

There is no definitive treatment, but reassurance that the hallucinations are a normal consequence of vision loss — rather than a sign of mental illness — is itself therapeutic for many patients. Eye-movement exercises, changes in lighting, and treatment of the underlying eye condition may help; medications are occasionally used in distressing cases.
