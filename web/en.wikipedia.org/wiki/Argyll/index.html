Argyll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The indented coastline of Argyll is among the longest of any British county.

Argyll (Scottish Gaelic: Earra-Ghàidheal, meaning "coast of the Gaels"), sometimes called Argyllshire, is a historic county and registration county of western Scotland. It lies on Scotland's Atlantic seaboard and contains a deeply indented coastline of sea lochs, peninsulas, and islands, including the southern Hebrides.

Geography

Argyll borders Inverness-shire to the north, Perthshire and Dunbartonshire to the east, and faces Renfrewshire and Ayrshire across the Firth of Clyde. It includes the districts of Kintyre, Knapdale, Cowal, Mid Argyll, Lorn, and Ardnamurchan, and islands such as Mull, Islay, Jura, Tiree, and Lismore. The county town was Inveraray, seat of the Duke of Argyll. The historic county's area is now mostly within the council area of Argyll and Bute, with Ardnamurchan and Morvern in Highland.

History

Argyll was a heartland of the early Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata, whose kings were inaugurated at Dunadd near Kilmartin, an area with one of the richest concentrations of prehistoric monuments in Scotland. The region later fell under the Norse-influenced Lordship of the Isles, and from the later Middle Ages Clan Campbell rose to dominance, its chiefs becoming Earls and then Dukes of Argyll, based at Inveraray Castle. Argyll played a major role in the Wars of the Three Kingdoms and the Jacobite risings; the Glencoe Massacre of 1692 took place just beyond its northern border, while the 1685 Argyll Rising ended with the execution of the 9th Earl.

Economy and culture

Traditional industries include fishing, whisky distilling (notably on Islay and in Campbeltown), slate quarrying, and crofting. Tourism, forestry, fish farming, and renewable energy are now important. The region is strongly associated with Scottish Gaelic culture, though Gaelic speakers today form a minority, concentrated in the islands.
