Pachacuti

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The fitted stone terraces and temples of Machu Picchu, Pachacuti's royal estate]

Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui (r. c. 1438–1471/72), whose name means 'he who overturns space and time' (world-turner), was the ninth Sapa Inca of Cuzco who transformed a modest Andean kingdom into the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu), the largest state in the pre-Columbian Americas. Rising to power when he defended Cuzco against the Chanka invasion his father Viracocha Inca had fled, Pachacuti rebuilt Cuzco as an imperial capital — its plan said to be shaped like a puma, with the great temple of Coricancha and the fortress-temple of Sacsayhuamán begun under him — and launched the conquests his son Topa Inca and grandson Huayna Capac completed from Colombia to Chile.

From kingdom to empire

The Chanka crisis of c. 1438 was the hinge of Inca history: the young prince Cusi Yupanqui held Cuzco (the stones of the battlefield rising to fight beside him, in the myth), seized the throne his father had meant for another son, and took the name Pachacuti. His campaigns subdued the Lake Titicaca kingdoms (Colla and Lupaca), the Wanka and the coastward valleys; his administration — mit'a labour tax, quipu record-keeping, resettlement (mitma), royal estates and the cult of Inti — turned conquest into system.

The imperial city

Pachacuti's Cuzco, rebuilt in the imperial masonry style of fitted polygonal stone, radiated four roads to the empire's four quarters (suyu). His estate at Machu Picchu — his royal retreat in the cloud forest, unknown to the Spanish and thus surviving intact — has become the global emblem of Inca civilisation. Succession practice (his chosen heir Amaru failed as a soldier; Topa Inca, the conqueror of Chimor and Quito, succeeded) and the split-inheritance of royal mummies' estates drove the empire's relentless expansion and its eventual succession crisis under his great-grandsons Huáscar and Atahualpa.
