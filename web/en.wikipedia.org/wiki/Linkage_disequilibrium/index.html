Linkage disequilibrium

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) is a term in population genetics referring to the non-random association of alleles at different loci in a given population. Loci are said to be in linkage disequilibrium when the frequency of association of their different alleles is higher or lower than expected if the loci were independent and randomly associated.

Linkage disequilibrium is influenced by many factors, including selection, the rate of genetic recombination, the mutation rate, genetic drift, the system of mating, population structure, and genetic linkage. A major factor in the pattern of linkage disequilibrium around the genome is the rate of recombination; however, other factors, such as mutation, can also affect it.

Definition

Suppose that among the gametes that are formed in a sexually reproducing population, allele A occurs with frequency pA at one locus, and allele B occurs with frequency pB at another locus. The proportion of gametes that carry both A and B is pAB. If the alleles at the two loci were inherited independently, one would expect pAB to equal pA pB. The deviation from this expectation is quantified by the coefficient of linkage disequilibrium:

D = pAB − pA pB

If the two loci are in linkage equilibrium, then D = 0. Linkage disequilibrium is generally increased by physical proximity, and therefore generally decreases with distance between loci on a chromosome.

Measures

The coefficient D is the most basic measure, but its range depends on allele frequencies. Two widely used alternatives are the normalized measure D′ (Lewontin's D prime) and the correlation coefficient r2, which is preferred in association studies because it relates directly to statistical power. Both range between 0 and 1.

Role in genome-wide association studies

Linkage disequilibrium underlies genome-wide association studies (GWAS): a genotyped marker need not itself be causal if it is in strong LD with a causal variant. The HapMap project and later reference panels catalogued patterns of LD across human populations, enabling the design of genotyping arrays and imputation of untyped variants.
