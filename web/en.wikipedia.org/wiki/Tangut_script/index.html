The Tangut script (Tangut: 𗼇𗟲; Chinese: 西夏文, Xī Xià wén, "Western Xia script") was a logographic writing system devised for the Tangut language of the Western Xia dynasty (1038–1227) in northwestern China. Traditionally attributed to the official Yeli Renrong and promulgated by Emperor Jingzong in 1036, the script was modelled in appearance on Chinese characters but is structurally independent and considerably more complex.

Structure

More than 6,000 characters are attested. Like Chinese characters, Tangut graphs are built from stroke components arranged in a square frame, but they are assembled from a distinct inventory of about 755 components and use a different system of composition, analysed in the native dictionary Homophones and the riddle-like script primer Pearl in the Palm. No Tangut character is a simple pictograph; even the simplest graphs are abstract combinations. Characters are typically much denser than Chinese equivalents, with more strokes on average.

History and decipherment

The script was used for government documents, legal codes such as the Tiansheng Code, translations of Chinese classics and a large Buddhist canon, printed with both woodblock and movable type. After the Mongol destruction of Western Xia in 1227, Tangut continued in use for centuries — the latest known inscription dates to 1502 — before the language and script were forgotten. Recovery began with the excavation of the ruined city of Khara-Khoto by Pyotr Kozlov in 1908–1909, which yielded thousands of manuscripts and the bilingual glossary Pearl in the Palm. Tangutology, the study of the script and language, was pioneered by scholars including Nikolai Nevsky and continues today. The script was encoded in Unicode in 2016 (version 9.0).
