The Berliner Philharmonie is a concert hall in Berlin, Germany, and the home of the Berlin Philharmonic orchestra. Located on the southern edge of the Tiergarten at the Kulturforum, its main hall (Großer Saal) is celebrated both for its distinctive tent-like architecture by Hans Scharoun and for its "vineyard" seating and outstanding acoustics, which have made it a model for concert halls worldwide.

History

The orchestra's original home, the old Philharmonie on Bernburger Straße, was destroyed by bombing in January 1944. After the war the orchestra played in various venues until a competition for a new hall was won by Scharoun's radical design. The hall opened on 15 October 1963 with Herbert von Karajan conducting Beethoven's Ninth Symphony. A smaller Chamber Music Hall (Kammermusiksaal), designed by Edgar Wisniewski after Scharoun's sketches, followed in 1987. A fire during a lunchtime concert in May 2008 caused damage to the roof but the hall reopened within weeks.

Architecture and acoustics

Scharoun placed the orchestra in the centre of the hall, with the audience rising around it in irregular terraced blocks — the "vineyard" style — so that no listener sits far from the platform and the experience is communal rather than hierarchical. The pentagonal hall's convex tent-like roof and the hall's hanging acoustic reflectors ("clouds") produce clarity and warmth of sound. The concept influenced later halls including the Suntory Hall in Tokyo, the Walt Disney Concert Hall in Los Angeles and the Elbphilharmonie in Hamburg.

The main organ, built by Karl Schuke, has over 6,000 pipes and was extensively rebuilt by Gerhard Schmid in the 2010s. Since 2008 the orchestra's Digital Concert Hall has streamed performances from the Philharmonie worldwide.
