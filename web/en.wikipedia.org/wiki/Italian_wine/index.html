Italian wine (Italian: vino italiano) is produced in every region of Italy. Italy is the world's largest wine producer, as well as the country with the widest variety of indigenous grapevine varieties in commercial production. Viticulture on the peninsula is ancient: Greek colonists brought improved vines in the first millennium BCE, and the Romans spread winemaking throughout Europe — Italy was known to the Greeks as Oenotria, "land of wine".

Italy alternates with France as the largest producer and is usually the largest exporter by volume. Some 350 grape varieties are officially authorized for wine production, and estimates of distinct native varieties run to 500 or more.

Classification system

Italy's appellation system, established by law in 1963 and reformed in 1992 and 2010, has four tiers:

  • DOCG (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita) — the top tier, with government tasting and stricter rules; Barolo, Barbaresco, Brunello di Montalcino, and Chianti Classico are examples. There are more than 70 DOCGs.
  • DOC (Denominazione di Origine Controllata) — controlled designations of origin, numbering over 300.
  • IGT (Indicazione Geografica Tipica) — flexible regional wines; the category famously accommodates the "Super Tuscans" such as Sassicaia and Tignanello, which broke appellation rules in pursuit of quality.
  • Vino — generic wine, formerly vino da tavola.

Major regions and wines

Piedmont produces Barolo and Barbaresco from the Nebbiolo grape, plus Barbera and sparkling Asti. Tuscany is the land of Sangiovese: Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino, and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. The Veneto leads in volume with Prosecco (from Glera), Soave, Valpolicella, and Amarone. Sicily and Puglia in the south supply large volumes and rising-quality wines such as Etna Rosso, Nero d'Avola, and Primitivo. Trentino-Alto Adige is known for crisp whites, especially Pinot Grigio, which became one of the world's most popular white wines in the early 21st century.

History

After phylloxera and the economic hardships of the early 20th century, Italian wine rebuilt its reputation from the 1960s onward through the DOC laws, the Super Tuscan revolution, and a quality turn in the south. The 1986 methanol scandal, in which adulterated wine killed 23 people, accelerated reform and export-market repositioning toward premium wines.
