1931 Constitution of Ethiopia

Created16 July 1931 (promulgated)
Author(s)Tekle Hawariat Tekle Maryam and advisers
SignatoriesEmperor Haile Selassie I
PurposeFirst written constitution of the Ethiopian Empire; modernise and centralise imperial government
Superseded by1955 Constitution of Ethiopia

The 1931 Constitution of Ethiopia was the first written constitution in the country's history. It was promulgated by Emperor Haile Selassie I on 16 July 1931, months after his coronation, as the centrepiece of his programme to modernise the Ethiopian Empire and consolidate central government authority. Modelled substantially on the Meiji Constitution of Japan, it remained in force until replaced by the 1955 Constitution.

Background

Haile Selassie had pursued administrative reform since his years as regent (Ras Tafari Makonnen) under Empress Zewditu. He wanted a legal instrument that would fix the imperial succession in his own line, subordinate the great regional lords to the throne, and present Ethiopia abroad as a modern constitutional state — a status seen as protection against colonial encroachment. The drafting was entrusted principally to the intellectual and diplomat Tekle Hawariat Tekle Maryam, who drew on the Japanese constitution of 1889.

Provisions

The constitution comprised seven chapters and 55 articles. Its first article declared the territory of Ethiopia indivisible under the Emperor's authority; the third fixed succession to the throne in the line of Haile Selassie. The Emperor's person was declared "sacred", his dignity inviolable, and his power indisputable: he appointed and dismissed ministers and judges, commanded the armed forces, concluded treaties, and granted pardons.

It created a bicameral Parliament (the Parliament of Ethiopia) consisting of an upper Senate (Yeheg Mamria Meker Beth) composed of members appointed by the Emperor from the nobility, and a lower Chamber of Deputies (Yeheg Mangest Meker Beth) whose members were, until elections could be organised, chosen by the nobility and local chiefs. Parliament could be summoned and dissolved by the Emperor, who sanctioned all laws. The constitution also contained a chapter of rights and duties of the people, but these were framed in general terms and expressly conditioned "within the limits laid down by law".

Significance

Although the document concentrated power in the throne and created a parliament with little real independence, contemporaries and historians regard it as a landmark: the first attempt to define Ethiopian government by written law rather than custom alone. Its modernising ambitions were interrupted by the Italian invasion of 1935–36; Haile Selassie's restoration government after 1941 governed under it until the revised constitution of 1955, drafted for the silver jubilee of his coronation, expanded the franchise and the powers of the elected Chamber.
