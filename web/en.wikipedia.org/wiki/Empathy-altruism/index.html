Empathy-altruism

The empathy-altruism hypothesis is a theory in social psychology, developed principally by C. Daniel Batson, which states that feelings of empathy — sympathy, compassion, and tenderness toward a person in need — can produce genuinely altruistic motivation, whose ultimate goal is to reduce the other person's suffering rather than to benefit oneself.

The hypothesis

The hypothesis challenges psychological egoism, the view that all helping is ultimately self-interested. Batson argued that perceiving another person as in need and adopting their perspective produces empathic concern, which evokes altruistic motivation. By contrast, feeling personal distress produces egoistic motivation to relieve one's own discomfort.

Evidence and experiments

In a series of experiments from the 1980s onward, Batson and colleagues manipulated empathy (for example through perspective-taking instructions or perceived similarity to a person in need) and the ease of escaping the situation. When escape was easy, low-empathy participants tended to leave, but high-empathy participants still helped — a pattern predicted by the altruistic account rather than egoistic alternatives such as aversive-arousal reduction.

Criticism

Critics have proposed egoistic alternatives, including empathy-specific rewards (helping to feel good), empathy-specific punishments (helping to avoid guilt or censure), and negative-state relief. The debate remains an active one, with meta-analyses generally finding a reliable effect of empathic concern on helping behaviour.
