James Ussher (sometimes spelled Usher; 4 January 1581 – 21 March 1656) was the Church of Ireland Archbishop of Armagh and Primate of All Ireland from 1625 to 1656. A prolific scholar and church leader, he is today best known for the Ussher chronology, which calculated from biblical genealogies that the world was created on the evening preceding Sunday, 23 October 4004 BC.

Life

Ussher was born in Dublin and educated at the newly founded Trinity College Dublin, where he became a fellow and professor of theological controversies. Ordained in 1602, he travelled to England to collect patristic manuscripts and became known for his learning in ancient languages, church history, and chronology. He was consecrated Bishop of Meath in 1621 and Archbishop of Armagh in 1625. A Calvinist in theology, he drafted the strongly Reformed Irish Articles of Religion (1615). During the Irish Rebellion of 1641 he fled to England, where he was respected by both royalists and parliamentarians; Oliver Cromwell ordered his funeral in Westminster Abbey.

The chronology

The dating appeared in his Annales Veteris Testamenti (1650) and its continuation (1654). Ussher worked from the Hebrew text of the Old Testament, cross-referencing biblical regnal years with Babylonian, Persian, Greek, and Roman chronology, and fixing Creation at 4004 BC partly through correlations with the death of Nebuchadnezzar II. His dates were printed in the margins of authorized King James Bibles from 1701 onward, giving them enormous popular authority in the English-speaking world — and making the chronology a famous flashpoint in later debates over the age of the Earth and evolution.

Library

Ussher's vast personal library, bequeathed to Trinity College Dublin, included the medieval manuscripts that underpinned his scholarship; his collection helped make Trinity's library one of the most important in Ireland.
