Go (game)


A game of Go in progress on a 19×19 board.

Go is an abstract strategy board game for two players in which the aim is to surround more territory than the opponent. Invented in China more than 2,500 years ago, it is among the oldest board games still played in its original form. The game is known as wéiqí in Chinese, igo in Japanese and baduk in Korean, and is played by millions, mainly in East Asia, with a large professional scene in China, Japan and South Korea.

Rules

The two players, Black and White, alternately place stones of their colour on the empty intersections (points) of a board ruled with a 19×19 grid; smaller 9×9 and 13×13 boards are used for teaching and quick games. Stones of the same colour connected along the lines form a group, whose empty adjacent points are its liberties. When a group's last liberty is filled by the opponent, the group is captured and removed. A player may not play a move that leaves their own group without liberties (suicide, forbidden under most rule sets), nor a move that recreates a previous whole-board position (the ko rule). The game ends when both players pass consecutively; territory surrounded by a player's stones, together with captured stones or captured-stone equivalent under area scoring, determines the winner. White usually receives compensation points (komi, typically 6.5 or 7.5) for playing second. Skilled play revolves around concepts such as life and death, sente and gote, influence, and the balance between territory and thickness.

History and culture

Go originated in ancient China — tradition attributes it to the legendary emperor Yao — and spread to Korea and, by the eighth century, Japan, where it was cultivated at court and later organised into four hereditary Go houses under the Tokugawa shogunate. In the twentieth century the Japanese professional system dominated the game's theory, but South Korea and then China rose to prominence, and international tournaments now attract substantial prize money. Long considered a grand challenge for artificial intelligence because of its enormous search space (estimated at more states than atoms in the observable universe), Go fell to Google DeepMind's AlphaGo, which defeated European champion Fan Hui in 2015 and the Korean master Lee Sedol 4–1 in 2016, influencing the subsequent play even of top professionals.
