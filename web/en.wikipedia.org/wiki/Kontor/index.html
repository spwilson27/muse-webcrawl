A Kontor (German: [kɔnˈtoːɐ̯]; also Contor, from French comptoir, "counter") was a foreign trading post or merchant colony of the Hanseatic League in a non-Hanseatic city. A kontor was a substantial fortified or semi-autonomous enclave with its own wharves, warehouses, church, workshops and dwellings, governed by elected aldermen under its own statutes, and enjoying legal privileges negotiated with the host ruler.

The four great kontors

There were four principal kontors, forming the corners of the League's trading system:

  • Novgorod — the Peterhof, in Russia, source of furs and wax, the oldest kontor (from the 12th century), closed by Ivan III in 1494.
  • Bergen — the Tyskebryggen ("German Wharf", now Bryggen), in Norway, handling stockfish; founded c. 1360, it lasted longest, into the 18th century.
  • London — the Steelyard (Stalhof) on the Thames, trading cloth and wool; its privileges were revoked by Elizabeth I in 1598.
  • Bruges — in Flanders, the great entrepôt for Flemish cloth; as Bruges silted up, trade shifted to Antwerp in the 16th century.

Life in a kontor was tightly regulated: merchants lived communally and celibate (in Bergen, apprentices endured notoriously rough initiation "games"), trade was conducted according to fixed rules, and disputes were judged by the kontor's own court, appealing to Lübeck. Besides the four great kontors, the League maintained smaller trading stations (Faktoreien) and outposts from Iceland to Venice. The word survives in modern German meaning an office or counting house, and in the Kontorhäuser (office buildings) of Hamburg's Kontorhaus district.
