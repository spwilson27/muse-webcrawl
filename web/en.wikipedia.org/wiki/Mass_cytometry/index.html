Mass cytometry

Mass cytometry is a technique for analysing individual cells, commercialised as CyTOF (cytometry by time-of-flight), in which antibodies and other probes are labelled with heavy-metal isotope tags instead of fluorophores, and the labelled cells are read by inductively coupled plasma time-of-flight mass spectrometry.

Method

Cells are stained with antibodies conjugated to polymers carrying distinct isotopes of lanthanides and other metals. The cell suspension is nebulised into an argon plasma, which vaporises and ionises each cell; the resulting cloud of metal ions is measured in a time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Because mass channels overlap far less than fluorescence spectra, mass cytometry can measure more than forty parameters per cell simultaneously, enabling deep profiling of the immune system and other complex cell populations.

Applications and limitations

Mass cytometry is widely used in immunology, oncology, and vaccine research to map cell types and signalling states in blood and tissue. Extensions such as imaging mass cytometry apply the method to tissue sections. Its disadvantages relative to flow cytometry include much lower throughput, the destruction of the analysed cells (so no live-cell sorting is possible), and higher cost.
