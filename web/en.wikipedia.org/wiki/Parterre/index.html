Parterre

A parterre is a formal garden made up of plant beds arranged in a symmetrical pattern on a level substrate, usually consisting of low, tightly clipped hedges, coloured gravel, and flower beds separated by paths. The word comes from the French par terre, meaning "on the ground." Parterres are designed to be admired from above — from the windows of a house or from a raised terrace — rather than walked through at ground level.

History

Parterres developed in France in the late sixteenth century, evolving from the knot gardens of Renaissance gardens. Claude Mollet, gardener to Henri IV, is credited with early designs, and Jacques Boyceau's treatise on gardening (1638) codified the style. The form reached its height under André Le Nôtre, whose gardens at Versailles for Louis XIV featured vast parterres of swirling broderie patterns — scrollwork executed in clipped boxwood, coloured sand, and gravel that imitated embroidery. The style spread across Europe and was widely imitated in England, the Netherlands, Germany, and Austria.

The most elaborate parterres were those of broderie, laborious to maintain because the patterns required constant clipping. Simpler parterres à l'anglaise used shaped lawns. Many grand parterres were swept away in the eighteenth century as the informal English landscape garden came into fashion, but the form was revived in the nineteenth century and remains a feature of formal planting today.

Theatre usage

In theatre architecture, parterre also denotes a seating area: in French theatres the ground-floor area behind the orchestra stalls, and in English usage the rear stalls or "parterre boxes" along the sides. The garden term and the theatre term share the sense of a flat area at ground level.
