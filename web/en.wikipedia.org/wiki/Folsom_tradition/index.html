The Folsom tradition is a Paleo-Indian archaeological tradition that occupied much of central North America, especially the Great Plains, from about 10,800 to 10,200 years ago. The tradition is named after the type site at Folsom, New Mexico, where the first finds were made in 1926–1927.

The Folsom tradition is characterised by the use of Folsom points — finely made projectile points distinguished by a long longitudinal groove, or flute, struck from each face — as well as a wider assemblage of scrapers, gravers and other tools, and is generally associated with the hunting of the ancient bison Bison antiquus.

Discovery

In 1926 the cowboy George McJunkin had earlier noticed large bones in Wild Horse Arroyo near Folsom; excavations in 1926–27 led by Jesse Figgins of the Denver Museum of Natural History recovered a projectile point embedded between the ribs of an extinct bison. The in situ association of a human-made point with an extinct Pleistocene animal provided decisive evidence for the antiquity of humans in the Americas and overturned the then-prevailing view that settlement was only a few thousand years old.

Lithics and subsistence

Folsom points are smaller and more fully fluted than the earlier Clovis points, the flute often extending nearly the full length of the point. The technology was demanding and failure-prone, suggesting skilled, specialised production. Kill sites such as Lindenmeier in Colorado contain the remains of many bison, indicating communal hunting strategies on the Plains as the climate warmed at the end of the Pleistocene.
