Coup d'état

A coup d'état (French for "stroke of state"), often shortened to coup, is the sudden, illegal overthrow of an existing government by a small group, typically military officers or elements within the state itself, as opposed to a revolution, which involves mass popular mobilisation. Coups have been a frequent means of regime change throughout history and remain common in parts of Africa, Asia, and Latin America.

Troops seize a seat of government during a coup.

Characteristics

A coup typically involves the seizure of key points — the head of government's residence, broadcasting stations, airports, and communications hubs — and the arrest or exile of incumbent leaders, followed by a public announcement that a new authority (often a junta) is in control. Because the plotters are few, success depends on speed, secrecy, and the acquiescence of the rest of the armed forces and bureaucracy.

History and patterns

Famous coups include Napoleon Bonaparte's Coup of 18 Brumaire (1799), the 1973 Chilean coup that brought Augusto Pinochet to power, and numerous Cold War-era coups backed by foreign powers. Statistical studies have counted hundreds of coup attempts since 1950, roughly half successful. Political scientists associate coup risk with poverty, previous coups (the "coup trap"), weak institutions, and military grievances. Since the end of the Cold War, international bodies such as the African Union have increasingly refused to recognise governments installed by coups, though enforcement is uneven.

Related terms

A putsch is a failed or attempted coup (e.g., the 1923 Beer Hall Putsch). A self-coup (autogolpe) is when an incumbent leader illegally extends their own power, as Alberto Fujimori did in Peru in 1992. A "palace coup" replaces a leader from within the ruling circle without broader violence.
