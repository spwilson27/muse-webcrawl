Litotes

Litotes is a figure of speech in which understatement is used to emphasise a point by stating a negative, often through a double negative. The speaker affirms something by negating its opposite — for example, saying a person is "not bad" to mean that they are good, or "not uncommon" to mean "common". Litotes is a form of both understatement and irony.

Usage and effect

Litotes can convey modesty, caution, emphasis or dry humour. It is especially characteristic of Old English and Old Norse poetry, where phrases like "that was no easy journey" are frequent in works such as Beowulf. In modern English, familiar examples include "not too shabby", "no small feat", "not insignificant", and "he's no spring chicken".

Examples in literature

Examples include the biblical "a city not forsaken", Chaucer's and Shakespeare's frequent understatements, and Mark Antony's ironic "For Brutus is an honourable man" is sometimes contrasted with litotes. In everyday rhetoric, litotes allows a speaker to praise or criticise indirectly, softening or sharpening the effect depending on context.
