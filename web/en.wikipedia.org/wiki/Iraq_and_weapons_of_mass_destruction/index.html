Iraq and weapons of mass destruction

Iraq and weapons of mass destruction concerns the development, use, and eventual dismantling of Iraqi chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons programmes under the government of Saddam Hussein, and the controversy over Iraq's alleged stockpiles that formed the central public justification for the 2003 invasion of Iraq.

The programmes

During the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), Iraq built extensive chemical weapons capability, producing mustard gas, tabun, sarin, and VX, and used chemical weapons against Iranian troops and against Kurdish civilians, most infamously at Halabja in 1988. Iraq also pursued a crash nuclear weapons programme (halted at an early stage) and biological agents including anthrax and botulinum toxin. Its ballistic missile forces included the al-Hussein, an extended-range Scud variant.

Disarmament, 1991–2003

After the Gulf War, UN Security Council Resolution 687 required Iraq to destroy its WMD under the supervision of UNSCOM inspectors and the IAEA. Inspectors destroyed large quantities of weapons and facilities, but Iraqi obstruction and concealment culminated in the withdrawal of inspectors in 1998 and Operation Desert Fox. Inspections resumed under UNMOVIC and Hans Blix in late 2002; Blix reported cooperation had improved but unresolved disarmament questions remained.

2003 invasion and findings

The United States and United Kingdom invaded Iraq in March 2003 citing active stockpiles and reconstituted programmes, based heavily on intelligence claims — including the "Curveball" source on mobile biological laboratories and disputed reports of uranium purchases from Niger. The post-war Iraq Survey Group's Duelfer Report (2004) concluded that Iraq had destroyed its stockpiles in the 1990s and had no active WMD production at the time of the invasion, though Saddam had retained the intention and some capability to restart programmes once sanctions lapsed. The discrepancy between pre-war claims and findings became a major political scandal in both countries, examined by inquiries including the Butler Review and the Chilcot Report in the UK.
