Diplopia

Diplopia is the simultaneous perception of two images of a single object, commonly known as double vision. The images may be displaced horizontally, vertically, diagonally, or rotationally with respect to each other. Diplopia is a symptom, not a disease in itself, and results from an impairment in the movement of the eyes or in the optical properties of the visual system. It is an important symptom because it can be caused by potentially serious neurological or orbital conditions.

Types

Binocular diplopia is present only when both eyes are open and disappears when either eye is covered; it indicates misalignment of the two eyes, so that the image of an object falls on non-corresponding points of the two retinas. Monocular diplopia persists when one eye is closed and is usually caused by a problem within the affected eye itself, such as an irregular cornea, cataract, or uncorrected astigmatism.

Causes

Binocular diplopia arises from weakness or restriction of the extraocular muscles or their nerve supply — the oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV) and abducens (VI) cranial nerves. Causes include thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis, orbital fractures trapping a muscle, brainstem stroke, aneurysm compressing the oculomotor nerve, multiple sclerosis and decompensated childhood squint. Monocular diplopia is most often due to refractive or media problems in the eye and rarely to cerebral causes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Assessment begins by establishing whether the diplopia is monocular or binocular, then tests eye movements, alignment (cover test), pupil reactions and other neurological signs. Imaging of the brain and orbit is indicated for most new cases of binocular diplopia. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause; temporary measures include patching one eye or using prism lenses to realign the images, and corrective eye-muscle (strabismus) surgery may be needed for persistent cases.
