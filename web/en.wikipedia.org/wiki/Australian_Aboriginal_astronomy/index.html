Australian Aboriginal astronomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Emu in the Sky, formed by dark dust clouds in the Milky Way

Australian Aboriginal astronomy is the name given to the astronomical knowledge and traditions of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples of Australia. Their traditions are among the oldest continuous astronomical cultures in the world: the sky is used for calendars, navigation, law, and the encoding of stories, songlines, and food-gathering rules.

The Emu in the Sky

The best-known Aboriginal "constellation" is not formed from stars but from the dark dust lanes of the Milky Way. The Emu in the Sky stretches from the Coalsack (its head, near the Southern Cross) through Scorpius, and its position through the year signals seasonal activities—for the Kamilaroi and Euahlayi peoples of New South Wales, its appearance in autumn indicates that emus are laying eggs that can be collected, while later in the year its shape changes to show the emu sitting on its nest, and the eggs within are hatched.

Calendars and seasons

Many groups time seasonal change by the heliacal rising of stars and constellations. The Yolngu of Arnhem Land associate the rising of Scorpius with particular winds and foods; Tasmanian and mainland peoples used the Pleiades and other asterisms to mark seasons, ceremonies, and the availability of resources. The Sun and Moon are central ancestral figures in many traditions, and some groups hold sophisticated explanations of eclipses and of the tides' relation to the Moon's phases.

Songlines and navigation

Songlines—routes across the land and sea described in song—map terrestrial journeys onto the sky, so that the stars serve as a mnemonic chart for navigation and law. Torres Strait Islanders, accomplished navigators, used stars such as the Shark (Baizam) and the great canoe of Tagai to navigate and to time planting and fishing.

Rock art and stone arrangements

Some rock engravings and stone arrangements have been argued to encode astronomical alignments, including possible solstice alignments at the Wurdi Youang stone arrangement in Victoria, though interpretations remain debated. The field of archaeoastronomy in Australia has grown rapidly since the 2000s through collaboration between astronomers and Indigenous knowledge holders.
