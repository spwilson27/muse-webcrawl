The BBC Third Programme was a British radio network dedicated to serious culture — classical and contemporary music, drama, poetry, talks, and discussion — broadcast by the BBC from 29 September 1946 until 1967. Conceived by the Director-General William Haley as the apex of a "pyramid" of programmes of ascending cultural demand, it made no concessions to popular taste and acquired a devoted, if small, audience.

Programming

The Third Programme broadcast complete operas, symphony concerts, chamber music, and new works commissioned from contemporary composers. Its drama department produced landmark radio works, most famously Dylan Thomas's "play for voices" Under Milk Wood (first broadcast in 1954) and the English premieres of plays by Samuel Beckett. Talks ranged across philosophy, science, history, and the arts, given by the leading intellectuals of the day.

Decline and replacement

Critics in Parliament and the press attacked the network's small audience and cost. In 1957 its hours were reduced and its frequencies were used in the daytime by the more educational Network Three strand. When the BBC reorganised radio on 30 September 1967, the Third Programme was succeeded by BBC Radio 3, which absorbed its remit of serious music and arts broadcasting; a campaign, the Campaign for Better Broadcasting, was mounted in protest at the feared dilution of its standards.

Legacy

The Third Programme is remembered as a landmark in public-service broadcasting — an attempt to provide an uncompromising national cultural service — and its ethos is generally held to live on in Radio 3.
