Alpine climate

An alpine climate is the characteristic climate of high mountain regions above the tree line, where the dominant control on weather is elevation rather than latitude. In the Köppen classification such climates are grouped as ET (tundra) or, in some schemes, as a separate highland category H.

Characteristics

Air temperature falls with altitude at an average lapse rate of roughly 6.5 °C per kilometre, so alpine zones are cool to cold year-round, with no month averaging above about 10 °C. Weather is famously changeable: strong winds, intense solar and ultraviolet radiation, rapid cloud formation and large day–night temperature swings are typical. Precipitation varies widely — some ranges are very wet, others arid — and much of it falls as snow.

Distribution and ecology

Alpine climates occur in the Alps, Rockies, Andes, Himalayas, the Ethiopian Highlands and other ranges at heights that decrease toward the poles. Vegetation consists of cushion plants, grasses and dwarf shrubs adapted to a short growing season; animals such as the alpine chough, pika and snow vole are similarly specialised. These ecosystems are considered highly sensitive indicators of climate change, as warming pushes species upslope.
