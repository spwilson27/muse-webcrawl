Replacement theology

Replacement theology, also called supersessionism, is the Christian doctrine that the Christian Church has superseded Israel as God's chosen people, and that the New Covenant has replaced the Mosaic covenant. Adherents hold that promises made to Israel in the Hebrew Bible now apply to the Church, and that the ceremonial and national aspects of the old covenant have been fulfilled in Christ.

Forms

Scholars commonly distinguish several forms. Economic supersessionism holds that Israel's role in the plan of salvation was completed with the coming of Christ. Punitive supersessionism teaches that God rejected the Jews as a punishment for rejecting Jesus. Structural supersessionism describes readings of the canon in which the Old Testament is treated as mere background with no independent theological voice.

History

Elements of the doctrine appear in the Epistle to the Hebrews and were developed by Justin Martyr, who called Christians the "true Israel", and by later patristic writers. It remained the mainstream view in most churches for centuries and shaped Christian attitudes toward Jews, with often tragic consequences. After the Holocaust, many churches formally reconsidered the doctrine; the Second Vatican Council's Nostra aetate (1965) rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt, and numerous Protestant bodies have affirmed God's continuing covenant with the Jewish people.

Alternatives

Opposing frameworks include dispensationalism, which distinguishes Israel and the Church as separate peoples of God, and dual-covenant theology, which holds that Jews remain in covenant through the Torah while Christians come to God through Christ. Messianic Jewish movements generally reject replacement theology.
