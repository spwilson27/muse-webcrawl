Reformed Christianity


John Calvin (1509–1564), whose theology shaped the Reformed tradition.

Reformed Christianity, also called Calvinism, is a major branch of Protestantism that began during the 16th-century Reformation. It was originally developed by reformers such as Huldrych Zwingli, Martin Bucer, and Heinrich Bullinger, and was systematised by John Calvin in Geneva. Adherents are distributed worldwide, largely through Presbyterian, Congregational, and continental Reformed churches.

History

The Reformed movement began in Zurich in the 1520s under Zwingli and spread to Basel, Bern, Strasbourg, and Geneva. Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion (first edition 1536) provided its landmark systematic theology. From Geneva and Scotland (under John Knox) the tradition spread to France (the Huguenots), the Netherlands, Hungary, and the English-speaking world, where its adherents included the Puritans.

Theology

Central themes include the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture alone (sola scriptura), salvation by grace through faith, and covenant theology. Calvinism is popularly summarised in the "five points" (TULIP) formulated at the Synod of Dort (1618–19): total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, and perseverance of the saints. Reformed doctrine is expressed in confessions such as the Westminster Confession of Faith, the Belgic Confession, the Heidelberg Catechism, and the Second Helvetic Confession.

Worship and polity

Reformed worship is centred on the preaching of the Word, with the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper; Calvin taught a spiritual real presence of Christ in the Supper, distinct from both transubstantiation and a purely memorial view. Church government ranges from Presbyterian (rule by elders in graded assemblies) to Congregational.
