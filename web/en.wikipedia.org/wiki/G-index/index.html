G-index

The g-index is an author-level metric used in bibliometrics to quantify the scientific productivity and citation impact of a researcher. It was proposed in 2006 by Leo Egghe as an improvement on the h-index, giving more weight to highly cited papers.

Definition

Given a set of articles ranked in decreasing order of the number of citations they received, the g-index is the unique largest number such that the top g articles received together at least g2 citations. Thus a researcher with g-index 10 has at least 10 papers whose combined citations total 100 or more. Because it uses total citations of the top papers, the g-index — unlike the h-index — rewards a few very highly cited articles.

Discussion

The g-index is always at least as large as the h-index for the same publication set. Like other single-number metrics it has been criticized for oversimplifying scholarly impact and for being sensitive to database coverage and self-citation.
