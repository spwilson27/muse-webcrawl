In the United States, a uniform act is a proposed state law drafted by the Uniform Law Commission (ULC), known formally as the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws, and offered to the legislatures of the fifty states, the District of Columbia and U.S. territories for enactment without change, in order to promote consistency of law across jurisdictions.

Process

The Uniform Law Commission, founded in 1892 and composed of commissioners appointed by each state, studies areas of state law where uniformity is desirable, drafts acts through committees of lawyers, judges and academics with public comment, and approves final texts at its annual meeting. A uniform act has no legal force of its own: it becomes law only where a legislature adopts it, and states sometimes enact modified versions (sometimes called "model acts" when substantial local variation is expected).

Notable uniform acts

The most successful is the Uniform Commercial Code (1952), drafted jointly with the American Law Institute and adopted in some form by every state except in part Louisiana. Others include the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act, the Uniform Probate Code, the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act. More than 300 uniform acts have been promulgated since 1892.
