Countervailing duty

A countervailing duty (CVD) is an additional import duty imposed by a country on goods that have benefited from a subsidy in their country of origin. The purpose of a countervailing duty is to offset, or "countervail", the subsidy so that domestic producers can compete on more equal terms. Countervailing duties are one of the principal trade remedies available under international trade law, alongside anti-dumping duties and safeguard measures.

Legal basis

Countervailing duties are governed internationally by the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) Article VI and by the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (the SCM Agreement). Before imposing a countervailing duty, the importing country's investigating authority must normally establish three elements:

  • That a subsidy exists — a financial contribution by a government or public body that confers a benefit and is specific to an enterprise, industry, or region;
  • That the domestic industry producing the like product has suffered material injury, or is threatened with material injury; and
  • That the subsidized imports are the cause of that injury.

Investigations are usually initiated following a petition filed by, or on behalf of, the domestic industry. They typically involve questionnaires, verification visits, and public hearings, and can take a year or longer to complete.

Calculation and duration

The duty imposed is generally set at an amount no greater than the estimated subsidy per unit of the imported product, sometimes expressed as an ad valorem percentage. Countervailing duty orders are normally reviewed periodically and may remain in force for five years at a time, subject to "sunset" reviews that determine whether revocation would be likely to lead to a continuation or recurrence of subsidization and injury.

Practice by jurisdiction

In the United States, countervailing duty investigations are administered by the International Trade Administration of the Department of Commerce, which determines the subsidy margin, and the International Trade Commission, which determines injury. In the European Union, investigations are conducted by the European Commission, and definitive duties are imposed by regulation. Many other WTO members, including India, Canada, and Australia, operate similar regimes.
