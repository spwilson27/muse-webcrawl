Unconscionability

Unconscionability
A doctrine of equity applied to contracts
AreaContract law, equity
FormsProcedural and substantive unconscionability
Key sourcesUCC § 2-302; Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208

Unconscionability (sometimes known as unconscionable dealing/conduct in Australia) is a doctrine in contract law that allows a court to refuse to enforce a contract, or a clause of a contract, that is so one-sided or oppressive that it would be unfair — "against conscience" — to enforce it.

Elements

United States courts generally analyse unconscionability in two parts. Procedural unconscionability concerns defects in the bargaining process, such as unequal bargaining power, fine print, incomprehensible language, lack of meaningful choice, or high-pressure tactics. Substantive unconscionability concerns the terms themselves — prices grossly above market, harsh penalties, one-sided arbitration clauses, or waiver of basic rights. Many jurisdictions apply a "sliding scale": the more substantively unfair the terms, the less procedural unfairness need be shown.

Legal basis

Section 2-302 of the Uniform Commercial Code empowers courts in sale-of-goods cases to refuse enforcement of unconscionable contracts or clauses, and the doctrine is echoed in the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208 and applied more broadly at common law. Foundational cases include Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co. (D.C. Cir. 1965), involving harsh cross-collateralisation terms imposed on a low-income buyer.

Other jurisdictions

Equivalent protections exist elsewhere under other names: Australia recognises unconscionable conduct in statute and equity, English law relies on doctrines such as undue influence, duress and statutory consumer protection, and civil-law systems employ general clauses of good faith and abuse of rights.
