Bonded warehouse

A bonded warehouse, or bond, is a building or other secured area in which dutiable goods may be stored, manipulated, or undergo manufacturing operations without payment of duty. The goods are held under "bond" — a guarantee to the customs authority — and duty becomes payable only when the goods are released into free circulation in the domestic market. If the goods are re-exported, no duty is payable at all.

Operation

Bonded warehouses are licensed and supervised by the customs authority of the country concerned. In the United States, customs bonded warehouses are regulated under the Tariff Act and administered by U.S. Customs and Border Protection, with goods permitted to remain in bond for up to five years. In the European Union the equivalent arrangement is the customs warehousing procedure. Different classes of bonded warehouse exist for general merchandise, bulk liquids, duty-free goods, and goods undergoing processing.

Advantages

Importers use bonded warehouses to defer duty payments, improving cash flow; to store goods until market conditions are favourable; to perform operations such as cleaning, sorting, repacking and labelling; and to hold goods destined for re-export entirely outside the duty system. Bonded warehouses at airports and seaports also supply duty-free shops. A related concept is the foreign-trade zone (free trade zone), which offers similar benefits across a larger designated area.

History

The bonded warehouse system was developed in Britain in the early nineteenth century, notably through the Warehousing Act of 1803 promoted by William Pitt the Younger, allowing imported goods such as tobacco, wine and spirits to be lodged in approved warehouses at the ports — the great dock warehouses of London, Liverpool and Glasgow date from this system — with duty paid only on removal for home consumption.
