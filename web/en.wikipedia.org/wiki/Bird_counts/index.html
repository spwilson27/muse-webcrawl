Bird counts

Volunteer bird counts provide long-term data on bird populations.

Bird counts are structured surveys in which observers tally the birds seen or heard in a defined area over a defined period, providing data on the abundance, distribution and population trends of bird species. Counts range from single-site censuses to continent-wide volunteer programmes involving tens of thousands of participants, and they form one of the oldest and largest bodies of citizen science data in ecology.

Major programmes

The Christmas Bird Count, organized by the National Audubon Society since 1900, is the longest-running bird count: volunteers count all birds within a 15-mile-diameter circle on a single day between mid-December and early January. The North American Breeding Bird Survey, run since 1966, uses roadside point counts during the breeding season and is the principal source of population trend data for North American landbirds. Other major efforts include the British Trust for Ornithology's Breeding Bird Survey, hawk counts at migration watch sites, waterbird censuses, and the Great Backyard Bird Count and eBird's year-round recording.

Methods

Common methods include point counts, in which an observer records birds from a fixed location for a set time; transects walked or driven along a standard route; territory mapping; and complete-list protocols. Modern analyses correct for differences in observer skill, effort and detectability using statistical models, allowing counts to reveal declines such as the net loss of nearly three billion breeding birds in North America since 1970 estimated from survey and radar data.
