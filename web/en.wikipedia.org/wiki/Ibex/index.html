An ibex (plural ibex, ibexes, or ibices) is any of several species of wild goat (genus Capra) distinguished by the male's large recurved horns, which are transversely ridged at the front. Ibex are found in mountainous regions of Europe, Asia, and northeastern Africa.

Species

The principal species include the Alpine ibex (Capra ibex) of the European Alps; the Nubian ibex (C. nubiana) of the Middle East and northeastern Africa; the Siberian ibex (C. sibirica) of Central Asia; the Spanish or Iberian ibex (C. pyrenaica); the walia ibex (C. walie) of Ethiopia; and the wild goat (C. aegagrus), ancestor of the domestic goat. Some authorities also treat the markhor and the East and West Caucasian turs as close relatives within the same genus.

Description and behaviour

Males are larger than females and carry scimitar-shaped horns that can exceed one metre in length in the Alpine species; females have smaller, thinner horns. Ibex are highly agile climbers, able to scale steep rocky slopes and even near-vertical dam walls, where they lick mineral salts from the stone. They feed on grasses, herbs, shrubs, and mosses, moving to lower elevations in winter.

During the rut, males compete by clashing horns. Most species live in herds segregated by sex outside the breeding season. Predators include wolves, lynx, leopards, and eagles, which may take kids.

Conservation

The Alpine ibex was hunted almost to extinction in the 19th century, surviving only in Italy's Gran Paradiso area; reintroductions have restored it across much of the Alps, and all current Alpine populations descend from that single remnant stock. The walia ibex remains endangered, confined to the Simien Mountains of Ethiopia. The Pyrenean ibex, a subspecies of the Iberian ibex, became extinct in 2000 and was briefly "resurrected" by cloning in 2003, the first extinct taxon to be cloned, though the kid survived only minutes.
