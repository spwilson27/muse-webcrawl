Coins of the pound sterling

The coins of the pound sterling are the coins issued for circulation and in proof sets in the United Kingdom. Sterling coinage has a history of more than a thousand years, but the modern decimal series dates from Decimal Day, 15 February 1971, when the old pounds, shillings, and pence system was replaced by a pound divided into 100 new pence.

Current denominations

The circulating denominations are 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, and £2. The copper-plated 1p and 2p coins and the small silver-coloured 5p and 10p coins were reduced in size in the 1990s; the 20p (introduced in 1982) is seven-sided, as is the larger 50p. The original round £1 coin of 1983 was replaced in 2017 by a 12-sided, bimetallic coin with advanced security features, described by the Royal Mint as the most secure coin in the world. The bimetallic £2 coin, first issued for general circulation in 1998, frequently carries commemorative designs.

Since 2008, most denominations have featured Matthew Dent's design in which the reverse designs together compose the Royal Shield of Arms; the obverse bears the portrait of the monarch — Elizabeth II until her death in 2022, and Charles III thereafter, whose portrait notably faces left and is uncrowned.

History

Before decimalisation, the pound was divided into 20 shillings of 12 pence each, with coins including the farthing, halfpenny, penny, threepence, sixpence, shilling, florin, half crown, and crown. The Great Recoinage of 1816 put sterling on a firm footing with gold sovereigns and silver subsidiary coins. The Royal Mint, which produces the coinage, moved from London to Llantrisant in Wales in the late 1960s.

Commemorative and territorial issues

The 50p and £2 coins are regularly issued in commemorative editions marking events such as the Olympic Games and anniversaries of Shakespeare, the NHS, and the Battle of Britain. British Crown Dependencies and Overseas Territories — including Jersey, Guernsey, the Isle of Man, and Gibraltar — issue their own sterling coins at par, which are not generally legal tender in the United Kingdom itself.
