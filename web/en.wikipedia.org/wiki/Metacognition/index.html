Metacognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A student pausing over a notebook, reflecting on her own problem-solving
A student pausing over a notebook, reflecting on her own problem-solving

Metacognition is 'thinking about thinking' — the awareness and regulation of one's own cognitive processes. Coined in its modern sense by John Flavell (1979), it comprises metacognitive knowledge (what one knows about one's memory, comprehension and strategies) and metacognitive regulation (planning, monitoring and evaluating). It is strongly associated with learning outcomes: students who monitor their understanding learn more, and teaching metacognitive strategies is among education research's most robust interventions.

2. Applications

Phenomena

Classic metacognitive experiences include the feeling of knowing, the tip-of-the-tongue state, judgements of learning (often miscalibrated — rereading feels effective but retrieval practice works better) and the Dunning–Kruger effect, in which the incompetent lack the metacognition to see it. Metamemory, metacomprehension and theory of mind are its provinces; deficits appear in schizophrenia (insight), dementia (anosognosia) and normal ageing.

Applications

Self-regulated learning programmes, think-aloud protocols, and 'exam wrappers' operationalise metacognition in classrooms; in AI, metacognition names models' ability to estimate their own uncertainty — a live research problem, since language models' confidence is poorly calibrated.
