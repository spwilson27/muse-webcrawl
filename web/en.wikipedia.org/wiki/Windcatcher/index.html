Windcatcher

A windcatcher, wind tower, or wind scoop (Persian: بادگیر, bâdgir) is a traditional architectural element used to create cross-ventilation and passive cooling in buildings, especially in the hot, arid climates of Iran, the Persian Gulf region, and parts of North Africa and South Asia. Windcatchers have been used for millennia; elaborate examples crown houses and water reservoirs (ab anbars) in desert cities such as Yazd in Iran, which is famous for having the world's tallest windcatcher at the Dowlat Abad Garden.

How it works

The tower rises above the roof with openings at the top facing the prevailing wind (or in several directions, divided internally by partitions). Wind pressure drives fresh air down the shaft into the rooms below, while air on the leeward side is drawn up and out—a combination of wind-driven ventilation and the stack effect as warm air rises. In Iranian houses the air often passes over a qanat-fed pool or fountain in a basement room (sardab), so evaporation cools and humidifies the air before it circulates. When there is no wind, the tower acts as a chimney, venting warm air upward by day, while at night its cooled masonry can radiate heat away and draw air downward.

Regional variants

In Egypt the device is called a malqaf, a sloped rooftop scoop. In the Gulf states, towers with square plans and decorative plasterwork are known as barjeel. Simpler forms—roof vents and unidirectional scoops—are found in Hyderabad in Pakistan (where they are called mangh) and in vernacular buildings around the Mediterranean.

Modern use

Interest in low-energy building design has revived the windcatcher: modern versions with controllable dampers and fans are installed in schools, offices, and stations in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, marketed as passive ventilation terminals. Studies show windcatchers can substantially reduce cooling demand in suitable climates, though performance depends on wind availability, dust, noise, and security considerations.
