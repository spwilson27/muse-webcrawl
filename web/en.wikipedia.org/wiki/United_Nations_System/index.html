United Nations System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations System is the ensemble of organisations established under and associated with the United Nations: the UN itself (the General Assembly, Security Council, ECOSOC, the International Court of Justice, the Secretariat and the Trusteeship Council), the funds and programmes (such as UNDP, UNICEF, UNHCR and the World Food Programme), the specialised agencies (fifteen, including WHO, UNESCO, the IMF and the World Bank), and related organisations (the IAEA, the WTO's special relationship, the ICC). With headquarters in New York and major offices in Geneva, Vienna and Nairobi, the system employs on the order of a hundred thousand staff worldwide across its agencies.

Coordination runs through the Chief Executives Board chaired by the Secretary-General; finance mixes assessed contributions (the UN regular budget, on the order of US$3–4 billion a year in the mid-2020s, and a larger peacekeeping budget) with voluntary funding that dominates the development and humanitarian agencies. The system's coherence — or its fragmentation into autonomous agency baronies, each with its own governing board and funding base — is the perennial reform question, from the 2005 World Summit to the UN80 initiative of the mid-2020s. Its legal personality and immunities rest on the 1946 Convention on Privileges and Immunities.
