Squinch

In architecture, a squinch is a structural element used to support the base of a dome over a square room, by filling the upper corners of the chamber. It has the appearance of a small arch — or a succession of arches, or a niche, or a half-dome — bridging the corner at an angle.

A squinch carries the circular base of a dome across the corner of a square room

History

The squinch was developed in the architecture of the Sasanian Empire in Persia, where it was used in palaces such as that of Ardashir I at Firuzabad (3rd century CE). It spread through Islamic architecture, where squinches were often elaborated with muqarnas (stalactite vaulting), and into Romanesque and Byzantine practice.

Comparison with the pendentive

The squinch is the main alternative to the pendentive, a spherical triangle of masonry used in Byzantine architecture (as at Hagia Sophia) to make the same square-to-circle transition. Whereas pendentives form a continuous curved surface blending into the dome, squinches are distinct arched or corbelled elements at each corner.
