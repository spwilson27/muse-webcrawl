Foreign policy of Japan

The foreign policy of Japan is conducted by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and shaped by the country's postwar constitutional constraints, its alliance with the United States, and its position as one of the world's largest economies. Japan is a member of the United Nations, the G7, the G20, the OECD, APEC, and ASEAN dialogue frameworks.

Postwar foundations

After the Second World War, Japan adopted the 1947 constitution, whose Article 9 renounces war and the maintenance of war potential. Under the so-called Yoshida Doctrine, Japan relied on the U.S. security umbrella — formalized in the Treaty of San Francisco and the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty of 1951, revised in 1960 — while concentrating on economic recovery. Japan joined the United Nations in 1956 and became a leading donor of official development assistance.

Key relationships

The alliance with the United States remains the cornerstone of Japanese foreign policy, with around 50,000 U.S. personnel stationed in Japan. Relations with China combine deep economic interdependence with territorial disputes over the Senkaku (Diaoyu) Islands and historical grievances. Japan also manages disputes with South Korea over Takeshima (Dokdo) and with Russia over the Northern Territories (Southern Kurils), which have prevented a formal peace treaty since 1945.

Recent directions

Since the 2010s Japan has promoted a "Free and Open Indo-Pacific" vision, deepened security cooperation through the Quad with the United States, Australia, and India, reinterpreted its constitution to permit collective self-defence (2015 security legislation), and announced major increases in defence spending. It has also championed trade agreements such as the CPTPP after the U.S. withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership.
