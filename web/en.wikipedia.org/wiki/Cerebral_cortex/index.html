The cerebral cortex, also known as the cerebral mantle, is the outer layer of grey matter of the cerebrum in mammals, including humans. It plays a key role in attention, perception, awareness, thought, memory, language, and consciousness. The human cerebral cortex is 2–4 mm thick, contains about 16 billion neurons, and is folded into ridges (gyri) and furrows (sulci) that greatly increase its surface area.

Structure

Most of the cortex is neocortex, organised into six horizontal layers distinguished by cell type and connections; older regions (allocortex), such as the hippocampus, have three or four layers. The cortical sheet is divided into two hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum, and each hemisphere into four main lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital. Korbinian Brodmann's 1909 map divided the cortex into 52 numbered areas based on cellular architecture, many of which correspond to distinct functions.

Function

Specific regions receive sensory input (primary visual, auditory, and somatosensory areas), command movement (the primary motor cortex of the frontal lobe), and support higher cognition: the prefrontal cortex for planning and decision-making, temporal areas including Wernicke's area for language comprehension, and the parietal cortex for spatial attention. Association areas integrate information across modalities, and long-range white-matter tracts beneath the cortex connect its regions into large-scale networks.

Development and evolution

The cortex develops from the embryonic telencephalon through the migration of neurons along radial glia. Its expansion and folding are most pronounced in primates, and the disproportionate growth of association cortex is considered central to human cognitive abilities. Disorders involving the cortex include epilepsy, Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, and developmental conditions affecting cortical migration.
