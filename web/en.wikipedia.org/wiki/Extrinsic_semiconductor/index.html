Extrinsic semiconductor

An extrinsic semiconductor is a semiconductor that has been doped — that is, into which chemical impurities have been deliberately introduced to change its electrical properties. In contrast, a pure, undoped semiconductor is called an intrinsic semiconductor. Doping adds either donor atoms, creating an n-type semiconductor with electrons as majority carriers, or acceptor atoms, creating a p-type semiconductor with holes as majority carriers.

n-type doping

Adding pentavalent impurities such as phosphorus, arsenic, or antimony to silicon introduces extra electrons that are only weakly bound, raising the electron concentration and the Fermi level toward the conduction band.

p-type doping

Adding trivalent impurities such as boron, aluminium, or gallium creates vacancies (holes) that accept electrons, so holes become the majority carriers and the Fermi level moves toward the valence band.

Applications

Joining n-type and p-type regions forms a p–n junction, the basis of diodes, transistors, solar cells, and integrated circuits. The degree of doping also controls resistivity over many orders of magnitude and determines whether the material behaves as a semiconductor or almost as a conductor (degenerate doping).
