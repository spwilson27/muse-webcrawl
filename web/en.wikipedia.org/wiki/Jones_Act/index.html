Jones Act

This article is about the maritime law. For other uses, see Jones Act (disambiguation).

The Merchant Marine Act of 1920 is a United States federal statute that provides for the promotion and maintenance of the American merchant marine. Among other purposes, the law regulates maritime commerce in U.S. waters and between U.S. ports. Section 27 of the act is known as the Jones Act, after its sponsor, Senator Wesley Jones, and deals with cabotage: it requires that goods shipped between U.S. ports be carried on ships that are built in the United States, owned by U.S. citizens, and crewed by U.S. citizens and permanent residents. Similar requirements appear separately for passenger vessels.

Provisions

The Jones Act requires vessels engaged in coastwise trade to be U.S.-built, U.S.-flagged, at least 75% U.S.-owned, and crewed predominantly by U.S. citizens. A separate provision, Section 33, gives injured seamen the right to sue their employers for negligence — a remedy often also referred to as part of "the Jones Act". The President may waive the requirements in the interest of national defense; waivers have been issued after hurricanes and other emergencies.

Support and criticism

Supporters, including the shipbuilding industry and maritime unions, argue that the act sustains a domestic shipbuilding base, a pool of trained mariners for wartime sealift, and maritime jobs. Critics — including many economists and officials in Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Alaska and Guam, which depend on U.S. shipping — argue that the scarcity and high cost of U.S.-built ships raises transport prices and consumer costs in non-contiguous states and territories, and discourages domestic shipping relative to rail and truck. Studies of the act's net economic effect have reached divergent conclusions, and reform proposals are periodically introduced in Congress without repealing the law.
