Coat of arms of Canada

The coat of arms of Canada

Adopted: 21 November 1921 (revised 1994)

Crest: A lion passant guardant Or holding a maple leaf Gules

Shield: Tierced in fess, the first and second divisions containing the quarterly arms of England, Scotland, Ireland and France; the third division Argent three maple leaves conjoined on one stem proper

Supporters: Dexter a lion Or holding the Union Flag; sinister a unicorn Argent armed, crined and unguled Or, holding the banner of royal France

Motto: A Mari Usque Ad Mare ("From Sea to Sea")

The Arms of Canada (French: Armoiries du Canada), also known as the Royal Coat of Arms of Canada or formally as the Arms of His Majesty the King in Right of Canada, is the formal coat of arms of the Canadian monarch and, by extension, of Canada. The arms were proclaimed by King George V on 21 November 1921, following the desire expressed at the time of Confederation in 1867 for a distinctive Canadian emblem.

Design and symbolism

The shield is divided quarterly in its upper portion, bearing the arms of the four founding nations of Canada: England (three gold lions on red), Scotland (a red lion within a double border), Ireland (a gold harp on blue), and France (gold fleurs-de-lis on blue). The lower third of the shield shows three red maple leaves joined on a single stem against a silver (white) field, representing Canada itself and its people.

Above the shield sits the royal helm and mantling in the national colours of red and white, surmounted by a crowned gold lion standing on a wreath, holding a red maple leaf in its right paw — symbolising the sacrifices the country made during the First World War. Below the shield, the motto ribbon bears the Latin phrase A Mari Usque Ad Mare, taken from Psalm 72, meaning "From Sea to Sea". This phrase was also adopted as Canada's national motto and reflects the country's span from the Atlantic to the Pacific — and, since the creation of Nunavut, to the Arctic Ocean as well.

The supporters are the lion of England on the dexter side, carrying the Union Flag, and the unicorn of Scotland on the sinister side, carrying the banner of royal France (three gold fleurs-de-lis on blue). A heraldic compartment of roses, thistles, shamrocks and lilies — the floral emblems of England, Scotland, Ireland and France — appears beneath the motto ribbon.

Evolution

Following the unveiling of the Canadian Red Ensign and the growing distinctiveness of Canadian identity after the First World War, the government sought new arms. A committee was formed in 1919, and the design was approved and proclaimed by royal proclamation in 1921. Minor amendments followed: in 1957 a revised rendering by Alan Beddoe softened the design and changed the maple leaves from green to red; in 1987 the arms were re-registered; and in 1994 an annulus bearing the motto of the Order of Canada, Desiderantes Meliorem Patriam ("They desire a better country"), was added around the shield for use by the federal government.

Use

The arms appear on federal government buildings, official documents, proclamations, Canadian passports, and the one-dollar coin's predecessor designs. They are protected under trademark law and may be used only with authorisation. A stylised version, the "Coat of Arms with flag" rendition, serves as a federal identity symbol.
