The French Academy of Sciences (French: Académie des sciences) is a learned society founded in 1666 by Louis XIV at the suggestion of Jean-Baptiste Colbert, to encourage and protect the spirit of French scientific research. It was at the forefront of scientific development in Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries, and is one of the five academies of the Institut de France.

History

The Academy originated in Colbert's plan to create a general academy. He got together a small group of scholars who met on 22 December 1666 in the King's library, and thereafter held twice-weekly working meetings. Unlike its English counterpart the Royal Society, the Academy was founded as an organ of government: its members were salaried, and it undertook official tasks such as evaluating inventions and standardising measurement — work that fed into the metric system during the Revolution.

The Academy was suppressed in 1793 during the French Revolution, along with the other royal academies, but reconstituted within the Institut de France in 1795. In the 19th and 20th centuries its membership included figures such as Laplace, Lavoisier (before the suppression), Pasteur, Marie Curie — admitted only in 1911 after a contested vote — and Henri Poincaré.

Role and organisation

The Academy today advises the French government on scientific matters, publishes the journal Comptes rendus, awards prizes and medals, and elects its own members, who serve for life. Membership is divided into two broad divisions — mathematical and physical sciences, and chemical, biological and medical sciences — with a section for integrated applications of science. Foreign associates are also elected. Current membership numbers roughly 260 full members plus foreign associates and corresponding members.
