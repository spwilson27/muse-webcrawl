Nudge theory

Nudge theory is a concept in behavioural science and economics proposing that indirect suggestions and positive reinforcement — "nudges" — can influence people's behaviour and decision-making more effectively than mandates, without forbidding any options or significantly changing economic incentives. The approach is organised around choice architecture: how options are presented, ordered and defaulted.

Origins

The theory was popularised by the economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein in their 2008 book Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness. They called the underlying philosophy libertarian paternalism: steer people in directions that improve their lives, as judged by themselves, while preserving freedom of choice. Thaler received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 2017.

Examples

  • Automatic enrolment in pension plans with an opt-out (defaults dramatically raise participation)
  • Placing healthier food at eye level in cafeterias
  • The fly image etched in urinals at Amsterdam's Schiphol Airport to reduce spillage
  • Letters telling taxpayers that most neighbours pay on time (social-norm nudges)
  • Organ-donation defaults (opt-out vs opt-in countries)

Policy adoption and criticism

Governments created dedicated units — the UK's Behavioural Insights Team (2010, "the Nudge Unit") and the US Social and Behavioral Sciences Team — and hundreds of trials have been run worldwide. Critics question the ethics of covert influence, the modest average effect sizes found in meta-analyses, and whether nudges distract from stronger regulation; supporters reply that choice architecture is unavoidable and should simply be designed well.
