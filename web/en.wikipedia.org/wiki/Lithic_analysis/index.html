Lithic analysis

Lithic analysis is the scientific study of stone tools and other chipped stone artefacts in archaeology. Because stone is nearly indestructible, lithics are often the most abundant — and for the Palaeolithic, the only — surviving evidence of early human behaviour, so their analysis is one of the primary means of understanding prehistoric technology, mobility, subsistence and cognition.

Approaches

Typology and classification

Artefacts are sorted into types (handaxes, scrapers, blades, projectile points, and so on) and described by attributes such as raw material, dimensions, retouch and shape. Typological sequences, such as François Bordes's classification of Mousterian tools, were historically used to define archaeological cultures and periods.

Technology and reduction sequences

Analysts reconstruct how tools were made, from raw-material procurement through core reduction, blank production, retouch, use, resharpening and discard. The French concept of the chaîne opératoire (operational sequence), developed by André Leroi-Gourhan, treats each artefact as one stage in a technical process. Refitting flakes back onto cores can reveal knapping gestures and site organisation in fine detail.

Use-wear and residue analysis

Microscopic examination of edges (pioneered by Sergei Semenov) identifies traces of cutting, scraping, polishing or hafting, revealing what materials a tool worked — meat, hide, wood, bone or plants. Residue analysis can detect blood, starch grains and other organic remains surviving on stone surfaces.

Sourcing

Geochemical techniques such as X-ray fluorescence and neutron activation analysis match artefacts to the geological sources of flint, chert or obsidian, reconstructing exchange networks and territories. Obsidian sourcing is particularly precise because each volcanic source has a distinctive chemical fingerprint.

Experimental archaeology

Modern knappers replicate prehistoric techniques — hard-hammer and soft-hammer percussion, pressure flaking, the Levallois method — to understand the skills, choices and constraints involved in stone tool production.

Debates

A famous debate between François Bordes and Lewis Binford concerned whether Mousterian tool assemblages represented distinct cultural traditions or different activities at different sites. Such debates helped shift lithic studies from pure description toward behavioural interpretation.
