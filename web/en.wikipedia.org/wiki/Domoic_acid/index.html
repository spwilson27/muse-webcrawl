Domoic acid

Domoic acid is a kainic acid–type neurotoxin that causes amnesic shellfish poisoning (ASP). It is produced by algae and accumulates in shellfish, sardines, and anchovies. When contaminated animals are consumed by humans, it can cause amnesia or other neurological symptoms, including seizures and, in extreme cases, death.

Occurrence

Domoic acid is a secondary metabolite and amino acid–like compound produced by certain species of the diatom genus Pseudo-nitzschia and by the red alga Chondria armata. During harmful algal blooms, filter-feeding shellfish such as mussels, clams, and scallops accumulate the toxin in their tissues, making them hazardous to eat despite cooking.

History

The toxin first drew wide attention in 1987, when more than 100 people in Prince Edward Island, Canada, became ill after eating cultured mussels contaminated with domoic acid; several elderly patients died and others suffered permanent short-term memory loss, giving the condition its name, amnesic shellfish poisoning. In 1998, domoic acid was identified as the cause of mass deaths of California sea lions that had fed on contaminated anchovies.

Mechanism of action

Domoic acid is a structural analogue of glutamic acid and a potent agonist of kainate receptors in the central nervous system. It overstimulates neurons, particularly in the hippocampus, causing excitotoxic damage. Symptoms include vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, dizziness, confusion, and seizures; severe cases can lead to coma and lasting memory impairment.

Monitoring and regulation

Coastal monitoring programmes measure domoic acid in shellfish meat and close harvesting areas when concentrations exceed regulatory limits, commonly 20 parts per million. Because the toxin is heat-stable, cooking does not make contaminated shellfish safe.
