Navigation light

A navigation light, also known as a running light or position light, is a coloured source of illumination fitted to a vessel or aircraft, standardised internationally so that observers can determine the craft's type, aspect, and direction of travel at night and avoid collisions.

Marine lights

Under the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs), a power-driven vessel underway must show a white masthead light, sidelights — green on the starboard side and red on the port side — and a white stern light. Sailing vessels underway show sidelights and a stern light but no masthead light, and may display an optional red-over-green all-round light at the masthead. Additional combinations signal special conditions: vessels at anchor show one or two white all-round lights; fishing vessels show distinctive red or green over white; vessels towing, dredging, or not under command each have prescribed patterns. The arcs of visibility are fixed by rule — sidelights cover 112.5 degrees, masthead lights 225 degrees, stern lights 135 degrees.

Aviation lights

Aircraft carry a similar scheme adapted from maritime practice: a red light on the left (port) wingtip, green on the right (starboard) wingtip, and white at the tail, supplemented by flashing anti-collision beacons and strobes. From the pattern of steady lights a pilot can judge whether another aircraft is approaching, crossing, or moving away.

History

Standardised coloured lights were adopted for steamships in the mid-nineteenth century as traffic grew; the red-port/green-starboard convention was codified in British regulations of the 1840s–1860s and later harmonised internationally, eventually in the 1972 COLREGs.
