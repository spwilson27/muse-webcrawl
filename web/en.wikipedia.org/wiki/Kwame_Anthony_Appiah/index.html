Kwame Anthony Appiah (born 1954) is a British-Ghanaian philosopher whose work on race, identity, culture and ethics has made him one of the leading public philosophers of his generation. Born in London to the Ghanaian statesman Joe Appiah and the writer Peggy Appiah (daughter of Stafford Cripps), raised in Kumasi, Ghana, and educated at Cambridge, he has taught at Yale, Harvard and Princeton and is professor of philosophy and law at New York University. For several years he wrote The Ethicist column in The New York Times Magazine, answering readers' moral dilemmas.

Race, identity, cosmopolitanism

In My Father's House (1992) examined African and Afro-American intellectual traditions, criticising racial essentialism while taking African philosophy seriously (see African philosophy). Appiah argues that race, though biologically unreal, is socially real through identities that people live by — identities he neither celebrates uncritically nor wishes away, but asks to be held loosely, as scripts each person may revise. His Cosmopolitanism: Ethics in a World of Strangers (2006) defends a "rooted cosmopolitanism": obligations to all humanity combined with legitimate partial loves — we are citizens of the world and of somewhere. The Honor Code (2010) showed how honour, not argument alone, ended duelling, foot-binding and the Atlantic slave trade's respectability; The Lies That Bind (2018) anatomises the essentialisms of creed, country, colour, class and culture.
