Buddy Holly

Charles Hardin Holley (September 7, 1936 – February 3, 1959), known as Buddy Holly, was an American singer, songwriter and musician who was a central and pioneering figure of mid-1950s rock and roll. He was born to a musical family in Lubbock, Texas, during the Great Depression, and learned to play guitar and sing alongside his siblings. Holly's style was influenced by gospel music, country music, rhythm and blues acts, and rock artists.

After opening for Elvis Presley in 1955, Holly began to record with producer Norman Petty in Clovis, New Mexico. He found success in 1957 as the frontman of the Crickets with such songs as "That'll Be the Day", "Peggy Sue", and "Everyday". Holly's records influenced subsequent popular music artists such as the Beatles and the Rolling Stones. He was among the first artists inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, in 1986.

Early life

Buddy Holly was born Charles Hardin Holley on September 7, 1936, in Lubbock, Texas, the fourth child of Lawrence Odell "L.O." Holley and Ella Pauline Drake. During his childhood, Holley was nicknamed "Buddy" by his family. He was influenced by the music of Hank Williams, Jimmie Rodgers, Hank Snow, Bob Wills, and the Carter Family. At age five, Holly won a talent contest singing "My Two-Timin' Woman".

Career

In 1952, Holly formed a duo with his friend Bob Montgomery, and they performed country and western songs on local radio station KDAV. Holly's band performed at the Lubbock Youth Center and obtained a regular spot on KDAV's Sunday Party show. After being spotted by a talent scout, he signed with Decca Records in 1956, where a clerical error spelled his surname "Holly".

Dissatisfied with Decca, Holly began recording at Norman Petty's studio in Clovis, New Mexico, in early 1957. With his band the Crickets, he recorded "That'll Be the Day", which topped the Billboard Best Sellers chart in September 1957. The Crickets' debut album, The "Chirping" Crickets, was released in November 1957. Holly also recorded under his own name with Petty, releasing "Peggy Sue" and "Rave On".

Death

In the early morning hours of February 3, 1959, Holly, Ritchie Valens, and J. P. "The Big Bopper" Richardson were killed in a plane crash near Clear Lake, Iowa, together with pilot Roger Peterson. The event later became known as "The Day the Music Died" after singer-songwriter Don McLean referred to it that way in his 1971 song "American Pie". Holly's pregnant wife, María Elena, learned of his death from a television news report and suffered a miscarriage shortly after.

Legacy

Holly's singing style, his use of two guitars, bass, and drums, and his songwriting influenced many later musicians. The Beatles chose their name partly in homage to the Crickets. Holly's Fender Stratocaster playing and his hiccupping vocal style were widely imitated. In 2004, Rolling Stone ranked Holly number 13 on its list of the 100 Greatest Artists of All Time.
