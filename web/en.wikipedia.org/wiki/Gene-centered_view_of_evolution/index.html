Gene-centered view of evolution

The gene-centered view of evolution (also called the selfish gene theory) is a framework for understanding evolution by natural selection in which genes are treated as the principal units of selection. In this view, organisms are temporary vehicles ("survival machines") constructed by genes to promote their own replication into future generations. Adaptive traits are explained by their effects on the propagation of the genes that influence them, rather than by benefits to the organism or the group.

History

The theory grew out of work on kin selection by R. A. Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, and especially W. D. Hamilton, whose concept of inclusive fitness (1964) explained altruism toward relatives. George C. Williams's Adaptation and Natural Selection (1966) argued against loose group-selection explanations and laid the theoretical groundwork. Richard Dawkins popularized the view in The Selfish Gene (1976), coining the term "meme" for cultural replicators, and developed it further in The Extended Phenotype (1982).

Key ideas

Genes that promote their own survival — whether by improving their bearer's survival, by inducing helpful behavior toward relatives who share copies, or through "green-beard" effects — increase in frequency over generations. The framework readily explains phenomena puzzling at the organism level, such as sterile worker castes in social insects, intragenomic conflict, meiotic drive, and parental care weighted by relatedness. It also underlies the concepts of kin selection and reciprocal altruism as clarified by Robert Trivers.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that the gene's-eye view neglects development, epigenetic inheritance, niche construction, and selection at higher levels; proponents respond that gene selectionism is a bookkeeping perspective compatible with multi-level selection when properly formulated. Philosophers such as Kim Sterelny and Elliott Sober have debated its explanatory scope, while the extended evolutionary synthesis movement seeks to broaden the standard framework.
