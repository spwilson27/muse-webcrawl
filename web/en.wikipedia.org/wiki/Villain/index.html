A villain (also known as the "bad guy", "blackguard", or "evil-doer") is a stock character, whether based on a historical narrative or a work of fiction, that acts as an evil character and serves as an obstacle the hero must struggle against. In many narratives the villain is the antagonist, though the two roles do not always coincide.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Old French vilain, ultimately from Latin villanus, a farmhand attached to a Roman villa. In feudal society it denoted a low-born commoner, and by extension a person of base character; the semantic slide from "peasant" to "scoundrel" reflects medieval class prejudice. Early literary villains include figures such as Shakespeare's Iago and Richard III, and the scheming antagonists of Gothic fiction and Victorian melodrama, whose stylised wickedness — the twirled moustache, the cape — became clichés.

Characteristics

Villains are typically characterised by selfishness, cruelty, ambition or a desire for power, and their plans drive the central conflict of a story. Modern fiction often gives villains complexity: sympathetic motivations, tragic backstories, or moral codes of their own, producing the related figure of the anti-villain. In superhero fiction the "supervillain" possesses powers matching the hero's. Critics note that a strong villain is often essential to a compelling hero, since the hero is defined by the opposition overcome.

Cultural significance

Memorable villains — from Darth Vader and the Joker to Lady Macbeth — frequently outlast their stories in popular culture, and lists such as the American Film Institute's "100 Heroes and Villains" codify their status. Some actors become closely identified with villainous roles, and the role can be a coveted showcase of range.
