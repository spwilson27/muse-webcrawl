Social liberalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social liberalism, also known as modern liberalism in the United States, is a political philosophy and variety of liberalism that endorses a regulated market economy and the expansion of civil and political rights, holding that legitimate government must also address economic and social issues such as poverty, health care and education. It emerged in the late nineteenth century as “new liberal” thinkers such as T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse argued that freedom requires not merely the absence of coercion but the effective capacity to act.

Social liberals support civil liberties and political democracy combined with social justice and a mixed economy: they accept private property and markets but endorse progressive taxation, welfare provision, labour protections and public services to equalise opportunity. In Britain the tradition shaped the Liberal governments of 1906–1914 and Beveridge's welfare state; in the United States it became the mainstream of the Democratic Party from the New Deal onward, and “liberal” there simply denotes this position.

Contrast with classical liberalism

Where classical liberals fear state power as the chief threat to liberty, social liberals argue that concentrated private power and material deprivation also constrain freedom, and that democratic government is the proper instrument to relieve them. Isaiah Berlin's distinction between negative and positive liberty (Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958) is the classic philosophical statement of the dispute. Opponents from the right criticise social liberalism as a halfway house to socialism; opponents from the left criticise it for preserving capitalist inequalities.
