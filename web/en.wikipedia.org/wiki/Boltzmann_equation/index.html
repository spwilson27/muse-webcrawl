The Boltzmann equation or Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) describes the statistical behaviour of a thermodynamic system not in a state of equilibrium. It was devised by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1872. The equation gives the time evolution of the probability density function f(x, p, t), the probability that a particle occupies a small volume of phase space around position x with momentum p at time t.

∂f/∂t + (p/m)·∇f + F·∇pf = (∂f/∂t)coll

The collision term

The right-hand side accounts for collisions between particles. Boltzmann assumed that collisions are binary, instantaneous and local, and invoked the Stosszahlansatz — the assumption of molecular chaos — under which the velocities of two colliding particles are uncorrelated before the collision. The collision integral then counts particles scattered into and out of each element of momentum space according to the differential cross-section of the interaction.

H-theorem

From the equation Boltzmann derived his H-theorem, showing that the quantity H = ∫ f ln f d³p can only decrease (or remain constant) with time for an isolated gas. Identifying −H with the entropy per particle provided the first microscopic, statistical explanation of the second law of thermodynamics and of irreversible approach to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. The theorem drew objections from Loschmidt (reversibility) and Zermelo (recurrence), which Boltzmann answered by stressing the statistical nature of the result.

Applications and solutions

The Boltzmann equation underlies the kinetic theory of gases and is used wherever particle transport matters: neutron transport in reactors, electron and phonon transport in semiconductors and solids, radiative transfer, plasma physics and rarefied gas dynamics. Exact solutions exist only in special cases; standard approximation schemes include the relaxation-time (BGK) approximation and the Chapman–Enskog expansion, which recovers the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid dynamics as a limiting case. Numerical methods include the lattice Boltzmann method and direct simulation Monte Carlo.

Generalizations

Related transport equations include the Vlasov equation for collisionless plasmas, the Landau and Balescu–Lenard equations with screened interactions, and quantum versions such as the Uehling–Uhlenbeck equation for bosons and fermions, where Pauli blocking and Bose enhancement factors modify the collision term.
