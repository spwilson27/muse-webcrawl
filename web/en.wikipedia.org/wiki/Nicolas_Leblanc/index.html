Nicolas Leblanc

Born6 December 1742, Ivoy-le-Pré, France
Died16 January 1806, Saint-Denis, France
Known forLeblanc process

Nicolas Leblanc (6 December 1742 – 16 January 1806) was a French physician and chemist who invented the Leblanc process for manufacturing soda ash (sodium carbonate) from common salt — a breakthrough that underpinned the industrial production of soap, glass, textiles and paper in the 19th century.

Life

Born at Ivoy-le-Pré in the Cher, Leblanc trained in medicine in Paris and became surgeon to Louis Philippe, Duke of Orléans. His scientific interests led him to study the crystallisation of salts.

The Leblanc process

In 1790 the French Academy of Sciences offered a prize for a practical method of making soda from salt. Leblanc patented his process in 1791: salt was treated with sulphuric acid to give salt cake (sodium sulphate), which was then roasted with limestone and coal to yield black ash, from which soda ash was leached. The Duke of Orléans financed a factory at Saint-Denis.

Revolution and ruin

During the French Revolution the factory was confiscated, Leblanc's patent was made public without compensation, and his patron was executed. Although the plant was later returned, Leblanc could not restart production profitably; impoverished, he took his own life in 1806. Paradoxically the process flourished in Britain, where it dominated soda manufacture until displaced by the Solvay process.

Legacy

The Leblanc process made alkali cheap and abundant but produced notorious pollution — hydrogen chloride gas and calcium sulphide waste — prompting early environmental legislation such as Britain's Alkali Act of 1863.
