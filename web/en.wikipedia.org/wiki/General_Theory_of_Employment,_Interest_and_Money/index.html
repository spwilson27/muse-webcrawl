The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money

[Image: the cover of the 1936 first edition of Keynes's General Theory]

The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money is a book by John Maynard Keynes, published in February 1936, that founded modern Macroeconomics and gave Keynesian economics its name. Written during the Great Depression, it attacked the classical doctrine that economies self-correct to full employment.

Argument

Keynes argued that output and employment are determined by effective (aggregate) demand: consumption depends on income via a marginal propensity to consume below one; investment is driven by volatile expectations ('animal spirits') and the marginal efficiency of capital against the interest rate, which is set by liquidity preference and the money supply. Saving and investment adjust through income changes — the multiplier — so an economy can rest at an under-employment equilibrium, and cutting wages or prices may worsen it through debt deflation and expectations. The policy corollary: government spending and tax policy should lean against the cycle. The book's difficult prose spawned an industry of interpretation (Hicks's IS–LM, 1937), but its core claims — demand matters, expectations are unstable, slumps can persist — still structure debate.
