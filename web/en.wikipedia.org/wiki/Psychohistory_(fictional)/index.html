Psychohistory is a fictional science in Isaac Asimov's Foundation universe. It combines history, sociology and mathematical statistics to make general predictions about the future behaviour of very large groups of people. Psychohistory is the central premise of the series: the mathematician Hari Seldon, foreseeing the fall of the Galactic Empire and a 30,000-year dark age, uses psychohistory to devise the Seldon Plan, shortening the interregnum to 1,000 years by establishing Foundations that steer history along a computed path.

Axioms and preconditions

Asimov's psychohistory rests on three requirements stated in the stories: (1) the population being modelled must be sufficiently large — on the order of the Empire's quintillions — for statistical treatment; (2) the population must remain ignorant of the psychohistorical predictions, so as not to change behaviour in response to them; and (3) humanity must be the only sentient species in the galaxy, so no unpredictable alien intelligence intervenes. Psychohistory can predict the behaviour of masses but not of individuals, a point Asimov compared to the kinetic theory of gases, in which molecular motions are individually unpredictable yet bulk properties obey exact laws.

The Seldon Plan and the Mule

The Plan was stored in psychohistorical equations visualised by the Prime Radiant and unfolded through timed "Seldon crises", in which the Foundation's development could proceed in only one viable direction. Its great disruption is the Mule, a mutant capable of manipulating emotions — an individual outlier the statistical science could not foresee — whose conquests throw the Plan into jeopardy until the hidden Second Foundation of psychohistorians intervenes.

Inspiration and legacy

Asimov drew on his reading of Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire and on statistical mechanics; editor John W. Campbell suggested the term "psychohistory", unaware it was already used (in a quite different sense) for a real psychological discipline applying psychoanalysis to historical figures — the reason articles on the fictional science are disambiguated. The idea has since influenced discussions of cliodynamics and big-data prediction, and is frequently cited in economics and complexity science as a thought experiment about the limits of forecasting social systems.
