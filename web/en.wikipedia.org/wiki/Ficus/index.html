Ficus is a genus of about 850 species of woody trees, shrubs, vines, epiphytes and hemiepiphytes in the family Moraceae. Collectively known as fig trees or figs, they are native throughout the tropics, with a few species extending into the semi-warm temperate zone.

Description

Most Ficus species are evergreen, though some temperate-zone species are deciduous. All produce a milky latex. The distinctive fruit — the fig — is a syconium, an inverted inflorescence lined with tiny flowers on its inner surface. Figs are pollinated by highly specific fig wasps in an obligate mutualism: each fig species generally depends on a single wasp species, and vice versa.

Ecology

Figs are keystone species in many tropical forests: because different species fruit at different times of year, they provide food for birds, bats and primates year-round, sustaining wildlife through seasons when other fruits are scarce. Some species begin life as epiphytes; "strangler figs" gradually envelop and kill their host tree, leaving a hollow lattice trunk.

Notable species

The common fig (Ficus carica) has been cultivated since antiquity for its edible fruit. The banyan (F. benghalensis) spreads by aerial prop roots into vast groves. The sacred fig (F. religiosa) is revered in Buddhism, Hinduism and Jainism — the Buddha is said to have attained enlightenment beneath one. The rubber plant (F. elastica) and weeping fig (F. benjamina) are popular houseplants.
