The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) is a United States federal law, enacted in 1974 and substantially expanded in 1976, that prohibits discrimination by creditors against credit applicants on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or the fact that an applicant's income derives from a public assistance program. It is implemented by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Regulation B and applies to virtually all forms of consumer and commercial credit, including mortgages, credit cards, auto loans, and small-business lending.

The ECOA ended widespread practices of denying credit to women and minorities.

Background and enactment

Before the ECOA, women in the United States routinely faced discrimination in credit markets: banks commonly refused credit cards to single women, required a husband's signature for married women's accounts, discounted a wife's income when evaluating a couple's loan application, and asked women about their childbearing plans. The original 1974 Act addressed sex and marital-status discrimination specifically in consumer credit; amendments in 1976 added the other prohibited bases and extended the law's protections. Alongside the Fair Housing Act and later legislation, the ECOA became a central pillar of federal fair-lending law.

Provisions

  • Prohibited bases: creditors may not discriminate on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex (interpreted by regulators to include sexual orientation and gender identity), marital status, age (provided the applicant can legally contract), receipt of public-assistance income, or the applicant's good-faith exercise of rights under consumer credit law.
  • Disparate impact: enforcement agencies and courts have applied the law not only to overt discrimination but also to neutral practices with unjustified discriminatory effects.
  • Notification: creditors must notify applicants of action taken on an application within 30 days and give specific reasons for adverse decisions.
  • Credit history: creditors must report spousal accounts in both spouses' names and must consider an applicant's own credit history rather than a spouse's alone.
  • Record retention and monitoring: Regulation B restricts the information creditors may request (for example, generally barring questions about race or sex on non-mortgage applications) while requiring collection of demographic data in mortgage lending to enable fair-lending monitoring.

Enforcement

The ECOA is enforced by the CFPB, the federal banking regulators, the Department of Justice (for pattern-or-practice cases), and the Federal Trade Commission, and it also gives individuals a private right of action with actual damages, punitive damages, and attorney's fees. Major fair-lending settlements — including mortgage redlining cases against banks in the 2010s and 2020s — have been brought under the ECOA together with the Fair Housing Act.
