Video nasty

Video nasty is a colloquial term popularised in the United Kingdom in the early 1980s for a number of films, typically low-budget horror and exploitation productions, distributed on video cassette that were criticised by the press, social commentators, and campaigners for their violent content. The moral panic surrounding the films contributed to the introduction of the Video Recordings Act 1984, which required pre-certification of videos released in the UK.

Background

Unlike cinema films, home video releases in Britain at the start of the 1980s were not subject to classification by the British Board of Film Censors, because the 1909 Cinematograph Act was interpreted as applying only to public exhibition. Distributors could therefore release uncut versions of films that had been cut or banned in cinemas, including titles such as Cannibal Holocaust, I Spit on Your Grave, The Driller Killer, Zombie Flesh Eaters, and Sam Raimi's The Evil Dead.

The panic and prosecution

Activist Mary Whitehouse and the National Viewers' and Listeners' Association campaigned against the tapes, and tabloid newspapers ran sustained coverage linking them to youth delinquency. The Director of Public Prosecutions drew up a list of 72 films liable for prosecution under the Obscene Publications Act 1959; 39 were successfully prosecuted. Police raids on video shops seized stock, and dealers faced fines and imprisonment.

Video Recordings Act 1984

The resulting Act made it an offence to supply an unclassified video work, designating the British Board of Film Classification as the classification authority. Many of the "nasties" remained banned or heavily cut for years afterwards. During the 1990s and 2000s, as standards relaxed, most were re-released uncut with 18 certificates, and the episode is now widely cited by historians as a classic moral panic. A technical flaw meant the Act was found unenforceable in 2009 — it had never been notified to the European Commission as required — and it was re-enacted as the Video Recordings Act 2010.

Legacy

The DPP list has become a collector's canon for horror fans; original pre-certification tapes ("pre-certs") are prized. Documentaries such as Video Nasties: Moral Panic, Censorship & Videotape (2010) chronicle the affair, and many once-prosecuted films are now regarded as significant works of the horror genre.
