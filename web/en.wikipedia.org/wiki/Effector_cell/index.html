Effector cell

In immunology, an effector cell is a lymphocyte that has been activated by antigen and has acquired the ability to carry out an immediate defensive function — killing infected or abnormal cells, or secreting large quantities of antibodies or cytokines. Effector cells are the short-lived, terminally active arm of the adaptive immune response, in contrast to naïve lymphocytes, which have not yet encountered antigen, and memory cells, which persist long-term to respond to re-exposure.

Types

Effector T cells include cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which kill virus-infected and tumour cells by releasing perforin and granzymes and by triggering apoptosis through the Fas pathway, and helper CD4+ effector cells of the Th1, Th2, Th17, and other subsets, which coordinate the response by secreting cytokines that activate macrophages, B cells, and other leukocytes.

Effector B cells, called plasma cells, are antibody factories: after activation and differentiation they secrete thousands of antibody molecules per second of the IgG, IgA, or IgE classes. Short-lived plasma cells in lymphoid tissue provide the first wave of antibody, while long-lived plasma cells resident in bone marrow sustain serum antibody levels.

The term is also used more loosely for any cell that "effects" a response — for example natural killer cells and activated macrophages in innate immunity — and, outside immunology, for cells such as muscle cells that execute the output of the nervous system.

Development and fate

Activation of a naïve lymphocyte by its specific antigen, together with co-stimulatory signals, triggers clonal expansion and differentiation into effector cells over several days. Once the antigen is cleared, roughly 90–95% of effector cells undergo apoptosis during the contraction phase; the survivors form the memory pool. This economy — a large, short-lived effector burst followed by long-lived memory — underlies both natural immunity and the action of vaccines.
