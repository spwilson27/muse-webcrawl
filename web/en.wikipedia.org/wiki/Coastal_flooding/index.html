Coastal flooding

Coastal flooding occurs when normally dry, low-lying land is flooded by seawater. It can result from storm surge driven by tropical or extratropical cyclones, exceptionally high ("king") tides, tsunamis, or combinations of these with heavy rainfall and river flooding. The extent of coastal flooding is a function of the elevation of the land relative to sea level, the height of the water, and barriers such as dunes, mangroves, levees, and seawalls. Sea level rise from climate change is increasing the frequency and severity of coastal floods worldwide.

Causes

Storm surge is the dominant cause of catastrophic coastal flooding: low atmospheric pressure and onshore winds pile water against the coast, as in the North Sea flood of 1953 that killed over 2,500 people in the Netherlands and England and prompted the Dutch Delta Works. Hurricane Katrina (2005) breached New Orleans' levees, and Hurricane Sandy's surge (2012) flooded parts of New York and New Jersey. In many cities, "nuisance" or sunny-day flooding at high tide has become routine as sea levels rise, damaging roads, drainage, and property even without storms.

Impacts and responses

Coastal zones host a large share of the world's population and economic assets; hundreds of millions of people live on land projected to be below annual flood levels by mid-century under high-emissions scenarios. Responses include hard defences (seawalls, surge barriers such as Rotterdam's Maeslantkering and the Thames Barrier), ecosystem-based protection (wetland and mangrove restoration), building elevation and codes, early-warning systems, and in some places planned relocation or managed retreat.
