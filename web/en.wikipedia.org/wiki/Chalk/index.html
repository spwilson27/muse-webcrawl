Chalk is a soft, white, porous, sedimentary carbonate rock. It is a form of limestone composed of the mineral calcite, originally formed deep under the sea by the compression of microscopic plankton that had settled to the sea floor. Chalk is common throughout Western Europe, where deposits underlie parts of France, Great Britain, Belgium, Denmark, and the Netherlands, and it is also found in parts of North America and Australia.

Formation

Chalk formed in the warm epicontinental seas of the Late Cretaceous Period (roughly 100–66 million years ago), when sea levels were far higher than today. Countless coccolithophores — single-celled algae armoured with tiny calcite plates called coccoliths — lived in the surface waters; on death their plates rained down and accumulated as a soft calcareous ooze, with remarkably little contamination by sand or mud. Flint nodules, derived from dissolved sponge spicules, are characteristically embedded in the rock. The great thickness and purity of chalk reflect the absence of nearby land to shed sediment into those seas.

Uses

Chalk landscapes support distinctive downland ecology and store water in chalk aquifers, a major source of drinking water in south-east England. Quarried chalk is used in cement manufacture, as an agricultural dressing to neutralise acidic soils, and in industry as a filler and whiting agent. The familiar writing material long used on blackboards was originally natural chalk, though most modern "chalk" sticks are made of gypsum, and tailors' chalk and climbing chalk (magnesium carbonate) are different substances again. The wine cellars of Champagne are excavated in chalk, whose cool, humid galleries suit the ageing of the wine.
