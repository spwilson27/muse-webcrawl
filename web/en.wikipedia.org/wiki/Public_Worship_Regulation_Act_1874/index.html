Public Worship Regulation Act 1874

The Public Worship Regulation Act 1874 (37 & 38 Vict. c. 85) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom intended to suppress Ritualism in the Church of England. It was introduced by Archbishop of Canterbury Archibald Campbell Tait, with the support of Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli, who described the practices targeted as "a Mass in masquerade".

Provisions

The Act established a new court presided over by a single judge, Lord Penzance, sitting for both provinces of Canterbury and York, to hear complaints about ritual practices such as the use of vestments, incense, altar candles, and the eastward position at communion. Bishops retained a power to veto prosecutions. Disobedience to the court's orders could lead to imprisonment for contempt.

Prosecutions and repeal

A number of Anglo-Catholic clergy — including Arthur Tooth, T. Pelham Dale, Richard William Enraght, and Sidney Faithorn Green — were prosecuted and in some cases imprisoned, becoming celebrated "martyrs" to their supporters and generating public sympathy that undermined the Act. Prosecutions effectively ceased by the late 1880s. The Act was repealed by the Statute Law (Repeals) Act 1977, though its disciplinary functions had long since been superseded by later measures, including the Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction Measure 1963.
