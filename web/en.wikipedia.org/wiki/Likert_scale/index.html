Likert scale

A Likert scale (pronounced "LICK-urt") is a psychometric scale commonly involved in research that employs questionnaires. It is the most widely used approach to scaling responses in survey research, such that the term (or more fully the Likert-type scale) is often used interchangeably with rating scale, although there are other types of rating scales.

History

The scale was developed by American social psychologist Rensis Likert, who described the technique in a 1932 report, "A Technique for the Measurement of Attitudes." Respondents specify their level of agreement or disagreement on a symmetric agree–disagree scale for a series of statements about an attitude object; the pattern of responses across items yields a score intended to measure the attitude.

Design

A classic Likert item offers five response levels: strongly disagree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree (or neutral), agree, and strongly agree. Four-, seven-, and other even- or odd-numbered formats are also common; even-numbered ("forced choice") formats omit the neutral midpoint. A Likert scale properly refers to the sum or average of several Likert items measuring the same construct, which is typically more reliable than a single item.

Analysis and controversy

There is long-standing debate over whether Likert data should be treated as ordinal data (analyzed with medians, modes, and nonparametric tests) or as interval data (means, standard deviations, and parametric tests). In practice, summed scales are often analyzed parametrically, and simulations generally find parametric methods robust for multi-item scales.
