Acalculia

SpecialtyNeurology, neuropsychology
SymptomsLoss of ability to perform arithmetic
CausesBrain injury, stroke, dementia

Acalculia is an acquired impairment in which patients have difficulty performing simple mathematical tasks, such as adding, subtracting, multiplying, or even comparing which of two numbers is larger. It is distinguished from dyscalculia, which is a developmental disorder of mathematical learning. The term was coined by the Swedish neurologist Salomon Eberhard Henschen in 1925.

Causes

Acalculia most often results from damage to the parietal lobe, especially the angular gyrus and intraparietal sulcus of the dominant hemisphere, caused by stroke, traumatic brain injury, tumours, or neurodegenerative disease. It is one of the four components of Gerstmann syndrome, alongside agraphia, finger agnosia, and left–right disorientation.

Types

Salomon Henschen and later clinicians distinguished primary acalculia (an isolated deficit of calculation itself) from secondary acalculia, in which calculation fails because of impairments in language, memory, attention, or spatial processing. Spatial acalculia, associated with right-hemisphere lesions, involves misaligning digits and misreading place value.

Assessment and rehabilitation

Assessment uses number-processing and calculation batteries testing transcoding (reading and writing numerals), arithmetic facts, and written and mental calculation. Rehabilitation may involve retraining number concepts, drill of arithmetic facts, and compensatory strategies such as calculators and written algorithms.
