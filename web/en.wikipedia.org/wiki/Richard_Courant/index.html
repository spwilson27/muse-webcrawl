Richard Courant (January 8, 1888 – January 27, 1972) was a German-American mathematician. He is best known to the general public for the book What Is Mathematics?, co-written with Herbert Robbins. He founded the institute at New York University that now bears his name, the Courant Institute of Mathematical Sciences.

Life and career

Courant was born in Lublinitz, in the Prussian province of Silesia, into a Jewish family. He studied at the University of Breslau and the University of Göttingen, where he became a student and assistant of David Hilbert, receiving his doctorate in 1910. After military service in the First World War, he returned to Göttingen and in 1920 became professor there, building its mathematical institute into a world-leading research centre with support from the Rockefeller Foundation.

Dismissed from his post after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, Courant emigrated first to Cambridge, England, and then in 1934 to New York University, where he built a graduate programme in applied mathematics from almost nothing into one of the strongest in the United States. During the Second World War his group contributed to military research, and after the war the institute became a major centre for computing and applied mathematics.

Work

Courant's research centred on partial differential equations, the calculus of variations, and mathematical physics. With Kurt Friedrichs and Hans Lewy he published the 1928 paper establishing the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, a fundamental convergence criterion for numerical solutions of partial differential equations. His two-volume Methods of Mathematical Physics (the first volume with Hilbert, 1924) remained a standard reference for decades. His popular book What Is Mathematics? (1941), written with Herbert Robbins, introduced generations of readers to modern mathematics and remains in print.

Legacy

The institute he founded at NYU was renamed the Courant Institute of Mathematical Sciences in his honour in 1964. He died in New Rochelle, New York, in 1972.
