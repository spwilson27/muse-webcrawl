Oral history is the collection and study of historical information about people, families, important events or everyday life using recorded interviews with people who have first-hand knowledge. It is both one of the oldest types of historical inquiry — predating the written word — and, in its modern recorded form, a distinct field of scholarship that developed in the twentieth century.

Development

Modern oral history is often dated to 1948, when Allan Nevins established the Columbia Center for Oral History at Columbia University to record the recollections of prominent Americans. The field broadened from the 1960s and 1970s, as cheaper recording equipment and "history from below" movements led historians to interview ordinary people — workers, women, ethnic minorities — whose experiences were absent from written archives. Projects such as the Federal Writers' Project slave narratives (1930s) are now recognised as important precursors, and oral history has also been central to documenting the Holocaust, the civil rights movement and other subjects.

Method and debates

Oral historians conduct planned, recorded interviews, preserve the recordings (with informed consent) in archives, and make transcripts available. Because memory is selective and reconstructive, scholars debate oral history's reliability as evidence; Italian historian Alessandro Portelli argued that its distinctive value lies precisely in subjectivity — in what memories reveal about meaning and identity, not only about facts. Ethical questions of consent, ownership and interpretation are governed by professional guidelines such as those of the Oral History Association.

Not to be confused with oral tradition, the transmission of cultural material by speech across generations.
