Volcanic hazards

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Volcanic hazards are the dangerous phenomena produced by volcanic activity that can cause loss of life, injury and damage to property and the environment. Several hazards may occur simultaneously during a single eruption, and their effects can extend from the immediate vicinity of the volcano to global scales in the case of volcanic winter.

Primary hazards

Lava flows usually advance slowly enough for people to escape but destroy everything in their path. Pyroclastic flows — superheated avalanches of gas, ash and rock — are the most lethal volcanic phenomenon, as in the destruction of Saint-Pierre, Martinique, in 1902. Tephra falls, especially volcanic ash, can collapse roofs, contaminate water and disrupt aviation over wide areas. Volcanic gases such as carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide can poison people and livestock and generate acid rain; the 1986 limnic eruption at Lake Nyos released a suffocating CO₂ cloud. Ballistic projectiles (including volcanic bombs) threaten areas near the vent.

Secondary hazards

Lahars (volcanic mudflows) form when eruptions melt snow and ice or when heavy rain remobilises ash; a lahar from Nevado del Ruiz destroyed Armero, Colombia, in 1985. Eruptions can also trigger landslides and debris avalanches (as at Mount St. Helens in 1980), tsunamis (Krakatoa, 1883), floods, wildfires and famine from climate effects.

Mitigation

Hazard maps based on a volcano's eruptive history guide land-use planning. Monitoring of seismicity, ground deformation and gas emissions by volcano observatories underpins forecasting and evacuation, as in the successful prediction of the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo.
