Gregory Goodwin Pincus

Gregory Goodwin Pincus (April 9, 1903 – August 22, 1967) was an American biologist and researcher who co-invented the combined oral contraceptive pill, one of the most socially consequential medical developments of the twentieth century.

Early research

Born in Woodbine, New Jersey, Pincus studied at Cornell University and Harvard, where his early work on mammalian reproduction and in vitro fertilisation of rabbit eggs attracted controversy and publicity. In 1944 he co-founded the Worcester Foundation for Experimental Biology in Massachusetts, which became the base for his research into reproductive hormones.

The Pill

In 1951 the birth control campaigner Margaret Sanger and philanthropist Katharine McCormick asked Pincus to develop an oral contraceptive; McCormick funded the work. With his colleague Min Chueh Chang, Pincus showed that the hormone progesterone could inhibit ovulation in animals. Working with gynaecologist John Rock, he organised human trials, including large field trials in Puerto Rico beginning in 1956, using a formulation developed with the pharmaceutical company G. D. Searle. The resulting pill, Enovid, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for contraceptive use in 1960. Pincus continued reproductive research until his death in 1967.
