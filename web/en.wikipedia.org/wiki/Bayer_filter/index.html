Bayer filter

A Bayer filter mosaic is a colour filter array (CFA) for arranging RGB colour filters on a square grid of photosensors. Its particular arrangement — 50% green, 25% red and 25% blue, in the pattern RGGB (or BGGR) — is used in nearly all single-chip digital image sensors in consumer cameras, camcorders and scanners. It is named after its inventor, Bryce Bayer of Eastman Kodak, who patented it in 1976.

How it works

Each photosensor (pixel) records light of only one colour because of the filter laid over it. Because the array contains twice as many green as red or blue elements — reflecting the human eye's greater sensitivity to green and to luminance detail — a complete colour value for every pixel must be reconstructed by interpolation from its neighbours, a process called demosaicing (or debayering). The raw output of a Bayer sensor is known as a "raw" image; converting it into a viewable colour image requires demosaicing plus white balance, gamma and colour-space conversion.

Advantages and limitations

The Bayer design lets a single sensor capture colour, keeping cameras compact and inexpensive. Its costs are reduced effective resolution, colour artefacts such as moiré and false colour on fine repetitive detail, and light loss through the filters. Alternatives and refinements include Fujifilm's X-Trans pattern (designed to suppress moiré), RGBW arrays, Foveon stacked sensors, and three-sensor (3CCD) systems that avoid demosaicing altogether.
