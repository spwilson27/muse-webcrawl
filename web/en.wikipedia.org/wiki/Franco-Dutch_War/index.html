The Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678), also known as the Dutch War (Guerre de Hollande), was fought between the Kingdom of France and the Dutch Republic, which was later joined by the Holy Roman Empire, Spain, Brandenburg-Prussia and Denmark. England fought alongside France in 1672–74 as part of the Third Anglo-Dutch War. The conflict ended with the Treaties of Nijmegen (1678), which gave France Franche-Comté and several Flemish towns.

The Rampjaar, 1672

Louis XIV, resentful of Dutch obstruction of his ambitions in the Spanish Netherlands and of Dutch trade dominance, invaded in April 1672 with some 120,000 men — the largest army yet seen in Europe. French forces under Turenne and Condé overran the eastern provinces in weeks. The year became known in Dutch memory as the Rampjaar ("disaster year"): the regime of Johan de Witt collapsed, De Witt and his brother were lynched by a mob in The Hague, and William III of Orange was appointed stadtholder. The Dutch halted the invasion by opening the sluices and flooding the Dutch Waterline; Admiral Michiel de Ruyter kept the Anglo-French fleet at bay at Solebay, the Schooneveld and the Texel.

Widening war

England made peace at Westminster in 1674, while the Emperor, Spain and Brandenburg joined the Dutch. Fighting spread to the Rhineland, Alsace, Flanders and Catalonia, as well as to the Caribbean and India. Turenne's brilliant 1674–75 campaigns in Alsace ended with his death at Sasbach. The war at sea continued with De Ruyter's death at Augusta (1676) in the Mediterranean. By 1677–78 France held the upper hand in Flanders (Cassel, Saint-Denis), which drove the allies to negotiate.

Peace and consequences

The Treaties of Nijmegen confirmed France in possession of Franche-Comté and a belt of fortresses in the Spanish Netherlands, while the Dutch preserved their independence and secured the withdrawal of French troops and a favourable trade treaty. The war established France as Europe's dominant power, made William III its implacable opponent (a rivalry culminating in the Nine Years' War), and pushed the Dutch Republic into a defensive posture despite its survival.
