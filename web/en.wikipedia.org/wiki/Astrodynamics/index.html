Astrodynamics

A Hohmann transfer ellipse, a fundamental astrodynamic manoeuvre.

Astrodynamics, also called orbital mechanics, is the study of the motion of artificial satellites and space vehicles moving under the influence of forces such as gravity, atmospheric drag and thrust. It applies the principles of classical mechanics — Newton's laws of motion and universal gravitation — together with Kepler's laws of planetary motion, and forms a core discipline of aerospace engineering and mission design.

Fundamentals

In the two-body idealisation, a spacecraft in free flight follows a conic-section trajectory — ellipse, parabola or hyperbola — about the primary body. Its orbit is specified by six Keplerian orbital elements: semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis and true anomaly. The vis-viva equation relates a vehicle's speed to its position on such an orbit, and the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation governs the velocity change (delta-v) achievable by burning propellant.

Manoeuvres

Astrodynamic techniques include the Hohmann transfer between circular orbits, bi-elliptic transfers, plane changes (which are expensive in delta-v), gravity assists in which a spacecraft gains or loses energy from a planet's motion, aerobraking, and low-energy transfers through Lagrange-point regions. Lambert's problem — finding the orbit connecting two position vectors in a given time — underlies interplanetary trajectory design.

Applications

The discipline is used to design launch trajectories, satellite constellations, rendezvous and docking, station-keeping, interplanetary missions such as the Voyager and Parker Solar Probe flights, and re-entry profiles. Perturbations from Earth's oblateness (the J2 effect), third bodies and drag must be modelled for precise work, drawing on techniques from celestial mechanics and numerical integration.
