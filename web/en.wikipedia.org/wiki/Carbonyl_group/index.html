Carbonyl group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In organic chemistry, a carbonyl group is a functional group with the formula C=O, composed of a carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom. It is a defining structural unit of several classes of organic compounds — aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, amides, acyl halides and anhydrides — and it plays a central role in organic reactivity and in biological molecules such as sugars and amino acids.

Structure and bonding

The carbonyl carbon is sp2-hybridised and trigonal planar, with bond angles near 120°. The C=O bond is short (about 1.21 Å) and strongly polarised toward oxygen, giving the group a substantial dipole moment: the carbon carries a partial positive charge and behaves as an electrophile, while the oxygen is nucleophilic and weakly basic. Resonance donation from an adjacent nitrogen, oxygen or halogen (as in amides and esters) reduces the group's reactivity compared with simple aldehydes and ketones.

Reactivity and spectroscopy

Carbonyl compounds undergo nucleophilic addition (aldehydes, ketones), nucleophilic acyl substitution (carboxylic acid derivatives), and reactions at the α-carbon through enols and enolates, such as aldol condensations. The group is readily identified by infrared spectroscopy, with a strong, characteristic absorption band around 1650–1850 cm−1, and by a highly deshielded signal in 13C NMR spectra near 200 ppm for aldehydes and ketones.

Terminology

"Carbonyl" also refers to the ligand carbon monoxide in organometallic chemistry (metal carbonyls), and to the carbonyl dihalides such as phosgene (carbonyl dichloride). Ketene and carbon dioxide contain cumulated C=O bonds but are usually treated as their own classes.
