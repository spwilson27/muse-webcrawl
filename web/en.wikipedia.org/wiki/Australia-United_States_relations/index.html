Australia–United States relations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The flags of Australia and the United States

Australia–United States relations are the bilateral relations between the Commonwealth of Australia and the United States of America. The two countries have been close allies since the Second World War, bound formally by the ANZUS Treaty of 1951. The United States is Australia's second-largest trading partner and largest source of foreign investment, and the two cooperate extensively in defence, intelligence, and foreign policy.

History

Relations began with American trading and whaling contacts in the early 19th century, and the first United States consulate in Sydney opened in 1836. Australia and the United States first fought side by side in the First World War, where General John Monash commanded American troops at the Battle of Hamel in 1918. The relationship became central during the Second World War: after the fall of Singapore in 1942, Prime Minister John Curtin declared that Australia "looks to America," and General Douglas MacArthur directed the South West Pacific campaign from Australia, where more than a million American personnel were stationed.

The alliance

The ANZUS Treaty, signed in San Francisco in 1951 by Australia, New Zealand, and the United States, commits the parties to consult and act to meet common danger in the Pacific area. Australia invoked the treaty for the first time after the September 11 attacks of 2001. Australian forces have served alongside the United States in the Korean War, Vietnam War, the Gulf War, Afghanistan, and Iraq. The alliance encompasses joint facilities such as Pine Gap, an annual ministerial consultation (AUSMIN), a regular rotation of U.S. Marines through Darwin since 2012, and membership in the Five Eyes intelligence partnership. In 2021 the AUKUS partnership added a commitment for Australia to acquire nuclear-powered submarines with American and British assistance.

Trade and investment

The Australia–United States Free Trade Agreement entered into force in 2005. The United States runs a trade surplus with Australia, and American investment stock in Australia exceeds that of any other country, spanning mining, finance, technology, and services.

Cultural and political ties

The two countries share a language, similar settler-colonial histories, and broadly aligned democratic institutions, though occasional frictions—over trade disputes, the Vietnam era, and climate policy—have punctuated an otherwise durable partnership that polling consistently shows is supported by majorities in both countries.
