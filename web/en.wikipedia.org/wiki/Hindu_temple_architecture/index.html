Hindu temple architecture

Hindu temple architecture comprises the styles of temple building that developed in the Indian subcontinent over more than a thousand years, governed by design principles laid down in the Vāstu Śāstra and the Āgama and Śilpa texts. A Hindu temple is conceived both as the dwelling of a deity and as a model of the cosmos, laid out on the geometric grid of the vāstupuruṣa-maṇḍala.

Principal styles

Two great regional styles are distinguished. The Nāgara style of North India is characterised by a beehive-shaped tower (śikhara) rising in curving tiers over the sanctuary, as at the Kandariya Mahadeva temple at Khajuraho and the Sun Temple at Konark. The Drāviḍa style of South India instead builds a stepped pyramidal tower (vimāna) over the shrine and, from the medieval period, monumental entrance towers called gopuram, as at the Brihadisvara Temple in Thanjavur and the temple city of Srirangam. The Vesara style of the Deccan mixes elements of both.

Elements of a temple

At the core lies the garbhagṛha ("womb chamber"), the small, dark sanctum housing the deity's image (mūrti), beneath the tower. Before it stand one or more halls (maṇḍapa) for worshippers, often richly carved with pillars, joined by a vestibule (antarāla). Complexes may add a temple tank, a flagstaff (dhvaja stambha), the deity's vehicle (vāhana), enclosure walls and subsidiary shrines, the whole arranged along an east–west axis. Walls and towers carry dense sculpture depicting deities, epics, daily life and auspicious figures.

Spread

The styles travelled with Hinduism into Southeast Asia, producing monuments such as Angkor Wat in Cambodia and Prambanan in Java, and they continue to shape temple building in India and the global Hindu diaspora today.
