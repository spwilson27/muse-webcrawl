Summit (also called OLCF-4) is a supercomputer developed by IBM for the United States Department of Energy at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee. At its launch in June 2018, Summit was the fastest supercomputer in the world, regaining the top position for the United States on the TOP500 list from China's Sunway TaihuLight, with a measured performance of 148.6 petaFLOPS. It held the No. 1 ranking until November 2020, when it was surpassed by Japan's Fugaku, and it was succeeded at Oak Ridge by the exascale machine Frontier in 2022.

Architecture

Summit consists of 4,608 compute nodes in a fat-tree network using dual-rail Mellanox EDR InfiniBand. Each node contains two 22-core IBM POWER9 processors and six NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs, giving the system more than 9,000 CPUs and 27,000 GPUs in total. Nodes have over 500 GB of coherent memory (DDR4 plus high-bandwidth HBM2 on the GPUs) and 1.6 TB of non-volatile memory, and the system's parallel file system can move data at about 2.5 TB/s. The machine occupies about 520 square metres and draws roughly 13 megawatts, cooled by thousands of gallons of water per minute. Unlike previous CPU-heavy designs, the overwhelming share of Summit's floating-point performance comes from its GPUs — an architecture optimised as much for artificial intelligence workloads (using low-precision tensor operations, where it can exceed an exaFLOP in mixed precision) as for traditional simulation.

Applications

Summit was built for open scientific research in energy, materials, astrophysics, earth systems, and health. Early flagship projects included genomics comparisons at unprecedented scale, climate modelling, and machine-learning analysis of Department of Energy experimental data. In 2020, Summit was used to screen more than 8,000 drug compounds for activity against SARS-CoV-2, identifying 77 small-molecule candidates in days — work highlighted as an example of supercomputing applied to the COVID-19 pandemic. A code running on Summit won the 2018 Gordon Bell Prize for a genomics algorithm that achieved exascale performance in mixed precision.
