Oceanids

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, the Oceanids (Greek: Ὠκεανίδες, Ōkeanídes) are the three thousand water nymphs who were the daughters of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys. They are a type of nymph associated with fresh water and the sea, and each was associated with a particular spring, river, sea, lake or pond. Their brothers, the Potamoi, were the gods of the rivers.

In Hesiod

Hesiod's Theogony lists some forty-one named Oceanids, "the eldest" of the three thousand, including Styx (the most honoured), Metis (mother of Athena), Eurynome, Doris (mother of the Nereids), Tyche, Calypso, Clymene and Perseis. Hesiod says that they are dispersed far and wide, serving the earth and the deep waters, together with Apollo and the river gods.

Individual Oceanids

Several Oceanids play prominent roles in myth: Styx, the river of the underworld by which the gods swore their unbreakable oaths; Metis, Zeus's first wife, swallowed while pregnant; Electra, mother of Iris and the Harpies; Doris, wife of Nereus; Clymene, mother of Phaethon in some accounts; and Callirhoe, mother of Geryon. The Oceanid sisters of Aeschylus's Prometheus Bound form the chorus that visits the chained Titan Prometheus.

Worship and depiction

Oceanids were depicted in classical art as beautiful young women, sometimes in groups attending major sea deities. Like other nymphs they could be invoked in prayer and received modest local cults, and they appear frequently in vase painting and later European art on mythological themes.
