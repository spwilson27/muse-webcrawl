Human rights in North Korea

Human rights in North Korea are widely considered among the worst in the world. The government of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) severely restricts the political and civil liberties of its citizens, including freedom of speech, freedom of movement, freedom of assembly, and freedom of religion. A 2014 report by a United Nations Commission of Inquiry found that "systematic, widespread and gross human rights violations" had been and were being committed by the state, in many instances constituting crimes against humanity.

General situation

North Korea is a one-party state ruled by the Workers' Party of Korea under the Kim family, which has governed since the state's founding in 1948. Power is concentrated in the Supreme Leader, and the ideology of Juche underpins the political system. Independent civil society, an independent judiciary, and opposition political activity do not exist in practice. The constitution nominally guarantees many rights, but these are subordinated to the interests of the state and the party.

Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and the United States Department of State have all described the human rights record of North Korea as exceptionally poor. The UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in the DPRK has repeatedly reported grave violations, and the UN General Assembly passes annual resolutions condemning them.

Political prison camps

The most notorious feature of the system is the network of political prison camps (kwanliso), where people accused of political crimes — and often their family members across three generations, under the principle of yeonjwaje (guilt by association) — are imprisoned. Defectors and satellite imagery have identified camps including Yodok (Camp 15), Kaechon (Camp 14), Hoeryong (Camp 22), and Bukchang (Camp 18). Testimony collected by the UN Commission of Inquiry described starvation, forced labour, torture, rape, forced abortions, infanticide, and executions in the camps. Estimates of the number of prisoners have ranged from 80,000 to 120,000.

Separate from the kwanliso are kyohwaso (re-education camps) for criminal and economic offences, and a network of local detention facilities and labour training camps (rodong danryeondae).

Songbun

Every North Korean is classified under songbun, a hereditary sociopolitical classification system based on the perceived loyalty of one's family background, particularly the activities of one's ancestors during and after the Korean War. The population is broadly divided into three classes: the "core" (haeksim), the "wavering" (dongyo), and the "hostile" (jeokdae) classes, with dozens of subcategories. Songbun affects access to residence in Pyongyang, education, employment, food distribution, and medical care.

Freedom of movement

Citizens cannot freely travel within the country or leave it. Internal travel requires official permission, and residence in Pyongyang is restricted to those considered loyal. Leaving the country without authorisation is a criminal offence; those caught attempting to cross into China may face imprisonment in labour camps, and forcibly repatriated defectors have reported torture and imprisonment. Nevertheless, tens of thousands of North Koreans have fled, mostly across the Chinese border; more than 30,000 have resettled in South Korea.

Freedom of expression and information

All media are state-controlled. Radios and televisions are fixed to receive only state broadcasts, and access to the global internet is limited to a tiny elite; ordinary citizens may use only a domestic intranet (Kwangmyong). Consumption of foreign media, including South Korean films and television dramas, is punished, though such media circulate widely through smuggled USB drives and other means. Laws such as the Reactionary Ideology and Culture Rejection Act prescribe severe penalties, reportedly including execution, for distributing foreign cultural content.

Religion

Although the constitution provides for freedom of religion, religious practice is tightly controlled and genuine religious freedom is generally considered non-existent. The state maintains a small number of showcase churches and temples in Pyongyang used for visitors, while underground Christian practice is severely punished; Open Doors has repeatedly ranked North Korea among the countries where Christians face the most extreme persecution.

Food and famine

Chronic food insecurity affects a large share of the population. The state-run Public Distribution System has frequently failed to provide adequate rations, and the famine of the 1990s — known in North Korea as the "Arduous March" — killed hundreds of thousands, with estimates ranging widely. UN agencies have estimated that millions of North Koreans, including a large proportion of children, suffer from undernourishment. Access to food is influenced by songbun and by the growth of unofficial markets (jangmadang), on which most households now depend.

Abductions

North Korea abducted foreign nationals, most prominently Japanese citizens in the 1970s and 1980s, some of whom were used to train spies in Japanese language and customs. In 2002 Kim Jong-il admitted to the abduction of 13 Japanese citizens; five were returned to Japan, while North Korea claimed the others had died — explanations Japan and the families dispute. South Korea estimates that hundreds of its citizens were abducted during and after the Korean War, in addition to prisoners of war not repatriated.

International response

The UN Commission of Inquiry, established by the Human Rights Council in 2013 and chaired by Michael Kirby, concluded in 2014 that crimes against humanity had been committed "pursuant to policies established at the highest level of the State" and recommended referral of the situation to the International Criminal Court. The UN Security Council has placed the human rights situation in the DPRK on its agenda, though no referral has occurred. North Korea rejects these findings, denouncing them as fabrications by hostile forces, and does not cooperate with the Special Rapporteur.
