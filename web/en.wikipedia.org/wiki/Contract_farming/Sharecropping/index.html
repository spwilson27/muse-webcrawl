Sharecropping

Sharecropping is a legal arrangement in which a landowner allows a tenant (the sharecropper) to use the land in return for a share of the crops produced on that land. Sharecropping has appeared in many societies and in various forms; it differs from tenant farming in that the landlord typically supplies inputs such as seed, tools and credit, and takes a larger share of the output.

United States

Sharecropping became widespread in the Southern United States after the American Civil War. Freed African Americans and poor whites, lacking land and capital, worked land owned by others in return for a share of the cotton crop, often one-half. The crop-lien system, under which merchants advanced supplies against the future crop at high interest, frequently left sharecroppers in chronic debt. Sharecropping, along with tenant farming, was a central institution of the Jim Crow South and declined with mechanisation and the Great Migration, largely disappearing by the mid-20th century.

Around the world

Analogous arrangements include the métayage system of France and Italy, and share tenancy systems in South and Southeast Asia and Latin America. Economists have analysed sharecropping as a response to risk, credit constraints and incentives: sharing output spreads risk between landlord and tenant but can blunt the tenant's incentive to supply effort, a trade-off formalised in principal–agent models.

Relation to modern contract farming

Modern contract farming within agribusiness supply chains resembles sharecropping in that firms supply inputs and specifications and purchase the output, though payment is usually a price or fee rather than a physical share of the crop, and land tenure arrangements differ.
