Contract farming

Contract farming involves agricultural production carried out on the basis of an agreement between farmers and buyers (such as processing firms, exporters, or retailers) that establishes conditions for the production and marketing of a farm product. The farmer agrees to provide an agreed quantity of a specific product, meeting quality standards and delivery schedules set by the buyer, while the buyer agrees to purchase the product, often at a predetermined price, and may supply inputs and technical advice.

Types

Arrangements range from simple marketing contracts, in which the farmer retains most production decisions, to production management contracts, in which the firm specifies and enforces detailed production methods. The model is especially common in poultry and hog production in the United States, in sugar and tea estates' outgrower schemes, and in horticulture for export in developing countries.

Advantages and criticisms

For farmers, contract farming can provide assured markets, access to credit, inputs, and technology. For firms, it secures reliable supplies of uniform quality without the costs of owning land. Critics note that bargaining power is often unequal: farmers may bear production risks, become dependent on a single buyer, and face opaque pricing formulae, while firms may renege on informal agreements when spot prices fall. The Food and Agriculture Organization has published guidance on drafting fair contract farming agreements.
