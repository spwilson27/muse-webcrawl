The National Constituent Assembly (French: Assemblée nationale constituante) was the constituent assembly formed from the National Assembly on 9 July 1789 during the first stages of the French Revolution. It governed France, together with the increasingly constrained monarchy of Louis XVI, until 30 September 1791, when it dissolved itself in favour of the new Legislative Assembly after completing the French Constitution of 1791.

Formation

The Assembly emerged from the Estates General of 1789. On 17 June 1789 the deputies of the Third Estate proclaimed themselves the National Assembly; after the Tennis Court Oath (20 June) and the king's capitulation, clergy and nobility joined them. On 9 July the Assembly took the additional name "Constituent" to signal its mission of giving France a constitution.

Achievements

Following the storming of the Bastille (14 July), the Assembly abolished the privileges of the feudal regime on the night of 4 August 1789 and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 August). It reorganised France into departments, nationalised church lands (assignats), enacted the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790), restructured taxation and the judiciary, abolished guilds and hereditary nobility, and proclaimed freedom of trade and the press. Its work culminated in the Constitution of 1791, establishing a constitutional monarchy with an elected Legislative Assembly.

End

By the self-denying ordinance proposed by Robespierre, members of the Constituent Assembly were barred from sitting in its successor. The Assembly's final sessions in September 1791 closed the first, reforming phase of the Revolution; few of its compromises survived the radicalisation that followed the Flight to Varennes and war in 1792.
