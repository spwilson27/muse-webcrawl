Melanin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Melanin (from Greek μέλας melas, "black, dark") is a family of biomolecules organized as oligomers or polymers, which among other functions provide the pigments of many organisms. Melanin pigments are produced in melanosomes within specialised cells called melanocytes. Although many animals and plants contain melanins, in vertebrates the melanin group is best known for colouring skin, hair and eyes.

Types

The main forms of melanin are:

  • Eumelanin — a brown-to-black pigment, the most common form, built from 5,6-dihydroxyindole units derived from the amino acid tyrosine.
  • Pheomelanin — a red-to-yellow pigment containing sulfur (benzothiazine units), responsible for red hair and freckles.
  • Neuromelanin — a dark pigment found in certain neurons of the brain, notably in the substantia nigra and locus coeruleus.
  • Allomelanins — nitrogen-free melanins found in plants and fungi.

Function

Melanin absorbs ultraviolet radiation and dissipates it as heat, protecting underlying tissues from UV damage; human skin pigmentation (tanning) is an adaptive increase in eumelanin production after sun exposure. In the eye, melanin in the iris and retinal pigment epithelium reduces stray light. Melanin also binds ions and some drugs and toxins, and acts as a free-radical scavenger. Congenital absence of melanin production due to defects in the enzyme tyrosinase causes oculocutaneous albinism.

Biosynthesis

Melanogenesis begins with the oxidation of L-tyrosine to L-DOPA and then dopaquinone by the enzyme tyrosinase; dopaquinone is then converted along divergent pathways into eumelanin or, in the presence of cysteine, pheomelanin. Production is regulated in humans by melanocyte-stimulating hormones acting through the MC1R receptor — variants of which are associated with red hair and fair skin.
