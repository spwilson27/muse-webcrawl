Florilegium

In medieval and early modern Latin literature, a florilegium (plural: florilegia) is a compilation of excerpts or extracts from other writings. The word comes from the Latin flos (flower) and legere (to gather): a florilegium is literally a "gathering of flowers" of wisdom, analogous to the Greek anthology (from anthos, flower). Florilegia were systematic collections of excerpts from the writings of authoritative authors — above all the Church Fathers and classical philosophers — assembled for the convenience of readers who could not consult the full works.

History

The genre developed in late antiquity and flourished throughout the Middle Ages. Early examples include compilations of sayings of the Desert Fathers, such as the Apophthegmata Patrum, and John of Damascus's Sacra Parallela. In the Byzantine Empire, florilegia became major instruments of theological controversy: doctrinal florilegia assembled patristic proof-texts on Christology and other disputed questions, and the excerpts themselves acquired authority, sometimes being cited from florilegia rather than from the original works.

In the Latin West, florilegia ranged from the collections of sententiae of Isidore of Seville to the great twelfth-century compilations that supported scholastic theology and canon law, such as Ivo of Chartres's collections and, indirectly, Peter Lombard's Sentences. Later medieval and Renaissance florilegia collected moral maxims, proverbs, classical anecdotes and poetic excerpts; famous printed examples include the Polyanthea of Domenico Nani Mirabelli (first published 1503), a vast alphabetical compendium that went through many editions.

Usage

Florilegia served preachers, teachers, students and controversialists. Because manuscripts were expensive and libraries small, a well-made florilegium put a portable library of authorities at the reader's elbow. The genre influenced the development of the commonplace book in the Renaissance, in which readers copied out striking passages under topical headings for later reuse.
