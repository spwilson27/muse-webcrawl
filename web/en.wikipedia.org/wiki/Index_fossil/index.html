Index fossil

Index fossils (also known as guide fossils or indicator fossils) are fossils used to define and identify geologic periods and to correlate rock layers (strata) across different locations. An index fossil must have a short vertical (temporal) range, wide geographic distribution, and rapid evolutionary changes, and it should be abundant and easily identifiable in the fossil record.

Principles

The use of index fossils rests on the principle of faunal succession, developed by the English engineer and geologist William Smith in the early 19th century. Smith observed that particular fossil assemblages succeed one another in a consistent order in the rock record, so that strata containing the same fossil species were deposited at broadly the same time, even when found far apart.

Examples

Trilobites are classic index fossils for Palaeozoic rocks, with genera such as Paradoxides characteristic of the Cambrian. Ammonites serve as the principal index fossils for Mesozoic marine strata because they evolved rapidly and are widespread. In Cenozoic deposits, foraminifera and other microfossils are used extensively, particularly by the petroleum industry, to date and correlate drill cores. Graptolites guide correlation of Ordovician and Silurian rocks, while certain brachiopods, conodonts and pollen grains are also widely used.

Applications

Index fossils are fundamental to biostratigraphy, the branch of stratigraphy that orders rock units into biozones. Combining several index fossils into assemblage zones allows geologists to correlate strata between continents and to build the geologic time scale. The method complements radiometric dating: fossils give relative ages and fine subdivisions, while isotopic measurements anchor those subdivisions in absolute time.
