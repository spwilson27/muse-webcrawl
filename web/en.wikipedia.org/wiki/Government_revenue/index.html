Government revenue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Taxation is the principal source of government revenue in most countries.

Government revenue is the money received by a government from taxes and other sources to finance government expenditure. Total government revenue together with government spending determines the fiscal balance: when revenue exceeds spending the government runs a surplus, and when spending exceeds revenue it runs a deficit, financed by borrowing and adding to the public debt.

Sources of revenue

Tax revenue is the dominant source in modern states and includes income tax on individuals and corporations, indirect taxes such as value-added tax and sales tax, excise duties on goods like fuel, alcohol, and tobacco, import duties, and property taxes. Non-tax revenue includes fees and charges for public services, fines, income from state-owned enterprises and sovereign assets, royalties on natural resources, and seigniorage. Some resource-rich states derive most revenue from oil and gas sales rather than taxation; in rentier states such as the Gulf monarchies, hydrocarbon income has historically funded the bulk of the budget.

Revenue and GDP

Government revenue as a share of gross domestic product varies widely: it exceeds 40 percent in much of Western Europe, where it finances extensive welfare states, while it is closer to 15–20 percent in many developing economies, where informal activity and weak administration constrain collection. Tax-to-GDP ratios are a standard measure of state capacity and fiscal space.

Revenue administration

Revenue is collected by agencies such as a ministry of finance, an internal revenue service, and customs authorities. Governments also raise funds by issuing bonds, but borrowing is conventionally treated as financing rather than revenue, since it must be repaid.
