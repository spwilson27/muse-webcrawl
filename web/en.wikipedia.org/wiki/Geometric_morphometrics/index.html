Geometric morphometrics

Landmarks digitised on a specimen

Geometric morphometrics is a method for the quantitative analysis of biological shape (form) based on the Cartesian coordinates of anatomical landmarks. Unlike traditional morphometrics, which analyses measurements such as lengths and ratios, geometric morphometrics retains the full geometry of the landmark configuration throughout the analysis, allowing shape differences to be visualised directly.

Landmarks and Procrustes analysis

Landmarks are discrete anatomical points — such as the tip of a tooth cusp or the junction of skull sutures — recorded in two or three dimensions from photographs, scans or digitisers. Specimens are then aligned by Generalised Procrustes Analysis, which translates, rotates and scales configurations to remove differences in position, orientation and size, leaving pure shape variables. Centroid size, the scaling measure, is retained as an independent estimate of size.

Visualising shape change

Shape differences are commonly displayed as thin-plate spline deformation grids, a technique derived from D'Arcy Thompson's transformation grids and formalised by Fred Bookstein. Multivariate methods such as principal component analysis and canonical variate analysis summarise the main patterns of variation among specimens or species.

Applications

The approach is standard in evolutionary biology, palaeontology, anthropology, developmental biology and systematics — for studying skull evolution in hominins, wing shape in insects, or shell form in molluscs — and has extensions using semilandmarks for curves and surfaces, and applications in medicine and forensics.
