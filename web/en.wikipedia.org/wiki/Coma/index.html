A comatose patient in intensive care

A coma is a deep state of prolonged unconsciousness in which a person cannot be awakened, fails to respond normally to painful stimuli, light, or sound, lacks a normal wake–sleep cycle, and does not initiate voluntary actions. A person in a state of coma is described as comatose. The word derives from the Greek κῶμα (koma), meaning "deep sleep".

Causes

Coma can result from many conditions, including traumatic brain injury, stroke, brain tumours, drug or alcohol intoxication, metabolic disturbances such as diabetic ketoacidosis or liver failure, infections of the central nervous system, and prolonged hypoxia. A coma generally results from dysfunction of the cerebral hemispheres or of the reticular activating system in the brainstem.

Assessment

Depth of coma is commonly graded with the Glasgow Coma Scale, which scores eye, verbal, and motor responses from 3 to 15; a score of 8 or below generally indicates coma. Clinicians also assess pupil responses, reflexes, breathing pattern, and imaging findings to locate the cause.

Management and prognosis

Initial management focuses on stabilising airway, breathing, and circulation, checking blood glucose, and treating reversible causes. Prognosis depends on the underlying cause, depth, and duration; outcomes range from full recovery to persistent vegetative state, minimally conscious state, or brain death. Coma rarely lasts longer than several weeks, though states of reduced consciousness can persist far longer.
