Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

The Fugitive Slave Act was passed by the United States Congress on 18 September 1850 as part of the Compromise of 1850. It required that enslaved people who escaped be returned to their enslavers, even if found in a free state, and obliged citizens and officials in free states to assist in their capture.


Enacted: 18 September 1850
Part of: Compromise of 1850
Repealed: 1864

Provisions

Alleged fugitives were denied jury trials and could not testify; federal commissioners were paid more for ruling for claimants than against them. Penalties punished those who aided escapees, greatly expanding federal enforcement in the North.

Resistance and effects

The law intensified abolitionist resistance, personal-liberty laws, rescues such as that of Shadrach Minkins, and the work of the Underground Railroad; many Black people fled to Canada. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was partly a response. The act deepened sectional conflict leading to the Civil War. See Frontier thesis.
