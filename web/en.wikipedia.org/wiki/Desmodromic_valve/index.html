Desmodromic valve

A desmodromic valve is a reciprocating engine poppet valve that is positively closed by a cam and leverage system, rather than by a spring. In a conventional valvetrain, a cam opens the valve and a spring closes it; at very high engine speeds the spring cannot close the valve quickly enough, causing "valve float". In a desmodromic system (from the Greek desmos, "bond", and dromos, "course" or "track"), separate cam lobes and rocker arms both open and close the valve, tying its motion positively to the camshaft.

History and use

Desmodromic designs were patented as early as the 1890s and appeared in racing engines in the 1910s–1950s, including designs by Delage and the Mercedes-Benz W196 Grand Prix car of 1954–55. The system is most famously associated with the Italian motorcycle manufacturer Ducati, whose engineer Fabio Taglioni developed desmodromic heads for Ducati's racing singles in 1956 and the 250 Desmo of 1958. Ducati has used "Desmo" valvetrains across its production range ever since, making it a signature technology; most other manufacturers abandoned the idea as spring materials and pneumatic valve systems improved.

Advantages and disadvantages

Positive closing eliminates valve float, permits more aggressive cam profiles, and avoids the parasitic power loss of stiff springs. Against this, desmodromic systems are complex and costly to manufacture, require precise adjustment of both opening and closing clearances, and are heavier. Ducati's system typically retains light closing springs to seat the valve at rest, but these play no role at speed.
