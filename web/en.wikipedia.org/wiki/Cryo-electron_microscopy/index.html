Cryo-electron microscopy

Cryo-electron microscopy
Abbreviation: cryo-EM
Nobel Prize: Chemistry, 2017
Laureates: Dubochet, Frank, Henderson

Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a form of transmission electron microscopy in which samples are studied at cryogenic temperatures, typically in vitreous ice. Single-particle analysis reconstructs three-dimensional structures of proteins and viruses at near-atomic resolution without crystals.

History

Richard Henderson obtained the first structure of bacteriorhodopsin by electron crystallography in 1975. Jacques Dubochet developed vitrification — plunge-freezing water so fast it forms amorphous ice — and Joachim Frank developed image-processing methods to average thousands of particle images. The three shared the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Method

Purified sample is applied to a grid, blotted and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. A microscope with a direct electron detector records movies of particles in many orientations; software classifies and aligns them to build a density map into which an atomic model is fitted. Direct detectors and better algorithms caused the "resolution revolution" of the 2010s.

Applications

Cryo-EM revealed structures of the ribosome, membrane proteins, amyloid fibrils and the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, and is now central to structural biology and drug design. Cryo-electron tomography extends the method to cells.
