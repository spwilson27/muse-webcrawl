Roof garden

A roof garden is a garden on the roof of a building. Besides the decorative benefit, roof gardens may provide food growing space, temperature control, hydrological benefits, habitat for wildlife, and recreational space. When the planted layer is primarily intended for environmental performance rather than access and use, it is generally called a green roof.

History

Planted roofs are ancient. The ziggurats of Mesopotamia had plantings on their terraces, and the Hanging Gardens of Babylon — one of the Seven Wonders of the World — were, in legend, a mountain of planted terraces irrigated by pumps. Sod roofs were common in Scandinavia and Iceland for insulation. In the twentieth century, modernist architects championed roof gardens: Le Corbusier included them among his "five points of architecture", and notable early examples include the gardens atop New York apartment buildings and department stores. Kensington Roof Gardens in London, laid out in the 1930s with themed gardens including flamingos, became a famous example.

Types and construction

Extensive green roofs have a thin growing medium (about 5–15 cm), lightweight low plants such as sedums and grasses, and are usually not accessible. Intensive roof gardens have deeper soil, support shrubs and even small trees, and are designed for people to use, but impose heavy loads requiring strong structures. A typical build-up, from the deck upward, comprises waterproofing, a root barrier, a drainage layer, a filter fleece, the growing medium (engineered to be lighter than topsoil), and vegetation. Irrigation is often needed, especially during establishment.

Benefits

Roof gardens insulate buildings, reducing heating and cooling costs; retain rainwater, easing pressure on storm drains during heavy rain; extend the life of roof membranes by shielding them from ultraviolet light and temperature extremes; mitigate the urban heat island effect; and create habitat for insects, birds, and plants in dense cities. They can also provide amenity space and community or commercial urban agriculture — rooftop farms operate in cities such as New York and Paris.

Challenges

Costs of installation and maintenance are higher than for conventional roofs, structural capacity limits design, and leaks, though rare in well-built systems, can be difficult to locate and repair. Wind exposure, shallow soil, and drought stress the planting, so species selection favours tough, drought-tolerant plants in extensive systems.

Many cities now encourage or mandate green roofs: Toronto adopted a green roof bylaw in 2009, and German cities such as Stuttgart have supported roof greening for decades; by the early twenty-first century Germany alone had well over a hundred million square metres of green roofs.
