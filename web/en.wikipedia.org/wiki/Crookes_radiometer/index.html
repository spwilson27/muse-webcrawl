Crookes radiometer
Inventor: William Crookes
Year: 1873
Also called: Light mill

The Crookes radiometer, also known as a light mill, is a glass bulb containing a partial vacuum and a set of vanes mounted on a spindle. The vanes rotate when exposed to light. It was invented in 1873 by chemist Sir William Crookes while measuring atomic weights with a sensitive balance disturbed by light.

Operation

Each vane is black on one side and polished metal on the other. In bright light the rotor spins, black sides retreating from the light. Crookes initially attributed this to radiation pressure.

Explanation

Radiation pressure is real but far too weak. The accepted explanation involves thermal transpiration and gas molecules: the black side warms, gas molecules leave it faster, and edge effects described by Osborne Reynolds and James Clerk Maxwell produce the net force. In a hard vacuum the mill stops; in too much gas, drag stops it.

Uses

Today it is sold as a novelty and used in physics teaching to discuss thermodynamics, kinetic theory and the history of the photon pressure concept behind solar sails.
