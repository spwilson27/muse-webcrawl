Leica Camera AG is a German manufacturer of cameras, optical lenses, photographic lenses, binoculars, rifle scopes and microscopes. The company was founded by Ernst Leitz in 1869 in Wetzlar as a maker of microscopes. The name Leica combines Leitz with camera. Leica's 35mm cameras defined photojournalism in the 20th century, and the red-dot logo remains a status symbol in photography.

The first 35mm camera

In 1913–14 the Leitz precision mechanic Oskar Barnack built the Ur-Leica, a compact still camera using 35mm cine film in a double-frame (24×36mm) format, with a collapsible Elmar lens designed by Max Berek. Delayed by the First World War, it entered production as the Leica I in 1925 and created the market for miniature cameras. The Leica II (1932) added a built-in rangefinder; the Leica III series added slow shutter speeds, and screw-mount lenses (LTM) were interchangeable across the range.

The M system

The Leica M3 of 1954 introduced the bayonet M mount, a bright-line viewfinder with automatically selected framelines, and combined rangefinder/viewfinder — a design language the digital M cameras retain. The M became the camera of Magnum photographers and defined reportage: Henri Cartier-Bresson, Robert Capa, Alfred Eisenstaedt and many others worked with Leicas. Film M models ran through the M7 (2002) and continue with the M6 reissue and the MP/MA; digital models began with the M8 (2006) and include the full-frame M9, M (Typ 240), M10 and M11 families, including the screen-less M-D and the monochrome-only Monochrom variants.

Other lines

Leica produced the reflex Leicaflex and R series (1964–2009), the compact Minilux and later C, D-Lux and V-Lux lines (many developed with Panasonic), and since 2014 the mirrorless L-mount system (T/TL, CL, and the full-frame SL series) shared with Panasonic and Sigma in the L-Mount Alliance. The medium-format S system, instant Sofort cameras, and the joint smartphone with Sharp/Leitz (Leitz Phone) extend the brand. Leica Geosystems (surveying) and Leica Microsystems became separate companies in the 1990s restructuring.

Company history

The Leitz family firm grew into one of Germany's great optical houses, employing forced labour during the Second World War yet also — as later research documented — helping many Jewish employees and others escape Nazi Germany (the so-called "Leica Freedom Train"). Renamed Leica in the 1980s, floated and re-privatised in the 2000s under investor Andreas Kaufmann (ACM), the company returned to profitability on the strength of the M and S systems and its luxury positioning, with Leica Stores and Galleries worldwide.
