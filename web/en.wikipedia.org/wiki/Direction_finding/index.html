Direction finding

FieldRadio navigation, signals intelligence
MethodsDirectional antennae, Doppler, interferometry, time difference of arrival
ApplicationsNavigation, search and rescue, wildlife tracking, military intelligence

Direction finding (DF), or radio direction finding (RDF), is the measurement of the direction from which a received signal was transmitted. Combining bearings from two or more stations allows the source to be located by triangulation. The technique has been used since the early 20th century for navigation and intelligence.

Methods

Early direction finders used rotating loop antennae and the Bellini–Tosi goniometer to find the bearing of maximum or minimum signal. Later systems use fixed arrays of antennae with techniques such as Watson-Watt phase comparison, Doppler direction finding, and correlative interferometry, achieving accuracies of a degree or better.

Applications

Radio direction finding was a key navigation aid for ships and aircraft, and airport DF stations guided pilots long before radar and satellite navigation. In both world wars, networks of DF stations — such as the Allied "huff-duff" (HF/DF) system — located enemy transmitters, notably U-boats. Today DF is used in search and rescue homing on emergency beacons, in wildlife radio-tracking, in spectrum enforcement, and in signals intelligence.
