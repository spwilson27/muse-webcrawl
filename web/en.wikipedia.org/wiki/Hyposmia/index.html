Hyposmia

Hyposmia, or microsmia, is a reduced ability to smell and to detect odours. A related condition is anosmia, the complete inability to detect odours. Hyposmia may be temporary or permanent and is classified among the quantitative olfactory disorders, alongside anosmia and hyperosmia; qualitative disorders include parosmia (distorted smell) and phantosmia (smelling odours that are not present).

Causes

Common causes include upper respiratory tract infections and inflammation of the nasal passages and sinuses (rhinosinusitis, often with nasal polyps), which obstruct airflow to or damage the olfactory epithelium, and allergic rhinitis. Post-viral hyposmia, including after influenza and COVID-19, is a well-recognised cause. Other causes are head injury damaging the olfactory nerve filaments, exposure to toxic chemicals, certain medications, ageing, and congenital conditions such as Kallmann syndrome. Reduced smell is also an early feature of several neurodegenerative diseases, notably Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease, where it may precede motor or cognitive symptoms by years.

Assessment and treatment

Smell can be assessed with standardised tests such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) or Sniffin' Sticks. Treatment depends on the cause: anti-inflammatory treatment or surgery for sinonasal disease, and olfactory training — repeated structured exposure to strong odours — which has evidence of benefit for post-infectious loss. Because smell contributes greatly to flavour perception and to the detection of hazards such as smoke, gas, and spoiled food, hyposmia can affect appetite, nutrition, safety, and quality of life.
