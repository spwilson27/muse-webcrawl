Robert Burns (25 January 1759 – 21 July 1796), also known familiarly as Rabbie Burns, was a Scottish poet and lyricist. He is widely regarded as the national poet of Scotland and is celebrated worldwide. A pioneer of the Romantic movement, he wrote in both Scots and English, producing poems and folk songs — among them "Auld Lang Syne", sung around the world at New Year — that became lasting emblems of Scottish culture. His birthday, 25 January, is marked by Burns suppers wherever Scots gather.

Life

Burns was born in Alloway, Ayrshire, the eldest son of a tenant farmer who valued education and arranged tutoring for his children. Burns farmed at Mossgiel and later Ellisland while writing the poems that made his name. His first collection, Poems, Chiefly in the Scottish Dialect (the "Kilmarnock volume", 1786), was an immediate success; a planned emigration to Jamaica was abandoned, and an Edinburgh edition of 1787 made him famous as the "ploughman poet". He married Jean Armour in 1788, settled at Ellisland farm near Dumfries, and from 1789 combined farming with work as an excise officer. His health failed in the 1790s, and he died at Dumfries in 1796, aged thirty-seven.

Works

Burns's poetry mixes lyric tenderness, satire and humour: "To a Mouse" (1785), whose line "the best laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley" supplied Steinbeck's title; "Tam o' Shanter" (1790), a comic-gothic narrative poem; "A Man's a Man for A' That", a hymn to human equality; and love songs such as "A Red, Red Rose" and "Ae Fond Kiss". He also devoted his last years to collecting and refining Scottish folk song for James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum and George Thomson's collections, contributing or reworking some 300 songs, among them "Scots Wha Hae" and "Auld Lang Syne", adapted from an older ballad.

Legacy

Burns's egalitarian and republican sympathies made him an icon far beyond Scotland; he was admired by Abraham Lincoln and celebrated in the Soviet Union. Burns suppers — with haggis, whisky, recitations and the "Address to a Haggis" — began within a few years of his death. Statues of Burns stand in towns across the world, and his face has appeared on Scottish banknotes. Modern scholarship, including the Oxford edition of his works, continues to reassess the self-educated farmer-poet whose vernacular voice reshaped British poetry.
