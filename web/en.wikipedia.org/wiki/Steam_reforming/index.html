Steam reforming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Steam reforming or steam methane reforming (SMR) is a method for producing syngas (hydrogen and carbon monoxide) by reaction of hydrocarbons with water. Commonly natural gas is the feedstock. The main purpose of this technology is hydrogen production. The reaction is strongly endothermic (requires heat).

Reactions

The steam reforming of natural gas proceeds in two main steps. First, methane reacts with steam over a nickel catalyst at high temperature (700–1,000 °C) and pressure:

CH4 + H2O ⇌ CO + 3 H2   (ΔH = +206 kJ/mol)

Second, the water–gas shift reaction converts carbon monoxide with additional steam to give more hydrogen:

CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2   (ΔH = −41 kJ/mol)

Process description

In industrial practice, desulfurised natural gas is mixed with steam and passed through tubes filled with nickel catalyst inside a fired furnace (the reformer). The resulting syngas is cooled, shifted, and purified, commonly by pressure swing adsorption, to yield high-purity hydrogen. Steam reforming is the cheapest and most widely used route to hydrogen, supplying hydrogen for petroleum refining, ammonia synthesis (via the Haber process), and methanol production.

Carbon emissions

Because the carbon in the feedstock is released as carbon dioxide, steam reforming of natural gas is a major industrial source of CO2 emissions; hydrogen made this way without carbon capture is sometimes called "grey hydrogen". Adding carbon capture and storage yields "blue hydrogen".
