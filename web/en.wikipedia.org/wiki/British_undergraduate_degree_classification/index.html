British undergraduate degree classification

The British undergraduate degree classification system is a grading structure for undergraduate degrees awarded by universities in the United Kingdom. Honours degrees are classified into four main classes — first, second (divided into upper and lower divisions), and third — based on the overall mark achieved by the student.

Classifications

Third-class honours3rd40–49%
Ordinary (pass) degree—Below honours standard

Universities set their own algorithms for combining module marks, weighting later years of study more heavily and applying borderline rules. The exact percentage boundaries are conventions rather than national regulations, and marking scales vary between institutions and disciplines; marks above 80% are rare in many humanities subjects, while higher marks are more common in mathematics and sciences.

Trends

The proportion of first-class degrees has risen substantially over recent decades: from around 7% of graduates in the mid-1990s to over 30% by the late 2010s, prompting debate about grade inflation. The share fell back following regulator pressure and the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, during which "no detriment" policies had temporarily boosted results. The upper second (2:1) is the most common classification and is the usual minimum requirement for graduate schemes and postgraduate study.

History

The classified honours system developed at the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, beginning with the Cambridge Mathematical Tripos, whose candidates were ranked in order of merit. The modern four-class structure spread throughout British higher education during the twentieth century.
