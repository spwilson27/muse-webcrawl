Neurocognitive

Neurocognitive functions are cognitive functions closely linked to the function of particular areas, neural pathways, or cortical networks in the brain, ultimately served by the substrate of the brain's neurological matrix, which changes over time as levels of neurocognitive function decline or, in development, increase. Processes within the brain that affect the ability to think, remember, and concentrate are neurocognitive functions. The term is most often used in neuropsychology and cognitive neuroscience, and in clinical contexts such as the diagnosis of neurocognitive disorders.

Domains

Neurocognitive assessment typically distinguishes several domains, which map roughly onto the six key domains used in diagnostic criteria for neurocognitive disorders:

  • Complex attention — sustained, divided, and selective attention and processing speed.
  • Executive function — planning, decision making, working memory, inhibition, and mental flexibility.
  • Learning and memory — immediate and delayed recall and recognition memory.
  • Language — expressive and receptive language, naming, fluency, grammar, and syntax.
  • Perceptual-motor function — visual perception, visuoconstructional reasoning, and perceptual-motor coordination.
  • Social cognition — recognition of emotions and theory of mind.

Assessment

Neurocognitive performance is measured with standardised tests administered by neuropsychologists, ranging from brief screens such as the Mini–Mental State Examination and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment to full batteries assessing each domain. Results are compared with age- and education-matched norms, and impairments are graded as mild or major depending on their effect on everyday functioning.

Disorders

The DSM-5 replaced the term dementia with major neurocognitive disorder and introduced mild neurocognitive disorder for earlier stages of decline. Causes include Alzheimer's disease, vascular disease, traumatic brain injury, HIV infection, substance use, and other medical conditions. Neurocognitive deficits are also core features of schizophrenia and other psychiatric disorders, where they strongly predict functional outcome.
