Shadow banking system

The shadow banking system is a term for the collection of non-bank financial intermediaries that provide services similar to those of traditional commercial banks but outside normal banking regulations. It includes entities such as hedge funds, money market funds, structured investment vehicles (SIVs), broker-dealers, and other non-bank financial institutions that engage in maturity transformation — borrowing short-term in liquid markets and lending long-term in less liquid ones — without access to central bank liquidity or public deposit insurance.

The term was coined by economist Paul McCulley of PIMCO in 2007 and gained wide attention in connection with the subprime mortgage crisis and the 2008 financial crisis, in which the failure or near-failure of several large shadow banking entities played a central role.

Structure and activities

Shadow banking performs credit intermediation through a chain of securitization and secured funding techniques. Typical activities include:

  • Securitization — pooling loans (mortgages, auto loans, credit card receivables) into asset-backed securities sold to investors.
  • Repurchase agreements (repos) — very short-term collateralized borrowing used by broker-dealers to fund inventories of securities.
  • Money market funds — funds that invest in short-term debt and offer shares redeemable on demand, functioning like uninsured deposit accounts.
  • Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP) conduits and SIVs — off-balance-sheet vehicles that funded long-term assets with short-term paper.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

Because shadow banks relied on wholesale funding that could be withdrawn quickly, they were subject to runs analogous to bank runs but without lender-of-last-resort protections. The collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 triggered a run on money market funds after the Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck," and forced emergency interventions by the US Federal Reserve and Treasury. Economists such as Gary Gorton have argued that the crisis was fundamentally a "run on repo" in the shadow banking system.

Scale and regulation

The Financial Stability Board estimated the global shadow banking sector at over $50 trillion before the crisis, and its broader "non-bank financial intermediation" measure exceeded $200 trillion in the early 2020s. Post-crisis reforms — including the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States, money market fund reforms, and FSB monitoring — sought to reduce the risks, though regulators continue to debate vulnerabilities in less-regulated corners such as private credit funds.
