Criminal law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Criminal law is the body of law that relates to crime. It prescribes conduct perceived as threatening, harmful or otherwise endangering to the property, health, safety and moral welfare of people, inclusive of one's self. Criminal law includes the punishment and rehabilitation of people who violate such laws, and varies according to jurisdiction.

Elements and procedure

Most systems require two elements for an offence: the actus reus (a prohibited act or omission) and the mens rea (a culpable mental state — intention, knowledge, recklessness or negligence — varying by offence). Some offences impose strict liability without mens rea. The prosecution bears the burden of proof — in most common-law systems, proof "beyond reasonable doubt" — and the accused enjoys protections including the Presumption of innocence, rights to silence, counsel and a fair and public trial, and protection against double jeopardy and retrospective criminal law. Outcomes range from fines and community orders to imprisonment and, in retentionist jurisdictions, Capital punishment.

Objectives

Theories of punishment include retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation and restoration to victims. Criminal law is distinguished from civil law, whose concern is dispute resolution and compensation; the same conduct may generate both a prosecution and a private claim. Sources include statutes and codes (in civil-law countries, comprehensive penal codes), case law, and international criminal law governing genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity, enforced by the International Criminal Court.

History

Early codes such as the Code of Hammurabi graded punishments by status and offence; Roman law, canon law and local custom shaped medieval practice, which included ordeals and corporal punishments. Enlightenment reformers, above all Cesare Beccaria (On Crimes and Punishments, 1764), argued for proportionate, certain and public punishments, driving modern codification and the decline of torture and public execution in many countries.
