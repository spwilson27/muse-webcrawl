Large intestine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The large intestine, also called the large bowel, is the last part of the gastrointestinal tract. About 1.5 metres long in adults, it absorbs water and electrolytes from indigestible food residue, hosts trillions of gut microbes, and forms, stores and expels faeces. It frames the coiled Small intestine on three sides.

Structure

From its start at the caecum — to which the appendix is attached — the large intestine continues as the ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon, then the rectum and anal canal. Unlike the small intestine it has no villi; its wall is gathered into pouches (haustra) by three longitudinal muscle bands (taeniae coli).

Function and microbiota

Bacteria in the colon ferment some remaining carbohydrates, producing gases and short-chain fatty acids the body can use, and synthesise vitamin K and some B vitamins. Water absorption turns liquid chyme into semi-solid faeces. Disorders of the large intestine include ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease (together, inflammatory bowel disease), diverticulitis, polyps and colorectal cancer, commonly screened for by colonoscopy.
