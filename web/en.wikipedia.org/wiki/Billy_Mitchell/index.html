William Lendrum Mitchell (29 December 1879 – 19 February 1936), known as Billy Mitchell, was a United States Army officer and airman widely regarded as the father of the United States Air Force. A senior American air commander in the First World War, he became the most prominent and outspoken advocate of air power in the interwar years, arguing that aircraft had revolutionised warfare and that an independent air force was essential.

Career

Born in Nice to an American senator's family and raised in Milwaukee, Mitchell enlisted during the Spanish–American War and joined the Signal Corps. In France in 1917–18 he commanded American air operations, planning large coordinated air actions at Saint-Mihiel and the Meuse-Argonne, and was promoted to brigadier general. After the war, as assistant chief of the Air Service, he staged celebrated demonstrations in 1921 in which his bombers sank captured warships, including the German battleship Ostfriesland, to prove that aircraft could sink capital ships.

Court-martial and legacy

Mitchell's increasingly public attacks on the Army and Navy leadership — culminating in a 1925 statement accusing them of "incompetency, criminal negligence, and almost treasonable administration" after the crash of the airship Shenandoah — led to his court-martial for insubordination. Convicted and suspended from duty, he resigned in 1926. Many of his predictions — the vulnerability of navies to air attack, the decisive role of strategic bombing, and even a Japanese air attack on Pearl Harbor — were vindicated in the Second World War. Congress posthumously restored his promotion and awarded him a special Congressional Gold Medal in 1946; the North American B-25 Mitchell bomber was named in his honour, and he is widely commemorated as a prophet of air power.
