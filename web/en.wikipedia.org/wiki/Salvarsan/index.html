Salvarsan

Salvarsan, also known as arsphenamine or compound 606, was the first effective treatment for syphilis and the first modern chemotherapeutic agent. It is an organoarsenic compound discovered in 1907 in the Frankfurt laboratory of Paul Ehrlich and marketed from 1910 by Hoechst. Salvarsan inaugurated the era of rational drug discovery by systematic screening of chemical compounds, realising Ehrlich's idea of a "magic bullet" that would kill a pathogen without destroying the host.

Discovery

Ehrlich's team, including Alfred Bertheim and Sahachiro Hata, systematically tested hundreds of arsenical compounds against the syphilis spirochaete Treponema pallidum. Preparation number 606 proved effective in infected rabbits and, in human trials, cured syphilis patients, though administration was difficult—it had to be freshly dissolved and injected intravenously—and side effects could be severe. A more soluble and easier-to-use successor, Neosalvarsan (compound 914), followed in 1912.

Significance

Salvarsan dominated syphilis therapy until penicillin replaced it in the 1940s. For decades its structure was drawn as an arsenic–arsenic double-bonded dimer by analogy with azobenzene; mass spectrometric work published in 2005 showed it is actually a mixture of cyclic trimers and pentamers with single As–As bonds. Ehrlich's screening approach, combining synthetic chemistry with biological testing, became the template for the pharmaceutical industry, and Salvarsan is widely regarded as the first blockbuster drug and the founding achievement of chemotherapy.
