Cumulus cloud

AbbreviationCu
GenusCumulus (heap, pile)
Altitude200–2,000 m (660–6,600 ft)

Cumulus clouds are clouds that have flat bases and are often described as looking like floating cotton balls. Their name derives from the Latin cumulus, meaning "heap" or "pile". Cumulus clouds are low-level clouds, generally less than 2,000 metres (6,600 ft) in altitude unless they are the more vertical cumulus congestus form. Clouds of the cumulus genus may appear alone, in lines, or in clusters.

Cumulus clouds are often precursors of other types of clouds, such as cumulonimbus, when influenced by weather factors such as instability, humidity, and a sufficient temperature gradient. Normally, cumulus clouds produce little or no precipitation, but they can grow into the precipitation-bearing cumulus congestus or cumulonimbus clouds. When cumulus clouds do produce precipitation, it is usually light, and the clouds themselves are called fair-weather cumulus or cumulus humilis.

Formation

Cumulus clouds are formed by convection. Air warmed by a heated surface rises; as it rises, it cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate until it reaches the lifting condensation level, where water vapour begins to condense into visible droplets. Because the rising air in a thermal is fairly uniform, the base of the cloud is flat, marking the condensation level, while the top continues to billow upward as long as the air inside remains warmer than its surroundings.

Species and varieties

The World Meteorological Organization recognizes several species of cumulus cloud:

  • Cumulus humilis – fair-weather cumulus; wider than they are tall, with little vertical development.
  • Cumulus mediocris – moderate vertical development, roughly as tall as they are wide.
  • Cumulus congestus – towering cumulus with sharp outlines and great vertical extent; may produce showers.
  • Cumulus fractus – ragged, fragmented cumulus with irregular shapes.

Forecasting significance

Morning cumulus humilis generally indicates fair weather. If cumulus grow taller through the day, atmospheric instability is increasing and thunderstorms may develop later as clouds evolve into cumulonimbus. Pilots take note of cumulus because the rising air beneath them produces turbulence, while glider pilots exploit the same thermals for lift.
