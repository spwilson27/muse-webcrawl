Catechism

A catechism (from Ancient Greek κατηχέω, "to teach orally") is a summary or exposition of doctrine, traditionally used in Christian religious teaching from New Testament times to the present. Catechisms are doctrinal manuals, often in the form of questions followed by answers to be memorised — a format that has been adopted for teaching in other fields as well.

History

The early Church instructed converts (catechumens) orally before baptism. Printed catechisms proliferated during the Reformation and Counter-Reformation: Martin Luther published his Large and Small Catechisms in 1529, and the Council of Trent commissioned the Roman Catechism (1566), the first universal catechism of the Catholic Church. Other famous examples include the Heidelberg Catechism (1563) of the Reformed churches, the Westminster Shorter Catechism (1647), and Peter Canisius's catechisms.

The Catechism of the Catholic Church

In 1992 Pope John Paul II promulgated the Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC), a comprehensive compendium of Catholic doctrine intended as a reference for local catechisms worldwide. It is organised in four parts — the Creed, the sacraments, the moral life (the Ten Commandments), and prayer (the Lord's Prayer) — echoing the traditional structure going back to the Roman Catechism.

Form and use

The classic catechetical method combines question-and-answer memorisation with explanation. Catechisms typically cover the Apostles' Creed, the Lord's Prayer, the Ten Commandments, and the sacraments. In extended usage, any question-and-answer manual of fundamentals — a "catechism" of politics, economics, or chemistry — borrows the name.
