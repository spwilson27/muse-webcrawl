Belgian literature

Belgian literature is literature written in Belgium, principally in its two main languages: Dutch (Flemish literature) in the north and French in the south, with a smaller body of work in German and in regional dialects. Because of this linguistic division, Belgian literature is often studied as two parallel traditions united by a shared territory and history.

History

In the Middle Ages the Southern Netherlands produced important Middle Dutch texts, including the works of Hadewijch and the Roman van Walewein. Under Spanish and Austrian Habsburg rule, the region's literary life declined relative to the Dutch Republic, but the 19th century saw a revival. Hendrik Conscience's novel The Lion of Flanders (1838) helped awaken Flemish consciousness and popularised Dutch-language writing in Flanders.

French-language Belgian writing flourished in the late 19th century: the symbolist poets Émile Verhaeren and Maurice Maeterlinck (who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1911) achieved European fame, as did the novelist Georges Rodenbach, whose Bruges-la-Morte (1892) evoked the decaying beauty of Bruges.

Twentieth century and after

Major Flemish prose writers of the 20th century include Willem Elsschot, Louis Paul Boon, and Hugo Claus, whose The Sorrow of Belgium (1983) is considered a landmark. In French, Belgium produced the detective novelist Georges Simenon, creator of Maigret; the surrealist poet Henri Michaux; and Amélie Nothomb, one of the most widely read contemporary francophone authors. Belgium is also a major centre of comics — the bande dessinée tradition of Hergé's Tintin, Franquin, and Peyo's Smurfs.
