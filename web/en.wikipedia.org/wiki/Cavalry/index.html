Cavalry

Cavalry (from the French word cavalerie, itself derived from cheval, "horse") are groups of soldiers or warriors who fight mounted on horseback. Until the 20th century, cavalry were the most mobile of the combat arms, operating as light cavalry in the roles of reconnaissance, screening, and skirmishing in many armies, or as heavy cavalry for decisive shock attacks by others. An individual soldier in the cavalry is known as a trooper, dragoon, lancer, hussar, or cuirassier, depending on the era and role.

Cavalry provided speed, mobility, and shock power on battlefields for over two thousand years.

History

Mounted warfare developed on the Eurasian steppe, where peoples such as the Scythians, Parthians, and later the Mongols built empires around horse archers. In the ancient Mediterranean, Alexander the Great's Companion cavalry delivered decisive charges, while Rome relied increasingly on allied cavalry. The medieval knight dominated European warfare for centuries, combining heavy armour, the couched lance, and the stirrup. Gunpowder gradually reduced the knight's battlefield supremacy, and cavalry evolved into pistol-armed reiters, dragoons who fought dismounted, and light hussars used for scouting and pursuit.

Roles

Cavalry traditionally performed reconnaissance, screening, raiding, pursuit of a broken enemy, and the shock charge against infantry or opposing cavalry. In the Napoleonic Wars, massed cavalry charges at battles such as Eylau and Waterloo could decide engagements, though disciplined infantry in square usually repulsed them.

Decline and modern use

The firepower of rifled weapons, machine guns, and artillery in the First World War made mounted charges against entrenched positions ruinous, though cavalry remained useful on the Eastern Front and in the Middle East. During the Second World War most cavalry units were mechanised, and the name "cavalry" survives today in armoured, reconnaissance, and helicopter (air cavalry) formations, as well as in ceremonial mounted units.
