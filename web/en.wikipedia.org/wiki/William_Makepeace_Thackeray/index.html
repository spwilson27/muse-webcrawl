William Makepeace Thackeray (18 July 1811 – 24 December 1863) was an English novelist, author and illustrator known for his satirical works, particularly his 1847–48 novel Vanity Fair, a panoramic portrait of British society, and the 1844 novel The Luck of Barry Lyndon. He is regarded, with Charles Dickens, as one of the two pre-eminent Victorian novelists.

Early life

Thackeray was born in Calcutta, where his father was a secretary to the East India Company board of revenue. Sent to England as a small child after his father's death, he was educated at Charterhouse and Trinity College, Cambridge, leaving without a degree. He lost much of his inheritance through gambling and failed investments, and turned to journalism and art to support his family.

Journalism and breakthrough

Writing under pseudonyms such as Michael Angelo Titmarsh and George Savage Fitz-Boodle, Thackeray contributed sketches, parodies and travel books (The Paris Sketch Book, The Irish Sketch Book) to magazines including Fraser's and Punch, where his "Snobs of England" papers (1846–47) made his reputation.

Vanity Fair and later novels

Vanity Fair, serialised in monthly parts in 1847–48 with his own illustrations, follows the fortune-hunting Becky Sharp and the gentle Amelia Sedley through the Napoleonic era; subtitled "A Novel without a Hero", it established Thackeray as Dickens's great rival. Later works include Pendennis (1850), the historical novel The History of Henry Esmond (1852), The Newcomes (1855) and The Virginians (1859). In later life he lectured in England and America and edited the Cornhill Magazine. He died suddenly in London on Christmas Eve 1863.
