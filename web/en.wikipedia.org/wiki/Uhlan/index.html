Uhlans (German: Ulanen; Polish: ułani) were originally Polish-Lithuanian light cavalry armed with lances, sabres, and pistols, recognisable by their distinctive square-topped cap, the czapka, derived from Tatar and Polish dress. Their name derives from the Turkic word oğlan ("young man" or "lad"), transmitted through the Lipka Tatars in Polish service.

An uhlan wearing the distinctive czapka, carrying a lance with pennant.

Origins

Uhlan regiments were raised in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in the 18th century. After the Partitions of Poland, uhlan formations were adopted by the Prussian, Austrian, and Russian armies, and the type spread across Europe during the Napoleonic Wars. Frederick the Great raised uhlan units recruited largely from Poles, and the Congress of Vienna's settlement left uhlan regiments throughout the armies of the partitioning powers.

Napoleonic Wars

Polish uhlans in French service, such as the lancers of the Imperial Guard, gained great fame; the very word "lancer" in many armies reflects the uhlan model. Lancers proved effective against infantry squares and in pursuit, and most European armies raised lancer regiments in the decades after 1815.

Later history

Uhlan regiments formed part of the German, Austro-Hungarian, and Russian armies in the First World War, though the lance was progressively abandoned as cavalry became dismounted infantry. In reborn Poland, uhlan (ułani) regiments became symbols of national military tradition and fought in the Polish–Soviet War and in the September Campaign of 1939 — contrary to popular myth, they did not charge German tanks with lances, though the image persisted in propaganda.
