Pneumatic tool

A pneumatic tool, air tool, or air-powered tool is a type of power tool driven by compressed air supplied by an air compressor. Compressed air drives a pneumatic motor—typically a vane or piston motor—or directly actuates the tool, as with nail guns and blow guns. Pneumatic tools are widely used in construction, manufacturing, automotive repair, and mining.

Advantages and disadvantages

Air tools have a high power-to-weight ratio because the heavy power source (the compressor) stays separate from the tool itself; they are durable, have few moving parts, cannot be overloaded easily (they simply stall), and produce no sparks or electric-shock hazard, making them suitable for wet or explosive environments. Against this, they require a compressor and hose, which tethers the user; the compressor and exhaust air are noisy; and efficiency is lower than that of electric tools because of losses in compression and distribution. Cordless battery tools have displaced air tools in many portable applications, while pneumatic tools remain common on factory assembly lines and in tyre shops.

Common types

  • Impact wrench – delivers high torque for loosening and tightening fasteners; iconic in pit crews and garages.
  • Air ratchet, drill, die grinder, and sander – rotary tools for machining and finishing.
  • Nail gun and stapler – drive fasteners with a piston stroke; dominant in framing and roofing.
  • Jackhammer (pneumatic breakers) – hand-held and machine-mounted demolition hammers.
  • Air hammer/chisel, needle scaler, rivet gun, and spray gun – for metalworking and painting.

History

Pneumatic power was developed in the nineteenth century for rock drilling in mines and tunnel projects such as the Mont Cenis Tunnel (1860s), and pneumatic drills and hammers spread through industry thereafter. The pneumatic riveting hammer was essential to steel construction and shipbuilding, and air tools became standard in twentieth-century mass production.
