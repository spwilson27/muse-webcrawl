Metronome

A metronome is a device that produces an audible click or other sound at a regular interval that can be set by the user, typically in beats per minute (BPM). Musicians use it while practising to maintain a consistent tempo. Most modern metronomes are electronic; the classic mechanical metronome uses a wind-up spring and an inverted pendulum whose period is adjusted by a sliding weight.

History

Earlier time-keeping devices for music date back to the 9th-century Andalusian polymath Abbas ibn Firnas and to Galileo-era pendulum studies. Dietrich Nikolaus Winkel invented a workable musical chronometer in Amsterdam in the 1810s; Johann Nepomuk Maelzel patented a similar device in 1815 and popularised it as "Maelzel's metronome." Beethoven was the first major composer to publish metronome marks (in 1817), and the abbreviation "M.M." (Maelzel's Metronome) still precedes tempo indications in scores.

Types

Mechanical metronomes are built in a pyramidal wooden case or pocket form; electronic metronomes add features such as accented downbeats, subdivisions, rhythms, and a tuning reference. Software metronomes are now common as apps. György Ligeti's Poème symphonique (1962), written for 100 mechanical metronomes ticking at different speeds until they wind down, is a famous artistic use of the device.
