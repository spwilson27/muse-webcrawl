Wildlife refuge

Purpose: Habitat and species conservation

Example: National Wildlife Refuge System (USA)

A wildlife refuge (also called a wildlife sanctuary or nature reserve in some jurisdictions) is a protected area designated for the conservation of wild animals and their habitats. Within a refuge, activities that would harm wildlife — particularly hunting, habitat destruction, and disturbance during breeding seasons — are restricted or prohibited, although many refuges permit regulated recreation such as birdwatching, fishing, and hiking.

United States system

The best-known example is the National Wildlife Refuge System of the United States, administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It began in 1903 when President Theodore Roosevelt designated Florida's Pelican Island as the first national wildlife refuge to protect egrets and other birds from plume hunters. The system now comprises more than 560 refuges and 38 wetland management districts covering roughly 850 million acres including marine areas.

Management

Refuge managers protect habitat through prescribed burning, water-level management for migratory waterfowl, invasive species control, and habitat restoration. Under the National Wildlife Refuge System Improvement Act of 1997, conservation of wildlife takes precedence over other uses, while six wildlife-dependent recreational uses — hunting, fishing, wildlife observation, photography, environmental education, and interpretation — are recognised as priority public uses where compatible.

International analogues

Similar designations exist worldwide, including national nature reserves in the United Kingdom, wildlife sanctuaries in India, and protected areas classified under the IUCN categories, notably Category IV habitat/species management areas.
