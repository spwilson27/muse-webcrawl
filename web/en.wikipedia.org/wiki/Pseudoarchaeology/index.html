Pseudoarchaeology


Pseudoarchaeological claims often centre on monuments such as the pyramids

Pseudoarchaeology — also known as alternative archaeology, fringe archaeology, fantastic archaeology, cult archaeology, and spooky archaeology — is the interpretation of the past from outside the archaeological science community, which rejects the accepted data-gathering and analytical methods of the discipline. Pseudoarchaeological interpretations often involve the literal reading of myth as history, speculation about lost advanced civilisations, and claims that professional archaeologists suppress evidence.

Characteristics

Critics identify common features of pseudoarchaeology: a lack of adherence to the scientific method, cherry-picking of evidence, appeals to mystery, the presentation of speculation as fact, and sometimes links to nationalist or racist ideologies. Pseudoarchaeologists often reject peer review and instead present their claims in popular books, television programmes and websites.

Examples

Popular subjects include claims that the Egyptian pyramids were built by Atlantis survivors or by extraterrestrials, the ancient astronaut hypothesis popularised by Erich von Däniken in Chariots of the Gods? (1968), the "lost civilisation" theories of Graham Hancock, and claims about hyperdiffusion from a single ancient source. The popular Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse brought renewed criticism from archaeologists, including the Society for American Archaeology, which asked that it be reclassified as fiction.

Responses

Professional archaeologists argue that pseudoarchaeology can undermine public understanding of the past and sometimes appropriates the heritage of Indigenous peoples by attributing their ancestors' achievements to outside visitors. Books such as Kenneth Feder's Frauds, Myths, and Mysteries systematically examine popular pseudoarchaeological claims.
