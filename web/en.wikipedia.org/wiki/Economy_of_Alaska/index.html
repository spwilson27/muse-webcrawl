The Trans-Alaska Pipeline, backbone of the oil economy

The economy of Alaska is dominated by oil, natural gas, fishing, tourism, mining and the federal government. Although the state's gross domestic product (about $65–70 billion) is among the smallest of U.S. states, its per-capita output ranks among the highest because of its small population (about 730,000). Oil revenues have historically funded the large majority of state government and the Alaska Permanent Fund, which pays an annual dividend to every resident.

Oil and gas

The discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 — North America's largest oil field — transformed Alaska. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline, completed in 1977, carries crude 800 miles south to the ice-free port of Valdez. Production peaked in 1988 at about 2 million barrels per day and has since declined to roughly a fifth of that level, though new North Slope projects (such as Pikka and Willow, approved in 2023) are expected to slow the decline. Oil taxes and royalties have typically furnished 80–90% of the state's unrestricted revenue at high prices, making the state budget highly sensitive to crude prices.

Fishing and seafood

Alaska's fishing industry is among the most productive in the world: more than half of all seafood caught in the United States by volume comes from Alaskan waters, including most of the nation's salmon, crab, pollock and cod. Dutch Harbor (Unalaska) is consistently the top U.S. fishing port by landings volume. The industry employs tens of thousands seasonally and is the largest private-sector employer in the state.

Tourism

Roughly two million visitors a year travel to Alaska, chiefly by cruise ship through the Inside Passage, generating several billion dollars in spending. Attractions include Denali National Park, glaciers, wildlife viewing and northern lights tourism.

Mining and other sectors

Alaska hosts some of the world's largest mines: Red Dog (one of the largest zinc mines), Fort Knox and Pogo (gold), Greens Creek (silver) and Usibelli (coal). Agriculture is small (concentrated in the Matanuska Valley) and timber, once significant in the Tongass National Forest, has declined sharply. The federal government — military bases such as Joint Base Elmendorf–Richardson and Fort Wainwright, plus land management — employs roughly a third of the workforce and is often described as one of the state's three economic "pillars" with oil and fishing.

Permanent Fund and dividend

A 1976 constitutional amendment created the Alaska Permanent Fund, investing at least 25% of resource royalties; the fund is worth roughly $75–80 billion. Since 1982 residents have received an annual Permanent Fund Dividend, varying from a few hundred dollars to $3,284 in 2022 (including an energy relief supplement). Debates over the size of the dividend versus funding state services have dominated Alaskan politics since oil prices fell in 2014–15.
