The Bonn–Oberkassel dog was a late Paleolithic dog (Canis familiaris) whose skeletal remains were found buried alongside two humans in Oberkassel, a suburb of Bonn in Germany. Dated to approximately 14,200 years ago, the burial is the oldest undisputed archaeological evidence of a domesticated dog.

Discovery

The remains were discovered in 1914 by workmen quarrying basalt at Oberkassel. The double grave contained a man about 40 years old, a woman about 25, grave goods, and the partial skeleton of a dog, all dusted with red hematite. The dog bones were only recognized as such in a first study in 1919; the skeleton was largely lost or dispersed, but a later re-examination (published in 2018 by Luc Janssens and colleagues) re-studied the surviving mandible and other elements.

The dog

Analysis showed the dog died at about 27–28 weeks of age. Its teeth and bones preserve evidence of a serious illness — most likely canine distemper — lasting for several weeks before death. Because a puppy this young and sick could not have fed or cared for itself, researchers concluded that the humans must have nursed and cared for it intensively, despite its having no practical working value at that age. This has been interpreted as evidence of an emotional bond between humans and dogs already present in the earliest phase of domestication.

Significance

While older possible dogs (such as the Goyet and Altai canids, c. 33,000–36,000 years old) remain contested as incipient wolves or failed domestication attempts, the Bonn–Oberkassel burial is widely accepted as the earliest confirmed domestic dog, indicating that dog domestication predates agriculture by several thousand years.
