Konik

The Konik (Polish: konik polski, "Polish little horse") is a Polish breed of small, hardy horse noted for its primitive appearance. Koniks are believed to descend in part from the wild tarpan, the Eurasian wild horse that survived in the forests of eastern Poland into the 19th century, and they retain many ancestral features: a mouse-dun or grullo coat, a dark dorsal stripe, zebra-like barring on the legs, and a heavy winter coat.

History

In the 1920s and 1930s, Polish biologist Tadeusz Vetulani began a deliberate breeding programme to reconstruct the tarpan from primitive farm horses of the Biłgoraj region, releasing his herd into the Białowieża Forest. Although breeding back a true wild horse proved impossible, the programme fixed the Konik as a distinct breed. The population suffered heavily during the Second World War but was rebuilt from surviving stock at research stations such as Popielno.

Use in conservation

Because Koniks thrive outdoors year-round on coarse forage with minimal human care, they have become a favourite tool of rewilding and conservation grazing projects. Herds introduced to nature reserves — famously the Oostvaardersplassen in the Netherlands, and reserves in Latvia, Germany, and the United Kingdom — help maintain open grassland mosaics by grazing and browsing. Their use is occasionally controversial when herds in fenced reserves outgrow available food in harsh winters, prompting debate about intervention and culling.

The breed stands about 130–140 cm at the withers, is sure-footed and long-lived, and is also used for light farm work, driving, and riding, particularly for children.
