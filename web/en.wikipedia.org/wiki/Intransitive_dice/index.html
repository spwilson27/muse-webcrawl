Intransitive dice

Intransitive dice are a set of dice for which the relation "is more likely to roll a higher number than" is not transitive. That is, a die A may roll higher than die B more than half the time, and die B may roll higher than die C more than half the time, yet die A does not necessarily roll higher than die C more than half the time; indeed, die C may be more likely to beat die A. The phenomenon is analogous to the game of rock-paper-scissors and to Penney's game with biased coins.

Example

A classic example uses three six-sided dice. Suppose die A has faces 2, 2, 4, 4, 9, 9; die B has faces 1, 1, 6, 6, 8, 8; and die C has faces 3, 3, 5, 5, 7, 7. Comparing all 36 equally likely pairs of rolls, A beats B with probability 5/9, B beats C with probability 5/9, and C also beats A with probability 5/9. No die in the set is the strongest against both opponents.

Efron's dice

Efron's dice, invented by statistician Bradley Efron, are a set of four dice in which each die beats the next with probability 2/3. The faces are 4,4,4,4,0,0; 3,3,3,3,3,3; 6,6,2,2,2,2; and 5,5,5,1,1,1. In a game where the first player chooses a die and the second player then chooses another, the second player can always select a die with a 2/3 advantage.

Properties and generalizations

Intransitive dice can be constructed with different numbers of sides and for sets of any size. The maximum win probability achievable in a cycle has been studied extensively, and intransitivity also arises in other random structures such as tournaments and voting paradoxes like the Condorcet paradox.
