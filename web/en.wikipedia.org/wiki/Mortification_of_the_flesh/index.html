Mortification of the flesh

Mortification of the flesh is an act by which an individual or group seeks to mortify or "put to death" their sinful nature, as part of the process of sanctification. In Christianity, common forms of mortification include fasting, abstinence, and self-denial practices such as wearing a cilice or performing acts of self-flagellation. The term is drawn from the King James Bible phrase "mortify the deeds of the body" (Romans 8:13) and St Paul's injunction to "put to death what is earthly in you" (Colossians 3:5).

In Christianity

Mortification has been practised since the early Church, notably by the Desert Fathers and later by monastic orders, saints and mystics who sought to discipline the body, share in Christ's sufferings, and do penance. Roman Catholic teaching presents mortification as a means of growth in virtue and reparation, accompanied by prayer and charity; practices range from small daily renunciations to, historically, more severe austerities. Some movements and orders, such as Opus Dei, retain voluntary use of the cilice and discipline by some members. Mainline Protestant traditions generally emphasise a metaphorical mortification of sin rather than physical austerities, though fasting is widely practised.

In other religions

Analogous ascetic practices appear in many religions: Hindu tapas and Jain fasting and self-restraint, Buddhist monastic austerities (though the Buddha rejected extreme self-mortification in the "Middle Way"), Islamic fasting during Ramadan, and Sufi ascetic disciplines.
