Federalism in Belgium

Federalism in Belgium is the system of government by which Belgium, originally a unitary state founded in 1830, was transformed through a series of state reforms (1970, 1980, 1988–89, 1993, 2001 and 2011) into a federal state. The Belgian federation is unusual in being built on two overlapping types of federated entities: three Regions, defined territorially, and three Communities, defined by language and culture.

Structure

The three Regions are the Flemish Region (Flanders), the Walloon Region (Wallonia) and the Brussels-Capital Region. The three Communities are the Flemish Community, the French Community and the small German-speaking Community in the east. In Flanders, the regional and community institutions were merged into a single Flemish Government and Parliament; in the French-speaking part they remain separate.

Regions hold competences over territory-related matters such as economy, employment, agriculture, water policy, housing, public works, energy, transport, environment and town and country planning. Communities are responsible for culture, education, language use, health policy and assistance to individuals. The federal level retains defence, justice, finance, social security, foreign policy and important parts of public health and home affairs.

History

Belgium began as a unitary, French-dominated state even though Dutch speakers formed a majority. The Flemish Movement pressed for language equality through the 19th and 20th centuries; language laws fixed the language border in 1962–63. The first state reform in 1970 created cultural communities; the 1980 reform created regions; and the Saint Michael's Agreement of 1993 formally declared Belgium a federal state in the first article of its constitution. The sixth state reform (2011) followed the record 541-day government formation crisis.

Characteristics

Belgian federalism is marked by bilingual Brussels, constitutionally protected parity between Dutch and French speakers in the federal government, special majorities for institutional laws, and the absence of a hierarchy of norms between federal and federated laws — each level is supreme within its own competences, which are in principle exclusive.
