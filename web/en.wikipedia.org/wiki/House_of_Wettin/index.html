House of Wettin

House of Wettin
Country: Saxony, Thuringia, Belgium, Bulgaria, Poland, Portugal, United Kingdom
Founded: 10th century
Founder: Theodoric I (traditionally)
Titles: Elector and King of Saxony, King of Poland, King of Belgium, Tsar of Bulgaria, King of Portugal, King of the United Kingdom (Windsor cadet line)

The House of Wettin is a dynasty of German counts, dukes, prince-electors and kings that once ruled territories in the present-day German states of Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt and Thuringia for more than 800 years. Cadet branches of the family went on to reign in Belgium, Bulgaria, Poland, Portugal and the United Kingdom.

Origins

The family takes its name from Wettin Castle near Halle in Saxony-Anhalt. Its earliest reliably attested member is Theodoric I, count in the Hassegau in the late 9th and early 10th centuries. The Wettins rose as margraves of Meissen and Lusatia and as landgraves of Thuringia, becoming one of the most powerful houses of the Holy Roman Empire.

Ernestine and Albertine lines

In the Leipzig Partition of 1485 the dynasty split into two main branches. The Ernestine line, descended from Elector Ernest, retained the electoral dignity until 1547 and later fragmented into the Saxon duchies of Thuringia (Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach, Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg, Saxe-Meiningen, Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, and others). The Albertine line, descended from Duke Albert, gained the electorate of Saxony and in 1806 the Saxon crown.

Reigns abroad

Augustus the Strong and his son Augustus III were kings of Poland in the 18th century. From the Ernestine duchy of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha came Leopold I, first king of the Belgians (1831), Ferdinand II of Portugal, Ferdinand I of Bulgaria, and Prince Albert, consort of Queen Victoria — through whom the British royal house (renamed Windsor in 1917) descends from the Wettins.

After 1918

The German branches lost their thrones in the revolutions of November 1918. The Belgian branch continues to reign as the Belgian royal family. Heads and members of the Saxon and Thuringian branches remain active in cultural and charitable life in Germany.
