Lazare Hoche

Louis Lazare Hoche (24 June 1768 – 19 September 1797) was a French military leader of the French Revolutionary Wars. He rose from the ranks to command armies of the Republic by the age of 25, defeated the royalist counter-revolution in the Vendée and Brittany, and led the unsuccessful French expedition to Ireland in 1796. Regarded by many contemporaries as a rival in talent to Napoleon Bonaparte, he died of disease at 29.

Early life and rise

Hoche was born at Versailles, the son of a royal kennel attendant. He enlisted in the French Guards as a private soldier at sixteen and rose steadily through the non-commissioned ranks. The Revolution opened rapid promotion to talent: he distinguished himself at the defence of Dunkirk and at Neerwinden, and by October 1793, at 25, he was given command of the Army of the Moselle. After initial setbacks he defeated the Austrians and Prussians at Frœschwiller and relieved Landau, winning the Battle of Wörth in December 1793.

Imprisonment and the West

Suspected by the Committee of Public Safety, Hoche was arrested in the spring of 1794 and imprisoned in Paris, escaping the guillotine only through the fall of Robespierre in Thermidor. Released, he was sent west against the royalist insurgency. In 1795 he repelled the British-backed émigré landing at Quiberon Bay and, combining military pressure with clemency, largely pacified the Vendée and Brittany by 1796 — a settlement known as the Pacification of the West.

Expedition to Ireland and death

In December 1796 Hoche commanded a major expedition of some 15,000 troops intended to land in Ireland and support the United Irishmen against British rule. Storms scattered the fleet off Bantry Bay; Hoche's frigate was separated from the main force, and the landing was abandoned. In 1797 he took command of the Army of Sambre-et-Meuse and won the Battle of Neuwied over the Austrians before an armistice halted operations. He died suddenly at Wetzlar in September 1797, probably of tuberculosis or pneumonia, amid unfounded rumours of poisoning.

Legacy

Hoche's name is inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. A major square and avenue in Paris bear his name — the Avenue Hoche — as does a station of the Paris Métro. Historians have long speculated on the rivalry that might have developed between Hoche and Bonaparte had he lived.
