Praetorian prefect

The praetorian prefect (Latin: praefectus praetorio) was a high office in the Roman Empire. Originating as the commander of the Praetorian Guard, the imperial bodyguard, it gradually evolved into a senior administrative and judicial post, becoming the emperor's chief minister and the second most powerful figure in the state after the emperor himself.

History

Augustus created the prefecture in 2 BC, appointing two equestrian prefects to command the newly organised Praetorian Guard. Because the Guard was the only substantial armed force near the emperor, its commanders wielded immense influence; prefects such as Sejanus under Tiberius, Burrus under Nero, and Tigellinus became notorious or celebrated kingmakers. Prefects frequently participated in the overthrow and accession of emperors.

Legal and administrative powers

From the Severan period onward, prefects were often distinguished jurists — Papinian, Ulpian, and Paul all held the office — and the prefecture acquired supreme appellate jurisdiction: judgments of the praetorian prefect, given vice sacra on the emperor's behalf, could not be appealed. The prefect also supervised military supply, the annona, and aspects of finance and provincial administration.

Late Empire

Constantine the Great disbanded the Praetorian Guard in 312 and stripped the office of its military character. Thereafter praetorian prefects became the civilian governors of the great territorial prefectures — the Gauls, Italy, Illyricum, and the East — each administering a group of dioceses, with responsibility for justice, taxation, and food supply. The prefecture of the East was the most senior administrative post of the Byzantine state and survived into the later Byzantine period in reduced form.
