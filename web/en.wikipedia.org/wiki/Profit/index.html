Profit (economics)

[Image: a factory owner reviewing a ledger showing a healthy profit]

In Economics and Accounting, profit is the financial gain from an activity: in accounting, revenue minus explicit costs; in economics, revenue minus all costs including opportunity costs (implicit costs such as the owner's forgone salary and the normal return on capital). The remainder above normal profit is economic (super-normal) profit.

Role and theory

Under perfect Competition, economic profit tends to zero in the long run as entry competes it away; persistent profit signals market power, risk, innovation rents (Schumpeter) or luck. Frank Knight (1921) explained profit as the return to bearing uninsurable uncertainty. Profit is the engine of a capitalist economy — the signal that resources are valued more in their current use, guiding investment — and its distribution between owners, workers (wages) and the state (taxation) is the distributional conflict at the centre of classical political economy from David Ricardo to Karl Marx, whose theory treated profit (surplus value) as unpaid labour. Modern debates cover profit margins and markups, buybacks, and the profit share of national income.
