Control theory

Control theory is a field of control engineering and applied mathematics that deals with the control of dynamical systems in engineered processes and machines. The objective is to develop a model for controlling such systems using a control action in an optimum manner without delay or overshoot and ensuring control stability.

Overview

A controller compares a measured output (the process variable) with a desired reference (the setpoint) and adjusts inputs to the system (through actuators) to drive the error between them toward zero. Control may be open-loop, in which the action does not depend on the output, or closed-loop (feedback control), in which it does. Feedback makes control robust to disturbances and model uncertainty, but poorly designed feedback can destabilize a system.

History

Although control systems of various kinds date to antiquity — float regulators in water clocks and the centrifugal governor of the steam engine — the mathematical theory developed in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. James Clerk Maxwell analyzed governor stability in 1868; Aleksandr Lyapunov founded stability theory; and in the 1930s–40s, Harry Nyquist and Hendrik Bode developed frequency-response methods at Bell Laboratories, while Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) unified control and communication. State-space methods, pioneered by Rudolf Kálmán around 1960, underpin modern control theory.

Classical and modern control

Classical control theory uses transfer functions in the Laplace domain, root-locus and frequency-response design, and is largely limited to single-input single-output systems. Modern control theory uses state-space models in the time domain, handles multivariable systems, and includes optimal control (such as the linear-quadratic regulator), robust control (H∞ methods), adaptive control, and nonlinear control.

Applications

Control theory is applied in cruise control and autopilots, spacecraft guidance, process industries, robotics, power systems, disk drives, and biomedical devices such as the artificial pancreas.
