Feedback

Feedback occurs when outputs of a system are routed back as inputs as part of a chain of cause-and-effect that forms a circuit or loop. The system can then be said to feed back into itself. Feedback is a central concept in control theory, electronics, biology, economics, and many other fields.

Types

Negative feedback occurs when the fed-back output opposes the input, tending to stabilize the system around an equilibrium. Examples include a thermostat regulating temperature, the governor of a steam engine, and hormonal regulation of blood glucose. Negative feedback generally promotes stability and reduces sensitivity to disturbances and component variation.

Positive feedback occurs when the fed-back output reinforces the input, amplifying change and driving the system away from its starting state. Examples include the audio squeal of a microphone near a loudspeaker, the cascade of blood clotting, action potential generation in neurons, and runaway processes such as nuclear chain reactions. Positive feedback loops often produce rapid change until some limiting factor intervenes.

History

Self-regulating mechanisms using feedback existed in ancient water clocks and in the windmill fantail, and the centrifugal governor became an icon of the industrial revolution. In 1927 Harold Stephen Black conceived the negative-feedback amplifier, dramatically reducing distortion in telephone repeaters. Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics (1948) and W. Ross Ashby's work established feedback as a unifying principle across machines and organisms.

Applications

Feedback is essential in amplifiers, oscillators, control systems, and phase-locked loops in electronics; in physiological homeostasis; in ecological and climate systems (such as ice–albedo feedback); and in social and economic dynamics, where expectations can reinforce market movements.
