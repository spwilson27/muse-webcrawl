Thermostat

A thermostat is a regulating device component which senses the temperature of a physical system and performs actions so that the system's temperature is maintained near a desired setpoint. Thermostats are used in any device or system that heats or cools to a setpoint temperature, in applications including building heating, central heating, air conditioners, HVAC systems, water heaters, and kitchen equipment such as ovens and refrigerators. The name is derived from the Greek words thermos (hot) and statos (standing, stationary).

Operation

A thermostat operates as a closed-loop control device: it switches heating or cooling equipment on or off, or regulates the flow of a heat transfer fluid, in response to the sensed temperature. To prevent rapid cycling (short cycling), most thermostats incorporate a small deadband or hysteresis around the setpoint, so the system switches on slightly below and off slightly above the target temperature.

Types

Traditional thermostats use physical sensors: bimetallic strips that bend with temperature to operate a switch or mercury tilt switch, wax pellets that expand on heating (also used in automobile cooling-system thermostats), gas-filled bellows, and pneumatic controls. Electronic thermostats use thermistors or resistance temperature detectors with digital control; programmable thermostats allow different setpoints by time of day and day of week, and smart thermostats add occupancy sensing, learning algorithms, and remote control via the internet.

History

Temperature regulators date to the seventeenth century, including Cornelis Drebbel's mercury thermostat for incubators. Warren S. Johnson patented an electric room thermostat in 1883, and Albert Butz's damper-flapper devices led to the company that became Honeywell. The round "T87" thermostat designed by Henry Dreyfuss for Honeywell in 1953 became an iconic industrial design.
