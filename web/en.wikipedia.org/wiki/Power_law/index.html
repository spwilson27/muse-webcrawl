Power law

In statistics, a power law is a functional relationship between two quantities where a relative change in one quantity results in a proportional relative change in the other quantity, independent of the initial size of those quantities: one quantity varies as a power of another. For instance, considering the area of a square in terms of the length of its side, doubling the length multiplies the area by a factor of four.

p(x) ∝ x−α

Empirical power laws describe phenomena in many fields, including physics, biology, geography, sociology, economics and finance. Well-known examples include the Pareto distribution of incomes, Zipf's law for word frequencies and city sizes, the Gutenberg–Richter law for earthquake magnitudes, and allometric scaling laws in biology.

Properties

Power-law distributions are scale invariant: multiplying the argument by a constant factor merely rescales the function itself, leaving its shape unchanged. On a log–log plot, a power-law relationship appears as a straight line. Many power-law distributions are heavy-tailed, with a significant probability of extreme values; when the exponent α ≤ 2 the theoretical variance is infinite, and when α ≤ 1 the mean diverges as well.

Generating mechanisms

Mechanisms that generate power laws include preferential attachment ("the rich get richer") in growing networks, multiplicative random processes with a lower reflecting barrier, self-organised criticality, optimisation and mixtures of exponential distributions. The study of power laws in networks grew rapidly after the identification of scale-free networks in the late 1990s.

Testing and criticism

Identifying genuine power laws in empirical data is statistically demanding: simple log–log regression can be misleading, and modern methodology (notably the maximum-likelihood and goodness-of-fit framework of Clauset, Shalizi and Newman, 2009) shows that many claimed power laws are better fit by log-normal or other heavy-tailed distributions, or by power laws with exponential cutoffs valid only over a limited range.

Examples

  • Zipf's law (word frequency, city populations)
  • Pareto distribution (wealth and income)
  • Gutenberg–Richter law (earthquake magnitudes)
  • Kleiber's law (metabolic rate scales as mass3/4)
  • Stefan–Boltzmann law (radiation ∝ T4)
  • Inverse-square laws (gravity, electrostatics, illumination)
  • 1/f noise in many physical and electronic systems
