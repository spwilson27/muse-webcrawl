The Water-Babies

The Water-Babies, A Fairy Tale for a Land Baby is a children's novel by Charles Kingsley, first published in 1863. Serialised in Macmillan's Magazine from August 1862 to March 1863, it appeared in book form later that year and became one of the most popular English children's books of the Victorian era. The novel is at once a fairy tale, a moral fable, and a satire on contemporary scientific debates, and it remained in print and widely read in Britain well into the twentieth century, although aspects of it are now regarded as dated.

Plot summary

The hero, Tom, is a young chimney sweep who works for the brutal Mr Grimes in the north of England. Sent to clean the chimneys of a great house, Harthover Place, Tom becomes lost in the maze of flues and emerges in the bedroom of Ellie, the young daughter of the house. Mistaken for a thief, he flees across the moors and, exhausted and filthy, plunges into a stream in the valley of Vendale to wash himself — and drowns, or seems to drown. The fairies transform him into a "water-baby", a tiny creature a few inches long, living in the stream among the caddis-flies, dragonfly larvae, otters and trout.

Under the guidance of the fairies — Mrs Bedonebyasyoudid, who punishes or rewards creatures according to how they treat others; her sister Mrs Doasyouwouldbedoneby, who is all kindness; and Mother Carey, the mysterious mother of all creatures — Tom learns the moral lessons of his new life. He journeys to the Other-end-of-Nowhere and St Brandan's Isle, helps his former master Grimes, now trapped after his own death inside a chimney in the afterlife, and is finally reunited with Ellie. Having proved himself by doing the things he did not like to do, Tom becomes "a great man of science" in his restored human life and marries Ellie.

Themes and context

Kingsley, an Anglican clergyman and keen naturalist, wrote the book partly as a children's story for his youngest son Grenville and partly as a vehicle for his scientific and religious views. The book is a notable early popular response to Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859): Kingsley was one of the first prominent churchmen to welcome Darwin's theory, and The Water-Babies contains playful references to evolution, natural selection and contemporary scientists such as Thomas Henry Huxley and Richard Owen, including the famous joke about the "great hippopotamus test". At the same time, the novel protests against child labour — the use of climbing boys by chimney sweeps — a cause that contributed to the Chimney Sweepers Regulation Act of 1864.

The book's moral framework reflects Victorian didacticism: cleanliness is equated with godliness, and Tom's physical transformation follows his moral education. Modern reprints are often abridged, and the text contains passages — including ethnic and religious caricatures of the Irish, Jews, Americans and others — that modern readers find objectionable.

Illustrations and reception

The first edition was illustrated by J. Noel Paton; the better-known 1886 edition carried over one hundred drawings by Linley Sambourne, whose images of Tom among the water creatures became definitive. The book has been adapted for stage, radio, and an animated film of 1978. It is commonly, though not universally, listed among the founding works of modern children's fantasy, alongside Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865).
