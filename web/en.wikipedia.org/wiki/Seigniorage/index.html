Seigniorage

Seigniorage
DefinitionFace value of money minus its cost of production
FieldMonetary economics
RelatedInflation tax, fiat money, central banking

Seigniorage (from Old French seigneuriage, the right of the lord, or seigneur) is the difference between the value of money and the cost to produce and distribute it. The term originated with commodity money: a ruler who took bullion to the mint and received back coins of lower precious-metal content than the bullion's face value pocketed the difference as a fee for the sovereign's stamp that guaranteed the coinage.

Modern meaning

With fiat money, seigniorage takes broader forms. For coinage and banknotes, it is the gap between face value and the (now trivial) production cost. In monetary economics, seigniorage is often measured as the revenue a government obtains by creating new base money — the central bank acquires interest-bearing assets (typically government securities) in exchange for non-interest-bearing currency and reserves, and remits the interest earnings to the treasury. A common approximation values real seigniorage as the change in the monetary base divided by the price level.

Inflation tax

Because newly created money dilutes the purchasing power of existing balances, seigniorage is closely related to the inflation tax: holders of money pay, through inflation, for the real resources the issuer obtains. Moderate inflation yields a stable revenue stream — in advanced economies seigniorage is typically well under one percent of GDP — but attempts to extract large revenues by printing money produce accelerating inflation and eventually hyperinflation, as the real base of the tax (willingly held money balances) collapses; this limit is illustrated by the Cagan model of hyperinflation. Historically, seigniorage through coin debasement — reducing the silver or gold content of coins — was a major fiscal tool of pre-modern rulers, from Roman emperors to Henry VIII's Great Debasement.

Distribution

In modern systems, central-bank seigniorage is normally transferred to the state; in the euro area it is shared among national central banks. The concept has also been applied by analogy to privately issued money, such as fully reserved "stablecoins," whose issuers earn the interest on reserve assets.
