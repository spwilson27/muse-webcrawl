Jonathan Edwards (5 October 1703 – 22 March 1758) was an American revivalist preacher, philosopher and Congregationalist theologian. Widely regarded as one of America's most important and original philosophical theologians, Edwards helped ignite and shape the First Great Awakening. His sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God", preached at Enfield, Connecticut in 1741, is a classic of American literature and a landmark of fire-and-brimstone preaching — though Edwards himself delivered it in a calm, measured tone.

Life

Edwards was born in East Windsor, Connecticut, the son of a minister, and entered Yale College before the age of thirteen. After a powerful conversion experience he devoted himself to theology and rigorous study, rising at four in the morning and writing thousands of pages of notebooks. In 1727 he became pastor at Northampton, Massachusetts, assisting and then succeeding his grandfather Solomon Stoddard.

Revival and writings

A local revival in Northampton in 1734–35, described in Edwards's A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God (1737), helped launch the wider Great Awakening. Edwards defended the revival against critics in works such as Religious Affections (1746), which analysed genuine religious experience. Dismissed by his Northampton congregation in 1750 after a dispute over admission to communion, he ministered to a frontier congregation and Native American mission at Stockbridge, writing his major philosophical works there: Freedom of the Will (1754) and The Nature of True Virtue. In 1758 he became president of the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), but died within weeks from a smallpox inoculation.

Theology

Edwards wed a Reformed, Calvinist doctrine of God's absolute sovereignty to a sophisticated philosophical idealism influenced by Locke and Newton. He held that true virtue consists in benevolence toward "being in general" and that the will is always determined by the strongest motive. His complete writings, published by Yale University in a multi-volume edition, secure his reputation as colonial America's foremost intellectual.
