Reciprocity (social psychology)

In social psychology, reciprocity is a social norm of responding to a positive action with another positive action, rewarding kind actions. People generally feel obliged to return favours, gifts, and concessions, and they tend to dislike and sanction those who fail to reciprocate. The norm of reciprocity is considered a near-universal feature of human societies, described by anthropologists in gift-exchange systems and by sociologist Alvin Gouldner in a classic 1960 analysis.

The norm of reciprocity obliges people to return favours and gifts.

The norm of reciprocity

Gouldner argued that the norm comprises two related expectations: people should help those who have helped them, and people should not injure those who have helped them. Reciprocity stabilises social relationships by creating obligations that bind exchange partners over time. Anthropologist Marcel Mauss had earlier described the triple obligation — to give, to receive, and to reciprocate — in gift economies, and Marshall Sahlins distinguished generalised, balanced, and negative reciprocity along a spectrum of social distance.

Experimental findings

Classic experiments demonstrate the norm's power: in Dennis Regan's 1971 study, participants who received an unsolicited soft drink from a confederate later bought substantially more raffle tickets from him, even more than participants who merely liked him. Reciprocity also shapes negotiation (concessions invite concessions), the "door-in-the-face" persuasion technique, and marketing practices such as free samples. Negative reciprocity — returning harm for harm — underlies retaliation and some forms of punishment.

Applications and criticism

Reciprocity is central to theories of cooperation and altruism, including Robert Trivers's theory of reciprocal altruism in evolutionary biology. It is exploited in influence and sales techniques, a theme popularised in Robert Cialdini's Influence. Researchers note cultural and individual variation in how strongly the norm is felt, and that unwanted favours can create resented obligations.
