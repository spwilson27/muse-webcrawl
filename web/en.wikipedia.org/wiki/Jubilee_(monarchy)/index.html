Jubilee (monarchy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a monarch's jubilee celebration]

A jubilee in the context of a monarchy is a major celebration marking a significant anniversary of a monarch's reign, such as 25, 50, 60, or 70 years on the throne. The most commonly celebrated milestones are the Silver Jubilee (25 years), Golden Jubilee (50 years), Diamond Jubilee (60 years), and Platinum Jubilee (70 years).

History

The tradition of royal jubilees derives from the ancient Hebrew concept of the jubilee year, a year of emancipation and restoration occurring every fiftieth year, described in the Book of Leviticus. In Britain, the first widely celebrated royal jubilee was that of King George III in 1809, marking the beginning of his 50th year on the throne.

Queen Victoria's Golden Jubilee in 1887 and Diamond Jubilee in 1897 were grand imperial occasions. Queen Elizabeth II celebrated Silver (1977), Golden (2002), Diamond (2012), and Platinum (2022) Jubilees, becoming the first British monarch to reach a Platinum Jubilee.

Celebrations

Jubilee celebrations typically include thanksgiving services, military parades, street parties, concerts, and the lighting of beacons. Commemorative medals, coins, and stamps are often issued, and public holidays may be declared.
