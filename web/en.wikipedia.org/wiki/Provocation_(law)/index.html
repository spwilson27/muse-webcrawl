Provocation (law)

In criminal law, provocation is a partial defence by which a person charged with murder may instead be convicted of the lesser offence of manslaughter, on the ground that the killing occurred in the heat of passion caused by the victim's provocative conduct. The defence, inherited from English common law, survives in a number of Commonwealth and American jurisdictions, though it has been reformed or abolished in several.

Elements

Traditionally the defence required: (1) provocative conduct by the deceased sufficient to deprive a reasonable person of self-control; (2) that the accused actually acted on the sudden provocation before passion had time to cool; and (3) a causal link between the provocation and the killing. Words alone were historically insufficient, though modern statutes usually allow provocative words or gestures to qualify.

Reform and abolition

The defence has been criticised for excusing male violence, particularly killings of female partners, and for embodying gendered assumptions about honour and self-control. New Zealand abolished provocation in 2009; several Australian states abolished or heavily restricted it (Tasmania 2003, Victoria 2005, and New South Wales restricted it to "extreme provocation" in 2014). In England and Wales, the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 replaced provocation with a "loss of control" defence and expressly excluded sexual infidelity as a qualifying trigger.

Other uses

Provocation also appears in tort and military law, and the related concept of "agent provocateur" arises in the law of incitement and entrapment. In some civil-law systems, provoked killings are handled through sentencing discretion or specific mitigated homicide offences rather than a discrete defence.
