Deer park

A deer park is an enclosed area of parkland set aside for the keeping and hunting of deer. Deer parks were widespread in medieval and early modern Europe, particularly in England, where they were created by royal licence as status symbols and managed sources of venison for aristocratic households. Many survive, transformed into landscape parks around country houses or into public parks, with herds of deer maintained as a link to their origins.

History

The keeping of deer in enclosures dates to antiquity — Roman vivaria and Anglo-Saxon enclosures ("hays") preceded the Norman Conquest — but the deer park became a defining feature of the English landscape after 1066. Creating a park required a royal licence to empark, and by around 1300 there may have been more than 3,000 parks in England. A park was enclosed by a park pale: a substantial fence of cleft oak stakes, often set on an internal ditch and bank designed so that deer could leap in from outside but not escape. The deer, usually fallow deer — a species introduced to Britain in the Norman period — were managed by a parker, culled for the lord's table, and supplemented by grazing cattle and the pannage of pigs.

Parkland landscape

Deer parks shaped a distinctive landscape of open grassland with scattered mature trees, especially pollarded oaks, which survive in famous examples such as Richmond Park and Bushey Park in London, Dunham Massey in Cheshire, and the royal parks of Windsor. In the 18th century many medieval parks were reshaped by landscape designers such as Capability Brown, whose sweeping parkland around country houses preserved the open, tree-dotted character of the deer park while softening its utilitarian features.

Modern deer parks

Today, deer parks exist in many countries both as heritage landscapes and as attractions. Well-known examples include Dyrehaven (Jægersborg Deer Park) north of Copenhagen in Denmark, a former royal hunting ground inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the par force hunting landscape of North Zealand; the deer parks of Nara in Japan, where sika deer roam among temples; and numerous safari and wildlife parks in which deer are kept for viewing and conservation breeding.
