Illegalism

Illegalism is an anarchist philosophy and practice that emerged in France, Italy, Belgium, and Switzerland during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Illegalists rejected legal employment and embraced crime — theft, counterfeiting, burglary, and robbery — as a legitimate expression of revolt against capitalism, the state, and bourgeois morality. The tendency grew out of individualist anarchism and the idea of "individual reclamation": the expropriation of wealth by the individual as a blow against an exploitative order.

Illegalist exploits were sensationalized by the French press of the Belle Époque.

Origins

Illegalism drew on Max Stirner's egoist philosophy and on the propaganda of the deed current within anarchism. Clément Duval, a French anarchist burglar tried in 1887, became an early model after defending his theft as "reclamation" of stolen social wealth. Marius Jacob, whose gang the "Workers of the Night" carried out scores of burglaries in the 1900s while avoiding violence and donating part of the proceeds, represented a disciplined, almost professional variant of the practice.

The Bonnot Gang

The most notorious illegalists were the Bonnot Gang (La Bande à Bonnot), an anarchist criminal group active in France and Belgium in 1911–1912. Associated with the individualist journal L'Anarchie, Jules Bonnot and his accomplices pioneered the use of automobiles as getaway vehicles and committed a series of armed robberies and murders that transfixed the French public. The gang was destroyed in police sieges at Choisy-le-Roi and Nogent-sur-Marne in the spring of 1912.

Criticism

Illegalism was fiercely debated within the anarchist movement. Anarcho-syndicalists and many communists argued that criminal practice isolated militants, invited repression, and degenerated into mere banditry disconnected from collective emancipation. Illegalists replied that legal work itself was submission, and that refusing wage labor was itself a revolutionary stance.
