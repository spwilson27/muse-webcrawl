Fourth Party System

The Fourth Party System is the term used in political science and history for the period of political party organisation in the United States from about 1896 to 1932, also called the Progressive Era at the national level. It was dominated by the Republican Party, excepting the split of 1912 that gave the presidency to Woodrow Wilson's Democrats for two terms (1913–1921).

Characteristics

The system began with the fiercely contested election of 1896, in which Republican William McKinley defeated Democrat William Jennings Bryan, realigning the parties around issues of industrialisation, the gold standard, tariffs and America's new role overseas. The era saw the institutionalisation of the parties after the upheavals of the 1890s: voter turnout fell from its 19th-century highs, party organisations professionalised, and reforms such as the Australian (secret) ballot, direct primaries, and civil-service rules changed how campaigns worked.

At the state and local level the Progressive movement pushed regulation of railroads and corporations, conservation, prohibition, women's suffrage, and direct-democracy devices such as the initiative and referendum. National politics was marked by Republican dominance in the Northeast and Midwest and one-party Democratic rule in the segregated South.

End

The system collapsed with the Great Depression: the election of 1932 swept Franklin D. Roosevelt and the Democrats to power and inaugurated the Fifth Party System, the New Deal coalition.
