Magnetic north

Magnetic north is the direction that a compass needle points as it aligns with the Earth's magnetic field. More precisely, it is the direction of the horizontal component of the magnetic field lines where the field points downwards toward the Earth at the north magnetic pole — a wandering location on the Earth's surface. Magnetic north differs from true north (geographic north, the direction of the rotational axis) and from grid north; the angular difference between magnetic and true north at a given place is the magnetic declination, and it changes with both location and time.

The north magnetic pole

The north magnetic pole is the point where the Earth's magnetic field points vertically downward. Located for centuries in the Canadian Arctic — it was first reached by James Clark Ross in 1831 on the Boothia Peninsula — the pole has drifted over time, and since the late 20th century its motion has accelerated from roughly 10–15 km per year to speeds exceeding 50 km per year, moving away from Canada toward Siberia across the Arctic Ocean.

Declination and navigation

Navigators using a magnetic compass must correct for declination to find true north; charts and digital models such as the World Magnetic Model provide declination values, updated every five years. Because the pole's rapid movement outpaced the model's predictions, an out-of-cycle update of the World Magnetic Model was released in 2019.

Terminology

By a longstanding quirk of terminology, the magnetic pole near geographic north is physically a magnetic south pole, since it attracts the north-seeking end of a compass needle (opposite polarities attract). In physics the point is sometimes called the "north geomagnetic pole" based on its location rather than polarity.
