Artemisinin (also known as qinghaosu) and its derivatives are a group of drugs used in the treatment of malaria caused by the parasite Plasmodium falciparum. The compound was discovered in 1972 by the Chinese scientist Tu Youyou, who isolated it from the herb Artemisia annua (sweet wormwood), used for centuries in traditional Chinese medicine. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) are now the standard first-line treatment for falciparum malaria worldwide, and their deployment is credited with saving millions of lives. Tu Youyou shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

Discovery

During the Vietnam War, China launched a secret military research programme, Project 523 (started 23 May 1967), to find new antimalarial drugs for its North Vietnamese allies, whose troops were suffering from chloroquine-resistant malaria. Tu Youyou's team screened hundreds of traditional remedies and found that an extract of qinghao (Artemisia annua), prepared with a low-temperature ether extraction suggested by a 4th-century text by Ge Hong, consistently suppressed malaria parasites in animals. The active compound was isolated in 1972 and its structure — a sesquiterpene lactone with an unusual peroxide bridge essential to its activity — was determined by 1975.

Medical use

Artemisinin and its semisynthetic derivatives — artemether, artesunate and dihydroartemisinin — act extremely rapidly against the blood stages of the malaria parasite, clearing fever and parasites faster than older drugs. Because of their short half-lives, they are combined with longer-acting partner drugs in ACTs such as artemether–lumefantrine. Intravenous artesunate is the treatment of choice for severe malaria, reducing mortality compared with quinine.

Resistance

Partial resistance to artemisinin, manifested as delayed parasite clearance and linked to mutations in the parasite's kelch13 gene, emerged in western Cambodia in the late 2000s and has spread in mainland Southeast Asia; it has also been reported in parts of Africa. Preserving the efficacy of ACTs is a central concern of malaria-control programmes.

Production

Artemisinin is obtained by extraction from cultivated A. annua and, since 2013, also by semi-synthesis from artemisinic acid produced by engineered yeast, a project developed at the University of California, Berkeley and commercialised by Sanofi to stabilise supply and price.
