History of Poland (1918–1939)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Marshal Józef Piłsudski dominated interwar Polish politics.

The history of Poland from 1918 to 1939 covers the Second Polish Republic, re-established after 123 years of partition between Russia, Prussia/Germany and Austria. Józef Piłsudski became head of state in November 1918; the new republic fought a series of border wars, most importantly the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921, adopted democratic then increasingly authoritarian government after Piłsudski's May Coup of 1926, and was destroyed by the German and Soviet invasions of September 1939 that began the Second World War.

Rebirth and border wars

Poland's independence was recognised in the Treaty of Versailles; its borders were settled by war and plebiscite: the Greater Poland Uprising, three Silesian Uprisings, the Polish–Ukrainian War over Lwów, and the Polish–Lithuanian conflict over Wilno (Vilnius). In 1920 the Red Army advanced on Warsaw, threatening the state's existence, but was defeated in the Battle of Warsaw — the "Miracle on the Vistula" — and the Treaty of Riga (1921) fixed the eastern frontier.

Politics: democracy to Sanation

The March Constitution of 1921 created a parliamentary republic weakened by fragmented parties and a large national-minority population (about a third of citizens were Ukrainians, Jews, Belarusians and Germans). In May 1926 Piłsudski seized power in a coup, inaugurating the Sanation regime, which ruled through a non-party bloc, restricted the opposition (the Brześć trials), and after Piłsudski's death in 1935 hardened under the "colonels' regime" and the April Constitution.

Economy and society

Interwar Poland unified three legal and railway systems, built the port of Gdynia from scratch, created the Central Industrial District in the 1930s, and stabilised the złoty under Władysław Grabski's reforms, but remained largely agrarian and poor, and was hit hard by the Great Depression. Polish culture and science flourished — the Lwów–Warsaw school of logic, mathematicians such as Stefan Banach, and the Enigma codebreakers who first broke German ciphers.

Foreign policy and the road to war

Wedged between Germany and the USSR, Poland signed non-aggression pacts with the Soviet Union (1932) and Germany (1934) and sought equality between them. In 1938 it annexed Trans-Olza from Czechoslovakia. German demands for Danzig and an extraterritorial corridor, backed by British and French guarantees to Poland in March 1939, led to the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the invasions of 1 and 17 September 1939.
