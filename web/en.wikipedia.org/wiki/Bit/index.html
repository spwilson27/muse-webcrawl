Bit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A single binary digit shown as a glowing 0 and 1]

The bit is the basic unit of information in computing and digital communications, representing a logical state with one of two possible values — most commonly '1' or '0', true or false, on or off. The word is a contraction of 'binary digit', coined by John Tukey in 1947 and adopted into information theory by Claude Shannon in 1948.

Physically a bit may be stored as a transistor's state, a magnetic orientation, an optical mark, or a voltage level. Eight bits form a byte, the usual unit of addressable memory; larger quantities use the prefixes kilo-, mega-, giga- and so on (in computing, often powers of 1024, formally kibi-, mebi-, gibibytes).

In information theory

Shannon defined information in bits: one bit is the information gained when a fair coin lands — resolving uncertainty between two equally likely outcomes. Entropy, data compression limits, and channel capacities are all measured in bits (or bits per second).

Confusingly for consumers, storage and memory are usually counted in bytes while network speeds are advertised in bits per second.
