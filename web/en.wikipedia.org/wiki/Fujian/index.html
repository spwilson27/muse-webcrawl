Fujian (福建; Fújiàn) is a province on the southeastern coast of China, in East China. Its capital is Fuzhou, while its largest city by population is Quanzhou. Fujian lies across the Taiwan Strait from Taiwan; part of the province — the island of Kinmen (Quemoy) and the Matsu Islands — is administered by the Republic of China (Taiwan) as Fujian Province, ROC, though the People's Republic of China claims them as part of its own Fujian.

Etymology

The name Fujian derives from the combination of Fuzhou and Jianning Fu (later Jianzhou), two administrative centres of the Tang dynasty. The province was established under that name during the Tang era.

Geography

Fujian is mostly mountainous — often described as "eight parts mountain, one part water, one part farmland" — with the Wuyi Mountains forming its border with Jiangxi to the northwest. The province has a long, indented coastline on the East China Sea and the Taiwan Strait, with numerous bays and islands. Its climate is subtropical, humid and monsoonal, and it is frequently affected by typhoons in summer and autumn. Major rivers include the Min River, the longest in the province.

History

The region was home to the ancient kingdom of Minyue, conquered by the Han dynasty in 110 BC. Under the Tang and Song dynasties, Fujian flourished as a centre of maritime trade; Quanzhou (known in the West as Zayton) was one of the largest ports in the world during the Song and Yuan dynasties, visited by Marco Polo. The Fujianese diaspora became one of the largest sources of overseas Chinese emigration to Southeast Asia, Taiwan and beyond.

Economy

Fujian is one of China's more prosperous provinces, with an economy based on manufacturing, electronics, footwear and clothing (notably in Jinjiang and Putian), tea production, fishing and maritime trade. Xiamen is one of China's original four Special Economic Zones and a major port city. Famous exports include oolong tea — Tieguanyin from Anxi and rock teas from the Wuyi Mountains — and Shoushan stone carvings.

Culture and languages

Fujian is linguistically one of the most diverse provinces in China. Varieties of Min Chinese — including Hokkien (Minnan), Fuzhou dialect (Mindong), Putian dialect, Minbei and Hakka in the west — are widely spoken alongside Mandarin. The Fujian tulou, enormous communal earthen buildings of the Hakka and Minnan peoples, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The sea goddess Mazu, whose cult originated on Meizhou Island off Putian, is widely venerated in Fujian and Taiwan.

Administrative divisions

Fujian consists of nine prefecture-level cities: Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou, Zhangzhou, Putian, Longyan, Sanming, Nanping and Ningde.
