Atatürk's reforms

Atatürk's reforms (Turkish: Atatürk İnkılâpları or Atatürk Devrimleri) were a sweeping series of political, legal, cultural, social, and economic policy changes implemented in the Republic of Turkey under the leadership of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk between 1922 and the late 1930s. Their goal was to transform the remnants of the Ottoman Empire into a modern, secular, unitary nation-state.

Political and legal reforms

The reforms began with the abolition of the Ottoman sultanate in 1922 and the caliphate in 1924, followed by the proclamation of the republic in 1923. A new constitution was adopted in 1924, and European-derived codes replaced Islamic law: the Swiss civil code and Italian penal code were adapted in 1926, secularizing marriage, inheritance, and the courts. The Hat Law (1925) discouraged traditional religious dress in public.

Language and education

In 1928 Turkey adopted a new Latin-based Turkish alphabet, replacing the Arabic script, and a campaign of language reform sought to purge Arabic and Persian loanwords. Education was unified under the state in 1924, religious schools were closed, and universities were reformed. Women gained the right to vote in municipal elections in 1930 and in national elections in 1934 — earlier than in several European countries.

Economic and social reforms

The state promoted industrialization through five-year plans, adopted the metric system and the Gregorian calendar, introduced surnames (the Surname Law of 1934, which granted Mustafa Kemal the surname Atatürk), and encouraged Western dress and arts. Secularism (laiklik) was written into the constitution in 1937.

Legacy

The reforms remain foundational to the Turkish Republic and are collectively associated with Kemalism, though aspects — particularly strict secularism and language policy — have been debated and partially modified in later decades.
