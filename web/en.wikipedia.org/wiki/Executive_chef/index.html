Executive chef

An executive chef is the senior professional cook who has overall responsibility for the kitchen or kitchens of a restaurant, hotel, resort, or food-service group. In the classical French brigade de cuisine system, the position corresponds to the chef de cuisine at its highest level; in large organisations the executive chef supervises several outlets and a hierarchy of subordinate chefs, combining culinary leadership with budgeting, staffing, and menu strategy.

Role and responsibilities

The executive chef sets the culinary direction of an establishment: designing menus, establishing recipes and presentation standards, and ensuring consistency of quality and food safety across services. Administrative duties typically include food-cost control, purchasing and supplier relationships, kitchen payroll and scheduling, hiring and training, and compliance with hygiene regulations. In a single independent restaurant, the executive chef may also be the owner; in hotel groups and restaurant chains, the role is more managerial, with day-to-day cooking delegated to a head chef or chef de cuisine in each outlet.

Position in the brigade

The classical kitchen brigade, systematised by Auguste Escoffier, divides labour among specialised stations. Beneath the executive chef the typical hierarchy runs:

  • Chef de cuisine — in charge of a single kitchen.
  • Sous-chef — the deputy, coordinating service and standing in for the chef.
  • Chef de partie — in charge of a particular station (saucier, rôtisseur, garde manger, pâtissier, and others).
  • Commis chef — junior cook working under a chef de partie.
  • Kitchen porter / plongeur — washing and general support duties.

Titles vary internationally; in North American usage "executive chef" is the standard top title, while in the United Kingdom the senior cook in a single establishment is more often called the head chef. The title "executive chef" may also be conferred by trade bodies: the American Culinary Federation, for example, certifies a Certified Executive Chef credential based on experience, education, and examination.

Training and career path

Executive chefs usually arrive at the position after a decade or more of progressive experience in professional kitchens, frequently supplemented by culinary school training and apprenticeships. Many have worked across several stations and establishments, sometimes internationally. The modern celebrity chef phenomenon has added public-facing duties for some executive chefs, including media work, cookbooks, and brand endorsements, though for most the role remains centred on operations and leadership within the kitchen.
