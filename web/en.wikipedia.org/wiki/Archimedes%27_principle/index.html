Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle states that the upward buoyant force exerted on a body immersed in a fluid, whether fully or partially submerged, is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. Formulated by Archimedes of Syracuse in the 3rd century BC and set out in his treatise On Floating Bodies, it is a cornerstone of fluid statics.

Formulated byArchimedes of Syracuse
SourceOn Floating Bodies (c. 246 BC)
FormulaBuoyant force = ρ · V · g (density × displaced volume × gravity)

Explanation

Pressure in a fluid increases with depth, so the upward force on the bottom of a submerged object exceeds the downward force on its top; the net force equals the weight of the "missing" fluid. An object floats when its average density is less than the fluid's, submerging until the displaced fluid's weight equals its own.

The crown anecdote

According to Vitruvius, Archimedes was asked by King Hiero II whether a crown was pure gold. Realising in the bath that a body displaces its own volume of water, he could compare the crown's volume with that of equal-weight gold — and supposedly ran naked through Syracuse crying "Eureka!" ("I have found it!"). The tale may be embellished, but the displacement method it describes is sound and still used to measure density.

Applications

The principle governs ship design and loading, submarines and balloons (in air), hydrometers, and the calculation of stability for floating structures.
