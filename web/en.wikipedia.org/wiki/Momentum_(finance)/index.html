Momentum (finance)

In finance, momentum is the empirically observed tendency for rising asset prices to rise further, and falling prices to keep falling. For instance, it was shown that stocks with strong past performance tend to continue outperforming stocks with poor past performance in the next period, and vice versa, in line with the old Wall Street maxim "let your winners ride, cut your losers."

The existence of momentum is a market anomaly, which finance theory struggles to explain. The difficulty is that an increase in asset prices tends to be followed by more increases, and a decrease by more decreases, contrary to the efficient-market hypothesis, according to which asset prices should be nearly impossible to predict and new information should move prices in a more or less random walk.

Empirical evidence

The seminal study documenting momentum was published by Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman in 1993, showing that strategies buying past winners and selling past losers over three- to twelve-month horizons earned significant positive returns in United States stock markets from 1965 to 1989. Momentum has since been documented across many asset classes and countries, including equities, bonds, commodities, and currencies, and over periods longer than a century. It is one of the factors in the Carhart four-factor model, an extension of the Fama–French three-factor model.

Explanations

Behavioural finance explanations attribute momentum to investor biases such as underreaction to news, herding, the disposition effect, and delayed overreaction. Risk-based explanations argue that momentum returns compensate investors for bearing crash risk: momentum strategies can suffer severe, infrequent losses ("momentum crashes"), such as those in 1932 and 2009, when past losers sharply rebounded.

Momentum investing

Momentum investing is a system of buying stocks or other securities that have had high returns over the past three to twelve months, and selling those that have had poor returns over the same period. It is often implemented through trend-following and quantitative funds, and is sometimes combined with value factors, which historically have been negatively correlated with momentum.
