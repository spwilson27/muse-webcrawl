Convention on Cybercrime

The Convention on Cybercrime, also known as the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime or simply the Budapest Convention, is the first international treaty seeking to address Internet and computer crime (cybercrime) by harmonizing national laws, improving investigative techniques, and increasing cooperation among nations. It was drawn up by the Council of Europe in Strasbourg, France, with the active participation of the Council of Europe's observer states Canada, Japan, the Philippines, South Africa and the United States.

The Convention and its Explanatory Report were adopted by the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe at its 109th Session on 8 November 2001. It was opened for signature in Budapest, Hungary on 23 November 2001 and entered into force on 1 July 2004. As of recent years, dozens of states have ratified the Convention, and many more have signed it or been invited to accede.

Objectives

The Convention is the first international treaty on crimes committed via the Internet and other computer networks, dealing particularly with infringements of copyright, computer-related fraud, child pornography and violations of network security. It also contains a series of powers and procedures such as the search of computer networks and lawful interception. Its main objective, set out in the preamble, is to pursue a common criminal policy aimed at the protection of society against cybercrime, especially by adopting appropriate legislation and fostering international cooperation.

Structure

The Convention is divided into four chapters. The first defines key terms and requires parties to criminalize a list of offences against and by means of computers, including illegal access, illegal interception, data interference, system interference, misuse of devices, computer-related forgery and fraud, child pornography offences, and copyright infringements. The second chapter covers procedural law, including expedited preservation of stored computer data and production orders. The third chapter addresses international cooperation and extradition. The fourth contains final provisions.

Additional protocols

A first additional protocol, adopted in 2003, criminalizes the dissemination of racist and xenophobic material through computer systems. A second additional protocol on enhanced cooperation and disclosure of electronic evidence was opened for signature in May 2022.

Criticism

The treaty has been criticized for provisions that some civil liberties groups argue could compel service providers to help monitor communications, as well as for being drafted with limited input from developing countries. Russia has notably declined to join, objecting to provisions allowing foreign law-enforcement access to data.
