Lager

Lager (German: [ˈlaːɡɐ]) is a type of beer conditioned at low temperature. It may be pale, amber or dark. The German word Lager means "storage", reflecting the practice of storing beer in cool caves while it matured. Lager is distinguished from ale by its yeast: lager yeasts, Saccharomyces pastorianus, ferment at colder temperatures (roughly 7–13 °C) and settle to the bottom, and the beer is then stored near freezing for weeks or months. Pale lager, especially in the Pilsner style, is the most consumed and commercially available type of beer in the world.

History

Cold-stored brewing developed in Bavaria, where a 1553 decree restricted brewing to the cooler months and beer was cellared in caves under chestnut trees. In the 19th century several innovations converged: Josef Groll brewed the first golden Pilsner at Plzeň in 1842; Gabriel Sedlmayr and Anton Dreher developed improved amber and Vienna lagers; refrigeration (Carl von Linde's machines, adopted by breweries in the 1870s) freed lager brewing from the seasons; and Emil Christian Hansen's pure-yeast culture at Carlsberg (1883) stabilised quality. Lager yeast itself proved to be a hybrid of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the cold-tolerant Patagonian species Saccharomyces eubayanus. German and Czech emigrants spread lager brewing worldwide, and the pale, adjunct-light style developed in the United States became the global mass-market standard in the 20th century.

Brewing

Lager production follows the usual brewing stages — malting, mashing, lautering, boiling with hops, cooling — but fermentation is slower and colder than for ale, and the subsequent lagering period smooths flavour as yeast and proteins settle out. The result is typically a clean, crisp-tasting beer with fewer fruity esters than ale.

Styles

Recognised lager styles range from pale Pilsner and Munich Helles, through amber Märzen/Oktoberfest and Vienna lager, to dark Dunkel and Schwarzbier, and the strong Bock family (including Doppelbock and Eisbock).
