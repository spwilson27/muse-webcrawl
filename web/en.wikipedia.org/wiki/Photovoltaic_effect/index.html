Photovoltaic effect


The photovoltaic effect is the generation of voltage and electric current in a material upon exposure to light. It underlies the operation of solar cells.

The photovoltaic effect is the creation of voltage and electric current in a material upon exposure to light. It is a physical phenomenon closely related to the photoelectric effect, and forms the operating principle of photovoltaic (solar) cells.

Mechanism

In a typical silicon solar cell, photons with energy above the semiconductor's band gap are absorbed, creating electron–hole pairs. The built-in electric field of a p–n junction separates the charges — electrons toward the n-type side and holes toward the p-type side — producing a voltage across the device; connected to an external circuit, the separated charges drive a current. The maximum efficiency of a single-junction cell under sunlight is bounded by the Shockley–Queisser limit (about 33.7 per cent for an optimal band gap).

History

The effect was first observed by Edmond Becquerel in 1839, who found that illuminating an electrode in a conductive solution produced current. In 1876 William Grylls Adams and Richard Evans Day observed the effect in solid selenium, and in 1883 Charles Fritts built the first solid-state photovoltaic cell. Practical silicon cells arrived in 1954 at Bell Laboratories (Chapin, Fuller and Pearson), and photovoltaics were soon adopted for spacecraft before becoming a major terrestrial source of renewable electricity in the 21st century.
