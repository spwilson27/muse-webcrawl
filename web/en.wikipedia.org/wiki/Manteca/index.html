Manteca is the Spanish word for lard, and in culinary usage it refers to rendered pork fat, a fundamental cooking fat in Spanish, Mexican, and other Latin American cuisines. The term is also used more broadly: in some regions manteca can denote butter or other spreadable fats (in Argentina and Uruguay, manteca commonly means butter), but in Mexican and Spanish cooking it specifically means lard.

Preparation

Manteca is produced by rendering pork fat — slowly heating chopped or ground fat so that the pure fat melts out, leaving behind crisped solids known as chicharrones. Mexican-style manteca is often rendered with small pieces of meat attached to the fat, giving it a deeper colour and a roasted flavour prized for making carnitas. Freshly rendered lard is pale and mild; as it cools it sets into a soft white to cream-coloured solid.

Culinary uses

Manteca is the traditional fat for:

  • Tamales and masa dishes — whipped lard gives tamales their characteristic light, tender texture.
  • Refried beans (frijoles refritos) — lard provides the signature richness.
  • Carnitas — pork slowly confited in its own lard.
  • Flour tortillas — especially in northern Mexico and the American Southwest.
  • Pastry — in Spain, mantecados and polvorones are shortbread-like Christmas biscuits made with lard, whose name derives from manteca.

Nutrition and decline

During the twentieth century, lard fell out of favour in many countries amid concerns about saturated fat and cholesterol, and was widely replaced by vegetable shortening and oils. Lard contains less saturated fat than butter and is a source of vitamin D, and it has seen a modest revival among chefs who value its flavour and the flaky pastry it produces.
