The Panthay Rebellion (Chinese: 杜文秀起義; 1856–1873), known in Chinese historiography as the Du Wenxiu Rebellion, was a major revolt of the Hui Muslim population of Yunnan province against the ruling Qing dynasty. It was one of the great rebellions that shook 19th-century China, alongside the Taiping Rebellion and the Dungan Revolt in the northwest. The name "Panthay" derives from the Burmese word for Chinese Muslims, applied by British observers of the era.

Background

Yunnan's Hui community — descendants of Muslim soldiers, officials and merchants settled in the region since the Mongol Yuan dynasty — faced growing friction with Han Chinese neighbours, fuelled by disputes over silver-mining rights and a widespread belief that Qing officials favoured Han litigants. In 1856 a local clash near Shiyang escalated after officials authorised massacres of Hui villagers, provoking a general uprising across the province.

The Sultanate of Dali

The rebels rallied around Du Wenxiu (1823–1872), a Hui scholar from Yongchang, who captured Dali in western Yunnan and proclaimed a new state, the Pingnan Guo ("Pacified Southern State"), often rendered in Western sources as a sultanate. Du styled himself Qa'id Jami al-Muslimin ("Commander of the Community of Muslims") and ruled for some sixteen years. His regime controlled much of western Yunnan, minted its own coinage, and appointed both Muslim and Han officials; Du sought to reassure non-Muslims with proclamations against communal violence and even dispatched an embassy to Britain in 1872 seeking support that never came.

Collapse

The rebellion's fate turned on divisions among the Hui themselves. The movement's eastern wing under Ma Rulong accepted Qing amnesty and joined imperial forces, which gradually recovered Kunming and besieged Dali. After a long siege the city fell in early 1873; Du Wenxiu took poison before capture and died, and Qing troops massacred much of Dali's remaining population. Survivors fled into Burma and Siam, where Panthay communities and caravan trade networks persisted for decades.

Aftermath

The war devastated Yunnan: population losses across the province, from fighting, massacre and disease, are estimated in the millions. Hui communities were suppressed and their mosques restricted for a generation, although the community recovered during the late Qing and Republican periods.
