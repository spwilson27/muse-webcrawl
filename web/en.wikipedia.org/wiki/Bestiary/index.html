Bestiary

A bestiary (from Latin bestiarum vocabulum) is a compendium of beasts. Originating in the ancient world, bestiaries were made popular in the Middle Ages in illustrated volumes that described various animals and even rocks. The natural history and illustration of each beast was usually accompanied by a moral lesson. This reflected the belief that the world itself was the Word of God and that every living thing had its own special meaning.

History

The bestiary tradition descends from the Physiologus, an anonymous Greek text from around the 2nd century AD, which collected animal lore and allegorised it in Christian terms. Medieval bestiaries, especially the lavishly illuminated English and French manuscripts of the 12th and 13th centuries, expanded this material with descriptions drawn from Pliny the Elder, Isidore of Seville, and other authorities.

Contents

Bestiaries described real animals — lions, elephants, beavers — alongside legendary creatures such as the unicorn, the dragon, the basilisk and the phoenix. Entries typically gave the animal's supposed habits and then a moral or theological interpretation: the pelican, believed to revive its young with its own blood, became a symbol of Christ. Famous examples include the Aberdeen Bestiary and the Ashmole Bestiary. The genre influenced heraldry, church sculpture, and later natural history writing, even though its zoology mixed observation with fable.
