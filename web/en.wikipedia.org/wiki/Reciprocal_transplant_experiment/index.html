Reciprocal transplant experiment

A reciprocal transplant experiment is a classic experimental design in evolutionary ecology used to test for local adaptation. Individuals or populations from two (or more) different habitats are each raised both in their home environment and in the other population's environment, with fitness or performance measured in each combination.

Logic

If populations are locally adapted, a "local versus foreign" pattern is expected: on average, local individuals should outperform immigrants in their own habitat. A complementary "home versus away" criterion asks whether each population performs better at home than it does away. By crossing source population with test environment, the design separates genetic differences between populations from plastic responses to the environment, and reveals genotype-by-environment interactions.

History and use

The design descends from the common-garden and transplant studies of Turesson in the 1920s and the landmark reciprocal transplants of Clausen, Keck and Hiesey across California altitudinal gradients in the 1940s, which demonstrated ecotypic differentiation in plants such as Achillea and Potentilla. Meta-analyses of hundreds of later studies have found local adaptation to be common but not universal. The same design is also used with animals — for example intertidal snails and fishes — and, in modified form, to study adaptation to climate change by transplanting populations along temperature gradients.
