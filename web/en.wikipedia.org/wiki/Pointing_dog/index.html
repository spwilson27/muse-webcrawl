Pointing dog

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pointing dogs, sometimes called bird dogs, are a type of gun dog typically used in finding game. Gun dogs are traditionally divided into three classes: retrievers, flushing dogs, and pointing breeds. The name pointer comes from the dog's instinct to point, by stopping and aiming its muzzle towards game. This demonstrates to the hunter the location of their quarry and allows them to move into gun range. Pointers were selectively bred from dogs who had an abundant pointing and backing instinct. They typically start to acquire their pointing instinct as early as 2 months of age.

History

Pointing dogs may descend from dogs from Spain, specifically of the Old Spanish Pointer. Pointing dogs were originally used by hunters who netted game. The dog would freeze or set (as in Setter) and allow the hunter to cast the net over the game before it flushed. Flushing dogs were then introduced for hunting with firearms, and the pointing dog's role became locating game and holding steady while the hunter approached.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, British sportsmen refined pointing breeds on the grouse moors and lowland estates of England and Scotland, producing the English Pointer and the setters. On the European continent, versatile pointing breeds such as the German Shorthaired Pointer were developed to point, retrieve, and track both feathered and furred game.

Breeds

Pointing breeds include:

  • Wirehaired Pointing Griffon
  • Spinone Italiano

Training and work

A pointing dog works by ranging ahead of the hunter, quartering into the wind and using air scent to locate game birds. On scenting birds, the dog freezes in the characteristic point, body tense, one foreleg often raised, nose directed at the hidden birds. A well-trained dog remains steady to wing and shot, not moving until released, and may then be sent to retrieve. Dogs are also expected to honour or back the point of a bracemate by stopping when they see another dog on point.
