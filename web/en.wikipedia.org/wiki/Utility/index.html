Utility

[Image: a consumer happily drinking a glass of water on a hot day, illustrating marginal utility]

In Economics, utility is the satisfaction or benefit a consumer derives from consuming a good or service, and the central maximand of neoclassical consumer theory: rational agents are modelled as choosing the affordable bundle that maximises utility.

Concepts

The marginal revolution (Jevons, Menger, Walras, 1870s) explained prices by marginal utility — the extra satisfaction from one more unit — which diminishes as consumption rises, resolving the paradox that water, though essential, is cheap while diamonds are dear. Early writers treated utility as measurable (cardinal); modern theory needs only rankings (ordinal), represented by indifference curves subject to a budget constraint. Utility functions formalise preferences in models of choice, risk (expected utility, von Neumann–Morgenstern) and welfare. Behavioural economics documents systematic departures: framing, loss aversion and reference-dependence, where 'utility' splits into decision and experienced forms (Kahneman).
