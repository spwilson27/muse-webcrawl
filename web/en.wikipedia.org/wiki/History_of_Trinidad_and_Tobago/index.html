History of Trinidad and Tobago

The history of Trinidad and Tobago begins with the settlements of the islands by Indigenous peoples thousands of years before European contact. Both islands were visited by Christopher Columbus on his third voyage in 1498 and claimed in the name of Spain. Trinidad remained a Spanish possession until 1797, but it was largely settled by French colonists. Tobago changed hands between the British, French, Dutch and Courlanders more times than any other Caribbean island, but it was finally ceded to Britain in 1814. The two islands were administratively joined in 1889. Trinidad and Tobago achieved independence from the United Kingdom on 31 August 1962 and became a republic in 1976.

Trinidad and Tobago lie just off the northeastern coast of Venezuela.

Pre-Columbian history

Human settlement in Trinidad dates back at least 7,000 years. The earliest settlers were Amerindians of the Ortoiroid tradition, followed by Saladoid peoples who brought agriculture and ceramics around 250 BC. The Arawakan-speaking Nepuyo and the Cariban-speaking Kalina (Caribs) were the main groups on Trinidad at European contact; Tobago was occupied by Kalinago and Galibi peoples.

Spanish colonisation

Columbus sighted Trinidad on 31 July 1498 and named it La Isla de la Trinidad. Spanish settlement was slow; San José de Oruña (St Joseph) was founded in 1592, but the island remained a backwater of the Spanish Empire, valued chiefly as a staging point for expeditions in search of El Dorado. In 1776 the Cedula of Population invited Catholic settlers — mostly French planters with their enslaved Africans — and the population grew rapidly.

British rule

A British fleet under Sir Ralph Abercromby captured Trinidad in 1797, and the island was formally ceded by the Treaty of Amiens in 1802. After the abolition of slavery in 1834, planters turned to indentured labour; the first Indian indentured labourers arrived aboard the Fatel Rozack in 1845. Tobago was annexed to Trinidad in 1889. Oil was discovered in the early twentieth century and became the backbone of the economy.

Independence

The People's National Movement under Eric Williams led the country to independence on 31 August 1962. Trinidad and Tobago joined the Commonwealth and the United Nations. Williams, remembered as the "Father of the Nation", served as prime minister until his death in 1981. The country became a republic on 1 August 1976, with Ellis Clarke as its first president. Key events since independence include the 1970 Black Power Revolution, the 1990 attempted coup by the Jamaat al Muslimeen, and continuing dependence on oil and natural gas revenues.
