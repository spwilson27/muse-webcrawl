Saya (music)

The saya is an Afro-Bolivian music and dance genre, one of the most prominent expressions of Bolivia's African-descendant community, concentrated mainly in the Yungas region of La Paz Department — in towns such as Tocaña, Mururata, and Coroico. The word saya derives from Kikongo ensaya, referring to a figure of authority or a gathering led by a singer.

Saya
Stylistic origins: African rhythms in Bolivia
Cultural origins: Afro-Bolivian communities, Yungas
Instruments: Drums (caja, tambor), requinto, bells, shakers

Characteristics

Saya is performed by large ensembles in which lines of dancers move in synchronised steps while percussionists play a distinctive rhythmic pattern on drums of different sizes; a lead singer (the capataz or mayoral, recalling the colonial overseer) directs the group, and a prominent role is given to the sound of bells worn on the trousers of the male dancers. Lyrics are sung in Spanish, often with call-and-response choruses, and treat themes of identity, history, love, and social protest.

History and recognition

The Afro-Bolivian community descends from enslaved Africans brought in the colonial era to work in the mines of Potosí and, later, agricultural estates in the Yungas. Long marginalised, the community used saya in the 1980s and 1990s as a vehicle of cultural revival and political visibility, contributing to constitutional recognition of Afro-Bolivians. In 2011 the Plurinational State of Bolivia declared Afro-Bolivian saya and caporales-related cultural expressions part of the nation's cultural heritage, and Bolivia has sought UNESCO recognition for saya. A commercialised offshoot, the saya caporal heard in Andean folk-pop, is distinct from the traditional Afro-Bolivian saya — a distinction community organisations actively defend.
