Pride parade

A pride parade (also known as a pride event, pride festival, pride march, or pride protest) is an outdoor event celebrating lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ) social acceptance, achievements, legal rights, and pride. The events sometimes also serve as demonstrations for legal rights such as same-sex marriage. Most pride events occur annually, many in June to commemorate the 1969 Stonewall riots in New York City, a pivotal moment in the modern LGBTQ social movement.

History

On 28 June 1970, the first pride marches took place in Chicago, Los Angeles, New York, and San Francisco, marking the first anniversary of the Stonewall uprising, in which patrons of the Stonewall Inn resisted a police raid. Activist Brenda Howard, known as the "Mother of Pride", coordinated the New York march — the Christopher Street Liberation Day march — and helped popularize the idea of a week of events around it. Early marches were explicitly political protests of thousands rather than millions; attendance and local acceptance grew through the 1980s and 1990s, sometimes in the face of organized opposition and legal challenges over permits. Fred Sargeant, a participant in the riots, helped organize the 1970 New York march.

Symbols and character

The rainbow flag, designed by Gilbert Baker in 1978, is the best-known symbol of pride. Modern events range from solemn commemorations — some, such as Dyke Marches and Trans Marches, retain a strong protest character — to large civic festivals with floats, corporate sponsors, and hundreds of thousands or millions of participants. The largest gatherings include São Paulo's Parada do Orgulho LGBT, commonly cited as the world's largest parade, and major events in New York, Madrid (WorldPride 2017), Toronto, Sydney (Gay and Lesbian Mardi Gras), and Taipei, the largest in Asia.

Spread and opposition

Pride events now occur in hundreds of cities worldwide, including in places where participants face substantial risk: the first Moscow Pride (2006) and various Eastern European marches, such as Belgrade and Budapest, have met with bans, counter-protests, and violence, and organizing such marches is itself a form of activism. In some countries homosexuality remains criminalized and no public events are possible. Debates within LGBTQ communities concern the commercialization of pride, the participation of police and corporations, and the balance between celebration and protest.
