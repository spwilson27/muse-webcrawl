Weald

The Weald is a region of South East England, the name being derived from an Old English term for forest. The name was originally used for the dense woodland which, before and during the Middle Ages, separated the settlements of the early Saxon kingdoms. Its boundaries enclose an area which includes parts of Kent, Sussex, Hampshire, Surrey and, marginally, Berkshire.

The Weald lies between the parallel chalk escarpments of the North and South Downs. It consists geologically of a mixture of sandstone ridges and low-lying clay vales, the result of uplift and erosion of a dome-shaped layer of sedimentary rock, known as the Weald–Artois Anticline. The highest point in the region is Leith Hill near Dorking, at 294 metres (965 ft), and Blackdown in West Sussex at 280 metres (919 ft).

Divisions

The Weald is traditionally divided into the High Weald, a sandstone upland in the centre classified as an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty, and the surrounding Low Weald of clay vales and gentle hills. The Wealden Greensand forms an intermediate arc of hills. Historically the region was divided between the Weald of Kent and the Weald of Sussex.

History

The Weald was the centre of England's medieval iron industry, using its abundance of iron ore, water power and charcoal from its forests; Wealden iron was cast and wrought in hundreds of bloomeries, hammer ponds and furnaces. The industry declined as coke-smelting and the coalfields of the north grew, and the last furnaces closed in the early 19th century. The region also supported glassmaking, shipbuilding timber, hop growing and a distinctive tradition of timber-framed houses, including the "Wealden hall house".
