Linking number

In mathematics, the linking number is a numerical invariant that describes the linking of two closed curves in three-dimensional space. Intuitively, the linking number represents the number of times that each curve winds around the other. In knot theory, the linking number is always an integer, but may be positive or negative depending on the orientation of the two curves.

The linking number was introduced by Carl Friedrich Gauss in the form of the linking integral. It is an important object of study in knot theory, algebraic topology, and differential geometry, and has numerous applications in mathematics and science, including quantum computing, fluid dynamics, and DNA research.

Definition

For a straightforward geometric definition of the linking number, consider a projection of the two-curve link onto a plane in three-dimensional space. Mark each crossing point where one curve passes over the other, and assign a sign of +1 or -1 according to the right-hand rule. The linking number is then defined as half the sum of the signed crossings between the two distinct components:

link(A, B) = (1/2) · Σ (signs of crossings between A and B)

The division by two accounts for the fact that the two curves must cross an even number of times in any projection, and that each "winding" contributes two crossings. The result is independent of the chosen projection and of the regularity of the representation, making it a genuine invariant of the link.

Integral formulation

Gauss's original definition expresses the linking number as a double integral over the two curves. Given two closed curves parametrised by r1(s) and r2(t), the linking integral is:

link = (1/4π) ∮ ∮ [(r1 − r2) · (dr1 × dr2)] / |r1 − r2|3

This integral can also be interpreted in terms of the degree of the Gauss map, which sends pairs of points on the two curves to the unit sphere via the direction of the vector joining them.

Properties

The linking number is unchanged under ambient isotopy of the two curves, so linked curves with nonzero linking number cannot be separated without cutting. However, a linking number of zero does not guarantee that the curves are unlinked — the Whitehead link is a famous example of a nontrivial link whose linking number is zero. For a single closed curve, analogous quantities such as writhe and twist appear, related through the Călugăreanu–Whitehead–Fuller theorem.
