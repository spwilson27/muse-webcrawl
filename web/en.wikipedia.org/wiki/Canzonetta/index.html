Canzonetta

A canzonetta (Italian for "little song"; plural canzonette, also spelled canzonetta or canzonete) is a short, light Italian secular vocal piece, especially popular in the late sixteenth century. Canzonette were written for three to six voices, often with dance-like rhythms and simple, predominantly chordal (homophonic) textures, in deliberate contrast to the complex counterpoint of the more serious madrigal.

History

The canzonetta emerged in Italy in the 1570s and 1580s as publishers and composers sought lighter fare for amateur singers. It is closely related to the villanella and the dance-song called the balletto. Leading composers included Orazio Vecchi, Luca Marenzio, Claudio Monteverdi (whose first two published books, in 1584 and 1587, were canzonette for three voices), Felice Anerio, and Giovanni Giacomo Gastoldi, whose balletti of 1591 were widely imitated across Europe.

The form spread quickly beyond Italy. In England, Thomas Morley's canzonets and balletts of the 1590s adapted the Italian model and helped ignite the English madrigal vogue; in Germany, Hans Leo Hassler and others published canzonette in the Italian style. Typical features include strophic form with refrains, "fa-la" refrains in dance types, short phrases, and syllabic text setting.

Later usage

The word "canzonetta" was later used loosely as a title for short, song-like instrumental or vocal pieces by composers of subsequent centuries, without implying the Renaissance form. Examples include the slow movement ("Canzonetta") of Tchaikovsky's Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 35, and short piano pieces by various Romantic composers. In baroque opera and cantata, the diminutive could also denote a brief aria or song section.
