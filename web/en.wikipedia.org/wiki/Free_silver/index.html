Free silver was a major economic policy issue in the United States in the late nineteenth century. Its advocates — the "silverites" — demanded the "free and unlimited coinage of silver", meaning a policy of bimetallism in which the United States Mint would accept silver bullion from anyone and strike it into legal-tender dollars at the statutory ratio of 16 ounces of silver to 1 ounce of gold, regardless of the metal's market price.

Background

The Coinage Act of 1873 ended the coinage of standard silver dollars — denounced by silver supporters as the "Crime of '73" — and effectively placed the United States on the gold standard. As silver production boomed in the American West and prices of farm goods fell in a long deflation, indebted farmers and silver miners argued that expanding the money supply with silver would raise prices and ease debts. Congress partially relented with the Bland–Allison Act (1878) and the Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890), which required limited government silver purchases, but these fell short of free coinage. The Sherman Act was repealed after the Panic of 1893 drained the Treasury's gold reserve.

The campaign of 1896

Free silver became the rallying cry of the Democratic Party in the election of 1896. William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech at the Democratic National Convention — "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold" — won him the nomination at age 36. Bryan was also endorsed by the Populist Party, but he lost the election to Republican William McKinley, who defended the gold standard. The discovery of gold in the Klondike and South Africa, together with improved extraction methods, soon expanded the money supply anyway, and the Gold Standard Act of 1900 formally ended the bimetallism debate; the free silver movement faded.
