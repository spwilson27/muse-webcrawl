Contradiction

In logic, a contradiction is a situation in which inconsistent statements are both taken to be true, or a statement that is necessarily false because it asserts and denies the same proposition. In classical logic a contradiction is symbolised by ⊥ (the "bottom" or falsum sign) and is expressed, for a proposition p, as p ∧ ¬p.

A proposition and its negation cannot both be true in classical logic.

Law of noncontradiction

Aristotle formulated the law of noncontradiction: contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time — for example, "A is B" and "A is not B" are mutually exclusive. Aristotle called it "the most certain of all principles" in the Metaphysics, arguing that anyone who denies it must still rely on it in order to speak meaningfully.

Proof by contradiction

In mathematics, reductio ad absurdum or proof by contradiction establishes a claim by assuming its negation and deriving a contradiction, thereby showing the assumption must be false. Famous examples include the proofs that √2 is irrational and that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

Principle of explosion

In classical logic, from a contradiction anything follows (ex falso quodlibet, the principle of explosion): once both p and ¬p are admitted, every proposition becomes provable, rendering the system trivial. Paraconsistent logics reject this principle and tolerate some contradictions without collapse. Dialetheism, defended by Graham Priest, holds that some contradictions — such as the liar paradox — may actually be true.

In philosophy and rhetoric

In Hegel and Marxist dialectics, "contradiction" refers to opposing forces or tendencies whose tension drives change. In everyday language, a contradiction may simply mean inconsistent statements or actions, as in a self-contradictory testimony.
