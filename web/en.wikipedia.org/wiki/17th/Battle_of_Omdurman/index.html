The Battle of Omdurman was fought on 2 September 1898 between the Anglo-Egyptian army of General Herbert Kitchener and the Mahdist forces of the Khalifa Abdallahi ibn Muhammad outside Omdurman, across the Nile from Khartoum. The battle ended Mahdist rule in Sudan. Among the British cavalry present was the 21st Lancers — not the 17th Lancers, who were not present in Sudan — whose celebrated charge against an unexpectedly large Mahdist force in a hidden wadi became the most famous cavalry episode of the battle; a young Winston Churchill, serving with both the 4th Hussars (attached) and the 21st Lancers, rode in the charge.

Background

Following the death of General Gordon at Khartoum in 1885, Britain and Egypt had withdrawn from Sudan. In 1896 Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian army began the reconquest, advancing methodically up the Nile with railway and gunboat support, winning at Firket, Atbara, and finally advancing on the Mahdist capital.

The battle

Kitchener's force of some 8,200 British and 17,600 Egyptian and Sudanese troops, equipped with Maxim guns, modern artillery, and gunboats, faced perhaps 52,000 Mahdist warriors. Attacks launched across open ground were shattered by rifle and artillery fire with enormous losses; Mahdist casualties amounted to some 12,000 killed and 13,000 wounded, against fewer than 50 Anglo-Egyptian dead. The 21st Lancers' charge — sent to clear fleeing enemy from the road to Omdurman — ran into a concealed force of about 2,500 men in a depression; the regiment cut its way through in a short, costly melee, winning three Victoria Crosses.

Aftermath

Khartoum was reoccupied and Sudan was placed under an Anglo-Egyptian condominium in 1899. The battle became a byword for the disparity between industrial and pre-industrial warfare, summarised in Hilaire Belloc's couplet: "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not."
