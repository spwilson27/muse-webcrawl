The Holocaust

The Holocaust was the genocide of European Jews carried out by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during the Second World War. Between 1941 and 1945, some six million Jews — two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population — were systematically murdered, along with millions of other victims of Nazi persecution, including Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and people with disabilities.

Development

Nazi persecution began after Adolf Hitler came to power in 1933, with boycotts, the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripping Jews of citizenship, and the Kristallnacht pogrom of 1938. Following the invasion of Poland in 1939, Jews were confined in ghettos; after the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, Einsatzgruppen massacred over a million Jews by shooting. From late 1941 the regime implemented the "Final Solution": deportation to extermination camps such as Auschwitz, Treblinka, Belzec, Sobibor, Chelmno and Majdanek, where most victims were killed in gas chambers. Others died in concentration camps from starvation, disease and forced labour.

Aftermath

Liberation of the camps by the Allies in 1945 exposed the crimes to the world; leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg. The Holocaust profoundly shaped post-war international law, including the Genocide Convention of 1948, and remains central to historical memory, commemorated on International Holocaust Remembrance Day, 27 January.
