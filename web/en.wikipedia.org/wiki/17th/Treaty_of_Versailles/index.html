Treaty of Versailles

The Treaty of Versailles was the peace treaty that ended the state of war between Germany and the Allied Powers at the end of the First World War. It was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors of the Palace of Versailles, exactly five years after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, and came into force on 10 January 1920.

Terms

Article 231, the "war guilt" clause, assigned responsibility for the war to Germany and its allies, forming the legal basis for reparations, later set at 132 billion gold marks. Germany lost about 13% of its territory and all its overseas colonies; Alsace-Lorraine returned to France, and the Rhineland was demilitarised. The German army was limited to 100,000 men without tanks, heavy artillery or an air force, and the navy was drastically reduced. The treaty's first part established the League of Nations.

Reception and consequences

In Germany the treaty was widely denounced as a Diktat; the United States Senate refused ratification, and the US made a separate peace in 1921. Historians debate its severity and its role in the collapse of the Weimar Republic and the rise of Adolf Hitler, who made its overturning a central political demand. Reparations were effectively ended by the Lausanne Conference of 1932.
