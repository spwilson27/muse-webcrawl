The 17th Lancers (Duke of Cambridge's Own) was a cavalry regiment of the British Army, raised in 1759 as the 18th Regiment of (Light) Dragoons and most famous for its part in the Charge of the Light Brigade at the Battle of Balaclava in 1854. The doubled form "17th/17th" appears in some archival references to the regiment's lineage; in 1922 the regiment was amalgamated with the 21st Lancers to form the 17th/21st Lancers.

History

The regiment was raised in Ireland in 1759 by Colonel John Hale as part of the expansion of light cavalry during the Seven Years' War. Hale had brought home the despatches announcing the death of General Wolfe at Quebec, and the regiment's skull-and-crossbones badge and "Death or Glory" motto commemorated that event. Renumbered the 17th in 1761, it served in Germany, then in North America during the American Revolutionary War, fighting at White Plains, Monmouth, and in the southern campaigns.

Charge of the Light Brigade

During the Crimean War the regiment formed part of the Light Brigade under Lord Cardigan. On 25 October 1854, owing to a misunderstood order, the brigade charged the length of the North Valley at Balaclava directly into Russian artillery. Of the roughly 670 men of the brigade, over 100 were killed and many more wounded. The 17th Lancers, led by Captain William Morris, suffered heavily but retained cohesion; the charge was immortalised in Tennyson's poem.

Later service and amalgamation

The regiment later served in the Indian Mutiny, the Zulu War of 1879 — where it fought at Ulundi — and the Second Boer War. Converted to lancers in 1861, it adopted the lance and the distinctive czapka headdress. After service in the First World War, it was amalgamated in 1922 with the 21st Lancers (Empress of India's) to form the 17th/21st Lancers, whose lineage continues today in the Royal Lancers.
