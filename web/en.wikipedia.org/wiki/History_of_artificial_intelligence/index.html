History of artificial intelligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The 1956 Dartmouth workshop launched AI as a field.

The history of artificial intelligence (AI) began in antiquity with myths of artificial beings, and as a scientific field at the Dartmouth workshop of 1956, where John McCarthy coined the term. It has proceeded in cycles of optimism and "AI winters": early symbol-manipulating programmes and perceptrons, expert systems in the 1980s, statistical machine learning in the 1990s–2000s, and the deep-learning revolution after 2012, culminating in large language models in the 2020s.

Foundations and early optimism (1940s–1974)

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the imitation game; Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts had modelled artificial neurons in 1943. Early successes included the Logic Theorist (1956), the General Problem Solver, ELIZA (1966) and Shakey the robot. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) promised learning machines, but Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's Perceptrons (1969) exposed its limits.

Winters and expert systems (1974–1993)

Overpromising, the Lighthill report (1973) and funding cuts brought the first AI winter. Rule-based expert systems such as MYCIN and XCON revived commercial interest in the early 1980s, and Japan's Fifth Generation project spurred Western funding, but the collapse of the Lisp machine market and the brittleness of hand-coded knowledge produced a second winter around 1987–1993.

Statistical learning and deep learning (1993–present)

Probabilistic methods, support vector machines and reinforcement learning quietly matured; IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov at chess in 1997 and Watson won Jeopardy! in 2011. In 2012 AlexNet's deep convolutional network transformed computer vision, powered by GPUs and big data. The transformer architecture (2017) underlies large language models such as GPT-3 (2020) and ChatGPT (2022), which brought AI to mass attention, while AlphaGo (2016) and AlphaFold (2020) demonstrated deep learning's scientific reach.
