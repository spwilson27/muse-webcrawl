Language universals

Language universals are properties claimed to hold for all human languages. Some are absolute — for example, all languages have vowels and consonants, distinguish nouns and verbs in some fashion, and can form questions — while most known candidates are statistical or implicational: they state tendencies, or that if a language has property X it also has property Y. The study of universals is central to linguistic typology and to debates about whether language reflects an innate universal grammar, general cognitive constraints, or the pressures of communication and history.

Greenberg's universals

Joseph H. Greenberg's 1963 paper proposed 45 universals based on a sample of 30 languages, mostly concerning word order. Famous examples include Universal 1 ("In declarative sentences with nominal subject and object, the dominant order is almost always one in which the subject precedes the object"), Universal 3 (languages with dominant VSO order are always prepositional), and Universal 41 (if a language has grammatical gender, it always has number). Implicational universals — of the form "if a language has X, it has Y" — became the standard format of typological generalisation, later tested against databases such as the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS).

Chomsky and universal grammar

In generative linguistics, Noam Chomsky's theory of universal grammar (UG) holds that children are born with an innate, species-specific endowment that constrains the form of possible human languages, explaining the speed and uniformity of first-language acquisition. Proposed UG constraints include structure-dependence of rules and binding principles. Critics have challenged many proposed universals as artefacts of limited samples, functional pressures (ease of processing, communication efficiency), or general cognition; Daniel Everett's claims about Pirahã — that it lacks recursion and other presumed universals — became a prominent test case in this debate. Contemporary work often seeks statistical universals explained by competing motivations in grammar rather than hard innate constraints.

Examples

Widely accepted absolute or near-absolute universals: all languages have consonants and vowels; all can express negation and questions; all have pronouns and words for basic categories such as body parts; syllables prefer simple CV structure. Strong statistical universals: subject-before-object order dominates (over 90% of languages); most languages are suffixing rather than prefixing; languages with object-before-verb order tend to have postpositions.
