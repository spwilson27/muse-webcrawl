Tropospheric propagation

Radio waves refracted and ducted within the troposphere

Tropospheric propagation describes the ways in which radio waves travel through the troposphere, the lowest layer of the Earth's atmosphere. It is of particular importance for VHF, UHF and microwave signals, which normally travel roughly in straight lines and are limited to line-of-sight distances.

Mechanisms

Several phenomena allow signals to travel beyond the normal horizon:

  • Refraction — the refractive index of air normally decreases with height, gently bending radio waves toward the Earth and extending the radio horizon slightly beyond the optical horizon.
  • Tropospheric ducting — under temperature inversions, a sharp change in refractive index can trap waves in a layer of the atmosphere, guiding them for hundreds of kilometres with little loss.
  • Tropospheric scatter (troposcatter) — scattering from turbulence and irregularities in the troposphere allows reliable over-the-horizon communication on specially engineered links.
  • Rain scatter and knife-edge diffraction — scattering from precipitation and bending over obstacles, used especially by radio amateurs.

Practical effects

Tropospheric enhancements can bring distant television and FM radio stations into range, a phenomenon exploited by DXing hobbyists, but they can also cause co-channel interference between distant services. Ducting over warm seas, such as the Mediterranean and the Persian Gulf, is especially common.
