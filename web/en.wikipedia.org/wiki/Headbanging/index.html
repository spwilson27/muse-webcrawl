Headbanging

Headbanging is the action of shaking one's head rhythmically and energetically in time to music. It is most strongly associated with heavy metal and hard rock audiences and performers, and typically involves long hair being swung in arcs or circles. Variations include the "windmill", in which the head is rotated in a circle, and up-and-down styles used across metal subgenres.

Origins

The term "headbanger" dates to the late 1960s. Led Zeppelin's first US tour in 1969 is often cited: front-row fans at a Boston Tea Party concert were reportedly banging their heads against the stage in time to the music. Angus Young of AC/DC popularised a duck-walking, head-shaking stage style in the 1970s, and by the early 1980s headbanging was a defining gesture of metal culture alongside the "devil horns" sign.

Health effects

Medical literature documents injuries associated with vigorous headbanging, including whiplash, neck strain and, in occasional case reports, more serious conditions such as subdural haematoma and carotid artery dissection. A 2008 study in the BMJ by Australian researchers modeled headbanging dynamics and concluded that injury risk increases at tempos above about 130 beats per minute when the range of motion is large; their (partly tongue-in-cheek) recommendations included limiting neck movement and substituting less violent motions. Musicians such as Metallica bassist Jason Newsted and Megadeth's Dave Mustaine have reported chronic neck and spine problems attributed partly to years of headbanging.
