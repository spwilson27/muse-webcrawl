Islam and peace

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The relationship between Islam and peace is a subject discussed in Islamic theology, law and contemporary public debate. The Arabic word Islām derives from the Semitic root s-l-m, which is associated with peace, safety and submission; salām means "peace". Muslims greet one another with as-salāmu ʿalaykum ("peace be upon you"), and "peace" (salām) is one of the names of God in Islam.

Scriptural sources

The Quran repeatedly associates guidance with "paths of peace" (Q 5:16) and states that God invites humankind to the "abode of peace" (Q 10:25). Verses such as Q 2:256 ("There is no compulsion in religion") and Q 8:61 ("If they incline to peace, incline thou to it") are frequently cited in discussions of peaceful relations. Other verses address warfare in contexts of self-defence and persecution, and classical jurists developed a body of law governing both, including the rules collected under jihad and international law (siyar).

Historical practice

Early Islamic history includes both treaties — most famously the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah between Muhammad and the Quraysh of Mecca — and armed conflict. Later Muslim empires concluded numerous peace settlements, and the status of dār al-ṣulḥ ("abode of treaty") was recognised by many jurists alongside the categories of dār al-Islām and dār al-ḥarb.

Modern discussions

In the modern era, Muslim scholars and organisations have issued statements emphasising peace, coexistence and the rejection of terrorism, including the Amman Message (2004) and the Marrakesh Declaration (2016) on the rights of religious minorities. Academic and popular writers differ over how scriptural and historical evidence should be weighed; Orientalist, Islamist and reformist readings of the tradition have all been influential.
