Ulnar nerve

The ulnar nerve is a nerve in the human body that runs near the ulna bone. It is the largest unprotected nerve in the body (meaning unprotected by muscle or bone), and striking it at the elbow — where it passes behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus — produces the sharp, tingling sensation colloquially called hitting one's "funny bone". The nerve arises from the medial cord of the brachial plexus (roots C8 and T1) and provides motor and sensory innervation to parts of the forearm and hand.

From: Medial cord of brachial plexus (C8, T1)
Innervates: Flexor carpi ulnaris, medial flexor digitorum profundus, most intrinsic hand muscles
Sensory: Medial hand and 1½ digits

Course and function

Descending the arm, the ulnar nerve passes posterior to the medial epicondyle through the cubital tunnel, then runs down the ulnar forearm between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. It supplies flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus. At the wrist it traverses Guyon's canal and divides into superficial (sensory) and deep motor branches; the deep branch supplies the hypothenar muscles, interossei, the medial two lumbricals, adductor pollicis and the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis. Sensation covers the medial hand and the little finger and medial ring finger.

Clinical significance

Compression at the elbow (cubital tunnel syndrome) is the second most common entrapment neuropathy after carpal tunnel syndrome, causing numbness of the ring and little fingers and, in severe cases, claw hand (main-en-griffe) and wasting of the interossei (Froment's sign). At the wrist, compression in Guyon's canal can result from ganglia or cycling pressure. Management ranges from splinting and activity modification to surgical decompression or anterior transposition of the nerve at the elbow.
