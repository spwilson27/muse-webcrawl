An analog computer (spelled analogue computer in British English) is a type of computer that uses a continuously variable physical quantity — such as electrical voltage, mechanical rotation, or hydraulic pressure — to model the problem being solved. In contrast, a digital computer represents data as discrete numerical values. Analog computers were widely used in scientific and industrial applications where differential equations arise, and for much of the twentieth century they were faster than digital machines at such tasks.

History

Early analog devices include the ancient Antikythera mechanism, slide rules, planimeters, and nomograms. In the 1930s Vannevar Bush built the differential analyser, a mechanical analog computer for solving differential equations. Electronic analog computers, built around operational amplifiers, flourished from the 1940s to the 1960s in aerospace simulation, process control, and systems engineering, with manufacturers such as Electronic Associates and Beckman. From the 1970s, increasingly powerful digital computers displaced them in most roles.

Operation

An electronic analog computer is programmed by wiring together computing elements — summers, integrators, multipliers, and function generators — so that the voltages in the circuit obey the same equations as the system being modelled. Coefficients are set with precision potentiometers, and solutions appear as continuously varying voltages that can be plotted or read on meters. Because all variables evolve simultaneously, analog computers solve differential equations in parallel and in real time, but their accuracy is limited to roughly three or four significant figures by component tolerances and noise.

Applications and legacy

Analog computers guided anti-aircraft guns, simulated aircraft and spacecraft dynamics, modelled nuclear reactors and chemical plants, and served in early space programmes. Hybrid digital–analog systems combined the speed of analog integration with digital logic and storage. Interest in analog computation has revived in the twenty-first century for specialised low-power and neuromorphic computing tasks.
