Yatir Forest (Hebrew: יער יתיר) is a planted forest on the southern slopes of Mount Hebron, on the edge of the Negev Desert in Israel, northeast of Beersheba. Planted by the Jewish National Fund beginning in 1964, it covers roughly 30,000 dunams (about 30 square kilometres) and is the largest planted forest in Israel. It is named after the ancient town of Yattir (Jattir), whose ruins lie within the forest.

Planting and species

The forest consists mainly of Aleppo pines, together with cypress, carob, pistachio, and other drought-tolerant species. Planting a large forest at the desert fringe — the area receives only about 250–275 mm of rain a year — was long considered an ambitious experiment in dryland afforestation.

Research

Yatir is the site of a long-running research station of the Weizmann Institute of Science, part of the FLUXNET network, which measures carbon, water, and energy exchange between the forest and the atmosphere. Studies there have shown that the semi-arid forest sequesters carbon at rates comparable to many wetter forests, and have examined the trade-off between carbon uptake and the warming effect of the forest's lower surface albedo — findings influential in debates about planting trees in drylands to combat climate change.

Recreation

The forest contains hiking and cycling trails, picnic areas, the Yatir winery region nearby, and archaeological remains of the ancient town of Yattir, including a Byzantine church and mosaic.
