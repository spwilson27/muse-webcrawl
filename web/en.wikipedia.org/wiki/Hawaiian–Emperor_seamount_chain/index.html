The Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain is a mostly undersea mountain range in the Pacific Ocean that reaches above sea level as the Hawaiian Islands. It consists of the Hawaiian Ridge and the Emperor Seamounts, together a vast region of island and seamount formation created by the flow of magma from a hotspot in the Earth's mantle, the Hawaii hotspot. The chain stretches about 6,200 kilometres (3,900 mi) from the Aleutian Trench in the far northwest Pacific to Lōʻihi Seamount, the youngest volcano in the chain, about 35 kilometres (22 mi) southeast of the Island of Hawaiʻi.

Formation

As the Pacific Plate moves over the relatively fixed Hawaii hotspot, magma rises through the plate and builds a volcano. Over millions of years the plate carries the volcano away from the hotspot; the volcano becomes dormant, erodes, and eventually sinks beneath the sea to become a seamount, while a new volcano forms over the hotspot. The chain therefore records the direction and speed of Pacific Plate motion: the volcanoes become progressively older toward the northwest.

The bend

The most famous feature of the chain is a sharp bend of about 60 degrees between the older Emperor Seamounts (which trend north–south) and the younger Hawaiian Ridge (which trends northwest–southeast). The bend has been dated to roughly 47 million years ago and was long interpreted as marking a dramatic change in the direction of Pacific Plate motion. More recent research suggests that movement of the hotspot itself in the mantle also contributed to the geometry of the bend.
