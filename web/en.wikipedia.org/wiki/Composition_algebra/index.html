Composition algebra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The normed division algebras: reals, complex numbers, quaternions, and octonions

In mathematics, a composition algebra A over a field K is a not-necessarily-associative algebra over K together with a nondegenerate quadratic form N : A → K, called the norm, which satisfies

N(xy) = N(x) N(y)

for all x, y in A. That is, the norm is multiplicative: the algebra "admits composition" of its quadratic form. Composition algebras generalize the familiar norm identities for the real numbers, complex numbers, quaternions, and octonions, in which the (squared) norm of a product equals the product of the norms.

Classification

A fundamental theorem, due to Hurwitz (1898) for the normed-division-algebra case and generalized by Jacobson and others, says that composition algebras over a field occur only in dimensions 1, 2, 4, and 8. Over the real numbers, the positive-definite cases give exactly four normed division algebras: ℝ, ℂ, the quaternions ℍ, and the octonions 𝕆. Allowing indefinite ("split") norm forms yields the split-complex numbers, split-quaternions, and split-octonions as well. Higher-dimensional composition algebras with isotropic norms also exist — for example, algebras of dimension 16 and beyond arise if the requirement of a multiplicative identity or division is relaxed, as in certain generalizations studied through the Cayley–Dickson and related constructions.

Construction

The standard examples are produced by the Cayley–Dickson process, which doubles a composition algebra to produce a new one of twice the dimension by adjoining a new square root of −1 (or +1 in the split case). Starting from ℝ, this gives ℂ, then ℍ, then 𝕆. Each step sacrifices a property: the complex numbers lose the ordering of the reals, the quaternions lose commutativity, and the octonions lose associativity; the next doubling (the sedenions) loses the composition property for the norm altogether and acquires zero divisors.

Properties and applications

Composition algebras are connected with quadratic forms, spin groups, and exceptional Lie groups — the automorphism group of the octonions is the exceptional group G₂, and the other exceptional groups can be described using composition algebras. The two-square, four-square, and eight-square identities (Brahmagupta–Fibonacci, Euler, and Degen) are the historical ancestors of the theory, expressing that products of sums of 2, 4, or 8 squares are again sums of the same number of squares; Hurwitz's theorem shows no such identity exists in other dimensions.
