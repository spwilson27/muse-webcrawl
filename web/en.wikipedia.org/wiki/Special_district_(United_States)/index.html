Special district (United States)

Special districts, also known as special purpose districts, are independent units of local government in the United States created for a limited, specific purpose. They are distinct from counties, cities and school districts in that they generally provide a single service, or a small set of related services, within a defined geographic area.

Types

Common types of special districts include fire protection districts, water supply and irrigation districts, sewer and sanitation districts, transit districts, port authorities, library districts, mosquito control districts, cemetery districts and reclamation districts. There are roughly 38,000 special districts in the United States, according to the U.S. Census Bureau's Census of Governments.

Governance

Most special districts are governed by a board of directors, whose members may be elected by the voters of the district or appointed by other government bodies. Special districts typically have the authority to levy taxes, charge fees for services, and issue bonds to finance capital projects.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue that special districts allow services to be tailored to the areas that benefit from them and can cross municipal boundaries. Critics note that their low public visibility can lead to weak accountability, and that their proliferation adds layers of government.
