Spanish architecture

Spanish architecture is the architecture of what is now Spain, and of Spanish architects worldwide. The term includes buildings which were constructed within the current borders of Spain prior to its existence as a nation, when the land was called Iberia, Hispania, or was divided between several Christian and Muslim kingdoms. Spanish architecture demonstrates great historical and geographical diversity, reflecting the country's long history of cultural exchange between the Iberian Peninsula, North Africa, and the rest of Europe.

Prehistory and Roman Hispania

The Iberian Peninsula preserves prehistoric structures such as the talayots of the Balearic Islands and the dolmens of Antequera. Roman occupation brought aqueducts, theatres, and bridges; the Aqueduct of Segovia and the theatre at Mérida remain among the best-preserved Roman monuments in Europe. The Romans also introduced the basilica plan and widespread use of concrete vaulting.

Islamic architecture

From 711, much of the peninsula came under Muslim rule as al-Andalus. Islamic architecture in Spain produced masterpieces such as the Great Mosque of Córdoba (begun 785), famous for its double-tiered horseshoe arches, and the Alhambra palace complex in Granada, with its intricate stucco, tilework, and muqarnas. The Giralda in Seville, originally a minaret, exemplifies Almohad design.

Mudéjar

Mudéjar architecture arose from the coexistence of Muslim craftsmen in Christian kingdoms, blending Islamic decorative techniques—brickwork, glazed tiles, carved plaster—with Romanesque and Gothic forms. It is especially characteristic of Aragon, where towers in Teruel and Zaragoza are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Gothic and Renaissance

Spanish Gothic reached its height in the cathedrals of Burgos, León, and Toledo. In the late Gothic period the Isabelline style, named for Isabella I of Castile, added exuberant surface ornament. Renaissance architecture arrived with the Plateresque style, richly decorated façades likened to silversmiths' work, followed by the austere Herrerian style of Juan de Herrera, architect of the monastery-palace of El Escorial.

Baroque and Neoclassicism

Spanish Baroque, particularly the Churrigueresque style, produced dramatically ornamented churches and squares such as Salamanca's Plaza Mayor. In the 18th century, Neoclassicism brought monuments like Madrid's Prado Museum and the Puerta de Alcalá.

Modernism and contemporary architecture

In Catalonia, Modernisme produced the works of Antoni Gaudí, including the Sagrada Família and Casa Batlló in Barcelona, which combine Gothic and Art Nouveau influences with structural innovation. In the late 20th and 21st centuries, Spanish architects such as Santiago Calatrava and Rafael Moneo gained international prominence, and Frank Gehry's Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997) became an icon of contemporary architecture and urban regeneration.
