Gwich'in

Regions: Alaska (United States); Yukon and Northwest Territories (Canada)

Languages: Gwich'in, English

Related groups: Other Athabaskan peoples

The Gwich'in (sometimes spelled Kutchin) are an Athabaskan-speaking First Nations people of Canada and an Alaska Native people. They live in the northwestern part of North America, mostly north of the Arctic Circle, in present-day Alaska, Yukon, and the Northwest Territories.

Name and language

The name Gwich'in means "one who dwells" or "resident of [a region]". Bands were historically identified by local prefixes referring to their home territories, such as the Teetl'it Gwich'in ("people of the head of the waters") of the Peel River area. The Gwich'in language belongs to the Athabaskan family and is spoken in several communities, though the number of fluent speakers has declined and revitalization efforts are under way.

History

The Gwich'in are one of the northernmost Indigenous peoples of North America. Their traditional economy centred on hunting — especially the Porcupine caribou herd — fishing, and trading. Contact with European traders from the late 18th century brought firearms, trade goods, and epidemic disease through posts such as Fort Yukon, established by the Hudson's Bay Company in 1847.

The Porcupine caribou herd

Gwich'in culture and subsistence remain closely tied to the Porcupine caribou herd, which migrates to calving grounds on the coastal plain of the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge in Alaska. Gwich'in communities have been prominent opponents of oil and gas development on the refuge's coastal plain, which they call "the sacred place where life begins".

Governance

In Canada, Gwich'in communities are organized under bodies such as the Gwich'in Tribal Council in the Northwest Territories and the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation in Yukon, the latter based in Old Crow. The Gwich'in Comprehensive Land Claim Agreement was signed in 1992.
