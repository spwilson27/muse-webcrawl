Shanty town

A shanty town is a settlement of improvised housing — shacks or shanties built from scrap materials such as corrugated metal, plywood, plastic sheeting, and cardboard — typically located on the outskirts of cities or on land the occupants do not legally own. Shanty towns usually lack reliable access to clean water, sanitation, electricity, and other municipal services, and are associated with poverty and rapid urbanization.

Such settlements are known by many regional names: favelas in Brazil, townships in South Africa, bustees in India, barrios de ranchos in Venezuela, gecekondus in Turkey, and bidonvilles in Francophone countries. During the Great Depression, homeless encampments in the United States were called "Hoovervilles." By United Nations estimates, around a billion people — roughly one in eight of the world's population — live in slums and informal settlements.

Development

Shanty towns typically begin when rural migrants arrive in a city seeking work and, unable to afford formal housing, occupy vacant land and build shelters from whatever materials are at hand. Over time some settlements consolidate: residents improve their houses, organize politically, and press for tenure and services, while others are demolished or relocated by authorities.

Responses

Government responses have ranged from forced clearance and resettlement to in-situ upgrading — providing water, sewers, roads, and legal tenure — and sites-and-services schemes. Development economists such as John F. C. Turner argued that self-built housing is often part of the solution to urban housing shortages rather than merely a problem, influencing modern community-led upgrading practice.
