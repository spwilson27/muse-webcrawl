Quercus ithaburensis, the Mount Tabor oak, is a species of oak in the beech family Fagaceae. It is native to the eastern Mediterranean, from southeastern Italy and Greece through Turkey to Syria, Lebanon, Israel, and Jordan.

It takes its name from Mount Tabor in the Lower Galilee, where it forms extensive natural woodlands, including the well-known forest reserve near Beit Keshet.

Description

The Mount Tabor oak is a large deciduous tree, growing 10–20 metres tall with a broad, spreading crown. The leaves are oblong, 5–10 cm long, with shallow lobes or coarse teeth along the margin; they are densely hairy when young and become smoother with age.

Its acorns are among the largest of any oak, 3–5 cm long, seated in a distinctive cup covered in long, recurved scales. The acorns ripen in the second year.

Distribution and habitat

The species grows in Mediterranean woodland and open forest, typically on basaltic and limestone soils at low to moderate elevations. In Israel and the Palestinian territories it is a dominant species of the natural oak woodlands of the Galilee, the Golan Heights, and parts of the coastal plain.

Two subspecies are generally recognised: subsp. ithaburensis in the Levant and subsp. macrolepis, the Valonia oak, in Greece, Italy, and western Turkey, whose large acorn cups are rich in tannin.

Uses

The acorn cups of the Valonia oak were historically an important export commodity, used in tanning leather ("valonia"). The acorns were also ground for flour in times of scarcity and used as fodder. The tree provides shade and habitat in parkland and is protected in nature reserves in Israel.
