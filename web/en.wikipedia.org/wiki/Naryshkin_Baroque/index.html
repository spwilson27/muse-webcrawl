Naryshkin Baroque

Naryshkin Baroque, also known as Moscow Baroque, is a style of Russian architecture and decoration that flourished in Moscow and its environs from the 1680s to the early 1700s, in the years around the accession of Peter the Great. It takes its name from the Naryshkin family — the family of Peter's mother, Natalya Naryshkina — who were among its principal patrons. The style is characterised by the fusion of traditional Russian church forms with elaborate white-stone carved ornament drawn from Western European Baroque sources.

Characteristics

Typical Naryshkin Baroque churches are built of red brick contrasted with lavish white limestone carving: twisted columns, shell-topped window surrounds, scalloped cornices and ornate frames that appear to be applied to the surface of the building. Many are "churches under the bells" (i pod zvony), in which the bell tower is incorporated into the main volume, and many are arranged in tiers — a cube surmounted by one or two diminishing octagons crowned by a cupola, giving a strongly vertical, tower-like silhouette.

Major monuments

The most celebrated example is the Church of the Intercession at Fili (1690–1694), built by Lev Naryshkin, the tsar's uncle, a tiered church of exquisite carved decoration. Other landmarks include the Church of the Trinity at Troitse-Lykovo, the Church of the Resurrection at Kadashi, the Church of St Nicholas at Khamovniki with its rich ornament, and the refectory of the Simonov Monastery. The style extended to secular building too, as in the Sukharev Tower (1692–1701), which housed Moscow's School of Mathematics and Navigation before its demolition in 1934.

Context and influence

Naryshkin Baroque emerged as boyar patrons, enriched and exposed to Polish and Ukrainian Baroque through Russia's western wars and contacts, commissioned buildings that displayed both piety and fashionable novelty. It represents the last flowering of medieval Russian architecture before Peter the Great's foundation of St Petersburg imported a fully Western, classical Baroque. Elements of its ornamental vocabulary survived in provincial church building well into the 18th century.
