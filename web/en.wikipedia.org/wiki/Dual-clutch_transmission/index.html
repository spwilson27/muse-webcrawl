Dual-clutch transmission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schematic of a dual-clutch transmission with odd and even gear shafts

A dual-clutch transmission (DCT), sometimes called a twin-clutch gearbox, is a type of multi-shaft automated manual transmission in which gear changes are executed by two separate computer-controlled clutches — one for the odd-numbered gears and one for the even-numbered gears — with no clutch pedal. Because the next gear can be pre-selected on the idle shaft, shifts occur in milliseconds with little or no interruption of torque.

Design

A DCT is effectively two manual gearboxes in one housing. One clutch drives the odd gears (1, 3, 5, 7) and the other the even gears (2, 4, 6) plus reverse. Electro-hydraulic or electromechanical actuators select gears and swap the clutches under the control of a transmission control unit. Clutches may be wet (running in oil, common in high-torque applications) or dry (used in smaller cars for efficiency).

History and applications

The concept was patented by Adolphe Kégresse in 1939 and developed by Porsche for racing in the 1980s as the PDK (Porsche-Doppelkupplungsgetriebe). The first mass-production road car application was Volkswagen Group's DSG (Direktschaltgetriebe) in 2003. DCTs are now offered by many manufacturers under names such as PowerShift, S tronic, EDC and TCT, in applications from small hatchbacks to supercars and commercial vehicles. They typically offer faster shifts and better fuel economy than conventional torque-converter automatics, though low-speed manoeuvring smoothness and clutch wear have been noted drawbacks.
