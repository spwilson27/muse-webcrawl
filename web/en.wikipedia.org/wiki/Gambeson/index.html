Gambeson

A gambeson (also aketon, padded jack or arming doublet) is a padded defensive jacket worn as armour separately, or combined with mail and plate armour. Gambesons were produced with a sewing technique called quilting. Usually sewn from linen or wool, the stuffing varied and could be, for example, scrap cloth or horse hair. During the 14th century, illustrations usually show buttons or laces up the front.

History

Padded garments have ancient roots — the linen linothorax of Greek hoplites and the quilted armour of many other cultures served the same purpose: to absorb and spread the force of blows and to cushion rigid armour worn over it. In medieval Europe the gambeson was the foundation of almost every soldier's protection from roughly the 10th to the 16th century.

An independent gambeson of many layers of linen was surprisingly effective armour for common infantry, capable of resisting sword cuts and even glancing arrow strikes. Under mail, a gambeson prevented the rings from being driven into wounds and absorbed blunt trauma against which mail alone offered little defence. Knights wore a lighter version, the aketon or arming doublet, with laces (points) to which pieces of plate harness and mail voiders were tied.

Construction

Surviving descriptions and later reproductions suggest gambesons of 18–30 layers of linen, stitched in vertical channels. The garment was often stuffed with tow, cotton, wool or old rags. Late medieval examples were carefully tailored, with a narrow waist and wide skirts, and could be expensive, richly decorated items for the nobility.

Related garments

Cognate padded armours include the jazerant, the quilted jack of the later Middle Ages (including the famous multi-layered jacks of the Wars of the Roses period), and the padded garments worn beneath Japanese and Chinese armour.
