Technical drawing


Technical drawing: orthographic views with dimensions and tolerances

Technical drawing, drafting or draughting is the act and discipline of composing drawings that visually communicate how something functions or is constructed. Technical drawings are used in engineering, architecture, and manufacturing to specify the geometry, dimensions, materials, and tolerances of objects, so that they can be made, assembled, inspected, and maintained without ambiguity.

Methods

Traditionally, drawings were produced by hand on paper or film with pencils, pens, rulers, compasses, protractors, and drawing boards, and reproduced as blueprints or diazo prints. Since the 1980s, production has shifted to computer-aided design (CAD) software, and increasingly to 3D models carrying product and manufacturing information (model-based definition), though 2D drawings remain common and often legally definitive.

Conventions

Technical drawings follow standardised conventions so that any trained reader interprets them the same way:

  • Projections: objects are shown in orthographic projection (front, top, and side views), using either first-angle (ISO) or third-angle (ASME) arrangement; pictorial views use isometric or oblique projection.
  • Dimensioning and tolerancing: sizes are stated in dimension lines with tolerances defining permissible variation, formalised in GD&T.
  • Line types and symbols: continuous, hidden, centre, and section lines have standard meanings, as do symbols for surface finish, welds, and threads.
  • Scales and sheets: standard scales (1:1, 1:2, 1:50, …) and standard sheet sizes (ISO A-series, ANSI sizes), with title blocks identifying the drawing, its author, revision, and approval.

Standards

International practice is codified in the ISO 128 and ISO 129 series on technical product documentation, in ASME Y14 standards in the United States, and in national standards such as BS 8888 in the United Kingdom.

Applications

Technical drawings are produced for mechanical parts, buildings (plans, elevations, sections), electrical and plumbing systems, civil infrastructure, patents, and archaeology, among many other fields. In law, an engineering drawing may form part of a contract or a patent specification, and drafters are trained to make them complete, unambiguous, and economical.
