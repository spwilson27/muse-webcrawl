Harold Pinter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Harold Pinter in the 2000s, black-clad and unsmiling at a rehearsal table — the pause made flesh]

Harold Pinter CH CBE (10 October 1930 – 24 December 2008) was a British playwright, screenwriter, director and actor, winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2005. His early plays — The Birthday Party (1958), The Caretaker (1960), The Homecoming (1965) — created the 'comedy of menace' and the adjective Pinteresque: ordinary rooms, unexplained threats, dialogue of evasions, and the famous pause that carries the unsaid.

Born in Hackney, London, to a Jewish tailor's family, Pinter was a conscientious objector, an actor in rep under the name David Baron, and a poet before The Birthday Party's catastrophic first London run (closed in a week, one famous defence by Harold Hobson) gave way to The Caretaker's triumph. His later plays — Old Times, No Man's Land, Betrayal (1978, told backwards) — explore memory and territory; his late political plays and speeches grew ever more direct.

The pause and the menace

Pinter's dialogue — banalities weaponised, questions as attacks, silence as occupation — came from his ear for London speech and his theatre of territorial animals: every Pinter room has an owner and an intruder. As a screenwriter (The Servant, The Go-Between, The French Lieutenant's Woman) he was British cinema's finest adapter; as a director and actor he served his own and others' texts with actor's knowledge.

The citizen

His final decades were consumed by politics: against the Iraq War, for the Palestinians, against American power — his 2005 Nobel lecture ('Art, Truth and Politics'), delivered by video as cancer took his voice, was the era's fiercest literary-political document. He died on Christmas Eve 2008; the Pinter Theatre bears his name, and 'Pinteresque' is in the dictionary.
