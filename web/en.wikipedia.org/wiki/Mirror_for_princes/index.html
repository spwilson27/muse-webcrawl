Mirror for princes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mirrors held up an ideal image of kingship for rulers to imitate.

Mirrors for princes (Latin: specula principum; singular speculum principis) are a genre of political writing, flourishing from antiquity through the early modern period, consisting of texts addressed to kings, princes, and other rulers instructing them in how to rule justly and wisely. The genre takes its name from the metaphor of the book as a mirror in which the prince may see an image of ideal kingship — and of himself.

Traditions

Advice literature for rulers is ancient and cross-cultural: Egyptian instructions such as the Teaching for King Merikare, the Indian Arthashastra attributed to Kautilya, and Chinese works on statecraft all predate the Western genre. In the Islamic world, the tradition produced classics such as al-Ghazali's Counsel for Kings and Nizam al-Mulk's Siyasatnama (Book of Government), and is related to the wider adab literature and the frame-tale collection Kalila wa Dimna.

In medieval Christian Europe, mirrors combined classical ethics, biblical exemplars, and Christian moral teaching. Notable examples include John of Salisbury's Policraticus (1159), Giles of Rome's De regimine principum (c. 1280), one of the most copied works of the later Middle Ages, and, in the North, the Norwegian Konungs skuggsjá ("King's Mirror", c. 1250).

Renaissance and decline

Renaissance humanists revived the genre: Erasmus's Education of a Christian Prince (1516) urged the ruler toward virtue, peace, and learning. The most famous and controversial example is Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince (written 1513, published 1532), which broke with the genre's moralising conventions by advising rulers on the effective acquisition and maintenance of power — often read as a subversion of the mirror tradition from within. As political thought shifted toward institutions, law, and eventually political science, the mirror for princes faded as a living genre, surviving mainly as a subject of historical study.
