Phenethylamine

Chemical formula: C8H11N

Structure: A benzene ring joined by a two-carbon chain to an amine group

Class: Trace amine; parent compound of the substituted phenethylamines

Phenethylamine (also called 2-phenylethylamine, β-phenylethylamine, or PEA) is an organic compound, a natural monoamine alkaloid, and a trace amine that functions as a central nervous system stimulant in humans. Structurally, it consists of a phenyl ring attached to an amino group by a two-carbon (ethyl) bridge, and it is the parent compound of the large class of substituted phenethylamines.

Biological role

Phenethylamine is biosynthesized in the body by decarboxylation of the amino acid phenylalanine and is rapidly metabolized by monoamine oxidase B and other enzymes, giving it a half-life of only minutes when taken orally. It acts as an agonist at the trace amine-associated receptor TAAR1 and promotes the release of norepinephrine and dopamine. It occurs naturally in some foods, notably chocolate (formed during fermentation and roasting of cacao), where a popular but scientifically unsupported theory once attributed chocolate's mood effects to its phenethylamine content despite rapid metabolism preventing significant amounts from reaching the brain.

Substituted phenethylamines

Modifying the phenethylamine scaffold yields a vast pharmacological family: the catecholamine neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine; the stimulant amphetamine and methamphetamine; the empathogen MDMA; the psychedelic mescaline and the 2C series; the hormone-like drug ephedrine; and many others. Alexander Shulgin's book PiHKAL ("Phenethylamines I Have Known and Loved", 1991) catalogued the chemistry and effects of 179 such compounds. Many substituted phenethylamines are controlled substances in various jurisdictions.
