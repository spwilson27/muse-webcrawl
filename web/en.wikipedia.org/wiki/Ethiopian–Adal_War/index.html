The Ethiopian–Adal War (1529–1543) was a military conflict between the Christian Ethiopian Empire under Emperors Lebna Dengel and Gelawdewos and the Muslim Sultanate of Adal led by Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, known in Ethiopian tradition as Ahmad Gran ("the Left-handed"). It was one of the most destructive wars in Ethiopian history.

Course of the war

After years of border raiding, Ahmad invaded in force in 1529, defeating the imperial army at Shimbra Kure. Equipped with matchlocks supplied through Ottoman-connected ports, Adal's forces overran most of the highlands by 1535, burning churches and monasteries—including, reportedly, Lalibela's region—and driving the emperor into exile as a fugitive. Ethiopia appealed to Portugal, and in 1541 a detachment of some 400 musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama, son of Vasco da Gama, landed at Massawa. Da Gama was captured and executed in 1542 after the Battle of Wofla, but the surviving Portuguese joined Gelawdewos's army, and at the Battle of Wayna Daga (21 February 1543) Ahmad was killed and his army routed, ending the invasion.

Consequences

Both states were left exhausted. The war weakened Ethiopia, coinciding with the great Oromo migrations that reshaped the region's demography, and Adal disintegrated into successor emirates around Harar. The conflict entrenched religious frontiers in the Horn of Africa and is remembered in both Christian chronicles and the Adal chronicle Futuh al-Habasha ("The Conquest of Abyssinia").
