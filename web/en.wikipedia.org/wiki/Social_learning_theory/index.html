Social learning theory

Social learning theory, developed by psychologist Albert Bandura, proposes that people learn behaviour through observing others — their actions and the consequences those actions bring — as well as through direct reinforcement. It bridged behaviourism and cognitive psychology by insisting that cognition mediates learning.

The Bobo doll experiments

In Bandura's famous experiments of 1961–1963, children who watched an adult model behave aggressively toward a Bobo doll were far more likely to imitate the aggression than children who saw a non-aggressive model or no model. Children also learned from consequences: seeing the model punished reduced imitation — evidence of vicarious reinforcement — even though the children had acquired the behaviour.

Processes and concepts

Observational learning requires attention, retention, motor reproduction and motivation. Bandura later emphasised reciprocal determinism — behaviour, personal factors and environment influence each other — and self-efficacy, the belief in one's ability to succeed, which became central to his later social cognitive theory.

Applications

The theory underlies research on media violence, modelling in therapy and education, and health behaviour change.
