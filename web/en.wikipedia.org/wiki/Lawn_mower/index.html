A lawn mower (also known as a mower or grass cutter) is a machine that uses one or more blades to cut a grass surface to an even height. The height of the cut grass may be fixed by the designer or adjustable by the operator, typically by a single master lever or by a lever on each wheel.

History

The first lawn mower was invented by Edwin Budding of Stroud, England, who was granted a British patent in 1830. Budding's mower, designed primarily to cut the lawns on sports grounds and extensive gardens, was a cylinder (reel) mower pushed from behind. Budding's idea came from a rotary cutting machine used in a cloth mill. Mowers powered by steam appeared in the late 19th century, and the first petrol-driven mowers were produced in the 1890s and 1900s.

Types

  • Cylinder (reel) mowers — a fixed horizontal cutting blade with a rotating reel of helical blades; they give a fine, scissor-like cut and are used on lawns and sports pitches.
  • Rotary mowers — a single blade rotates about a vertical axis at high speed; the most common domestic type.
  • Hover mowers — rotary mowers mounted on an air cushion.
  • Ride-on mowers and lawn tractors — the operator sits on the machine.
  • Robotic mowers — battery-powered machines that mow autonomously within a boundary wire.
  • Zero-turn mowers — ride-on machines that can pivot on the spot, common in professional landscaping.

Power sources

Early mowers were human-powered. Petrol engines dominated the twentieth century; electric mowers may be corded or battery-powered. Battery and robotic mowers have grown rapidly in popularity as lithium-ion batteries have improved, while petrol mowers face increasing emissions regulation.

Safety and environment

Rotary mowers are a significant cause of domestic injuries, from contact with the spinning blade and from thrown debris. Small petrol engines are also notable sources of air pollution relative to their size, which has encouraged the shift to electric machines.
