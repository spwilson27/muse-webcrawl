Free indirect speech

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Free indirect speech (also called free indirect discourse or style indirect libre) is a narrative technique in which a character's thoughts or speech are presented in the third person and past tense of narration, but retain the character's own idiom, exclamations, questions and deictic expressions (such as "now" or "tomorrow"). Unlike ordinary indirect speech, it carries no reporting clause ("she thought that…") and no quotation marks, so the narrator's voice and the character's voice merge.

The realist novel of the nineteenth century made free indirect speech its signature instrument.

Example

Plain indirect speech keeps a clear frame:

She looked at the letter. She thought that she would have to leave tomorrow, and wondered whether he would ever write again.

In free indirect speech the frame falls away, and the thought speaks in its own words while keeping third person and past tense:

She looked at the letter. Tomorrow she would have to leave — and would he ever write again? Not likely!

History and use

The technique was developed to mastery in the novels of Jane Austen and Gustave Flaubert, whose Madame Bovary (1857) is its classic exemplar, and it became a central device of modernist fiction in writers such as Virginia Woolf and James Joyce. Scholars debate its origins; some find early instances in medieval French narrative, and Charles Bally coined the term style indirect libre in 1912.

Effects

Free indirect speech lets a narrative slip fluidly between outside description and a character's consciousness, creating intimacy, irony or ambiguity: the reader may be unsure whether an opinion belongs to the character or to the narrator, an uncertainty Flaubert exploited to devastating satirical effect. It is closely related to, but distinct from, the interior monologue and the broader stream of consciousness technique.
