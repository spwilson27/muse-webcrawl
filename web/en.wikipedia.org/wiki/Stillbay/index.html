Stillbay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Stillbay (also spelled Still Bay or Stilbaai) is a Middle Stone Age stone-tool industry of southern Africa, dated to roughly 72,000–71,000 years ago during Marine Isotope Stage 4. It is named after the town of Stilbaai in the Western Cape, South Africa, where such tools were first described in the 1920s by A. J. H. Goodwin and C. van Riet Lowe. The Stillbay is notable for the sophistication of its artefacts and for its association with early expressions of modern human behaviour.

Characteristics

The defining artefacts of the Stillbay are finely made bifacial points — thin, leaf-shaped ("foliate") tools flaked on both faces, often with pressure flaking in the final stages, and heat treatment of silcrete used to improve its flaking quality before knapping. Points were probably used as spear tips and knives. Associated finds at sites such as Blombos Cave include engraved ochre pieces and perforated shell beads, which are among the earliest evidence of symbolic behaviour, along with the earliest known use of compound adhesives and, at Sibudu, early bone tools.

Sites and chronology

Stillbay assemblages are known from coastal caves and shelters along the southern and western Cape coasts, including Blombos Cave, Diepkloof Rock Shelter, Hollow Rock Shelter, and Sibudu Cave further east in KwaZulu-Natal. Optically stimulated luminescence dating suggests the industry was relatively short-lived, on the order of a few thousand years, and it was followed by the distinct Howiesons Poort industry. The brevity and wide distribution of the Stillbay — and of the similar Howiesons Poort — have been interpreted as evidence of long-distance social networks and information sharing among early Homo sapiens populations, possibly in response to the climatic stresses of the glacial conditions of MIS 4.

Significance

Together with the related industries of the southern African Middle Stone Age, the Stillbay figures prominently in debates about the origins of behavioural modernity, showing that complex toolmaking, heat treatment, hafting, and symbolic artefacts appeared tens of thousands of years before the Upper Paleolithic "revolution" in Europe.
