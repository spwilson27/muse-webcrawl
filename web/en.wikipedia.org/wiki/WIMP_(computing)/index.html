WIMP (computing)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In human–computer interaction, WIMP stands for "windows, icons, menus, pointer", denoting a style of interaction that uses these elements of a graphical user interface (GUI). The term was coined in the 1980s, and the paradigm was pioneered at Xerox PARC (notably in the Alto and Star systems) drawing on earlier work by Douglas Engelbart and Ivan Sutherland.

Elements

Windows are resizable screen regions, often overlapping, that present documents and applications. Icons are small pictograms representing files, programs and actions. Menus present lists of commands from which the user selects. The pointer, controlled by a mouse or similar pointing device, selects and manipulates objects, enabling pointing, clicking and dragging as the core interaction vocabulary.

History and influence

The WIMP model was commercialised by the Apple Lisa (1983) and Macintosh (1984), and later by Microsoft Windows, becoming the dominant desktop metaphor through the 1980s and 1990s. Critics have long argued that WIMP interfaces constrain interaction to two-dimensional pointing and have proposed post-WIMP interfaces — exemplified by multi-touch gestures, zooming interfaces and voice or tangible interaction — many of which became mainstream with smartphones, while the desktop retains the classic WIMP arrangement.
