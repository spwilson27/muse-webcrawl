Temple of Literature, Hanoi

Location: Dong Da district, Hanoi, Vietnam
Founded: 1070 (temple); 1076 (Imperial Academy)
Dedicated to: Confucius
Area: 54,331 m²

The Temple of Literature (Vietnamese: Văn Miếu) is a temple of Confucius in Hanoi, northern Vietnam. Founded in 1070 under Emperor Lý Thánh Tông, it hosted the Imperial Academy (Quốc Tử Giám), Vietnam's first national university, from 1076 to 1779. It is one of several temples in Vietnam dedicated to Confucius, sages and scholars, and appears on the back of the 100,000 Vietnamese đồng banknote.

Layout

The complex covers about 54,000 square metres and is arranged in five courtyards along a north–south axis, surrounded by a brick wall and entered through the Văn Miếu gate. Landmarks include the Khue Van pavilion (Khuê Văn Các), built in 1805 — a small wooden structure on four pillars that has become a symbol of Hanoi — and the Well of Heavenly Clarity (Thiên Quang Tỉnh), a square pond flanked by gardens.

Doctors' steles

The third courtyard holds the famous Steles of Doctors: 82 stone steles, each mounted on the back of a stone tortoise, recording the names and biographies of graduates of the royal examinations held between 1442 and 1779. Originally 116 steles were erected; the surviving 82 were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2010.

History and present use

The temple was damaged during the First Indochina War and restored in the 1990s. It remains a place of veneration: students come before examinations to pray for success and to have their photographs taken at graduation, and calligraphers set up stalls outside during the Tết (Lunar New Year) festival to write auspicious characters for visitors.
