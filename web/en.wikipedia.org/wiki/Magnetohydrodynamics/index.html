Magnetohydrodynamics

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD; also called magneto-fluid dynamics or hydromagnetics) is a model of electrically conducting fluids that treats them as continuous media in which the magnetic field and the fluid velocity evolve together. Examples of such fluids include plasmas, liquid metals, salt water, and electrolytes. The word magnetohydrodynamics is derived from magneto- meaning magnetic field, hydro- meaning water, and dynamics meaning movement. The field of MHD was initiated by Hannes Alfvén, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970.

Governing equations

MHD combines the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid dynamics with Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism. In the ideal MHD approximation, the fluid is treated as a perfect conductor, so the magnetic field is "frozen in" to the flow (Alfvén's theorem). The momentum equation gains the Lorentz force term:

ρ (∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + J × B + ρg + viscous terms

together with the induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B − η∇ × B), where η is the magnetic diffusivity. The ratio of advection to diffusion is measured by the magnetic Reynolds number.

Waves and structures

An MHD plasma supports characteristic wave modes absent in ordinary fluids: Alfvén waves, which propagate along field lines like vibrations of a plucked string, and fast and slow magnetosonic waves. Magnetic pressure and tension govern phenomena from sunspots and solar flares to the dynamics of accretion disks and galactic magnetic fields.

Applications

MHD is the standard framework for much of plasma astrophysics and space physics, including the solar wind and magnetospheres. Engineering applications include magnetic confinement fusion (tokamaks and stellarators are analysed largely in MHD terms), liquid-metal cooling and pumping, electromagnetic casting, and MHD generators, which extract electric power directly from a hot conducting gas. Instabilities described by MHD — such as kink and tearing modes — set practical limits on fusion devices.
