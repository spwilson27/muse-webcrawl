Sagebrush Rebellion

The Sagebrush Rebellion was a movement in the western United States during the 1970s and early 1980s that sought to reduce federal control over public lands. It arose in response to the passage of the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 (FLPMA), which ended the federal policy of disposing of public lands and declared that they would generally remain in federal ownership, administered by the Bureau of Land Management.

Background

The federal government owns roughly half of the land in the eleven contiguous western states — about 85 percent of Nevada, for example — a legacy of the public-domain history of the West. Ranchers, miners and local governments in the region complained about federal grazing rules, restrictions on access to resources, and decisions made by distant agencies, and argued that the lands should be transferred to the states or governed with far greater local control.

The movement

In 1979, Nevada passed legislation claiming state ownership of some 49 million acres of federal land, and several other western states — Utah, Wyoming, New Mexico and Arizona among them — enacted similar measures or studies. The movement gained national prominence in the 1980 presidential campaign, when Ronald Reagan declared "I am a Sagebrush Rebel" and promised to address western grievances. His Secretary of the Interior, James G. Watt, pursued policies favourable to resource development and state interests, which defused much of the rebellion's momentum.

Legacy

Lawsuits asserting state claims to federal lands failed in court, and the land transfers the rebels sought did not occur. The movement's themes — federal "overreach", grazing rights and local control of public lands — have repeatedly resurfaced in western politics, including in the "wise use" movement of the 1990s and later disputes over monuments and grazing on federal land.
