Edward Gibbon (8 May 1737 – 16 January 1794) was an English essayist, historian, and politician best known for his monumental work The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, published in six volumes between 1776 and 1788. The work, noted for its prose style, ironic tone, and extensive use of primary sources, covered the history of the Roman Empire from the end of the 1st century AD to the fall of Constantinople in 1453.

Early life

Gibbon was born in Putney, Surrey, the grandson of a wealthy merchant and son of Edward Gibbon, a member of Parliament. His health as a child was poor, and his education was irregular; he later wrote that he owed his learning chiefly to voracious private reading. At Magdalen College, Oxford (1752–1753), he converted to Roman Catholicism, which alarmed his father, who sent him to Lausanne, Switzerland, under the care of a Calvinist pastor. There Gibbon reconverted to Protestantism, mastered French and Latin literature, and fell in love with Suzanne Curchod — a match his father forbade and Gibbon abandoned, remarking, in his famous phrase, "I sighed as a lover, I obeyed as a son."

The Decline and Fall

After travels in Italy in 1764 — where, "musing amidst the ruins of the Capitol" at Rome as Franciscan friars sang vespers, he conceived the idea of his history — Gibbon settled in London, entered Parliament in 1774, and published the first volume of the Decline and Fall in 1776. It was an immediate sensation, though its chapters 15 and 16, attributing the growth of Christianity to worldly causes and treating the early Church with irony, provoked fierce controversy. The final volumes appeared in 1788, written largely at Lausanne. The work's narrative sweep, erudition, and balanced periods made it a classic of English prose and a foundation of modern historical method, even where later scholarship has revised its conclusions.

Later life

Gibbon lived his last years between London and Lausanne, working on his Memoirs, published posthumously. He died in London on 16 January 1794, following complications after surgery to drain a long-concealed hydrocele.
