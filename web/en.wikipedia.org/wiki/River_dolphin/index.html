River dolphin

GroupCetacea (toothed whales)
HabitatLarge rivers and estuaries
FamiliesPlatanistidae, Iniidae, Pontoporiidae (Lipotidae extinct)

River dolphins are a polyphyletic group of freshwater cetaceans that live in large river systems in Asia and South America. Unlike oceanic dolphins, they typically have long narrow beaks, small eyes and highly developed echolocation adapted to murky water. Living groups include the Ganges and Indus river dolphins (genus Platanista), the Amazon river dolphin or boto (Inia geoffrensis), the Araguaian river dolphin, the Bolivian river dolphin, and the franciscana, which inhabits coastal estuaries of south-eastern South America.

Characteristics

River dolphins swim with unfused neck vertebrae that let them turn their heads, and their broad flippers allow manoeuvring in flooded forests. The Amazon river dolphin is the largest, reaching about 2.5 m, and adult males turn a distinctive pink. The South Asian species are functionally blind, navigating entirely by echolocation.

Conservation

All river dolphins are threatened by dam construction, entanglement in fishing gear, pollution and habitat loss. The Chinese baiji was declared functionally extinct in 2006 after a dedicated survey of the Yangtze found none, making it the first cetacean driven to extinction by human activity.
