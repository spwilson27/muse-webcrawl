Tenant

A tenant is a person who occupies land or property rented from a landlord. The arrangement is known as a tenancy (or leasehold), under which the tenant holds a legal interest in the property for a defined term in exchange for rent, while the freehold or superior interest remains with the landlord. Tenancy is a central institution of property law in both common law and civil law systems, covering homes, farms, shops, offices and industrial premises.

Types of tenancy

Common law recognises several estates in land held by tenants. A tenancy for years (fixed-term tenancy) runs for a definite period. A periodic tenancy continues from period to period (e.g. month to month) until terminated by notice. A tenancy at will can be ended by either party at any time, while a tenancy at sufferance arises when a tenant remains after the term expires without the landlord's consent ("holdover"). Historically, the word tenant also described anyone holding land from a lord under feudal tenure, including tenants-in-chief who held directly from the Crown.

Rights and obligations

A tenant generally owes rent, must use the property in a tenant-like manner, avoid damage beyond fair wear and tear, and return possession at the end of the term. In return, the tenant is entitled to quiet enjoyment — possession free from substantial interference by the landlord. Modern residential tenancy law in many jurisdictions adds statutory protections: implied warranties of habitability, duties on landlords to repair and maintain essential services, limits on security deposits, notice periods and court processes for eviction, and in some places rent regulation. Commercial tenants typically negotiate fuller repairing and insuring leases with fewer statutory protections.

Termination and eviction

Tenancies end by expiry, mutual surrender, or notice given under the agreement or statute; a landlord who wants possession from a residential tenant who will not leave must normally obtain a court order, and self-help eviction (changing locks, cutting utilities) is unlawful in most developed legal systems. Grounds for early termination commonly include rent arrears, breach of covenant, and — where permitted — no-fault grounds following proper notice.

Tenant organisations

Tenants have formed unions and associations since the 19th century to campaign for rent control, security of tenure and better housing conditions; tenants' movements played major roles in events such as the Glasgow rent strikes of 1915, which led to wartime rent restriction legislation in Britain.
