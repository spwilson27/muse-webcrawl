Right to silence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A courtroom witness chair, empty, in a shaft of window light
A courtroom witness chair, empty, in a shaft of window light

The right to silence is a legal principle guaranteeing any person the right to refuse to answer questions from law-enforcement officers or court officials — an aspect of the privilege against self-incrimination. In the United States it is embodied in the Fifth Amendment and the Miranda warning ('You have the right to remain silent…'); in England and Wales it was qualified in 1994 to allow adverse inferences from silence in specified circumstances.

Contents1. History
2. Debates

History

The privilege grew from resistance to the oath ex officio of the ecclesiastical and Star Chamber courts — John Lilburne's refusal (1630s–40s) is its founding English drama — and from the maxim nemo tenetur se ipsum accusare. Miranda v. Arizona (1966) made it a global cultural phrase; European human-rights law (the ECHR, Article 6 jurisprudence) protects silence while tolerating regulated inferences.

Debates

The right's scope is contested everywhere: police interrogation science (false confessions), terrorism and organised-crime exceptions, compelled decryption, and the English reforms' effect remain live arguments between crime control and due process — the privilege standing, for its defenders, as the mark distinguishing accusatorial justice from inquisition.
