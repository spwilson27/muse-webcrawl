Cantonment

A cantonment is a military or police garrison town, a term derived from the French word cantonnement. The word is used most prominently in South Asia, where cantonments were established by the British in India and remain administrative features of modern India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.

History

Cantonments originated in the 18th and 19th centuries as permanent military stations outside existing cities, in part to keep European troops in healthier surroundings and at a distance from the local population. They developed into self-contained towns with barracks, officers' bungalows, parade grounds, churches, schools and markets, characterised by broad, tree-lined roads and extensive open spaces.

Administration

In India, cantonments are governed by Cantonment Boards under the Cantonments Act of 2006; there are 62 cantonments, including those at Delhi, Pune (Khadki and Camp), Secunderabad and Meerut. Although defence land, many cantonments now house large civilian populations and function as distinctive neighbourhoods within their cities, often noted for their greenery and colonial architecture. Similar boards administer cantonments in Pakistan (such as Rawalpindi and Lahore cantonments) and Bangladesh (including Dhaka Cantonment).

Other uses

In the United States, "cantonment" was historically used for military posts, as in Cantonment Clinch in Florida. The term also appears in military usage for the temporary quartering of troops.
