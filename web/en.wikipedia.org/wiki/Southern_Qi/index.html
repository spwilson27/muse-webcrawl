The Southern Qi (Chinese: 南齊; pinyin: Nán Qí), known in historiography as Xiao Qi after its ruling clan, was a Chinese imperial dynasty and the second of the four Southern dynasties during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. It ruled southern China from 479 to 502, succeeding the Liu Song and preceding the Liang dynasty.

Founding

The dynasty was founded by Xiao Daocheng, a Liu Song general of humble background from Lanling, who seized power after a series of purges and civil wars at the Liu Song court and took the throne as Emperor Gao in 479. His son, Emperor Wu (r. 482–493), presided over the Yongming era, remembered as a period of prosperity, administrative reform, and flourishing court poetry — the Yongming style pioneered by Shen Yue and others reshaped Chinese verse.

Decline

After Emperor Wu's death, succession disputes plunged the court into violence. Emperor Ming (r. 494–498) massacred many princes of the imperial clan; his son, the Marquis of Donghun (Xiao Baojuan), became notorious for extravagance and cruelty. Rebellions followed, and in 501 the general Xiao Yan besieged the capital Jiankang, forced the abdication of the boy emperor He, and in 502 founded the Liang dynasty, ending Southern Qi after only 23 years — the shortest of the southern dynasties.

Culture

Despite its brevity, Southern Qi was culturally brilliant. The literary anthology tradition advanced, landscape poetry matured, and Buddhism continued to spread with imperial patronage. The dynasty is chronicled in the Book of Qi (Qishu), compiled by Xiao Zixian, himself a grandson of the founder — the only dynastic history of the period written by a member of its ruling house.
