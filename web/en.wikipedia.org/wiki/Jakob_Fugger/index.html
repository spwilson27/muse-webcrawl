Jakob Fugger of the Lily (German: Jakob Fugger von der Lilie; 6 March 1459 – 30 December 1525), known as Jakob the Rich, was a German merchant, mining entrepreneur and banker of Augsburg. Head of the Fugger family's business empire, he was arguably the richest private individual of his era and one of the wealthiest people of the early modern period, exercising decisive influence over European politics through his loans to the Habsburg dynasty.

Career

The youngest son of Jakob Fugger the Elder, Jakob was originally destined for the Church but entered the family firm after his brothers' deaths. Based for a time in Venice, he learned Italian double-entry bookkeeping and expanded the family's trade in textiles, spices and metals. His great coup was securing the silver and copper mines of Tyrol and Hungary in return for loans to Archduke Sigismund and Emperor Maximilian I, giving the Fuggers a near-monopoly of European copper production.

Financier of emperors

Fugger bankrolled Maximilian I's wars and diplomacy and, most consequentially, financed the election of Charles of Spain as Holy Roman Emperor Charles V in 1519, providing the enormous sums used to secure the electors' votes. He famously reminded Charles of the debt in a letter of 1523. His banking network also handled transfers for the papacy, including the indulgence preaching that helped provoke Martin Luther's protest and the Reformation.

Foundations and legacy

In 1516–1523 Fugger founded the Fuggerei in Augsburg, the world's oldest social housing settlement still in use, where needy Catholic citizens could live for a nominal annual rent of one Rhenish florin and prayers for the founder. He also endowed the Fugger Chapel in St. Anna's Church, an early work of German Renaissance art. He died childless in 1525 and was succeeded by his nephew Anton Fugger.
