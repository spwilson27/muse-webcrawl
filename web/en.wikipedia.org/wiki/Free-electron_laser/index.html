Free-electron laser

A free-electron laser (FEL) is a type of laser whose lasing medium consists of very-high-speed electrons moving freely through a magnetic structure called an undulator or wiggler. Unlike conventional lasers, which rely on bound atomic or molecular states, a free-electron laser can in principle be tuned over a very wide range of wavelengths, from microwaves through the infrared and visible to ultraviolet and X-rays.

Principle of operation

A beam of electrons is accelerated to relativistic speeds and injected into an undulator — an array of magnets with alternating poles. The alternating field forces the electrons into a sinusoidal path, causing them to emit synchrotron radiation. The emitted light interacts with the electron beam and organizes the electrons into microbunches spaced at the optical wavelength; these bunches then radiate coherently and in phase, amplifying the light exponentially. In high-gain FELs this process, called self-amplified spontaneous emission (SASE), can generate extremely bright, short pulses without an optical resonator.

History

The FEL was invented by John Madey at Stanford University, where the first device operated in the infrared in 1976. Large X-ray free-electron lasers built since the 2000s — including LCLS at SLAC (first lasing 2009), SACLA in Japan, and the European XFEL (opened 2017) — produce femtosecond X-ray pulses of unprecedented brightness.

Applications

X-ray FELs enable "diffraction before destruction" imaging of single molecules, viruses, and nanocrystals, and studies of chemical reactions and matter under extreme conditions. Smaller FELs serve in materials research, medicine, and spectroscopy in the infrared and terahertz ranges.
