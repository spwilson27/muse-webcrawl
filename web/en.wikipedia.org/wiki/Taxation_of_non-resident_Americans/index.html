Taxation of non-resident Americans

The United States taxes its citizens on worldwide income even when they live permanently abroad.

The taxation of non-resident Americans refers to the obligation of United States citizens and permanent residents living outside the United States to file U.S. tax returns and potentially pay U.S. taxes on their worldwide income. The United States is one of only two countries in the world—the other being Eritrea—that taxes its non-resident citizens; almost all other countries tax on the basis of residence or source of income.

How it works

Non-resident citizens must file Form 1040 annually if their income exceeds filing thresholds, reporting worldwide income. Two reliefs mitigate double taxation: the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, which excludes a capped amount of earned income (about $126,500 for 2024), and the Foreign Tax Credit, which credits income taxes paid to the country of residence. Housing amounts may also be excluded. Treaties and totalization agreements modify the rules for some countries and for social security taxes.

Reporting obligations

Beyond the tax return, non-resident Americans must report foreign financial accounts on the FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) when balances exceed $10,000 in aggregate, and specified foreign assets on Form 8938 under FATCA. Penalties for failures can be severe. FATCA also imposes reporting on foreign banks, which has led some to refuse or close accounts held by Americans abroad.

Criticism and reform debate

Advocacy groups such as American Citizens Abroad criticise citizenship-based taxation for compliance costs, accidental Americans (people unaware they hold U.S. citizenship), and barriers to banking and business abroad, and note a rise in citizenship renunciations. Defenders argue the system prevents affluent citizens from escaping U.S. tax by moving to low-tax jurisdictions. Legislative proposals for residence-based taxation are regularly introduced but have not been enacted.
