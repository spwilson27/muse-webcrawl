Buddhism in Korea

Buddhism in Korea
Introduced372 CE (to Goguryeo)
Main schoolsSeon (Zen), Jogye Order, Taego Order, Won Buddhism
HeritageTripitaka Koreana at Haeinsa

Korean Buddhism is distinguished from other forms of Buddhism by its emphasis on reconciling doctrinal differences (the "interpenetration" or tongbulgyo tendency) and by the centrality of Seon (Zen) meditation, combined in most orders with Pure Land and esoteric practice. Buddhism was introduced to the Korean peninsula from China in the 4th century CE — to Goguryeo in 372 and to Baekje in 384 — and became the state religion of the Three Kingdoms and of Goryeo (918–1392).

History

Under Goryeo, Buddhism flourished institutionally; the carving of the entire Buddhist canon on over 80,000 wooden printing blocks — the Tripitaka Koreana, still housed at Haeinsa temple — was undertaken as a spiritual defence against Mongol invasion. The succeeding Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) adopted Neo-Confucianism and suppressed Buddhism, driving monks into mountain temples, where Seon masters such as Hyujeong organised monk militias against the Japanese invasions of the 1590s. The great Seon master Jinul (1158–1210) is credited with unifying Korean practice around "sudden enlightenment followed by gradual cultivation".

Modern era

After liberation in 1945 Korean Buddhism rebuilt; the celibate Jogye Order is the largest order, while the Taego Order permits married clergy. New movements such as Won Buddhism (founded 1916) emerged from the tradition. Today roughly a fifth to a quarter of South Koreans identify as Buddhist in surveys, and temple-stay programmes and the lotus lantern festival are major cultural institutions.
