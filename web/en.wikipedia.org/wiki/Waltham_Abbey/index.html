Waltham Abbey

Waltham Abbey is a market town and civil parish in the Epping Forest district of Essex, England, on the River Lea adjoining the northeastern edge of London. It takes its name from the Abbey Church of Waltham Holy Cross and St Lawrence, one of England's notable surviving Norman churches and the reputed burial place of King Harold Godwinson, the last Anglo-Saxon king of England.

History

Legend holds that a miraculous crucifix, the Holy Cross of Waltham, was discovered at Montacute and brought to Waltham by Tovi the Proud in the eleventh century. King Harold is said to have refounded the church around 1060, and tradition maintains he was buried at Waltham after the Battle of Hastings in 1066, though the location of any grave is uncertain. In 1177 Henry II refounded Waltham as an Augustinian abbey, richly endowed in expiation for the murder of Thomas Becket. The abbey grew wealthy and was the last monastery in England to be dissolved under Henry VIII, surrendering in March 1540. The great Norman nave and tower of the abbey church survive as the parish church; most of the monastic buildings were demolished.

The town

The surrounding area includes the Royal Gunpowder Mills at Waltham Abbey, which produced explosives for some three centuries and is now a heritage site, and the Lee Valley White Water Centre built for the 2012 London Olympics. The town is associated with the denizen playwright — and purported inspiration for a Dickens character — and sits within the Metropolitan Green Belt, with the Lea Valley parklands to the west.

Notes on the name

The town was formerly called Waltham Holy Cross, and should be distinguished from Walthamstow and Waltham Cross, neighbouring settlements sharing the "Waltham" element from the Old English for a settlement in or by a wood.
