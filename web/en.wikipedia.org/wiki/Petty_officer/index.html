Petty officer

A petty officer (PO) is a non-commissioned officer in many navies and coast guards. Petty officers rank above junior enlisted sailors and below commissioned officers, forming the technical and supervisory backbone of a ship's crew. The term derives from the French petit, meaning "small" — petty officers were originally subordinate assistants to commissioned officers, appointed to oversee specific tasks aboard ship.

History

The petty officer class originated in the age of sail, when experienced seamen were appointed to act as assistants to the ship's officers — the boatswain's mates, gunner's mates and carpenter's mates, for example, each responsible for a particular department or "part of ship". In the Royal Navy, formal grades of petty officer were established in the nineteenth century, divided into first and second class. The United States Navy created its petty officer grades in the nineteenth century as well, formalising the chevron-and-eagle insignia still worn today.

Grades and responsibilities

In the United States Navy and Coast Guard, petty officers hold pay grades E-4 through E-6: petty officer third class, second class and first class. They are technical specialists in a rating — such as electronics, engineering, aviation or administration — and supervise junior sailors while remaining hands-on practitioners of their trade. Above them stand the chief petty officers (E-7 to E-9), who are treated as a distinct leadership tier.

In the Royal Navy, the petty officer rate sits between leading rate and chief petty officer, and its holder typically acts as a section or departmental supervisor. Equivalent ranks exist in most Commonwealth and many other navies.

Role

Petty officers translate officers' orders into practical work: they plan maintenance, train junior personnel, stand watches, enforce discipline and safety standards, and provide the day-to-day leadership that keeps a warship functioning. Navies commonly describe their petty officer corps as the "backbone" of the service.
