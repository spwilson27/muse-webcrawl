Erich Ludendorff

Erich Friedrich Wilhelm Ludendorff (9 April 1865 – 20 December 1937) was a German general who, with Hindenburg, directed Germany's war effort from 1916 to 1918.

Erich Friedrich Wilhelm Ludendorff (9 April 1865 – 20 December 1937) was a German general in the First World War who became the dominant figure in Germany's war effort. As First Quartermaster General from 1916, he and Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg formed the Third Supreme Command, effectively a military dictatorship that mobilised German society for total war. After defeat in 1918 he promoted the "stab-in-the-back" myth and became a leading figure of the radical nationalist right.

Early career

Ludendorff was born in Kruszewnia, Posen, the son of a landowner of modest means. A brilliant staff officer, he rose through the Great General Staff and helped shape the Schlieffen Plan, mastering its railway mobilisation timetables. His insistence on heavy siege artillery and his criticism of army preparedness briefly stalled his career, but at the outbreak of war in 1914 he distinguished himself leading the capture of the Belgian fortress of Liège, personally demanding the citadel's surrender.

First World War

Transferred east as chief of staff to Hindenburg, Ludendorff planned the encirclement victory at Tannenberg (August 1914) and the Masurian Lakes battles, making the pair national heroes. In August 1916 they took over the Supreme Army Command (OHL). Ludendorff directed the Hindenburg Programme of war production, the Auxiliary Service Law subordinating civilian labour, unrestricted submarine warfare, and — after victory over Russia — the harsh Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. In spring 1918 he launched a series of great offensives in the west that won ground but exhausted the army; when the Allied counter-offensives broke the German front that summer, he demanded an armistice, then resigned in October 1918 and fled to Sweden in disguise.

Postwar politics

Returning in 1919, Ludendorff spread the claim that the undefeated army had been betrayed by Jews, socialists and republicans at home — the Dolchstoß legend. He took part in Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923, marching beside him in Munich; acquitted at trial, he sat in the Reichstag for the National Socialist Freedom Movement and ran for president in 1925, receiving barely one per cent of the vote. Increasingly consumed by conspiracy theories about Jews, Freemasons and Jesuits, which he propagated with his second wife Mathilde in their Volkswarte publishing house, he founded the esoteric Tannenbergbund and drifted apart from Hitler. He died in Munich in 1937 and was given a state funeral against his wishes.
