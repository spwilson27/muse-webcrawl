Delphic maxims

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Delphic maxims are a collection of 147 aphorisms inscribed at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi. Originally attributed to the god Apollo himself and delivered through the Delphic oracle, the maxims were later said to have been given by the Seven Sages of Greece, whose names differ between sources. They formed a kind of ethical code for ancient Greek society, combining practical wisdom, religious duty, and social custom.

The most famous of the maxims is "Know thyself" (gnōthi seauton), inscribed in the forecourt of the temple. Another, "Nothing in excess" (mēden agan), together with "Surety brings ruin" (eggua para d'atē), stood alongside it. The full list of 147 sayings was preserved by the fifth-century AD anthologist Stobaeus, who attributed them to Sosiades.

History

The maxims were inscribed on the walls of the pronaos of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, one of the most important religious sites of the ancient Greek world. They are generally dated to the Archaic period, and their attribution to the Seven Sages — figures such as Thales of Miletus, Solon of Athens, and Bias of Priene — reflects their status as distilled, widely shared moral teaching rather than the work of a single author.

Shorter collections of Delphic sayings circulated widely. The geographer Pausanias mentions the famous sayings in the forecourt, and the sayings "know thyself" and "nothing in excess" were quoted throughout classical literature, including by Plato, who discusses them in the Protagoras and the Charmides.

The maxims

The 147 maxims preserved by Stobaeus range across every sphere of life. They include religious precepts ("Follow God", "Worship the gods"), family duties ("Honour your parents", "Control your wife"), civic counsel ("Obey the law", "Be fond of education"), and personal discipline ("Control your temper", "Restrain the stomach", "Use time sparingly", "Despise strife", "Accept old age"). Many are strikingly terse: "Be happy with what you have", "Think as a mortal", "Know what you have learned", and "Do not grow tired of learning".

Other notable maxims include "Help your friends", "Cling to discipline", "Pursue honour", "Long for wisdom", "Praise the good", "Avoid murder", "Pray for things possible", and the closing advice "Die for your country" in some recensions. The sayings were meant to be memorised and applied, functioning as a pocket guide to the well-ordered life.

Know thyself

"Know thyself" is the best known of the maxims and one of the most quoted phrases of antiquity. In Plato's dialogues it is connected with Socrates, whose philosophical mission was framed as an exploration of self-knowledge and the limits of human wisdom. Interpretations have ranged from a warning to know one's mortal limits before the gods, to an injunction toward self-examination as the foundation of ethical life.

Legacy

The maxims influenced Greek and Roman moral philosophy and were frequently cited by later writers. Their concise, proverb-like form helped shape the genre of the aphorism in Western literature. Excavations at Delphi and the recovery of inscribed collections — including a Hellenistic inscription of the maxims found at Miletopolis — have confirmed the wide circulation of the sayings in antiquity.
