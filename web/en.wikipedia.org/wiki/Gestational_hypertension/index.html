Gestational hypertension

Specialty: Obstetrics
Onset: After 20 weeks of pregnancy
Key feature: New hypertension without proteinuria or organ dysfunction

Gestational hypertension or pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH) is the development of new hypertension in a pregnant woman after 20 weeks of gestation, without the presence of protein in the urine or other features of pre-eclampsia. It is generally defined as a systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher, or a diastolic pressure of 90 mmHg or higher, on two occasions at least four hours apart, in a woman who was previously normotensive.

Diagnosis and classification

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are classified into chronic hypertension (present before pregnancy or before 20 weeks), gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and pre-eclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension. Gestational hypertension differs from pre-eclampsia in the absence of proteinuria and of systemic features such as thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, or new-onset headache or visual symptoms. A proportion of women initially diagnosed with gestational hypertension — particularly when it presents early — will progress to pre-eclampsia.

Management

Management involves close surveillance of mother and fetus, with serial blood pressure measurements, laboratory testing, and assessment of fetal growth. Antihypertensive medication such as labetalol, methyldopa, or nifedipine may be used when pressures reach severe ranges. Delivery is the definitive treatment, and timing is individualised: uncomplicated gestational hypertension is usually delivered by around 37 weeks. Blood pressure typically normalises within days to weeks after delivery; if hypertension persists beyond 12 weeks postpartum it is reclassified as chronic hypertension. Women with a history of gestational hypertension have an increased lifetime risk of cardiovascular disease.
