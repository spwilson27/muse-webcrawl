Great Plains

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Plains is a broad expanse of flat, mostly treeless grassland in central North America, lying west of the Mississippi River tallgrass prairies and east of the Rocky Mountains. It stretches some 3,200 km from Texas and New Mexico north through Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska, the Dakotas and Montana into Canada's prairie provinces. A semi-arid steppe on a great slab of sedimentary rock, lifted a kilometre above sea level at its western edge, it was long written off as the "Great American Desert" on early maps — and became instead one of the world's great grain and cattle regions.

History and ecology

For millennia the plains were hunting ground for Indigenous peoples — after horses spread north from Spanish herds, the Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, Crow and many others built brilliant equestrian cultures around the American bison, whose herds numbered in the tens of millions. US expansion after the Civil War destroyed that world in a generation: railroads, homesteaders (1862 Homestead Act), barbed wire, and the deliberate slaughter of the bison (to a few hundred animals by the 1880s) emptied the plains for cattle ranching and wheat farming. Marginal rainfall made farming a gamble: the boom of wet years ended in the Dust Bowl of the 1930s, when ploughed-up sod blew away in black blizzards and drove the Okie exodus of Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath. Modern plains agriculture leans on irrigation from the Ogallala Aquifer — a fossil water reserve being steadily depleted — while many counties have fewer people than in 1900, a depopulation that fuels both "Buffalo Commons" proposals to restore prairie and bison, and the cultural mythology of the American frontier. The plains remain the home of the continent's most violent weather, from blizzards to Tornado Alley's supercells east of the Rockies.
