Eric John Ernest Hobsbawm (9 June 1917 – 1 October 2012) was a British historian of the rise of industrial capitalism, socialism and nationalism. A life-long Marxist, his socio-political convictions influenced the character of his work. His best-known works include his tetralogy about the "long 19th century" and the "short 20th century": The Age of Revolution: Europe 1789–1848 (1962), The Age of Capital: 1848–1875 (1975), The Age of Empire: 1875–1914 (1987) and The Age of Extremes: The Short Twentieth Century, 1914–1991 (1994). He also wrote extensively on social history, labour movements, banditry, jazz (under the pseudonym Francis Newton), and the "invention of tradition".

Early life

Hobsbawm was born in Alexandria to a Jewish family — his father a British colonial official from London's East End, his mother from a Viennese middle-class family — and was raised in Vienna and then Berlin, where the rise of Nazism politicized him; he joined the Communist Party as a schoolboy in 1932. Orphaned in his teens, he moved to London in 1933, won a scholarship to King's College, Cambridge, and took a first in history. During the Second World War he served in the Royal Engineers and the Education Corps. He was appointed to a lectureship at Birkbeck College, London, in 1947, where he spent most of his career, becoming professor in 1970.

Career and ideas

Hobsbawm was a founding member of the Communist Party Historians Group and of the journal Past & Present. His early work, including Primitive Rebels (1959) and Bandits (1969), analysed "pre-political" movements of the dispossessed, popularizing the concept of the "social bandit". With Terence Ranger he edited The Invention of Tradition (1983), whose title essay showed how apparently ancient rituals were often recent fabrications. The Age of Extremes, his survey of 1914–1991, was translated into dozens of languages and is among the most widely read works of twentieth-century history, though its publication in some countries was delayed by disputes over its treatment of communism.

Politics and controversy

Unlike many contemporaries, Hobsbawm remained a member of the Communist Party of Great Britain until its dissolution in 1991, long after the Soviet invasions of Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968), which he criticized but which did not move him to resign. His refusal to renounce his past commitments, and interviews in which he weighed revolutionary ideals against their human costs, drew sustained criticism from other historians and commentators, even as his scholarship won honours including appointment as a Companion of Honour in 1998. His memoirs, Interesting Times (2002), recount his formation as "a Central European of the age of catastrophe".
