Digital signature

A digital signature is a mathematical scheme for verifying the authenticity of digital messages or documents. A valid digital signature gives a recipient reason to believe that the message was created by a known sender (authentication), that the sender cannot deny having sent it (non-repudiation), and that the message was not altered in transit (integrity). Digital signatures are a standard element of most public-key cryptography protocol suites and are used for software distribution, financial transactions and contract management.

How it works

A signature scheme consists of three algorithms: key generation, which produces a private signing key and a corresponding public verification key; signing, which takes the private key and a message and produces a signature; and verification, which takes the public key, the message and the signature and accepts or rejects it. In practice the message is first passed through a cryptographic hash function, and the resulting digest is signed, so signatures can be applied to messages of any length efficiently.

Security rests on the infeasibility of deriving the private key from the public key or of forging a signature without it. The concept was first described publicly by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976; the RSA algorithm, presented in 1978, provided the first practical realisation.

Common algorithms

  • RSA signatures — based on the difficulty of factoring large integers; widely used with padding schemes such as PKCS #1 and PSS.
  • DSA — the Digital Signature Algorithm, based on discrete logarithms and standardised in the US Digital Signature Standard.
  • ECDSA — an elliptic-curve analogue of DSA offering shorter keys for equivalent security.
  • EdDSA — a modern scheme using twisted Edwards curves, including the widely deployed Ed25519 variant.

Applications and law

Digital signatures authenticate software updates, secure email (S/MIME and OpenPGP), TLS certificates and blockchain transactions, in which each transfer of funds is authorised by a signature. Many jurisdictions give digital signatures legal effect: the EU's eIDAS regulation recognises qualified electronic signatures as equivalent to handwritten ones, and the United States ESIGN Act of 2000 grants electronic signatures broad legal validity. A digital signature is distinct from an electronic signature in the wider legal sense, which can include any electronic indication of agreement.
