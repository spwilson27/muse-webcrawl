Monetary reform

Monetary reform is any proposal or change designed to alter how a monetary system operates — how money is created, issued, controlled and valued. Historically, monetary reforms have ranged from technical changes, such as replacing a currency or moving between metallic standards, to radical proposals that seek to change the role of banks in creating money or to remove the state's control over the currency.

Currency reform: the exchange of old notes for new, as in Germany in 1948.

Types of monetary reform

  • Currency reforms and redenomination — replacing an old unit with a new one, often after hyperinflation, as in the German currency reform of 1948 that introduced the Deutsche Mark, the Brazilian Real Plan of 1994, or Turkey's removal of six zeros from the lira in 2005.
  • Changes of standard — adoption or abandonment of the gold standard, bimetallism, or fiat money; the Bretton Woods system (1944) and its collapse in 1971–73 are major examples.
  • Monetary union — several states adopting a common currency, as with the introduction of the euro.
  • Reform of money creation — proposals such as the Chicago Plan (1933), 100%-reserve banking, "sovereign money" (for example the Swiss Vollgeld initiative rejected by referendum in 2018, and Iceland's post-2008 monetary reform report), which would transfer money creation from commercial banks to the central bank.
  • Complementary and alternative currencies — local currencies, time banks, and cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin (2009), created in reaction to the 2008 financial crisis.
  • Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) — state-issued digital money under development or in use in many jurisdictions.

History and debate

Monetary reform movements have often followed financial crises and debt deflations. In the late 19th-century United States, the free silver movement and William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech (1896) campaigned against the deflationary gold standard. In the 1930s, economists at the University of Chicago and Irving Fisher advocated debt-deflation analysis and full-reserve banking. In the United Kingdom, organisations such as Positive Money have promoted sovereign-money reform, while critics argue such schemes would do little to prevent lending booms and could disrupt credit provision.

Economists distinguish between reforms that change the anchor of the system (what backs or constrains the money supply) and reforms that change its institutional architecture (who issues money and how reserves and payments are organised). Both are the subject of continuing debate in monetary economics.
