On 6 and 9 August 1945 the United States detonated two atomic bombs over the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The bombings killed between 129,000 and 226,000 people, most of them civilians, and remain the only uses of nuclear weapons in armed conflict. Together with the Soviet declaration of war and invasion of Manchuria on 9 August, they precipitated Japan's surrender and the end of the Second World War.

Background

The weapons were developed by the Manhattan Project, a vast secret programme employing over 130,000 people, which tested the first device at Trinity, New Mexico, on 16 July 1945. With Germany defeated, attention turned to Japan: the Potsdam Declaration of 26 July demanded unconditional surrender of the Japanese armed forces and was publicly rejected ("mokusatsu") by the Japanese government. American planners faced the prospect of an invasion of the home islands (Operation Downfall), whose likely cost had been underscored by the carnage at Iwo Jima and Okinawa.

The bombings

On 6 August the B-29 Enola Gay, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, dropped the uranium bomb "Little Boy" on Hiroshima, an embarkation port and army headquarters city. It exploded 580 metres above the city with a yield of about 15 kilotons, destroying some 12 km² and killing tens of thousands at once. On 9 August the B-29 Bockscar flew to its primary target, Kokura, found it obscured by cloud and smoke, and diverted to Nagasaki, where the plutonium bomb "Fat Man" detonated over the Urakami valley, killing tens of thousands more. Survivors — the hibakusha — suffered burns, radiation sickness and elevated lifetime cancer rates, and campaigned for decades for recognition and nuclear abolition; Tsutomu Yamaguchi is recognised as a survivor of both bombs.

Surrender and debate

Even after both bombs and the Soviet entry, Japan's Supreme War Council deadlocked; Emperor Hirohito's intervention broke the tie, and his surrender rescript was broadcast on 15 August. The formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945. The bombings remain ethically and historically contested — debated in terms of military necessity, the relative weight of the Soviet invasion, the terms offered, and the emerging rivalry with the USSR — and are commemorated at the Hiroshima Peace Memorial (Genbaku Dome) and the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum.
