Straight-line mechanism

The Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage, the first exact straight-line mechanism

A straight-line mechanism is a mechanism that produces a straight-line path for a point, or approximates such a path closely. Converting rotational motion into linear motion with a linkage was a central problem of early machine design, especially for guiding the piston rod of a steam engine, where a crosshead sliding in guides was expensive and wore quickly.

Approximate mechanisms

The first widely used approximate straight-line mechanism was the Watt linkage, devised by James Watt in 1784 for his double-acting steam engine; Watt considered it one of his most ingenious inventions. The related parallel motion combined the Watt linkage with a pantograph. Other approximations include the Chebyshev linkage, studied by Pafnuty Chebyshev, and the Roberts linkage, whose coupler curve contains a long, nearly straight segment.

Exact mechanisms

The first exact straight-line mechanism was the Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage, described by the French officer Charles-Nicolas Peaucellier in 1864 and independently by the Lithuanian mathematician Lipman Lipkin around 1871. It is based on the geometry of circle inversion: a rhombus of hinged bars arranged with two longer equal arms forces a point to trace an exact straight line when its companion point moves on a circle through the centre of inversion. Later, Harry Hart invented further exact mechanisms, including Hart's inversor (1874), and Sarrus's linkage achieves exact straight motion in three dimensions using hinged plates.

Applications

Although sliding guides and later linear bearings displaced linkage solutions in most steam engines, straight-line mechanisms remain important in kinematics education, in suspension design, and in mechanisms where sliding contact is undesirable.
