A dipole antenna is one of the simplest and most widely used types of radio antenna. It consists of two identical conductive elements — rods or wires — arranged symmetrically and fed at the centre by a transmission line. The archetypal form is the half-wave dipole, whose total length is approximately half the wavelength of the operating frequency. Heinrich Hertz used dipole antennas in his pioneering 1886–1889 experiments demonstrating the existence of electromagnetic waves, and the dipole remains the reference antenna against which the gain of others is measured (the unit dBd).

Characteristics

Radio frequency current applied at the centre drives the two elements in opposite phase; the resulting radiation pattern is a torus (doughnut) shape, with maximum radiation perpendicular to the axis of the elements and nulls off the ends. A resonant half-wave dipole in free space presents a feedpoint impedance of about 73 + j42.5 Ω, the reactive part being cancelled by trimming the length slightly; in practice it is fed with 50–75 Ω coaxial cable, often through a balun to suppress feedline radiation. The closely related folded dipole offers an impedance of about 300 Ω, matching twin-lead line, and wider bandwidth.

Variants and applications

Shortened, loaded and trap dipoles serve amateur radio operators on the HF bands; inverted-V configurations save space. At VHF and UHF, simple dipoles serve as driven elements in Yagi–Uda arrays and as feeds for parabolic reflectors. The "rabbit ears" of old television sets are adjustable dipoles. The related monopole antenna — a quarter-wave element over a ground plane — can be analysed as half of a dipole using image theory.

History

After Hertz's demonstrations, Guglielmo Marconi and other early wireless pioneers adopted elevated wire antennas derived from the dipole, though Marconi soon turned to grounded monopoles for long-distance work. The half-wave dipole became the workhorse of shortwave broadcasting and amateur radio in the twentieth century, and its theory was placed on a rigorous footing by workers such as Henry Pocklington and Sergei Schelkunoff.
