Meteor shower

A meteor shower is a celestial event in which a number of meteors are observed to radiate, or originate, from one point in the night sky. These meteors are caused by streams of cosmic debris called meteoroids entering Earth's atmosphere at extremely high speeds on parallel trajectories. Most meteoroids are smaller than a grain of sand, so almost all of them disintegrate and never hit the Earth's surface.

Cause

Meteor showers occur when Earth passes through the trail of debris left by a comet (occasionally an asteroid) as it orbits the Sun. The apparent point of origin, the radiant, lies in the direction of the constellation for which the shower is named. Activity is quantified by the zenithal hourly rate (ZHR), the number of meteors a single observer would see per hour under ideal conditions at peak.

Major showers

Well-known annual showers include the Quadrantids (January), Lyrids (April), Eta Aquariids (May, from Halley's Comet), Perseids (August, from Comet Swift–Tuttle, often the most reliable display of the year), Orionids (October), Leonids (November), and Geminids (December, from the asteroid 3200 Phaethon, frequently the strongest shower of the year). Occasionally a shower produces a meteor storm, with rates of over 1,000 meteors per hour, as the Leonids did in 1833, 1966, and around 1999–2002.

Observing

Showers are best observed after midnight from dark sites away from light pollution, when the observer's side of Earth is turning into the meteoroid stream. No optical aid is needed; the naked eye gives the widest field of view.
