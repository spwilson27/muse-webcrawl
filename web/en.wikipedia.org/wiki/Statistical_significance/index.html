Statistical significance

In statistical hypothesis testing, a result has statistical significance when a test statistic is sufficiently improbable under the null hypothesis that the null hypothesis can be rejected at a chosen significance level. The concept was developed in the 1920s by Ronald Fisher, who suggested the 5% level (p < 0.05) as a convenient threshold, later formalised in the Neyman–Pearson framework of hypothesis tests.

Concept

Significance is assessed using the p-value: the probability, computed under the null hypothesis, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed. If the p-value is smaller than the significance level α — conventionally 0.05, with 0.01 and 0.001 used for stronger evidence — the result is declared statistically significant. Equivalently, a confidence interval for the parameter of interest excludes the null value.

A significance level of 5% means that, if the null hypothesis is true, a result this extreme would occur by chance in about 1 in 20 studies of the same design; such a false rejection is called a Type I error.

History

The earliest significance tests appear in John Arbuthnot's 1710 analysis of birth ratios and in Laplace's work. Karl Pearson developed the chi-squared test in 1900, Fisher systematised testing in Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925), and Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson recast testing as a decision between rival hypotheses with controlled error rates in 1933.

Misuse and criticism

Statistical significance is widely misunderstood: a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and significance says nothing about the size or practical importance of an effect. Common problems include p-hacking (testing many hypotheses or stopping rules until significance appears), publication bias toward significant results, and treating 0.05 as a sharp boundary between truth and falsehood. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement on p-values, and in 2019 hundreds of scientists called in Nature for statistical significance to be retired as a dichotomous criterion, in favour of reporting effect sizes and uncertainty.
