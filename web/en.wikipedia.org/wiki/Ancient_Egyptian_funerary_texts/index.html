Ancient Egyptian funerary texts

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancient Egyptian funerary texts are a collection of religious documents, written across some two thousand years of Egyptian history, that consist of spells, prayers, hymns, and descriptions of the afterlife intended to guide and protect the dead in the next world. The corpus evolved through three great stages — the Pyramid Texts, the Coffin Texts, and the Book of the Dead — alongside royal underworld books of the New Kingdom and later compositions such as the Books of Breathing.

Pyramid Texts (Old Kingdom)

The Pyramid Texts, first inscribed in the pyramid of Unas at Saqqara around 2350 BC, are the oldest large religious corpus in Egypt. Carved on the walls of royal burial chambers, their utterances aim to resurrect the king, sustain him with offerings, and enable his ascent to join the gods — identifying him with Osiris and with Ra in the celestial realm. Initially a strictly royal privilege, their themes were gradually democratised.

Coffin Texts (Middle Kingdom)

In the First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, spells descended from the Pyramid Texts, plus much new material, were painted on the coffins of officials and wealthy commoners. The Coffin Texts (about 1,185 spells) reflect a wider access to the afterlife and include the celebrated "Book of Two Ways", an illustrated map of routes through the underworld — the earliest known example of a map of the afterlife in any culture.

Book of the Dead (New Kingdom to Late Period)

The Book of the Dead — the Egyptians called it the "Book of Coming Forth by Day" — gathered around 200 spells on papyrus scrolls buried with the dead from the New Kingdom (c. 1550 BC) onward. Famous spells include the judgement scene (Spell 125) with the negative confession and weighing of the heart, Spell 30B inscribed on heart scarabs begging the heart not to betray its owner, and transformation spells allowing the deceased to take the form of birds or gods. Scribes produced versions to order, with vignettes illustrating the journey; the Papyrus of Ani is the most celebrated surviving example.

Underworld books and later texts

New Kingdom royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings display a separate series of "books" describing the sun god's nightly journey: the Amduat ("That Which Is in the Afterworld"), the Book of Gates, the Book of Caverns, the Book of the Earth, and the Litany of Ra. After the New Kingdom, funerary literature contracted into shorter compositions such as the Books of Breathing of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, and elements of the tradition survived in Coptic and folk practice.
