American and British English differences

The English language was introduced to the Americas by British colonisation in the early 17th century, and over the following centuries American English and British English developed differences in spelling, vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation, even though the two varieties remain mutually intelligible.

Spelling

Many systematic spelling differences derive from Noah Webster's American reforms: American English prefers color, center and organize where British English generally has colour, centre and organise (though -ize is also acceptable in British usage). Other contrasts include traveled/travelled, program/programme (except in computing), and single versus double consonants in inflected forms.

Vocabulary

Everyday vocabulary often differs: American truck, apartment, elevator, gasoline and sidewalk correspond to British lorry, flat, lift, petrol and pavement. Some words carry different meanings in each variety — for example pants means trousers in American English but underpants in British English, and a billion, once different in British usage, is now the same in both.

Grammar and usage

Differences include the use of collective nouns (British "the team are" versus American "the team is"), prepositions ("at the weekend" versus "on the weekend"), past tense forms (learned/learnt, gotten/got), and the mandative subjunctive, which is more common in American English. Punctuation conventions also differ in details such as the placement of quotation marks and the use of periods in abbreviations.

Pronunciation

The most salient pronunciation difference is rhoticity: most American accents pronounce the /r/ after vowels, while most accents in England do not. Vowel qualities, stress placement in words like advertisement and laboratory, and the realisation of the letter "t" also differ systematically.
