"Rip Van Winkle" is a short story by the American author Washington Irving, first published in 1819 in The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent. The story follows a kind-hearted but idleness-prone Dutch-American villager in colonial America who falls asleep in the Catskill Mountains and awakens twenty years later, having slept through the American Revolution. It is one of the best-known early works of American literature and a staple of American folklore.

Plot

Rip Van Winkle, a good-natured farmer henpecked by his shrewish wife, Dame Van Winkle, wanders into the Catskills with his dog Wolf to escape work and scolding. In a mountain hollow he encounters a group of strangely silent, old-fashioned Dutchmen playing ninepins — the crew of Henry Hudson's Half Moon — and drinks from their flagon. He falls into a deep sleep and wakes twenty years later to find his beard a foot long, his gun rusted, his dog gone, and his village transformed: the portrait of King George on the inn sign has been repainted as George Washington, his wife is dead, and his children are grown. An old friend recognises him, and Peter Vanderdonk, the village historian, confirms the tale of the mountain spirits; Rip settles in with his married daughter and becomes a village storyteller and patriarch.

Sources and composition

Irving wrote the story overnight, partly inspired by a conversation about German folk tales with his brother-in-law; folklorists compare it to the German legend of Peter Claus and to European "sleeping hero" and fairy-time tales, as well as Epimenides of Crete and the Seven Sleepers. The frame narrator, Diedrich Knickerbocker, is Irving's fictional Dutch historian, previously used in his 1809 History of New York.

Themes and legacy

Readings of the story emphasise the transformation of American identity across the Revolution, the escape from labour and domestic conflict, and the costs of change. The story entered the language ("a Rip Van Winkle" denotes someone oblivious to change) and inspired the long-running stage adaptation made famous by actor Joseph Jefferson III, operas, cartoons, and countless retellings. Villages and landmarks in the Catskills still trade on the tale.
