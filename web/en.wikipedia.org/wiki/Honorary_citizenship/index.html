Honorary citizenship

Honorary citizenship is a status bestowed by a city, state, or country on a foreigner or citizen who is deemed worthy of the distinction as an honour, without the full legal rights and duties of ordinary citizenship. It is usually symbolic and typically confers no right to live or work in the granting jurisdiction, no passport, and no voting rights, though practices differ.

National honorary citizenship

Several countries have granted honorary citizenship by act of their legislature. The United States has conferred it on only eight people, including Winston Churchill (1963), Raoul Wallenberg, William and Hannah Penn, Mother Teresa, the Marquis de Lafayette, Casimir Pulaski, and Bernardo de Gálvez. Canada has granted honorary citizenship to figures such as Nelson Mandela, the 14th Dalai Lama, Aung San Suu Kyi (later revoked in 2018 over the Rohingya crisis), and Malala Yousafzai. France made honorary citizens of figures such as the abolitionist-era heroes honoured by the Assemblée nationale during the Revolution.

City-level honours

More common are municipal honours: the "freedom of the city" in the United Kingdom and Ireland, honorary citizenship of towns in Germany (Ehrenbürger), and similar awards worldwide. Such honours are often given to visiting dignitaries, military units, benefactors, and distinguished former residents, and they may occasionally carry quaint historical privileges, such as driving sheep across London Bridge—rights that are ceremonial in practice.

Revocation

Because the status is honorific, it can also be withdrawn. Cities across Germany have revoked honorary citizenships granted during the Nazi era, most prominently those of Adolf Hitler. Canada's revocation of Aung San Suu Kyi's honorary citizenship shows that modern governments may strip the honour when a recipient's later conduct conflicts with the values the award was meant to recognize.
