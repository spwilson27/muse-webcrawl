Lübeck

Lübeck (German: [ˈlyːbɛk]) is a city in Schleswig-Holstein, northern Germany, on the River Trave near the Baltic Sea. With around 220,000 inhabitants it is the second-largest city in the state, and its medieval Old Town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Founded in 1143 by Adolf II of Schauenburg and refounded by Henry the Lion in 1159, Lübeck became the leading city — the "Queen" — of the Hanseatic League, dominating Baltic trade for several centuries. Its merchants traded herring, salt, timber and grain across northern Europe, and Lübeck law was adopted by many Baltic ports. After the league's decline Lübeck remained a free imperial city until it lost its centuries-old statehood under Nazi Germany's Greater Hamburg Act of 1937. The Old Town suffered heavily in a 1942 air raid but was substantially rebuilt.

Landmarks and culture

The city's skyline is marked by seven church towers, among them the twin-towered Marienkirche, a model of Brick Gothic architecture. The Holstentor, a massive fifteenth-century city gate, is one of Germany's best-known landmarks. Lübeck is also famed for its marzipan, notably the firm Niederegger (founded 1806), and as the hometown of Thomas Mann and Willy Brandt; it is sometimes called the "City of Seven Towers" and the "Marzipan City".
