Asmodeus (Latin: Asmodaeus; Hebrew: אַשְׁמְדַאי, Ashmedai) is a demon prominent in Jewish and Christian demonology, traditionally regarded as a king of demons and associated with lust. The name likely derives from the Avestan Aēshma-daēva, the Zoroastrian demon of wrath. Asmodeus figures in the deuterocanonical Book of Tobit, in Talmudic and midrashic tales about King Solomon, and in Renaissance grimoires.

Book of Tobit

In the Book of Tobit, Asmodeus loves Sarah, daughter of Raguel, and kills her seven husbands on their wedding nights before the marriages can be consummated. Following instructions from the archangel Raphael, Tobias burns the heart and liver of a fish, whose smoke drives the demon away; Raphael pursues and binds him in the desert of Upper Egypt, freeing Sarah to marry Tobias.

Solomon legends

In the Testament of Solomon and in Talmudic tradition (notably tractate Gittin), Asmodeus (Ashmedai), king of the demons, is bound by Solomon and compelled to help build the Temple in Jerusalem; he reveals the location of the shamir, the creature or substance able to cut stone without iron. In a popular tale, Ashmedai tricks Solomon, seizes his magic ring and throne, and hurls the king far away, forcing Solomon to wander as a beggar until he recovers the ring from a fish's belly.

Later demonology

Medieval and early modern writers systematized Asmodeus's rank and functions. In the Malleus Maleficarum he is the demon of lust; Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum and the Lesser Key of Solomon describe Asmoday as a great king of hell with three heads — of a bull, a man, and a ram — riding a dragon and teaching arithmetic, astronomy, geometry, and handicrafts. In some classifications he is one of the seven princes of Hell, each tied to a deadly sin, Asmodeus to lust. Islam knows him as Sakhr, the jinn who stole Solomon's (Sulayman's) ring.

In literature

Asmodeus gives his name to Alain-René Lesage's novel Le Diable boiteux (1707; "The Devil on Two Sticks"), in which the lame demon lifts the roofs of Madrid to show the student Don Cleophas the secrets within — a device ("Asmodeus's flight") imitated in many later works. He appears widely in modern fiction, games, and popular culture.
