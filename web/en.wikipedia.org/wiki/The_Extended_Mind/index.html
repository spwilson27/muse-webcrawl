The Extended Mind

AuthorsAndy Clark and David Chalmers
Published1998
JournalAnalysis 58(1): 7–19
FieldPhilosophy of mind, cognitive science
Key thesisActive externalism / extended cognition

"The Extended Mind" is a 1998 paper in philosophy and cognitive science by Andy Clark and David Chalmers, published in the journal Analysis. It argues for active externalism: the thesis that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain or even the body, but can extend into the environment when external objects play the right functional role in driving cognition. The paper launched the extended cognition research program and is one of the most cited works in contemporary philosophy of mind.

Argument

Clark and Chalmers begin with cases such as a player using a letter rack in Scrabble, a sailor's instruments, and rotating shapes in the video game Tetris — physical actions that offload computation onto the world. Their canonical thought experiment contrasts Inga, who remembers the way to the Museum of Modern Art, with Otto, an Alzheimer's patient who retrieves the same information reliably from a notebook he always carries and automatically trusts. By the parity principle — if a resource plays the same functional role as an internal memory, it counts as part of the cognitive process — Otto's notebook is literally part of his memory, and Otto believes the museum is on 53rd Street before he consults it. Beliefs, and hence minds, can extend beyond the skull.

Criticism and responses

Critics including Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa charged the thesis with a "coupling–constitution fallacy": that a causal coupling between brain and tool does not make the tool a constituent of cognition, and warned of "cognitive bloat." Defenders reply that the mark of the cognitive is flexible, and the view has influenced work on embodied and distributed cognition, education, and human–computer interaction, and is elaborated in Clark's later Supersizing the Mind (2008). The thesis resonates with collaborative production models celebrated in The Cathedral and the Bazaar, where the tooling environment is integral to how software gets made.
