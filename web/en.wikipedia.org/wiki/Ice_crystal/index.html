Ice crystal

An ice crystal is any of a number of macroscopic crystalline forms in which the molecular structure of ice is arranged in ordered patterns extending in all directions. Ice crystals form the basis of snowflakes, frost, hoar frost and the cirrus clouds of the upper atmosphere. Because ice at ordinary pressures (ice Ih) has a hexagonal crystal structure, its crystals typically display six-fold symmetry.

Formation

Ice crystals form when water vapour deposits directly onto a freezing nucleus in supersaturated air, or when supercooled droplets freeze. The habit a crystal adopts — plate, column, needle or dendrite — depends principally on the temperature and humidity at which it grows, a relationship mapped in the Nakaya diagram. As a crystal falls through layers of air with differing conditions, different growth modes can be recorded in its successive zones, producing complex snowflakes.

Optical effects

Populations of ice crystals in the atmosphere refract and reflect sunlight to produce optical phenomena such as halos, sun dogs (parhelia), circumzenithal arcs and light pillars, whose forms depend on crystal shape and orientation.

Classification

Magono and Lee's meteorological classification describes 80 distinct types of ice crystals and snow particles. Artificial ice crystals are grown in laboratories and cloud chambers to study cloud physics, precipitation formation and aircraft icing.
