Mary I (18 February 1516 – 17 November 1558), also known as Mary Tudor, was Queen of England and Ireland from July 1553 until her death in 1558. The only surviving child of Henry VIII and his first wife, Catherine of Aragon, she is remembered for her attempt to reverse the English Reformation and restore Roman Catholicism — during which nearly 300 Protestants were burned at the stake, earning her the posthumous epithet "Bloody Mary" in Protestant tradition.

Early life and exclusion

Mary was a cherished only child, given a humanist education under the influence of her mother and the Spanish scholar Juan Luis Vives, whose treatise on the education of women was written for her. When Henry annulled his marriage to Catherine, Mary was declared illegitimate, separated from her mother — whom she never saw again — and forced to serve in the household of her infant half-sister Elizabeth. She submitted to her father's demands in 1536 only under extreme pressure, and was restored to the succession by the Act of 1544, after Elizabeth. Under her brother Edward VI, her refusal to abandon the Mass brought her into dangerous conflict with the Protestant regime.

Accession

On Edward's death in July 1553, the Duke of Northumberland proclaimed Lady Jane Grey queen under Edward's "Devise". Mary rallied support in East Anglia, and the country rallied to her as the lawful heir; the Privy Council proclaimed her queen on 19 July. She entered London in triumph in August 1553, the first undisputed queen regnant of England.

Spanish marriage and Wyatt's rebellion

Mary's determination to marry her cousin Philip of Spain — soon Philip II — provoked alarm and, in January 1554, Wyatt's rebellion in Kent, which briefly reached London before collapsing. Lady Jane Grey and her husband were executed in its aftermath, and Elizabeth was imprisoned in the Tower, though later released. Mary and Philip married at Winchester Cathedral in July 1554, under a treaty designed to keep England independent of Spanish control. Mary twice believed herself pregnant; no child came, and Philip spent most of the marriage abroad.

Catholic restoration and persecution

Mary's government repealed the Edwardian religious laws, revived the heresy statutes, and in November 1554 received England back into communion with Rome through Cardinal Reginald Pole, the last Catholic Archbishop of Canterbury. From February 1555 Protestants were burned for heresy: about 284 people died, including bishops Latimer and Ridley and Archbishop Cranmer, whose burnings at Oxford were broadcast to posterity by John Foxe's Book of Martyrs. The persecution, intended to terrify England back to Catholicism, instead durably associated Mary's faith with cruelty in the national memory.

War, Calais and death

In 1557 Philip drew England into his war with France; in January 1558 the French seized Calais, England's last possession in France — a loss Mary reportedly said would be found written on her heart. Ill, isolated and without an heir, she died on 17 November 1558, probably of influenza or an abdominal tumour, and was succeeded by Elizabeth, whose religious settlement undid Mary's restoration. Mary is buried in Westminster Abbey in a tomb she shares, by Elizabeth's arrangement of the inscription, with her half-sister.
