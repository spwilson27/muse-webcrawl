Grammatical conjugation

A verb conjugation table

In linguistics, conjugation is the creation of derived forms of a verb from its principal parts by inflection (alteration of form according to rules of grammar). For instance, the verb break can be conjugated to form the words break, breaks, broke, broken and breaking. Conjugation may be affected by person, number, gender, tense, aspect, mood, voice, case and other grammatical categories.

Verbal agreement

In many languages, verbs change form to agree with their subject in person and number—a process called agreement. In Spanish, for example, hablo means "I speak" and hablan means "they speak", with the ending alone signalling the subject. English retains only limited agreement, such as the third-person singular -s in "she speaks".

Regular and irregular verbs

Verbs that follow a predictable pattern are called regular verbs, while those that do not—such as English go/went or be/was—are irregular. In Latin, verbs are grouped into conjugation classes (traditionally four), each with its own set of endings; similar class systems exist in many languages.

Periphrastic forms

Not all verb forms are produced by inflection alone. Periphrastic (multi-word) forms, such as the English future "will go" or the perfect "has gone", combine auxiliary verbs with participles or infinitives to express categories a language cannot or does not mark on a single verb.
