Executive Office of the President of the United States

The Executive Office of the President of the United States (EOP) is the group of agencies and staff that support the work of the president of the United States. It was created in 1939 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt under the Reorganization Act of 1939, following the recommendation of the Brownlow Committee that "the President needs help." The EOP is headed by the White House chief of staff and today employs roughly 1,800 people.

History

Before 1939, presidents had only a handful of personal staff paid out of general appropriations. The Brownlow Committee (the President's Committee on Administrative Management) concluded in 1937 that the growing scale of the federal government required an institutional presidency. Reorganization Plan No. 1 of 1939 transferred the Bureau of the Budget from the Treasury Department and created the Executive Office, which has since grown to include numerous policy councils and offices.

Principal components

The EOP includes the White House Office (the president's personal and political staff), the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), the National Security Council, the Council of Economic Advisers, the Council on Environmental Quality, the Office of the United States Trade Representative, the Office of Science and Technology Policy, the Office of National Drug Control Policy, and the Office of the Vice President, among others. Some components, such as OMB and the Trade Representative, are headed by Senate-confirmed appointees; most White House Office staff serve at the president's pleasure without confirmation.

Role

The Executive Office coordinates policy development, budgeting, communications, and national security decision-making across the executive branch. Its size and influence have made it a recurring subject of debate about the centralisation of power in the modern presidency.
