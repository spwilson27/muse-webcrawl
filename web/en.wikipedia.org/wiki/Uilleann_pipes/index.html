Uilleann pipes

The uilleann pipes (Irish: píobaí uilleann, "elbow pipes") are the characteristic national bagpipe of Ireland. Earlier known as "union pipes", their current name is a partial translation of the Irish term, referring to the method of blowing the bag by means of a bellows worked by the player's elbow. Unlike the Scottish Great Highland bagpipe, the uilleann pipes are played seated, indoors, with a sweeter and quieter tone and a range of over two octaves.

Components

Air is supplied by a bellows strapped to the waist and pumped by the elbow, inflating a bag held under the other arm. The chanter, played with both hands, has a double reed and a two-octave range including a fully chromatic capability on concert sets. Three drones (tenor, baritone and bass) rest across the player's knee and sound continuous notes, while three regulators — closed pipes fitted with keys — allow the player to produce chords and rhythmic accompaniment with the wrist or fingers.

History

The instrument developed in the early-to-mid eighteenth century, probably from the pastoral pipes, and flourished in the hands of travelling pipers in the nineteenth century. After near-extinction in the mid-twentieth century, interest revived with the Irish folk revival; the organisation Na Píobairí Uilleann was founded in 1968 to promote the instrument. Celebrated players include Leo Rowsome, Willie Clancy, Seamus Ennis, Liam O'Flynn of Planxty, Paddy Moloney of The Chieftains, and Davy Spillane. In 2017 UNESCO inscribed uilleann piping on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
