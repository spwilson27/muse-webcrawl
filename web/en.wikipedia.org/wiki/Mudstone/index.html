Mudstone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mudstone is a fine-grained sedimentary rock lacking the fissility of shale.

Mudstone is a fine-grained sedimentary rock composed predominantly of silt-sized and clay-sized particles (grains smaller than about 0.0625 mm). It is the lithified equivalent of mud. Mudstone is similar in composition to shale but differs in that it lacks fissility — the tendency to split into thin layers along closely spaced laminations — and instead breaks into blocky or massive fragments. In the classification of mudrocks, a rock with more silt is termed a siltstone, one that splits into sheets is a shale, and one that is blocky and composed of mixed silt and clay is a mudstone.

Formation

Mudstones form where currents are weak enough for the finest sediment to settle out of suspension: in floodplains, lake beds, deep ocean floors, deltas, and sheltered lagoons. Over time, burial compacts the mud and expels water, and mineral cements lithify it into rock. Because the grains are so small, mudstones commonly preserve delicate fossils, raindrop impressions, and mudcracks, and they record low-energy depositional environments.

Properties and significance

Mudstone is typically dull, soft, and coloured grey, brown, red, or green depending on its mineralogy and the oxidation state of its iron. It has very low permeability, so thick mudstone units act as seals over oil and gas reservoirs and as barriers to groundwater flow; organic-rich mudstones (black shales and mudstones) are the source rocks for most petroleum, and with hydraulic fracturing they can themselves serve as unconventional reservoirs.

In carbonate classification (Dunham), "mudstone" also denotes a limestone texture in which carbonate mud supports less than 10 percent grains — a usage unrelated to the siliciclastic rock described here.
