Allied invasion of Sicily

The Allied invasion of Sicily, codenamed Operation Husky, was a major campaign of the Second World War in which the Allied forces invaded the island of Sicily in July 1943 and took it from the Axis powers (Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany). It began the Italian campaign, helped precipitate the fall of Mussolini, and was at the time the largest amphibious operation of the war.

Background and deception

After victory in North Africa, the Allies chose Sicily as the next objective at the Casablanca Conference. An elaborate deception, Operation Mincemeat, planted false documents on a corpse washed ashore in Spain suggesting the targets would be Greece and Sardinia, diverting German forces from Sicily.

The landings

On the night of 9–10 July 1943, American and British airborne troops dropped ahead of the seaborne assault, suffering heavy losses from wind and friendly fire, including the disastrous glider operation at the Ponte Grande. At dawn some 160,000 troops of Patton's U.S. Seventh Army and Montgomery's British Eighth Army came ashore along the south and southeast coasts, supported by the largest armada yet assembled.

The campaign

The two armies raced to Messina in the northeast: Montgomery advanced slowly against stiff resistance around Mount Etna while Patton swept rapidly across the island and took Palermo before turning east. Axis forces conducted a skilful evacuation across the Strait of Messina, ferrying some 100,000 troops and much equipment to the mainland before the Allies entered Messina on 17 August.

Consequences

The loss of Sicily toppled Mussolini on 25 July 1943; Italy signed an armistice in September, and the Allies invaded the Italian mainland, beginning a long and costly campaign up the peninsula.
