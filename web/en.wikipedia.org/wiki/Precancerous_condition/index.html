Precancerous condition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A precancerous condition (or premalignant condition) is a condition, lesion, or disease state that, if left untreated, may develop into cancer. Precancerous changes are abnormal cells whose appearance and growth pattern suggest an increased risk of malignancy, but which have not yet acquired the ability to invade surrounding tissue or metastasize. Not every precancerous lesion progresses to cancer; many remain stable or regress.

Examples

  • Actinic keratosis — rough, scaly patches on sun-damaged skin that can precede squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Barrett's esophagus — replacement of the normal esophageal lining by intestinal-type epithelium, associated with esophageal adenocarcinoma.
  • Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia — dysplasia of the cervix, usually driven by persistent human papillomavirus infection and detected by screening.
  • Colorectal adenomas — benign polyps a subset of which transform over years into colorectal cancer; their removal at colonoscopy prevents cancer.
  • Ductal carcinoma in situ — abnormal cells confined to the milk ducts of the breast.
  • Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) — a plasma-cell condition that can progress to multiple myeloma.

Detection and management

Management depends on the lesion and its estimated risk: options range from surveillance ("watchful waiting") with periodic re-examination, to local ablation, chemoprevention, or surgical excision. Population screening programmes — cervical cytology, HPV testing, mammography, and colorectal screening — are largely aimed at detecting and treating precancerous states before invasion occurs.
