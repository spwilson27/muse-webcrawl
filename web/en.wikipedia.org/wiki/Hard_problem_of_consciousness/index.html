Hard problem of consciousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The hard problem asks why physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective experience at all.

The hard problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience — why there is "something it is like" to be a conscious organism. The term was coined by the philosopher David Chalmers in 1995, contrasting it with the "easy problems" of consciousness, such as explaining how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, controls behaviour and reports mental states.

Formulation

Chalmers argued that even a complete physical account of brain function would leave unanswered the question of why those functions are accompanied by experience. Easy problems, however difficult in practice, concern the performance of functions and so are in principle solvable by standard neuroscientific methods. The hard problem, by contrast, concerns qualia: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain.

"Why is it that when our cognitive systems engage in visual and auditory information-processing, we have visual or auditory experience: the quality of deep blue, the sensation of middle C? ... Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all?" — David Chalmers, Facing up to the problem of consciousness (1995)

Related arguments

  • Explanatory gap (Joseph Levine, 1983): even if an identity between a mental and a physical state is true, it may remain unexplained why that state feels the way it does.
  • Mary the colour scientist (Frank Jackson, 1982): a scientist who knows all physical facts about colour vision but has never seen colour seems to learn something new on first seeing red.
  • Philosophical zombies: beings physically identical to conscious humans but lacking experience appear conceivable, suggesting consciousness is not logically entailed by the physical facts.
  • What is it like to be a bat? (Thomas Nagel, 1974): subjective character of experience is inaccessible to purely objective physical description.

Responses

Positions divide broadly into dualist and physicalist camps. Chalmers himself defends a form of naturalistic dualism (property dualism), treating experience as a fundamental feature of the world. Other responses include panpsychism, illusionism (Keith Frankish), eliminative materialism, and Daniel Dennett's denial that the problem is well posed. Integrated information theory and global workspace theory are among the scientific theories of consciousness sometimes claimed to address it. Critics such as Patricia Churchland have argued that the problem may dissolve as neuroscience matures, much as "vital force" puzzles dissolved with biochemistry.
