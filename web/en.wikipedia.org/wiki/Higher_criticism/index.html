Higher criticism

Higher criticism, also known as the historical-critical method, is the scholarly study of ancient texts — above all the Bible — that seeks to establish when, where, by whom and from what sources a text was written, as opposed to "lower criticism" (textual criticism), which aims to reconstruct the original wording of a text from its manuscripts. Higher criticism investigates the origins, authorship, dating and composition history of biblical books.

Development

The method developed in German universities in the 18th and 19th centuries, drawing on the work of scholars such as Johann Salomo Semler, Johann Gottfried Eichhorn (who popularised the term), and Julius Wellhausen, whose documentary hypothesis proposed that the Pentateuch was compiled from four distinguishable sources (J, E, D and P). Source criticism, form criticism (associated with Hermann Gunkel) and redaction criticism became its principal sub-disciplines.

Reception

Higher criticism transformed biblical scholarship and modern theology but was controversial: conservative churches long resisted its conclusions about Mosaic authorship and the historical reliability of scripture, while the Roman Catholic Church, after early opposition, cautiously accepted historical-critical methods during the 20th century, notably with the encyclical Divino afflante Spiritu (1943) and the Second Vatican Council. The approach is now standard in academic biblical studies.
