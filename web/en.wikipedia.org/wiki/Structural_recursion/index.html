Structural recursion

In computer science, structural recursion is a method of recursion where the recursive call is made on a structurally smaller piece of the input — a subterm of the original data, such as the tail of a list or a subtree of a tree. Because the data consumed is finite and each call operates on a strictly smaller component, functions defined by structural recursion are guaranteed to terminate, provided their other operations terminate.

Examples

Computing the length of a list is structurally recursive: the length of an empty list is zero, and the length of a list with head x and tail xs is one plus the length of xs. Similarly, summing the nodes of a binary tree recurses on the left and right subtrees. These definitions mirror the inductive definition of the data type itself, and in functional programming they correspond to the fold or catamorphism pattern.

Relation to primitive recursion

Structural recursion is closely related to primitive recursion on the natural numbers, where the recursive call is on the immediate predecessor. Languages and proof assistants that enforce totality — such as Agda and Coq — accept recursive definitions that are structurally decreasing, allowing them to guarantee that every well-typed program terminates.

Limitations

Some natural algorithms do not obviously recurse on direct subcomponents: quicksort recurses on partitions of its input, and the Ackermann function recurses with arguments that are smaller only in a lexicographic sense. Such programs require more general termination arguments, such as well-founded recursion, and fall outside the simplest form of structural recursion.
