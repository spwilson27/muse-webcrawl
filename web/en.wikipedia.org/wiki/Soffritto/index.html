Soffritto

Soffritto (Italian, literally "under-fried") is a base of finely chopped aromatic vegetables gently cooked in oil or butter, used to flavour a wide range of Italian dishes — soups, sauces, braises, risottos and ragùs. The classic Italian soffritto consists of onion, carrot and celery, roughly in the proportions of the French mirepoix, sometimes with garlic, parsley, pancetta or prosciutto added according to the dish and region. The vegetables are cooked slowly over low heat until soft and sweet, without browning aggressively, forming the flavour foundation (called battuto when raw) to which other ingredients are added.

Variations and relatives

Regional Italian cooking adjusts the base freely: Tuscan cooks may add parsley; southern recipes lean on olive oil and sometimes chilli; pancetta enriches many northern ragùs. The related Spanish and Latin American sofrito — onion, garlic, peppers and tomato — travelled with Spanish cooking to the Philippines (gisa) and the Caribbean, and is a cornerstone of those cuisines. Similar aromatic bases occur worldwide: French mirepoix, German Suppengrün (leek, carrot, celeriac), the Cajun and Creole "holy trinity" (onion, celery, bell pepper), and the Chinese and Korean combinations of ginger, garlic and scallion.

Technique

The vegetables are diced very fine so they nearly melt into the finished dish. Cooking is slow and gentle — ten to twenty minutes over low heat — to draw out sweetness through softening and light caramelisation rather than frying; the cookbooks of Pellegrino Artusi and later Marcella Hazan codified the soffritto as the obligatory opening step of countless Italian recipes, from ragù alla bolognese to minestrone.
