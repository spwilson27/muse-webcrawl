Dye laser

Dye laser
Gain mediumOrganic dye solution (e.g. rhodamine 6G)
Invented1965–1966 (Sorokin; Schäfer)
Typical range~300–1200 nm across many dyes
Key propertyBroadly tunable wavelength

A dye laser is a laser that uses an organic dye dissolved in a solvent as its lasing medium. Dye lasers were invented independently in 1966 by Peter Sorokin at IBM and by Fritz Schäfer and colleagues in Germany. Their defining feature is very wide tunability: by choosing the dye and adjusting the resonator optics, a single device can be tuned continuously over tens of nanometres, and the family of available dyes covers the ultraviolet through the near infrared.

Construction and operation

The dye solution is pumped optically, either by flashlamps or by another laser such as a frequency-doubled Nd:YAG or an argon-ion laser. Because dyes degrade under intense pumping, the solution is usually circulated rapidly through the laser cavity as a flowing jet or cuvette. A diffraction grating or prism in the cavity selects the operating wavelength, allowing fine tuning.

Applications

Tunability made dye lasers workhorses of spectroscopy and laser isotope separation, and they were used in astronomy as guide stars and in medicine for treating vascular skin lesions and in photodynamic therapy. Ring dye lasers produced some of the earliest ultrashort pulses. Since the 1990s, many applications have shifted to titanium–sapphire and other solid-state or diode systems, which avoid messy dye handling, but dye lasers remain in use where their tuning range and pulse characteristics are needed.
