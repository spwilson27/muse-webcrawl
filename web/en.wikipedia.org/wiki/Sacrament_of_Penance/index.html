Sacrament of Penance

The Sacrament of Penance (also commonly called the Sacrament of Reconciliation, the Sacrament of Confession, or simply Confession) is one of the seven sacraments of the Catholic Church and is also recognised, in varying forms, by the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican traditions. Through it, Christians obtain absolution for sins committed after baptism, through confession to a priest, contrition, and the priest's words of absolution.

History

In the early Church, grave sins were confessed publicly before the congregation, followed by a period of public penance before reconciliation. From about the 7th century, Irish monastic practice spread private, repeatable confession to a priest, accompanied by penances prescribed in penitential handbooks. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required every Christian to confess at least once a year. The Council of Trent (1551) defined the sacrament doctrinally against Reformation critiques, and the Rituale Romanum of 1614 standardised its celebration; the modern rite was revised after the Second Vatican Council in 1973.

Catholic practice

The penitent examines their conscience, confesses mortal sins in kind and number, expresses contrition (sorrow for sin with resolve to amend), receives a penance (prayers or good works), and is absolved by the priest acting in persona Christi. The seal of confession absolutely forbids the priest from revealing anything heard. Catholic teaching holds that sacramental confession is necessary for the forgiveness of mortal sins, and the rite may be celebrated individually, in communal services with individual confession, or (only in emergencies) with general absolution.

Other traditions

Eastern Orthodoxy practises confession before a priest, often with the penitent standing before an icon, and treats it as a mystery of healing. Lutheran churches retain private confession and absolution as a practice commended in the Augsburg Confession. Anglican practice permits auricular confession under the maxim "all may, some should, none must".
