Endocrinology

Endocrinology (from Greek endon, "within", and krīnō, "to separate", plus -logia) is a branch of biology and medicine dealing with the endocrine system, its diseases, and its specific secretions known as hormones. It is also concerned with the integration of developmental events such as proliferation, growth and differentiation, and with the psychological and behavioural activities of metabolism, growth and development, tissue function, sleep, digestion, respiration, excretion, mood, stress, lactation, movement, reproduction and sensory perception when these are caused by hormones.

The principal endocrine glands: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.

The endocrine system

The major glands of the human endocrine system include the pituitary gland (often called the "master gland"), the thyroid and parathyroid glands, the adrenal glands, the pancreatic islets of Langerhans, and the gonads (ovaries and testes), coordinated by the hypothalamus. Hormones — peptide, steroid and amino-acid-derived chemical messengers — travel through the bloodstream to target organs, where feedback loops, such as the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, maintain homeostasis.

History

The field is often traced to 1849, when Arnold Berthold showed that transplanting testes in castrated roosters prevented caponisation, implying a blood-borne signal. In 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling identified secretin, and Starling coined the word "hormone" in 1905. Landmark twentieth-century advances included the isolation of insulin by Banting, Best, Macleod and Collip in 1921–22, which transformed type 1 diabetes from a fatal disease into a manageable one.

Scope of practice

Endocrinologists diagnose and manage hormone imbalances and the conditions they cause: diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, thyroid cancer, Cushing's syndrome, Addison's disease, acromegaly, polycystic ovary syndrome, disorders of calcium and bone including osteoporosis, and tumours of endocrine glands. Diagnosis typically relies on measuring hormone levels in blood or urine, often with dynamic stimulation or suppression testing, combined with imaging.

Professional practice

In most countries endocrinology is a subspecialty of internal medicine (or of paediatrics for childhood disorders), requiring fellowship training after residency. Professional bodies include the Endocrine Society (founded 1916) and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists; leading journals include the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism.
