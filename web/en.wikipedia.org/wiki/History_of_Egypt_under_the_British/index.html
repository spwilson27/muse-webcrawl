History of Egypt under the British

The history of Egypt under the British covers the period from the British occupation of Egypt in 1882 until the departure of the last British troops following the Suez Crisis of 1956. Although Egypt was never formally annexed and remained nominally an Ottoman province until 1914 and a kingdom from 1922, British officials exercised decisive influence over its government, finances and foreign policy for most of this period.

The occupation of 1882

Egypt under Khedive Isma'il had accumulated enormous debts to European creditors, and a system of Anglo-French financial control followed. The nationalist 'Urabi revolt of 1881–1882 threatened European interests and the Suez Canal route to India. In July 1882 Britain bombarded Alexandria and defeated 'Urabi's army at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir, beginning an occupation that was repeatedly described as temporary but lasted decades.

The veiled protectorate

From 1883 to 1907 Egypt was governed in practice by the British consul-general, Lord Cromer, under what historians call a "veiled protectorate". The administration balanced the budget, expanded irrigation, built the first Aswan Dam (1902) and developed cotton exports, while Egyptian nationalism grew in response to incidents such as the Dinshaway affair of 1906.

Protectorate and nominal independence

When the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War in 1914, Britain declared Egypt a formal protectorate and deposed Khedive Abbas II. The wartime burdens imposed on Egypt fuelled the Egyptian revolution of 1919, led by Saad Zaghloul's Wafd Party. In 1922 Britain unilaterally declared Egyptian independence, reserving control over defence, imperial communications (the Suez Canal), foreign interests and the Sudan. The Anglo-Egyptian treaty of 1936 allowed British troops to remain in the Canal Zone.

The road to 1956

During the Second World War Egypt was a major Allied base. In February 1942 British forces surrounded Abdeen Palace to compel King Farouk to appoint a pro-Allied government, an incident that gravely damaged the monarchy's legitimacy. Growing nationalist pressure led to the abrogation of the 1936 treaty in 1951 and guerrilla conflict in the Canal Zone. The Egyptian revolution of 1952 overthrew the monarchy, and the 1954 Anglo-Egyptian agreement provided for the withdrawal of British troops, completed in June 1956. When Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal weeks later, Britain joined France and Israel in the Suez Crisis invasion, whose failure under American and Soviet pressure marked the end of British power in Egypt.
