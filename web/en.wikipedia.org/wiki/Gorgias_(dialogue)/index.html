Gorgias (dialogue)

Gorgias
AuthorPlato
Writtenc. 380 BC

SubjectRhetoric, justice, the good life

Gorgias is a Socratic dialogue written by the Greek philosopher Plato around 380 BC. In it, Socrates debates the nature of rhetoric and justice with the celebrated sophist Gorgias of Leontini, Gorgias's student Polus, and the Athenian politician Callicles. It is among Plato's longest and most impassioned dialogues, and contains the starkest confrontation in the corpus between Socratic ethics and the doctrine that might makes right.

Summary

Socrates first presses Gorgias to define rhetoric; when Gorgias calls it the art of persuasion in law courts and assemblies, Socrates argues that rhetoric, as commonly practised, is not an art (technē) at all but a knack of flattery — the counterfeit counterpart of cookery, as cookery is the counterfeit of medicine — producing pleasure rather than genuine good. Against Polus, Socrates defends the paradoxical theses that it is better to suffer injustice than to commit it, and that the unjust person is happiest when justly punished. Finally, Callicles champions natural justice — the rule of the stronger — and a life of maximal appetite; Socrates answers with the image of the ordered, temperate soul and closes with a myth of the judgement of the dead.

Themes and influence

The dialogue explores the distinction between persuasion and knowledge, between doing what seems best and what is good, and between power and genuine freedom. Its portrayal of Callicles has been read as a precursor of later immoralist and realist critiques of morality, and its critique of rhetoric remains central to debates about democratic politics, expertise and demagoguery.
