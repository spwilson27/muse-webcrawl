Harmattan

The harmattan is a season in West Africa that occurs between the end of November and the middle of March. It is characterised by a dry and dusty northeasterly trade wind, of the same name, which blows from the Sahara over West Africa into the Gulf of Guinea. The name derives from the Twi language of Ghana.

Characteristics

During the harmattan, temperatures in much of West Africa can drop to around 9 °C (48 °F) at night, while daytime temperatures may still rise above 30 °C (86 °F). Humidity falls dramatically, sometimes below 10 percent. The wind carries large quantities of fine Saharan dust and sand, which can severely reduce visibility, ground aircraft, and obscure the sun for days at a time, producing a heavy haze.

Effects

The dry air increases the risk of fire outbreaks and can damage crops, while the dust aggravates respiratory illnesses and eye irritation. In some regions the harmattan coincides with epidemics of meningococcal meningitis, associated with the dry, dusty conditions of the African "meningitis belt". Rivers and streams may dry up, trees shed their leaves, and the season marks a transition between the wet season and the following hot, dry months.

Conversely, the harmattan brings relief from the intense heat and humidity of the rainy season, and its cool nights are welcomed by many residents. The season is also significant for aviation and road transport, as dust haze frequently forces flight cancellations and delays across the region.
