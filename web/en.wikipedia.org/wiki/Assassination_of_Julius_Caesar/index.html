The assassination of Julius Caesar took place on 15 March 44 BC — the Ides of March — when a group of as many as sixty Roman senators calling themselves the Liberators stabbed the dictator to death at a meeting of the Senate in the Curia of Pompey in Rome. The conspiracy was led by Marcus Junius Brutus and Gaius Cassius Longinus, both former supporters of Pompey whom Caesar had pardoned.

Background

Caesar had won the civil war against Pompey and by 44 BC held overwhelming power: dictator for ten years, then dictator perpetuo (for life), with control of appointments and honours that suggested monarchy to a Senate jealous of the Republic's traditions. Fears that he intended to make himself king, sharpened by incidents such as the diadem episode at the Lupercalia, drove a broad group of senators to conspire.

The attack

On the Ides, Caesar was petitioned by Tillius Cimber, who seized his toga; Casca struck the first blow, and the conspirators closed in. Suetonius reports 23 wounds; Caesar fell dead at the base of Pompey's statue. According to tradition his last words to Brutus were rendered by Shakespeare as "Et tu, Brute?" — Suetonius instead records the Greek "Kai su, teknon?" ("You too, child?"), if he spoke at all.

Aftermath

The Liberators failed to restore the Republic. Mark Antony's funeral oration turned the crowd against them, and Caesar's heir Octavian, with Antony and Lepidus, formed the Second Triumvirate, proscribed their enemies and defeated Brutus and Cassius at Philippi in 42 BC. The ensuing civil wars ended the Republic and led, under Octavian (Augustus), to the Roman Empire.
