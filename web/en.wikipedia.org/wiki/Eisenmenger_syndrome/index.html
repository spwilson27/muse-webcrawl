Eisenmenger syndrome

Eisenmenger syndrome

Specialty: Cardiology

Cause: Longstanding left-to-right cardiac shunt causing pulmonary vascular disease

Signs: Cyanosis, clubbing, polycythaemia, breathlessness

Named after: Viktor Eisenmenger

Eisenmenger syndrome or Eisenmenger's syndrome is defined as the process in which a long-standing left-to-right cardiac shunt caused by a congenital heart defect (typically a ventricular septal defect, atrial septal defect or patent ductus arteriosus) causes pulmonary hypertension and eventual reversal of the shunt into a cyanotic right-to-left shunt.

Pathophysiology

Years of increased blood flow and pressure in the pulmonary circulation cause irreversible remodelling and thickening of the pulmonary arteries. As pulmonary vascular resistance rises to approach or exceed systemic levels, the direction of shunting reverses: deoxygenated blood bypasses the lungs and enters the systemic circulation, producing cyanosis. Because the pulmonary vascular changes are largely irreversible, surgical closure of the defect is contraindicated once Eisenmenger physiology is established.

Signs and symptoms

Features include central cyanosis, digital clubbing, increased red cell mass (polycythaemia), breathlessness, fatigue, syncope, haemoptysis and a high risk of arrhythmia and sudden death. Complications include paradoxical embolism, cerebral abscess, gout and bleeding disorders caused by abnormal platelets.

Treatment and prognosis

Management avoids interventions that lower systemic vascular resistance or complicate the physiology, and includes pulmonary vasodilator therapies such as endothelin receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors and prostacyclins, which have improved exercise capacity and survival. Definitive treatment is heart–lung transplantation or lung transplantation with repair of the defect. The condition is named for the Austrian physician Viktor Eisenmenger, who described a case in 1897; the modern definition derives from the work of Paul Wood in the 1950s.
