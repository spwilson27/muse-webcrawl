Louis Riel (22 October 1844 – 16 November 1885) was a Canadian politician, a founder of the province of Manitoba, and a political leader of the Métis people. He led two resistance movements against the Government of Canada and its first prime minister, John A. Macdonald: the Red River Resistance of 1869–1870 and the North-West Rebellion of 1885.

Red River Resistance

When the Hudson's Bay Company transferred Rupert's Land to Canada in 1869 without consulting its inhabitants, Riel led the Métis of the Red River Settlement in forming a provisional government, which negotiated the Manitoba Act and the entry of Manitoba into Confederation in 1870 with guarantees for Métis land and French-language rights. The execution of the Ontario Orangeman Thomas Scott by Riel's government made Riel a fugitive; he was nevertheless elected three times to the House of Commons in Ottawa, though he never took his seat, and was formally exiled in 1875.

North-West Rebellion and execution

Living in Montana, Riel was invited back in 1884 to lead Métis grievances in Saskatchewan. The movement escalated into armed rebellion in March 1885; after the Métis defeat at Batoche, Riel surrendered and was tried for high treason in Regina. Despite a jury recommendation for mercy and doubts about his mental state, he was hanged on 16 November 1885. His execution polarised Canada between French and English speakers and remains controversial; today Riel is widely honoured as a founder of Manitoba, where Louis Riel Day is a provincial holiday.
