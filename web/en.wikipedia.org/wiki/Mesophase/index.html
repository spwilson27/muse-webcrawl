Mesophase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry and chemical physics, a mesophase is a state of matter intermediate between liquid and solid. The term is most familiar from liquid crystals: a mesophase is a phase in which the molecules retain orientational order (and sometimes partial positional order) while losing the full three-dimensional positional order of a crystal, so that the material flows like a liquid yet exhibits anisotropic physical properties.

Liquid crystal mesophases

Georges Friedel's 1922 classification divided liquid crystal mesophases into three principal types:

  • Nematic: molecules have long-range orientational order but no positional order — the least ordered mesophase; a chiral variant forms the cholesteric (chiral nematic) phase used in displays and thermometers.
  • Smectic: molecules are arranged in layers, with orientational order and one-dimensional positional order; many sub-phases (SmA, SmC, etc.) are distinguished by tilt and in-layer structure.
  • Columnar (discotic): disc-shaped molecules stack into columns arranged on a two-dimensional lattice.

Mesophases may be thermotropic (appearing between temperatures) or lyotropic (appearing in solution at particular concentrations, as in soap–water systems and many biological membranes).

Other uses

In carbon science, "mesophase pitch" is the liquid-crystalline intermediate formed when pitch is heated, whose spherical mesophase droplets coalesce before solidifying; mesophase pitch is the precursor of high-modulus carbon fibres and mesocarbon microbeads. In metallurgy and food science the term mesophase is also used loosely for structurally intermediate phases in gels and emulsions.
