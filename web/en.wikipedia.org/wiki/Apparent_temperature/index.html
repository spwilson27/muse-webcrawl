Apparent temperature

Apparent temperature is the temperature equivalent perceived by the human body, caused by the combined effects of air temperature, humidity, and wind speed. It is commonly reported as the heat index in hot weather ("feels like" hotter) and as wind chill in cold weather ("feels like" colder). The concept was developed by R. G. Steadman in the late 1970s.

Heat index

When humidity is high, sweat evaporates less efficiently, reducing the body's ability to cool itself; the heat index quantifies this effect. The U.S. National Weather Service issues advisories and warnings based on heat index values, which above about 41 °C (105 °F) are considered dangerous for prolonged exposure.

Wind chill

In cold, windy conditions, moving air strips away the thin insulating layer of warm air at the skin, making exposed skin feel colder than the measured temperature and increasing the risk of frostbite. The modern wind chill formula, adopted in North America in 2001, is based on experiments by Paul Siple and Charles Passel in Antarctica and later human trials.

Unified indices

The Australian Bureau of Meteorology uses a Steadman-based apparent temperature year-round, and the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) attempts a more complete physiological model incorporating clothing and activity.
