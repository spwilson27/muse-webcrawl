Thundersnow

Thundersnow, also known as a winter thunderstorm or a thundersnowstorm, is a thunderstorm in which snow falls as the primary precipitation instead of rain. It is considered a rare phenomenon, associated mostly with lake-effect snow, although it can also occur in other winter storm types such as blizzards and nor'easters.

Formation

Thundersnow forms when the same ingredients needed for an ordinary thunderstorm — moisture, instability, and a lifting mechanism — occur within a winter storm system. Unlike most thunderstorms, which are driven by surface heating, thundersnow develops when temperatures throughout much of the atmosphere are below freezing and convective instability exists in a shallow layer, often within intense snow bands. The charge separation that produces lightning occurs in the mixed-phase region of the cloud, where supercooled water droplets, ice crystals, and graupel collide.

Occurrence

Thundersnow is most frequently reported in the Great Lakes region of the United States and Canada, where cold air passing over relatively warm lake water generates lake-effect snow bands with strong convective elements. It also occurs along the Wasatch Front in Utah near the Great Salt Lake, on the Japanese island of Honshu along the Sea of Japan, and in the United Kingdom and Ireland during cold polar maritime outbreaks. Ocean-effect snow events in New England can also produce it.

Characteristics

Lightning in thundersnow events is typically less frequent than in summertime thunderstorms, and the snow acts to muffle the sound of thunder, so it can usually only be heard within about two to three miles (3–5 km) of the lightning strike. Snowfall rates during thundersnow are often very heavy, sometimes exceeding 2 inches (5 cm) per hour, because the convection enhances precipitation production. Meteorologists sometimes use the presence of thundersnow as an indicator that a storm is producing its heaviest snowfall rates.

Research

Thundersnow has been studied with instrumented aircraft and radar to understand charge generation in winter clouds. Research flights into lake-effect storms off Lake Ontario found that cloud-top temperatures and the depth of the convective layer strongly influence whether lightning occurs.
