Thyroid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thyroid
Location: Front of the neck, below the Adam's apple
Hormones: Thyroxine (T4), triiodothyronine (T3), calcitonin
Control: TSH from the pituitary gland

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped endocrine gland in the front of the neck, wrapping the trachea below the larynx. It produces the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which regulate the body's metabolic rate, growth and development, and calcitonin, which modestly lowers blood calcium. Despite its roughly 20-gram size, it is among the most clinically important glands of the endocrine system.

Function and iodine

Thyroid hormones require iodine; deficiency causes the gland to swell (goitre) and, in pregnancy, can impair the child's brain development — historically the world's leading preventable cause of intellectual disability, largely solved by iodised salt. The pituitary's thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) controls output by negative feedback.

Disorders

Underactivity (hypothyroidism, often autoimmune Hashimoto's disease) causes fatigue, weight gain and cold intolerance, and is treated with daily levothyroxine. Overactivity (hyperthyroidism, often Graves' disease) causes weight loss, tremor, heat intolerance and bulging eyes, and is treated with antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine or surgery. Thyroid nodules are extremely common and usually benign; thyroid cancers have an excellent prognosis overall.
