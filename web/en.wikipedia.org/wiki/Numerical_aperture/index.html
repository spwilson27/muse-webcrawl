Numerical aperture

Numerical aperture
DefinitionNA = n sin θ
DimensionDimensionless
Typical range0.10–0.95 (air); up to ~1.6 (oil immersion)

In optics, the numerical aperture (NA) of an optical system is a dimensionless number that characterizes the range of angles over which the system can accept or emit light. By incorporating the refractive index of the medium, its definition is

NA = n sin θ

where n is the refractive index of the medium in which the lens is working (1.00 for air, about 1.33 for water, 1.51 for immersion oil), and θ is the maximal half-angle of the cone of light that can enter or exit the lens. In most areas of optics — and especially in microscopy — the term is used specifically for the cone of light accepted by an objective from a specimen point.

Microscopy

Numerical aperture determines the resolving power of a microscope objective. The smallest resolvable distance is approximately d = λ / (2 NA) (the Abbe limit), so higher NA objectives resolve finer detail. It also governs light-gathering ability and depth of field: brightness rises with NA² while depth of field falls. Because sin θ cannot exceed 1, dry objectives are limited to NA below 1.0 in theory and about 0.95 in practice. Immersion fluids between the cover glass and the front lens raise n, allowing NAs up to roughly 1.4–1.6.

Optical fibres

For a step-index optical fibre, the numerical aperture is set by the refractive indices of core (n1) and cladding (n2): NA = √(n1² − n2²). It defines the acceptance cone: light entering within this cone undergoes total internal reflection and propagates along the fibre. Single-mode fibres have NA around 0.10–0.14, while multimode fibres may exceed 0.2.

Relation to f-number

For a lens focused at infinity in air, NA ≈ 1 / (2 N), where N is the f-number. The approximation breaks down for high-aperture lenses, where the exact relation NA = 1 / √(1 + 4N²)·½-style corrections or the sine form must be used. In laser physics, the NA of a beam describes its divergence, and matched numerical apertures are required for efficient coupling into fibres.
