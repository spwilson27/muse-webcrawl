A supermax prison (short for super-maximum security) is a highly restrictive, high-security prison (or a unit within a prison) designed to house prisoners considered to pose the greatest threats to security, escape risk, or danger to staff and other prisoners. Inmates are typically confined to single cells for 22–24 hours a day under constant surveillance, with severely limited contact with other people, minimal programming, and movement only under restraint and escort.

History

The modern supermax emerged in the United States. After two correctional officers were murdered at the United States Penitentiary in Marion, Illinois, in 1983, that prison was placed on permanent lockdown, becoming the model for a generation of facilities. California opened Pelican Bay State Prison's Security Housing Unit in 1989, and in 1994 the federal government opened ADX Florence in Colorado — the only purpose-built federal supermax — housing notorious offenders including terrorists, cartel leaders and serial killers. By the early 2000s dozens of US states operated supermax prisons or units, holding tens of thousands of prisoners in isolation conditions; similar regimes exist in other countries, often under different names.

Conditions and operation

Cells are typically small, with solid steel doors, a concrete or steel bed, combined sink-toilet fixtures and a small shower in some designs. Meals arrive through slots; exercise takes place alone in walled yards or indoor rooms; visits are usually non-contact through glass or by video. Prisoners may earn gradual step-down privileges for good behaviour. Placement is administrative rather than part of the judicial sentence, and stays can last years or decades.

Criticism and reform

Human rights organisations, UN rapporteurs and medical bodies have criticised prolonged solitary confinement in supermax units as cruel and psychologically damaging, citing high rates of mental illness among isolated prisoners; the UN's Mandela Rules treat solitary confinement beyond 15 days as prohibited prolonged isolation. Litigation and reform efforts in the 2010s reduced some US supermax populations, including California's hunger-strike-driven settlement ending indeterminate SHU terms based solely on gang validation. Supporters argue the facilities are necessary to manage violent prisoners and protect staff.
