Guns, Germs, and Steel

Guns, Germs, and Steel
Author: Jared Diamond
Published: 1997
Subject: Geography, history, anthropology
Award: Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction (1998)

Guns, Germs, and Steel: The Fates of Human Societies is a 1997 transdisciplinary nonfiction book by American scientist and historian Jared Diamond. In 1998 it won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction and the Aventis Prize for Best Science Book. A documentary series based on the book was produced by the National Geographic Society in 2005.

Thesis

Diamond's central claim is that differences in power and technology between human societies originate primarily in environmental differences — geography, climate, and the availability of domesticable plants and animals — rather than in any biological superiority of particular peoples. The book opens with "Yali's question," posed by a New Guinean politician: "Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?"

Diamond argues that Eurasia's east–west axis allowed crops and livestock to spread at similar latitudes; that the Fertile Crescent's rich endowment of large-seeded grasses and domesticable mammals (sheep, goats, cattle, pigs, horses) enabled early farming, food surpluses, dense populations, and specialised societies; and that close contact with livestock gave Eurasians exposure to — and eventually resistance against — germs that devastated indigenous American, Australian, and Pacific peoples upon contact. Food production, in turn, supported writing, centralised states, and steel technology.

Reception and criticism

The book was highly influential in popularising geographical explanations of global inequality and was praised for synthesising archaeology, linguistics, epidemiology, and biogeography. Academic critics, including anthropologists and geographers, have questioned its environmental determinism, its treatment of political institutions and culture, and details of its account of China and other regions. Diamond returned to related themes in Collapse (2005) and The World Until Yesterday (2012).
