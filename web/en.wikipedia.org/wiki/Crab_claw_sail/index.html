Crab claw sail


Crab claw sail on a proa

The crab claw sail is a fore-and-aft triangular sail with spars along both the upper and lower edges. The sail is used by the Austronesian peoples of Maritime Southeast Asia and the Pacific, most famously on the Pacific proa, and is one of the oldest fore-and-aft rigs in the world. The two curved spars meet at the forward lower corner, giving the sail a shape likened to a crab's claw when filled by the wind.

Performance

Wind-tunnel and on-water studies, notably by C. A. Marchaj, found the crab claw to be an efficient low-aspect-ratio rig, generating useful lift and vortex lift from its curved spars at angles of attack where conventional sails stall. Its efficiency at low wind speeds and its ability to spill wind by easing the sheet contributed to the remarkable voyaging performance of Pacific canoes.

Distribution and variants

Related forms include the Oceanic lateen and the tanja sail of Indonesia. Crab claw sails remain in everyday use on fishing craft in Madagascar, the Philippines, and Pacific islands, and have been revived on experimental multihulls and voyaging canoes such as the Hōkūleʻa, whose sails follow the traditional Hawaiian form.
