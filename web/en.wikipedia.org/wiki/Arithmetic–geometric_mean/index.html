Arithmetic–geometric mean

The two sequences defining the arithmetic–geometric mean converge very rapidly to a common limit.

In mathematics, the arithmetic–geometric mean of two nonnegative real numbers x and y is the mutual limit of two sequences: the arithmetic means and the geometric means computed iteratively from the pair. It is denoted agm(x, y) or M(x, y).

Definition

Starting from a0 = x and g0 = y, define the sequences:

an+1 = (an + gn) / 2,    gn+1 = √(an gn)

Both sequences converge to the same number, the arithmetic–geometric mean of x and y. The convergence is extremely fast — roughly quadratic — which makes the mean useful in numerical computation.

History

The arithmetic–geometric mean was studied by Carl Friedrich Gauss, who connected it to elliptic integrals. Gauss observed that the complete elliptic integral of the first kind can be expressed through the mean: K(k) = π / (2 agm(1, √(1−k²))). Earlier, Lagrange and Legendre had investigated related iterations.

Properties and applications

The arithmetic–geometric mean lies between the geometric and arithmetic means of the two numbers. It is homogeneous: agm(cx, cy) = c·agm(x, y) for c > 0. Algorithms based on the mean, such as the Brent–Salamin algorithm, compute digits of π very efficiently. Variants of the iteration, including Gauss's arithmetic–geometric mean for complex numbers, play roles in the theory of elliptic functions and theta functions.
