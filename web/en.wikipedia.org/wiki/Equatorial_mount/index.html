Equatorial mount

An equatorial mount is a mount for instruments that compensates for Earth's rotation by having one rotational axis, the polar axis, parallel to the Earth's axis of rotation. This allows a telescope fixed on the mount to follow the diurnal motion of the celestial sphere by rotating on that single axis at a constant rate. Equatorial mounts are the standard choice for serious astronomical observation and astrophotography, in contrast to the simpler altazimuth mount.

Design

The two axes of an equatorial mount are the right ascension (or polar) axis, aligned with the celestial pole, and the declination axis, perpendicular to it. Once the polar axis is aimed at the north (or south) celestial pole, an object is kept centred by driving the right ascension axis westward at the sidereal rate, traditionally by clockwork and now usually by electric motors.

Types

  • The German equatorial mount, in which the telescope sits on one side of the polar axis and is balanced by a counterweight on the other, is the most common design for small and medium telescopes.
  • The fork mount supports the telescope between the tines of a fork on a polar axis, common with Schmidt–Cassegrain telescopes.
  • The English, horseshoe and cross-axis mounts were used for large professional instruments.

Alignment

Using an equatorial mount requires polar alignment: the polar axis must be pointed at the celestial pole, which for the northern hemisphere lies near the star Polaris. Precise alignment is essential for long-exposure photography.
