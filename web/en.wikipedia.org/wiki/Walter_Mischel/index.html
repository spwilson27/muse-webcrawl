Walter Mischel (February 22, 1930 – September 12, 2018) was an Austrian-born American psychologist specialising in personality theory and social psychology. He is famous for the Stanford marshmallow experiment, a landmark study of delayed gratification in children.

Walter Mischel

Early life

Mischel was born in Vienna in 1930. His family fled Austria after the Nazi Anschluss of 1938 and settled in Brooklyn, New York. He studied at New York University and earned his doctorate in clinical psychology at Ohio State University in 1956, then taught at Harvard, Stanford, and from 1983 at Columbia University.

Personality and assessment

In his 1968 book Personality and Assessment, Mischel challenged the assumption that behaviour is driven mainly by stable global traits, noting that correlations between trait measures and behaviour rarely exceeded about 0.30 — the so-called "personality coefficient". The book ignited the person–situation debate that reshaped personality psychology. Mischel later developed, with Yuichi Shoda, the Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS), which explains consistency through stable "if…then…" situation–behaviour signatures rather than broad traits.

The marshmallow test

Beginning in the late 1960s at Stanford's Bing Nursery School, Mischel offered preschoolers a choice between one small reward immediately or two if they waited alone with the treat for about 15 minutes. Follow-up studies found that children who waited longer tended to have better later outcomes in school performance and other measures, though later replications with larger, more diverse samples found the associations substantially reduced once family background was taken into account. Mischel popularised the work in his 2014 book The Marshmallow Test: Mastering Self-Control.
