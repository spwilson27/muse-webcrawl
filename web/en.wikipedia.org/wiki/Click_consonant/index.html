Click consonant

Click consonants, or clicks, are speech sounds that occur as consonants in many languages of southern Africa and in three languages of East Africa. They are non-pulmonic consonants produced with a velaric ingressive airstream mechanism: the back of the tongue seals against the velum while a second closure is formed further forward in the mouth; the tongue then lowers, rarefying the air in the enclosed pocket, and release of the forward closure produces a sharp clicking sound as air rushes in.

Types of clicks

The International Phonetic Alphabet recognizes five basic click types, distinguished by the place of the front closure: the bilabial click (ʘ), dental click (ǀ), (post)alveolar click (ǃ), palato-alveolar click (ǂ), and alveolar lateral click (ǁ). Clicks are among the most complex consonants in the world's languages because they are usually combined with a second articulation at the velum, yielding "click accompaniments" that contrast in voicing, aspiration, and nasalization; some languages have more than eighty distinct click phonemes.

Distribution

Clicks are best known from the Khoisan languages of the Kalahari region, such as Juǀ'hoan and Taa (!Xóõ), the latter famous for its enormous consonant inventory. Through contact, clicks spread into neighboring Bantu languages, including the Nguni languages Zulu and Xhosa, where they are written with the letters c, q, and x. Outside Africa, the only known language with a full click system is Damin, an extinct ceremonial register of the Lardil language of Australia. Click-like sounds also occur paralinguistically in many cultures — the "tsk-tsk" of disapproval, the sound used to urge on horses, and a kiss-like bilabial click among them.

In writing systems

The distinctive IPA letters for clicks derive from a pipe symbol used in Khoisan orthographies. In the Latin-based orthographies of southern African languages, clicks are most often written with the letters c, q, and x, sometimes with diacritics or digraphs marking the accompaniment, as in Xhosa names such as !Xu in some scholarly transcriptions.
