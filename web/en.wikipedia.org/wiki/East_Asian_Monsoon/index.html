The East Asian Monsoon is a large-scale seasonal wind system affecting much of East and Southeast Asia, including China, Japan, the Korean Peninsula, Taiwan and parts of the Russian Far East. It is part of the global monsoon circulation and is driven by the temperature contrast between the Asian landmass and the Pacific Ocean.

Summer monsoon

In summer, the continent heats faster than the ocean, drawing moist maritime air inland. The summer monsoon advances northward in stages, bringing the East Asian rainy season: the meiyu in China, baiu in Japan, changma in Korea and the plum rains of Taiwan, typically from May to July, followed by a hotter midsummer phase with tropical cyclones. The associated rain front (the mei-yu front) can stall and produce prolonged heavy rainfall and flooding, as in the severe Yangtze floods of 1998 and 2020.

Winter monsoon

In winter, the powerful Siberian High sends cold, dry air southeastward. Cold surges bring sharp temperature drops, and where the air crosses the Sea of Japan it picks up moisture that falls as heavy "sea-effect" snow on Japan's western coasts — among the heaviest snowfalls on Earth. The winter monsoon also brings the dry season to much of southern China and Southeast Asia.

Variability and impacts

The monsoon's strength varies from year to year under the influence of El Niño–Southern Oscillation, Eurasian snow cover and Tibetan Plateau heating; it also has a subtropical western Pacific component, sometimes distinguished as the East Asian subtropical monsoon from the tropical monsoon of the South China Sea and Indochina. The monsoon is vital to agriculture and water supplies for well over a billion people, and failures or excesses have shaped East Asian history. Palaeoclimate records from Chinese cave speleothems have reconstructed monsoon variations over hundreds of thousands of years.
