DDR SDRAM

Double Data Rate Synchronous Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DDR SDRAM) is a double data rate (DDR) synchronous dynamic random-access memory (SDRAM) class of memory integrated circuits used in computers. DDR SDRAM, also retroactively called DDR1 SDRAM, has been superseded by DDR2 SDRAM, DDR3 SDRAM, DDR4 SDRAM and DDR5 SDRAM. None of its successors are forward or backward compatible with DDR1 SDRAM, meaning DDR2, DDR3, DDR4 and DDR5 memory modules will not work in DDR1-equipped motherboards, and vice versa.

Compared to single data rate (SDR) SDRAM, the DDR SDRAM interface makes higher transfer rates possible by more strict control of the timing of the electrical data and clock signals. Implementations often have to use schemes such as phase-locked loops and self-calibration to reach the required timing accuracy. The interface uses double pumping (transferring data on both the rising and falling edges of the clock signal) to double data bus bandwidth without a corresponding increase in clock frequency.

History

The standard was developed by JEDEC and first demonstrated in the late 1990s; Samsung released the first commercial DDR SDRAM chip in 1998. DDR memory appeared in consumer PCs around 2000 and became the dominant PC memory type until DDR2 took over in the mid-2000s.

Specification

DDR1 operates at 2.5 V (2.6 V for DDR-400) with a 2-bit prefetch. Modules are 184-pin DIMMs for desktops. Speed grades are named DDR-200 through DDR-400 (PC1600 to PC3200), with data rates of up to 3.2 GB/s per channel. CAS latency is typically 2, 2.5, or 3 cycles.

Chips and modules

DDR SDRAM was sold as individual chips and as memory modules (DIMMs for desktops, 200-pin SO-DIMMs for laptops). Chip densities ranged from 64 Mbit to 1 Gbit, and unbuffered modules commonly held between 128 MB and 1 GB.
