Executive order

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An executive order is a directive by the president of the United States that manages operations of the federal government and has the force of law. Executive orders rest on authority granted by the Constitution (for example the President's role as chief executive and Commander-in-chief) or delegated by Congress; they bind executive agencies but can be revoked by a successor president, overridden by statute, or invalidated by courts.

Famous examples include Washington's Neutrality Proclamation (1793, a proclamation rather than a numbered order), Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1863), Franklin D. Roosevelt's order interning Japanese Americans (Executive Order 9066, 1942), Truman's desegregation of the armed forces (9981, 1948) and his seizure of the steel mills (10340, 1952) — struck down in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, whose concurrence by Justice Jackson supplied the standard framework for judging presidential power. Numbered orders run from the nineteenth century to well past 14,000 in total.

Use and controversy

Presidents increasingly use executive orders (and the related memoranda and proclamations) to act without Congress, especially in divided government; critics across parties denounce “government by executive order” when the other side holds the White House, and rapid reversals between administrations create policy “whiplash” in areas such as immigration and environmental regulation. Analogous instruments exist elsewhere: French décrets, Indian presidential ordinances (temporary legislation when Parliament is not sitting), and decrees in many presidential systems.
