Caustic (optics)

In optics, a caustic is the envelope of light rays reflected or refracted by a curved surface or object, or the projection of that envelope onto another surface. Familiar examples include the bright cusped curve of light at the bottom of a drinking glass, the shimmering network of light patterns on the floor of a swimming pool, and the bright envelope of rays inside a rainbow. The term derives from the Greek kaustikos, "burning", reflecting the fact that concentrated light can scorch at a caustic focus.

Geometry

For a point light source reflected in a circular mirror, the caustic is a nephroid-like curve with a cusp on the mirror's axis; light from a distant (parallel) source reflected in a circle produces a nephroid exactly. Refraction through a water droplet concentrates light along caustics that form the primary and supernumerary bows of a rainbow, as analysed in catastrophe theory. Caustics are mathematically the critical-value sets of the ray map and are classified using singularity theory.

In computer graphics

Rendering convincing caustics is a classic challenge in photorealistic graphics, since standard forward ray tracing samples them inefficiently. Techniques such as photon mapping (introduced by Henrik Wann Jensen in 1996) and bidirectional path tracing are used to capture caustic effects in film and design visualisation.
