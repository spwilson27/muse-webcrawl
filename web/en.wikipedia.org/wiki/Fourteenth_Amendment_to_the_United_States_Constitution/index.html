Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, adopted on 9 July 1868 as one of the Reconstruction Amendments, is among the most consequential provisions of American law. Its first section grants citizenship to all persons born or naturalised in the United States (overruling Dred Scott) and forbids the states to abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens, to deprive any person of life, liberty or property without due process of law, or to deny any person equal protection of the laws.

Doctrinal career

Early decisions narrowed it (the Slaughter-House Cases, 1873, gutting the Privileges or Immunities Clause), and the Lochner era turned its Due Process Clause to economic liberty; the 20th century reversed course — through "selective incorporation" most of the Bill of Rights was applied to the states under the Due Process Clause, substantive due process grounded privacy and autonomy rights (from Griswold and Roe to Obergefell, with Roe overruled in Dobbs, 2022), and the Equal Protection Clause powered the modern law of racial, sex and other classifications, beginning with Brown (1954). Other sections apportion representation (penalising disfranchisement, rarely enforced), bar certain ex-Confederate officeholders (Section 3, litigated again in 2024 over presidential eligibility — the Supreme Court held states could not enforce it against federal candidates absent congressional legislation), validate the Union's debt and repudiate the Confederacy's, and give Congress enforcement power (Section 5, itself judicially bounded since City of Boerne, 1997).
