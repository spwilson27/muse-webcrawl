Caesura

A caesura (plural caesurae or caesuras) is a break or pause in the middle of a line of verse, often corresponding to a syntactic boundary such as the end of a phrase or clause. The term comes from the Latin caesura, "a cutting", from caedere, "to cut". Caesurae are a defining feature of classical Latin and Greek verse, and they remained important in later European poetry, including Old English alliterative verse and French classical alexandrines.

Classical verse

In the dactylic hexameter of Greek and Latin epic, a caesura conventionally falls within the third foot (the penthemimeral caesura), though other positions occur, such as the hephthemimeral caesura in the fourth foot. Roman poets such as Virgil used the placement and variety of caesurae to shape the rhythm and emphasis of a line. A caesura is described as masculine when it follows a stressed (long) syllable and feminine when it follows an unstressed (short) syllable.

In later traditions

In French classical verse, the alexandrine of twelve syllables has an obligatory caesura after the sixth syllable. In Old English poetry, each half-line is separated by a caesura, around which the alliterative pattern is organised. Modern poets use the term more loosely for any strong mid-line pause.
