Stereotype

In social psychology, a stereotype is a generalised belief about a particular category of people. It is an expectation that people might have about every person of a particular group. The type of expectation can vary; it can be, for example, an expectation about the group's personality, preferences, appearance or ability. Stereotypes are sometimes overgeneralised, inaccurate, and resistant to new information, but can sometimes be accurate.

While such generalisations about groups of people may be useful when making quick decisions, they can be erroneous when applied to particular individuals and are among the reasons for prejudicial attitudes. Explicit stereotypes are those that a person consciously endorses and expresses; implicit stereotypes form in a person's subconscious and can affect behaviour without conscious awareness.

Stereotypes are cognitive schemas used to categorise people into groups.

Etymology

The term stereotype comes from the French adjective stéréotype, from the Greek words στερεός (stereos, "firm, solid") and τύπος (typos, "impression"), hence "solid impression on one or more senses". The term was first used in the printing trade in 1798 by Firmin Didot to describe a printing plate that duplicated any typography. The duplicate plate, or the stereotype, is used for printing instead of the original. Outside the printing industry, the first reference to "stereotype" was in 1850, as a noun meaning an image perpetuated without change. The term took on its contemporary psychological meaning in Walter Lippmann's 1922 book Public Opinion, where it described the "pictures in our heads" through which people perceive the world.

Relation to other concepts

Stereotypes are one component of attitudes toward a social group, alongside prejudice (the affective component) and discrimination (the behavioural component). In this tripartite view, stereotypes reflect expectations and beliefs about the characteristics of members of groups perceived as different from one's own. Stereotypes can be positive or negative, and can concern one's own group (autostereotypes) or another group (heterostereotypes).

Formation

Stereotypes can be formed through several mechanisms. One is the illusory correlation, in which people overestimate the association between two variables — for example, group membership and a rare behaviour — because distinctive events are more memorable. Another is socialisation: children acquire stereotypes from parents, peers, teachers, and mass media. Social role theory holds that stereotypes arise from observing members of groups in particular roles, such as occupational roles, and inferring that the typical characteristics of a role are inherent to the group. The "kernel of truth" hypothesis suggests some stereotypes arise from actual group differences, though they are often exaggerated.

Effects

Stereotypes can influence how people are judged and treated, sometimes in discriminatory ways. They also affect members of stereotyped groups themselves. Stereotype threat, first described by Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson in 1995, is the phenomenon in which people who are aware of a negative stereotype about their group perform worse on a relevant task when the stereotype is made salient. Stereotypes have also been linked to self-fulfilling prophecies, in which expectations about a person lead that person to conform to the expectations.

Accuracy

A long-running debate in social psychology concerns stereotype accuracy — the degree to which stereotypical beliefs correspond to real group differences. Some researchers argue that many stereotypes are among the most accurate findings in social psychology, while others emphasise that even broadly accurate stereotypes mislead when applied to individuals and that perceivers tend to exaggerate group differences.
