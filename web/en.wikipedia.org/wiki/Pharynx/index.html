Pharynx

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pharynx (from Greek φάρυγξ, "throat") is the part of the throat behind the mouth and nasal cavity, and above the oesophagus and trachea. It is found in vertebrates and in many invertebrates. In humans, the pharynx is part of both the digestive system and the respiratory system: food passes through it to the oesophagus, and air passes through it to the larynx and lungs.

Structure

The human pharynx is a conical muscular tube about 13 cm long, conventionally divided into three sections. The nasopharynx lies behind the nose and above the soft palate and contains the pharyngeal tonsil (adenoids) and the openings of the Eustachian tubes. The oropharynx lies behind the mouth, between the soft palate and the epiglottis, and contains the palatine and lingual tonsils. The laryngopharynx (hypopharynx) extends from the epiglottis to the oesophagus. The pharyngeal constrictor muscles and stylopharyngeus coordinate swallowing, propelling the bolus past the larynx, whose epiglottis folds down to protect the airway.

Function and clinical notes

Beyond conduction, the pharynx shapes the resonance of the voice. Inflammation of the pharynx — pharyngitis, the common "sore throat" — is most often viral, but group A streptococcal infection requires antibiotic treatment. The pharynx is also a site of sleep apnoea, where its soft tissues collapse during sleep, and of cancers associated with tobacco, alcohol and human papillomavirus.

In other animals

Many invertebrates possess a muscular pharynx used in feeding; in nematodes it is a triradiate pumping organ, and in some annelids and molluscs it can be everted or armed with jaws and teeth.
