Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) is a group of rare genetic conditions in which the skin and mucous membranes are extremely fragile and blister in response to minor mechanical friction or trauma. The name derives from the epidermis, the outer layer of skin, and bulla, a blister; "lysis" refers to the separation of the skin layers. Affected children are sometimes called "butterfly children", because their skin is said to be as delicate as a butterfly's wing.

Daily wound care and bandaging are central to living with severe forms of EB.

Classification

EB is divided into four major types according to the level of the skin at which blistering occurs:

  • EB simplex — blistering within the epidermis, usually caused by mutations in the keratin genes KRT5 and KRT14; the most common and generally mildest type.
  • Junctional EB — separation in the lamina lucida of the basement membrane zone, involving laminin-332, type XVII collagen, or integrin genes; some subtypes are lethal in infancy.
  • Dystrophic EB — blistering below the basement membrane due to mutations in COL7A1, encoding type VII collagen, the main component of anchoring fibrils; dominant and recessive forms occur, the recessive form causing severe scarring, fused fingers (pseudosyndactyly), and a high lifetime risk of aggressive squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Kindler syndrome — blistering at multiple levels, caused by mutations in the FERMT1 gene.

More than 30 clinical subtypes are recognized, reflecting mutations in at least 16 different genes.

Signs and symptoms

Severity ranges from mild blistering of the hands and feet to life-threatening disease. In severe forms, blisters cover much of the body and line the mouth, esophagus, eyes, and urinary tract, leading to pain, infection, malnutrition, anemia, scarring, and contractures. Wound care can take hours each day. Chronic wounds and inflammation strongly predispose patients with recessive dystrophic EB to early-onset skin cancer, the leading cause of death in that group.

Treatment

For most of its history EB had no specific therapy, and management consisted of meticulous wound care, non-adherent dressings, infection control, nutritional support, and treatment of complications. Recent advances have included gene and cell therapies: in 2023 the US FDA approved beremagene geperpavec (Vyjuvek), a topical gene therapy for dystrophic EB, and birch-bark extract gel (Filsuvez) for wound healing in dystrophic and junctional EB. Research continues into protein replacement, exon skipping, and gene-edited skin grafts — an approach famously used in 2015 to regenerate most of the skin of a child with junctional EB using genetically corrected stem cells.
