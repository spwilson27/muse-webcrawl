Crown ether

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crown ethers are cyclic chemical compounds consisting of a ring containing several ether groups, most commonly oligomers of ethylene oxide whose repeating unit is –CH2CH2O–. They are named for their crown-like three-dimensional shape and for their ability to "crown" certain metal cations, forming stable complexes.

Discovery and naming

Crown ethers were discovered by Charles J. Pedersen of DuPont in the 1960s; his work, together with that of Donald Cram and Jean-Marie Lehn on host–guest chemistry, was recognised by the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The name of a crown ether gives the total number of atoms in the ring followed by the number of oxygen atoms: 18-crown-6 has an 18-membered ring with six oxygens.

Complexation

The oxygen atoms line the inside of the ring and coordinate to a cation sitting in the cavity, while the hydrocarbon exterior makes the complex soluble in organic solvents. Binding is size-selective: 18-crown-6 binds potassium especially strongly, 15-crown-5 fits sodium, and 12-crown-4 fits lithium. This allows crown ethers to act as phase-transfer catalysts, carrying ionic reagents such as potassium permanganate into organic media.

Related compounds

Replacing oxygen with nitrogen gives azacrowns, and incorporating rigid aromatic or chiral elements yields more selective hosts. The related three-dimensional cryptands, developed by Lehn, bind cations even more strongly. Crown ethers are also of interest in supramolecular chemistry as components of rotaxanes, catenanes, and molecular machines.
