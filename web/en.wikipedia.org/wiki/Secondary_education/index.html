Secondary education

Secondary education is the stage of formal schooling that follows primary education and precedes tertiary (higher) education. In most systems it serves adolescents, roughly between the ages of 11 and 18, and is commonly divided into lower secondary (junior high or middle school) and upper secondary (senior high school) phases. In the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED), these correspond to levels 2 and 3.

Purpose and curriculum

Secondary education builds on literacy and numeracy from primary school and broadens students' knowledge across subjects such as mathematics, science, languages, history, geography, and the arts. In the upper secondary years, students often specialise, choose electives, or enter vocational and technical tracks that prepare them for employment, apprenticeships, or university entrance examinations.

Structures around the world

Systems vary widely. In the United States and Canada, students attend middle school (grades 6–8) and high school (grades 9–12), graduating with a diploma. In England and Wales, secondary school runs from age 11 to 16, ending with GCSE examinations, followed by an optional sixth form or college phase leading to A-levels. Many European systems separate students earlier into academic (gymnasium), technical, and vocational schools. In much of the world, secondary schooling is compulsory up to a specified leaving age, though access and completion remain uneven, particularly for girls in some regions.

History

Secondary schooling originated in grammar schools and academies serving a small elite, teaching Latin and classical subjects. Mass secondary education is largely a 20th-century development: as industrial economies demanded literate, skilled workers, governments made secondary attendance free and compulsory, and enrolment expanded dramatically after the Second World War.

Issues and debates

Recurring debates concern the balance between academic and vocational pathways, the role of high-stakes examinations, curriculum breadth versus depth, adolescent wellbeing, and equity of outcomes between social groups and regions.
