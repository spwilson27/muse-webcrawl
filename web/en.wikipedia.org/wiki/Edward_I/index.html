Edward I (17/18 June 1239 – 7 July 1307), also known as Edward Longshanks and the Hammer of the Scots (Latin: Malleus Scotorum), was King of England from 1272 to 1307. He succeeded his father, Henry III, having played a leading role in defeating the baronial rebels of Simon de Montfort at the Battle of Evesham (1265) during the Second Barons' War, and having gone on crusade to the Holy Land (1270–1272).

Conquest of Wales

Edward conquered Wales in campaigns of 1277 and 1282–83, defeating Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed the principality to the English crown, and Edward built a famous "iron ring" of castles — including Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech and Beaumaris — to secure his conquest. In 1301 he made his son, the future Edward II, the first English Prince of Wales.

Scotland

Invited to arbitrate the Scottish succession in 1290–92, Edward claimed overlordship of Scotland and installed John Balliol. When Balliol rebelled, Edward invaded in 1296, deposed him and seized the Stone of Scone. Resistance under William Wallace and Andrew Moray won at Stirling Bridge (1297), but Edward defeated Wallace at Falkirk (1298). Robert the Bruce's rising in 1306 renewed the war; Edward died at Burgh by Sands while marching north to campaign again.

Government and law

Edward was a formidable administrator and legislator: his statutes (Westminster I and II, Gloucester, Winchester, the so-called "English Justinian" reforms) reshaped land law, and his parliaments — including the Model Parliament of 1295 — broadened representation. He also financed his wars through heavy taxation and expelled the Jews from England by the Edict of Expulsion in 1290.
