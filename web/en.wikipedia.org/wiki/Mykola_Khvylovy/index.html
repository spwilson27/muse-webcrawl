Mykola Khvylovy (Ukrainian: Микола Хвильовий; born Mykola Fitilov; 13 December 1893 – 13 May 1933) was a Ukrainian writer and poet of the early Soviet period, a leading figure of the Ukrainian national communism movement and of the literary generation later known as the "Executed Renaissance". He was the founder of the influential literary organisation VAPLITE and a central voice in the Ukrainian Literary Discussion of 1925–1928.

Life

Khvylovy was born in Trostianets in what is now Sumy Oblast. He fought in the First World War and joined the Communist Party in 1919, participating in the civil war in Ukraine. Settling in Kharkiv, then the capital of Soviet Ukraine, he emerged in the early 1920s as an innovative prose writer with the collections Blue Etudes (1923) and Autumn (1924), which blended lyricism, irony, and psychological depth. His novel The Woodcocks (1927) was banned before its second part could appear.

The Literary Discussion

In his pamphlets Away from Moscow! and the cycle Quo Vadis?, Khvylovy argued that Ukrainian culture should orient itself toward Europe rather than toward Moscow — his slogans "Away from Moscow!" and "Towards psychological Europe" became rallying cries of the Ukrainization era and drew condemnation from Stalin personally. Under pressure, Khvylovy was forced into repeated public recantations, and VAPLITE was dissolved in 1928.

Death and legacy

As the Holodomor devastated the Ukrainian countryside and arrests of intellectuals intensified, Khvylovy shot himself on 13 May 1933, the day after the arrest of his friend Mykhailo Yalovy. His suicide note proclaimed his devotion to the party even in death. His works were banned in the USSR for decades and were republished only in the late 1980s. He is now regarded as one of the most important Ukrainian writers of the 20th century.
