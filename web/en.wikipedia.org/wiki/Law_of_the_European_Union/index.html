European Union law is a system of rules operating within the 27 member states of the European Union (EU). It has similarities to both international law, since the EU's founding treaties are agreements between sovereign states, and to the laws of federal states, since it creates binding norms directly applicable to individuals and reviewed by a supranational court. Through the doctrine of primacy, EU law overrides conflicting national law in areas within its competence.

The Treaties — principally the Treaty on European Union (TEU) and the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), as last substantially revised by the Treaty of Lisbon (2009) — constitute the primary law. The institutions enact secondary law in the form of regulations, which are directly applicable, directives, which bind as to the result to be achieved, and decisions.

Constitutional principles

In Van Gend en Loos (1963) the Court of Justice held that the Treaty created a "new legal order" whose provisions could confer rights on individuals enforceable in national courts — the doctrine of direct effect. In Costa v ENEL (1964) it declared the primacy of Community law over national law. Other foundational doctrines include indirect effect, state liability for breaches (Francovich), and the general principles of law, including fundamental rights now codified in the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.

Legislative process

Under the ordinary legislative procedure, the European Commission proposes legislation which must be adopted jointly by the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union, generally by qualified majority. The Union acts only within the competences conferred by the Treaties, and in shared competences must respect subsidiarity and proportionality.

Areas of law

EU law covers the internal market (free movement of goods, persons, services and capital), competition law, agriculture, trade through the common commercial policy, environmental and consumer protection, the economic and monetary union, and the area of freedom, security and justice. The Court of Justice ensures uniform interpretation, chiefly through preliminary references from national courts.
