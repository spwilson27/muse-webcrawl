Texcoco (altepetl)

Texcoco (Classical Nahuatl: Tetzcohco) was a major Acolhua city-state (altepetl) in the Valley of Mexico on the eastern shore of Lake Texcoco. It was one of the three founding members of the Aztec Triple Alliance, along with Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan, and was renowned as a centre of learning, law, engineering and poetry — sometimes called the "Athens of the Western World".

History

Texcoco was founded in the 12th or 13th century by Chichimec peoples and became the capital of the Acolhua domain. Under its ruler Techotlalatzin and his successors, the city adopted Nahua culture. The most famous Texcocan ruler was Nezahualcoyotl (reigned 1429–1472), a statesman, engineer and poet-king who reformed Texcoco's laws and built aqueducts, dikes and gardens, including the great dike that separated the brackish waters of Lake Texcoco from the fresh water around Tenochtitlan. His son Nezahualpilli continued the city's cultural flowering.

At its height, Texcoco received two-fifths of the imperial tribute (later adjusted) and exercised influence over the eastern Valley of Mexico. During the Spanish conquest, the Texcocan prince Ixtlilxochitl II allied with Hernán Cortés and provided crucial support, including the base from which brigantines were launched against Tenochtitlan. After the conquest, Texcoco became a colonial city; its pre-Hispanic archives were largely destroyed, though descendants of its royal house compiled important chronicles.

Culture

Texcoco was famed for its library and archives, its strict legal code, its engineers and architects, and its Nahuatl poetry. The modern city of Texcoco de Mora in the State of Mexico stands on the site.
