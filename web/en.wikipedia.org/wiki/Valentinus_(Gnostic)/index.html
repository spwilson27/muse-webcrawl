Valentinus (Greek: Οὐαλεντῖνος; c. 100 – c. 180 AD) was a Christian Gnostic theologian and the founder of Valentinianism, one of the most influential Gnostic movements of the second century. For a time his school was the most widespread of the early Christian sects, and Valentinus himself was, according to Tertullian, a candidate for the office of bishop of Rome.

Life

Little is known with certainty about Valentinus's life. Epiphanius of Salamis reports that he was born in Phrebonis in the Nile Delta and educated in Alexandria, the intellectual capital of Roman Egypt. He is said to have been a follower of Theudas, a disciple of Paul the Apostle. Around 136 AD Valentinus went to Rome, where his eloquence brought him prominence; he remained there for some two decades before leaving, reportedly after being passed over for episcopal office. He may have spent his final years in Cyprus.

Teachings

Valentinus's own writings survive only in fragments quoted by his opponents, but the system developed by his followers—preserved in sources such as the Nag Hammadi text the Tripartite Tractate and the Gospel of Truth, which some scholars attribute to Valentinus himself—describes a rich cosmology. At its centre is the Pleroma ("Fullness"), a realm of divine aeons emanating from the unknowable Father (Bythos). The fall of the youngest aeon, Sophia (Wisdom), gives rise to the Demiurge, the ignorant creator of the material world. Humanity is divided into three classes: the material (hylics), the psychic (psychics), and the spiritual (pneumatics), who possess the divine spark and are destined for salvation through gnosis—saving knowledge.

Legacy

Valentinianism split into eastern and western branches and survived into the fourth and fifth centuries despite condemnation by church fathers such as Irenaeus, whose Against Heresies is a major source for its doctrines. In the twentieth century, scholars such as Elaine Pagels renewed interest in Valentinus as a creative theologian rather than merely a "heretic".
