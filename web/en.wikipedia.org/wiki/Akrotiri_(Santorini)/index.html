Akrotiri (Santorini)

Akrotiri
Location: Santorini, Greece
Region: Aegean Sea
Type: Settlement
Part of: Minoan civilization
Abandoned: c. 1627 BC (Thera eruption)

Akrotiri (Greek: Ακρωτήρι) is a Minoan Bronze Age settlement on the volcanic Greek island of Santorini (Thera). The settlement was destroyed in the Theran eruption sometime in the 16th century BC and buried in volcanic ash, which preserved the remains of fine frescoes and many objects and artworks. Its remains were first excavated in 1967 by Greek archaeologist Spyridon Marinatos, and it has been frequently described as a Minoan "Pompeii".

History

The earliest evidence for human habitation of Akrotiri can be traced back as early as the fifth millennium BC, when it was a small fishing and farming village. By the end of the third millennium BC, this community developed and expanded significantly. One factor for Akrotiri's growth may be the trade relations it established with other Aegean cultures. Its strategic position on the primary sailing route between Cyprus and Minoan Crete also made it an important point for the copper trade, thus allowing it to become an important centre for processing copper.

Akrotiri was buried by the massive Theran eruption in the middle of the second millennium BC (during the Late Minoan IA period). As a result, like the Roman ruins of Pompeii after it, the remains are remarkably well preserved. Frescoes, pottery, furniture, advanced drainage systems (including what appear to be the world's earliest flush toilets) and three-story buildings have been discovered at the site.

Excavations

The site was first excavated in 1967 by Spyridon Marinatos, professor at the University of Athens. Many of the structures, streets, squares, and the remains of wall paintings are accessible to visitors, sheltered under a protective canopy. In 2005, part of the canopy roof collapsed, killing a visitor and injuring others; the site was closed for several years and reopened in April 2012.

Frescoes

The frescoes at Akrotiri are especially famous. They include the "Spring Fresco" with lilies and swallows, the "Boxing Boys", the "Fisherman", and a large procession of ships often called the "Miniature Frieze" or Flotilla Fresco, which depicts a fleet sailing between two harbour towns. Unlike the Knossos frescoes, those at Akrotiri have survived largely intact.
