Cruella de Vil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cruella de Vil, recognizable by her split black-and-white hair and fur coat.

Cruella de Vil is a fictional character created by English author Dodie Smith for her 1956 children's novel The Hundred and One Dalmatians. She is the story's principal antagonist: a wealthy, ruthless London heiress obsessed with obtaining the fur of Dalmatian puppies to make a spotted fur coat. Her name is a pun on "cruel" and "devil".

Appearances

In Smith's novel, Cruella is a former schoolmate of Mrs. Dearly. Married to a furrier, she lives in Hell Hall in Suffolk and schemes with two henchmen to steal the Dearlys' litter of Dalmatian puppies, gathering 97 pups in total before the dogs' rescue. She also appears in Smith's sequel, The Starlight Barking (1967).

The character achieved far wider fame through Walt Disney's animated film One Hundred and One Dalmatians (1961), in which she was voiced by Betty Lou Gerson and animated by Marc Davis. Davis's design gave her an emaciated figure, exaggerated gestures, and her iconic half-black, half-white hair. The film also introduced her theme song, "Cruella De Vil", written by Mel Leven.

Glenn Close portrayed Cruella in the live-action films 101 Dalmatians (1996) and 102 Dalmatians (2000). Emma Stone played a younger version of the character, the aspiring fashion designer Estella, in the prequel film Cruella (2021), which reimagines her origin in 1970s punk-era London. Cruella has also appeared in animated television series, video games, and as a featured villain in Disney's pantheon of antagonists.

Characterization

Cruella is depicted as vain, selfish, and sadistic, with a particular fondness for fur and no regard for animal life. She is famous for her dramatic entrances, her reckless driving, and her gaunt, skeletal appearance emphasized by heavy fur coats. In the Disney version she is a chain-smoking socialite whose mania for a Dalmatian-puppy coat drives the plot.

Legacy

Cruella ranks among the most recognizable villains in popular culture. The American Film Institute placed her 39th on its list of the greatest film villains in the "100 Years...100 Heroes and Villains" survey. Her name has become shorthand for ostentatious cruelty, and she has been widely parodied and referenced in fashion, music, and television.
