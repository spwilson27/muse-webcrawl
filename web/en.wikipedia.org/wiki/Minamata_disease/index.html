Minamata disease (Japanese: 水俣病, Minamata-byō) is a neurological disease caused by severe mercury poisoning. It was first discovered in the city of Minamata, Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan, in 1956, and was caused by the release of methylmercury in industrial wastewater from the Chisso Corporation's chemical factory, which continued from 1932 to 1968. The methylmercury bioaccumulated in shellfish and fish in Minamata Bay and the Shiranui Sea, which, when eaten by the local population, caused mercury poisoning. It is one of the "four big pollution diseases of Japan".

Minamata Bay. Contaminated seafood from the bay caused the outbreak first recognised in 1956.

Symptoms

Signs and symptoms include ataxia, numbness in the hands and feet, general muscle weakness, loss of peripheral vision, and damage to hearing and speech. In extreme cases, insanity, paralysis, coma and death follow within weeks of the onset of symptoms. A congenital form of the disease also affected fetuses in the womb: children were born with severe cerebral palsy-like symptoms even when their mothers showed few or no symptoms.

Discovery and response

On 1 May 1956, a hospital director in Minamata reported an epidemic of an unknown disease of the central nervous system. Local cats, which ate scraps of fish, had earlier been observed convulsing and dying — the "dancing cat fever". Researchers at Kumamoto University identified heavy-metal poisoning through contaminated seafood, and by 1959 had pinpointed mercury from the Chisso acetaldehyde plant, where methylmercury was formed as a by-product. Chisso suppressed its own confirmatory experiments and continued discharging waste; the Japanese government did not officially acknowledge the cause until 1968, the year the discharge stopped when the production process was abandoned.

Compensation and legacy

Victims struggled for decades for recognition and compensation. A compensation agreement in 1973, court rulings finding both Chisso and the government negligent (notably the 2004 Supreme Court ruling on the Kansai plaintiffs), and political settlements in 1995 and 2009–2010 gradually expanded relief. As of the early 2000s, 2,265 victims had been officially recognised, of whom over 1,700 had died; tens of thousands more have received some form of compensation or relief. A second outbreak, Niigata Minamata disease, was confirmed in 1965 along the Agano River, caused by the Shōwa Denkō plant. The disaster gave its name to the international Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013). The photograph "Tomoko Uemura in Her Bath" (1971) by W. Eugene Smith became an iconic image of the disease's human cost.
