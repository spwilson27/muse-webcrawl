European turtle dove

Scientific name: Streptopelia turtur

Family: Columbidae

Conservation status: Vulnerable

The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a migratory dove of the pigeon family, breeding across Europe, western Asia and northern Africa and wintering in sub-Saharan Africa. More slender and delicate than other doves, it is identified by its rich chestnut-and-black chequered back, pinkish breast, and a striking patch of black-and-white stripes on each side of the neck. Its soft, purring "turr-turr" song, from which both its English and scientific names derive, was a traditional sound of summer in the European countryside.

Behaviour

Turtle doves arrive on their breeding grounds in late spring and nest in hedgerows, scrub and woodland edge, building a flimsy platform of twigs and usually laying two white eggs. They feed almost entirely on seeds — historically those of fumitory and other arable weeds — and drink by suction, like other pigeons, without raising the head. The species is strongly migratory, crossing the Sahara on broad fronts in autumn.

Decline

The species has declined severely: numbers in Europe fell by well over half from the 1980s, and the British population dropped by more than 90%, prompting its listing as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Causes include the loss of weed seeds to intensive farming and herbicides, shortened breeding seasons, hunting on migration — especially around the Mediterranean — and the protozoan disease trichomonosis. International action plans and temporary hunting moratoria in western Europe have been introduced to aid recovery.
