Alginate is a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide obtained from the cell walls of brown algae. Its salts — especially sodium alginate — dissolve in water to give viscous solutions and form gels on contact with calcium ions, properties that make alginates widely used as thickeners, stabilisers, gelling agents and moulding materials in food, pharmaceuticals, dentistry and industry.

Structure and gelation

Alginate is a linear copolymer of (1→4)-linked β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) arranged in blocks. Stretches of consecutive G residues bind divalent cations such as Ca²⁺ cooperatively in the celebrated "egg-box" arrangement, crosslinking chains into a gel. The M/G ratio, which varies by species and tissue — stipes of Laminaria hyperborea being notably G-rich — determines gel strength and brittleness.

Production

Commercial alginate is extracted from wild and farmed brown seaweeds including giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera), Laminaria and Ascophyllum nodosum, harvested chiefly off Norway, Chile, China, France, the British Isles and the Americas. The seaweed is treated with alkali, and the extract is filtered, precipitated (as alginic acid or calcium alginate) and converted to the desired salt. Certain bacteria, notably Azotobacter and mucoid Pseudomonas, also produce alginate.

Uses

In food (E400–E405 series additives) alginates stabilise ice cream, dressings and restructured foods such as imitation crab and pimento olive stuffing, and they are the gelling agent of "spherification" in modernist cuisine. Calcium and sodium alginate fibre dressings absorb wound exudate and support haemostasis. Dentistry's familiar impression material is an irreversible alginate hydrocolloid. Other uses include textile printing pastes, paper coatings, pharmaceutical tablet binders, antacid raft-forming preparations (with sodium bicarbonate, for reflux), and cell encapsulation and 3D bioprinting in biotechnology, where alginate's gentle, ion-triggered gelation protects living cells.
