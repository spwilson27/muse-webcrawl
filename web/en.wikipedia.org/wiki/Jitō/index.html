Jitō (地頭) were medieval land stewards appointed by the Kamakura shogunate (and retained under the Ashikaga shogunate) to manage estates (shōen) and public lands throughout Japan. The office is considered one of the foundational institutions of the warrior government's encroachment on the estate system of the imperial court.

Origins

In 1185, after the defeat of the Taira in the Genpei War, Minamoto no Yoritomo obtained imperial sanction to appoint jitō on estates across the country, alongside the military governors called shugo. Jitō were drawn from Yoritomo's housemen (gokenin) and were granted rights to a share of the estate's produce in return for administering it, collecting taxes, and maintaining order.

Functions and conflicts

The jitō's duties included overseeing cultivation, apprehending criminals, and remitting the proprietor's dues. Because jitō income depended on estate revenue, friction with absentee proprietors in Kyoto — temples, shrines, and court nobles — was constant: jitō withheld dues, expanded their holdings, and gradually usurped proprietorial rights. Compromises such as jitō uke (contract stewardship) and shōen division (shitaji chūbun) were common.

Decline

During the Muromachi period the growing power of the shugo overshadowed the jitō; many jitō became local warrior lords (kokujin) or were absorbed into shugo domains. By the Sengoku period the title survived in places mainly as a hereditary local office, and the estate system itself disintegrated.
