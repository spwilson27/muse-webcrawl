Anglo-Saxon paganism

Anglo-Saxon paganism, sometimes termed Anglo-Saxon heathenism, was the set of religious beliefs and practices followed by the Anglo-Saxons between the 5th and 8th centuries AD, during the initial period of Early Medieval England. A variant of Germanic paganism found across much of north-western Europe, it was a heterogeneous belief system with regional and chronological variation.

Deities

Evidence suggests veneration of gods cognate with those of other Germanic peoples, including Woden (equivalent to the Norse Óðinn), Thunor (Þórr), Tiw (Týr), and the goddess Frig. Place-names incorporating these theonyms—such as Wodnesbury, Thundersley, and Tuesley—are scattered across England. Bede also mentions the goddesses Ēostre and Hreda, for whom months in the Old English calendar were named.

Practices

Archaeological evidence points to cremation and furnished inhumation burials, the deposition of offerings in wetlands and rivers, and the use of amulets and ritual objects. Sites such as Yeavering in Northumbria have been interpreted as containing pagan temples or cult buildings, while the royal ship burial at Sutton Hoo reflects elite funerary practice on the eve of conversion.

Conversion

From the late 6th century, Christian missions—the Gregorian mission from Rome and Irish missionaries from Iona—converted the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, a process largely complete by the late 7th century. Pagan practice persisted longest in more isolated regions and survived in fragmentary form in folklore, charms such as the Nine Herbs Charm, and customary law.

Sources

Because Anglo-Saxon paganism was a pre-literate tradition, knowledge of it derives from archaeology, place-name studies, later Christian writings such as Bede's Ecclesiastical History, and comparative evidence from Norse mythology. Modern reconstruction is therefore necessarily partial.
