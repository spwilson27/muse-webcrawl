Transitivity (grammar)

In linguistics, transitivity is a property of verbs that relates to whether a verb can take objects and how many such objects a verb can take. It is closely related to the notion of valency. Verbs are traditionally divided into intransitive verbs, which take no object, and transitive verbs, which take one object; a verb that takes two objects, typically a direct object and an indirect object, is called ditransitive.

Intransitive and transitive verbs

An intransitive verb has only a subject: in "the dog barked", barked cannot take a direct object. A transitive verb requires an object to complete its meaning: in "she read the book", read takes the book as its direct object. Many verbs in English are ambitransitive and can be used either way, as in "he is reading" versus "he is reading a novel".

Ditransitives and other patterns

Ditransitive verbs such as give take both a direct object and an indirect object: "he gave her a present". Some verbs take object complements or obligatory adverbials, and languages differ in how they mark transitivity grammatically. In languages with morphological case, the object of a transitive verb is usually marked with the accusative case, while ergative–absolutive languages group the object with the subject of an intransitive verb instead.

Transitivity in functional grammar

In systemic functional linguistics, transitivity is a broader system of clause analysis concerned with the types of processes (material, mental, relational, and others) and their participants and circumstances, rather than with objects alone.
