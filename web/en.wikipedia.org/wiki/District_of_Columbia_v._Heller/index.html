District of Columbia v. Heller

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570 (2008), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that the Second Amendment to the United States Constitution protects an individual's right to keep and bear arms, unconnected with service in a militia, for traditionally lawful purposes such as self-defence within the home.

Background

The Firearms Control Regulations Act of 1975, enacted by the District of Columbia, effectively banned the possession of handguns and required other firearms kept at home to be unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger lock. Dick Anthony Heller, a D.C. special police officer who carried a handgun in federal buildings, was denied a registration certificate for a handgun he wished to keep at home and sued, arguing the law violated the Second Amendment.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision authored by Justice Antonin Scalia, the Court affirmed the D.C. Circuit's invalidation of the law. The majority held that the Amendment's operative clause ("the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed") protects an individual right, and that the prefatory militia clause announces a purpose but does not limit that right. The Court stressed that the right is not unlimited: longstanding prohibitions on firearms in sensitive places, by felons and the mentally ill, and on dangerous and unusual weapons remain presumptively permissible. Justice Stevens, joined by three colleagues, dissented, reading the Amendment as protecting only militia-related possession.

Aftermath

Heller was the first Supreme Court case to hold that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess a firearm. Its holding, which applied to the federal government and the District, was incorporated against the states two years later in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010). The decision remains a central precedent in American gun law and in debates over originalism as a method of constitutional interpretation.
