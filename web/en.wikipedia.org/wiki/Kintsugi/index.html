Kintsugi (Japanese: 金継ぎ, "golden joinery"), also known as kintsukuroi (金繕い, "golden repair"), is the Japanese art of repairing broken pottery by mending the areas of breakage with urushi lacquer dusted or mixed with powdered gold, silver, or platinum. Rather than disguising the damage, the technique treats breakage and repair as part of the history of an object, making the repaired piece more beautiful and often more valuable than before.

Origins

Lacquer repair of ceramics is ancient in East Asia, but the gold-seamed aesthetic is usually traced to the Muromachi period. A famous anecdote tells that the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa (1436–1490), dissatisfied when a broken Chinese tea bowl returned from Ming China was crudely stapled with metal clamps, asked Japanese craftsmen for a more elegant solution; they developed repair with gold-dusted lacquer. The technique became closely associated with tea ceremony ceramics and the aesthetics of the tea masters such as Sen no Rikyū.

Technique

The fragments are cleaned and joined with urushi lacquer, often mixed with flour or clay as filler; missing sections may be rebuilt entirely of lacquer. The seams are then coated with lacquer and dusted with fine gold powder (maki-e technique), burnished, and cured in a humid cabinet over days to weeks. Related methods include yobitsugi, in which a shard from a different vessel patches a missing section.

Philosophy and influence

Kintsugi is frequently connected, somewhat loosely, with the aesthetics of wabi-sabi—the acceptance of transience and imperfection—and with the idea that an object's scars are integral to its beauty. In the 21st century the practice has been widely adopted internationally as a craft, a metaphor in psychology and self-help for resilience after trauma, and an inspiration in art and design.
