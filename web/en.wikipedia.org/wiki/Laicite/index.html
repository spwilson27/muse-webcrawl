Laïcité

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Laïcité is a French concept denoting the strict separation of religion from the state and the neutrality of the state in religious matters. Rooted in the Enlightenment and consolidated by the French Third Republic, it was given legal force by the Law of 9 December 1905 concerning the separation of the Churches and the State, which ended the system of state-recognized religions established by Napoleon's Concordat of 1801.

Under laïcité, the French Republic neither recognizes nor subsidizes any religion, guarantees freedom of conscience, and ensures the equal treatment of all citizens regardless of their beliefs. Religious symbols and practices are largely excluded from public administration, and public servants must observe strict neutrality while on duty.

History

The term laïcité derives from the Greek laos ("the people"). During the Third Republic, secular laws secularized public education (the Ferry laws of 1881–82 made primary schooling free, compulsory, and secular) and progressively removed religious influence from public institutions. The 1905 law, drafted under the government of Émile Combes and championed by Aristide Briand and Jean Jaurès, provided that "the Republic ensures freedom of conscience" and "guarantees the free exercise of religious worship," while declaring that it "does not recognize, salary or subsidize any religion."

Laïcité was inscribed in the Constitution of the Fourth Republic (1946) and Article 1 of the Constitution of the Fifth Republic (1958), which describes France as an "indivisible, secular (laïque), democratic and social Republic."

Application and debates

The principle has generated recurring controversy, notably over the wearing of religious symbols in public schools — addressed by the 2004 law banning conspicuous religious symbols in public primary and secondary schools — and the 2010 ban on full-face coverings in public spaces. Alsace-Moselle retains an exception, since it was German territory in 1905 and still operates under the Concordat system. Debates continue over the balance between state neutrality, freedom of religion, and social cohesion in a religiously diverse society.
