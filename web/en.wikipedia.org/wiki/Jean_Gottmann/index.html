Jean Gottmann

Jean Gottmann, geographer who coined the term Megalopolis

Jean Gottmann (10 October 1915 – 7 February 1994) was a French geographer best known for coining and popularizing the concept of the Megalopolis in his 1961 study of the urbanized northeastern seaboard of the United States.

Life

Born in Kharkov (then in the Russian Empire) to Jewish parents who fled the Revolution for France, Gottmann studied geography at the Sorbonne and worked with Albert Demangeon. During the Second World War he emigrated to the United States, teaching at Johns Hopkins and working for wartime agencies; after the war he divided his career between France — at the Institut d'études politiques in Paris — and the University of Oxford, where he was professor of geography and head of the School of Geography from 1968 to 1983.

Megalopolis

Commissioned by the Twentieth Century Fund, Gottmann's Megalopolis: The Urbanized Northeastern Seaboard of the United States (1961) argued that the chain of cities from Boston to Washington — some 500 miles long and home to over 37 million people at the time — formed a single, functionally integrated urban system, the first of its kind and a model of future urbanization worldwide. He stressed its concentration of government, finance, media, and education — the "hinge" and "incubator" of American society.

Other work

Gottmann also wrote on political geography (The Significance of Territory, 1973), where he analyzed territory through the opposing forces of iconography (symbols that divide) and circulation (movements that unify), and on Mediterranean and urban geography. His megalopolis concept remains foundational in urban studies, applied to regions such as Japan's Taiheiyō Belt and China's Yangtze Delta.
