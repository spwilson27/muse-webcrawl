Automatic train operation (ATO) is a technology used to enhance the level of automation in rail systems, by which trains are driven automatically to a greater or lesser extent. ATO works in conjunction with automatic train protection (ATP), which enforces safe speeds and separations, while ATO performs the driving functions: accelerating, coasting, braking to precise station stops, and controlling doors.

Grades of automation

The international standard IEC 62290 defines Grades of Automation (GoA). GoA1 is manual driving with ATP; GoA2 is semi-automatic operation, in which the system drives between stations but a driver starts the train and operates the doors — first used on London's Victoria line in 1967; GoA3 is driverless operation with an attendant on board; and GoA4 is unattended train operation, as on the Copenhagen Metro, Paris Métro Line 1, and Singapore's driverless lines.

Benefits and challenges

Automation permits higher frequencies and capacity, energy-optimised driving profiles, consistent stopping accuracy for platform screen doors, and reduced operating costs. Challenges include handling degraded modes and emergencies, mixed traffic on mainline railways, and public acceptance. On open main lines, ATO is being combined with ETCS to give "ATO over ETCS", with freight and passenger trials in several countries; full mainline automation is established in mining railways such as Rio Tinto's AutoHaul network in the Pilbara.
