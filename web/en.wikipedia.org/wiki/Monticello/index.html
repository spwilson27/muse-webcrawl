Monticello

Monticello was the primary plantation of Thomas Jefferson, the third president of the United States, who began designing and building it at age 26 after inheriting land from his father. Located just outside Charlottesville, Virginia, in Albemarle County, the plantation covers 5,000 acres and was worked by enslaved African Americans, whose labour built the house and produced its crops.

Architecture

Jefferson designed the house in the Neoclassical style, drawing on Andrea Palladio and the architecture he observed as minister to France, notably the Hôtel de Salm in Paris. The famous octagonal dome was added in the second building campaign (1796–1809). The house contains ingenious devices of Jefferson's design — a polygraph copying machine, a revolving bookstand, dumbwaiters, a seven-day clock, and his alcove bed. Terraces and service wings extend from the house, with Mulberry Row, the plantation's industrial and enslaved community's street, nearby.

Later history

After Jefferson's death in 1826, the estate passed through the hands of the Levy family, whose long stewardship preserved it. The Thomas Jefferson Foundation purchased Monticello in 1923 and operates it as a museum and educational institution. Monticello and the nearby University of Virginia were together designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987; the west front of the house appears on the reverse of the U.S. nickel.
