Shoji

A shoji (障子) is a traditional Japanese sliding panel consisting of a wooden lattice frame covered with translucent paper, usually washi. Used as a door, window, or room divider in traditional Japanese architecture, shoji diffuse light softly through a space while maintaining privacy, and can be slid aside or removed to reconfigure rooms.

Construction

The frame (kumiko) is made of lightweight wood, commonly cedar or cypress, joined into rectangular or decorative geometric patterns. Washi paper is pasted to one side; modern versions substitute plastic or laminated paper for durability and washability. Shoji slide in wooden tracks (shikii above and below) rather than swinging on hinges.

History and use

Paper-covered sliding panels developed from earlier screens and curtains during the Heian and Kamakura periods and became standard in the shoin-zukuri style of residential architecture. Outward-facing shoji are protected from weather by outer glass or wooden storm panels (amado). Because paper is easily torn but equally easily replaced, repapering shoji is a familiar household task, traditionally done at year-end.

Related fittings

Shoji contrast with fusuma, opaque sliding panels covered with paper or cloth on both sides, and with tatami-floored rooms they define the flexible interiors of the Japanese house. Their soft, diffused light has influenced modern architects and interior designers worldwide.
