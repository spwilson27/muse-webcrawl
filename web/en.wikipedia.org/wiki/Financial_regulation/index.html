Financial regulation

Financial regulation is a broad set of rules, practices and enforcement mechanisms that constrain and guide the behaviour of financial institutions and markets. Its aims typically include maintaining the stability of the financial system, protecting consumers and investors, preventing fraud and market abuse, and ensuring fair competition. Regulators are usually government or statutory bodies such as central banks and securities commissions.

Objectives and tools

Prudential regulation requires banks and insurers to hold adequate capital and liquidity (for example under the Basel Accords); conduct regulation governs how firms treat customers and disclose information; market regulation polices insider trading and manipulation. Tools include licensing, reporting requirements, examinations, stress tests, enforcement actions and resolution regimes for failing institutions.

History

Modern financial regulation in the United States followed the crash of 1929: the Securities Act of 1933 and Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the SEC and mandated disclosure, while the Glass–Steagall Act separated commercial and investment banking. Deregulation from the 1970s through the 1990s (including the repeal of Glass–Steagall in 1999) was followed, after the 2007–08 financial crisis, by reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act (2010) in the US and the Basel III capital standards internationally.

Institutions

Major regulators include the US Federal Reserve, SEC and CFTC; the European Central Bank and European Securities and Markets Authority; and the UK's Prudential Regulation Authority and Financial Conduct Authority. International bodies such as the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and IOSCO set standards implemented nationally.

Criticism

Regulation is criticised both for being too weak — permitting excessive risk-taking, regulatory capture and arbitrage — and for being too burdensome, raising compliance costs and pushing activity into less-regulated "shadow banking" sectors.
