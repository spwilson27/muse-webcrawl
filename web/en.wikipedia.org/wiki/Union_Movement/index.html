Union Movement

The Union Movement (UM) was a far-right political party in the United Kingdom, founded in 1948 by Oswald Mosley as the successor to his pre-war British Union of Fascists. Mosley formed the party by amalgamating dozens of small nationalist groups into a single "movement" advocating his post-war doctrine of "Europe a Nation".

Ideology and activity

The Union Movement called for a united European state led by a strong executive, combining former fascist themes with anti-immigration campaigns, especially in London's East End and Notting Hill, where its members were involved in street politics during the 1958 race riots. Its publications included the newspaper Union and the journal The European. The party contested a handful of elections with negligible results; Mosley's best-known campaign was in Kensington North in 1959, where he won about 8 percent of the vote.

Decline

After a poor showing in the 1966 general election, Mosley retired from active leadership and the party declined into a fringe group; it renamed itself the Action Party in 1973 and later the Action Society, effectively ceasing to function as a political party. Jeffrey Hamm, Mosley's lieutenant, led the remnant organisation into the 1990s.
