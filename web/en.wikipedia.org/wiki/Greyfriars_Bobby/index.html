Greyfriars Bobby (1855 – 14 January 1872) was a Skye Terrier or Dandie Dinmont Terrier who became known in 19th-century Edinburgh for supposedly spending 14 years guarding the grave of his owner until he died on 14 January 1872. The story continues to be well known in Scotland, through several books and films. A prominent commemorative statue and the nearby graves are a tourist attraction.

History

Bobby belonged to John Gray, who worked for the Edinburgh City Police as a nightwatchman. When John Gray died of tuberculosis in 1858, he was buried in Greyfriars Kirkyard, in the Old Town of Edinburgh. According to the traditional account, Bobby then became known locally for sitting at his master's grave, leaving only for food, for fourteen years until his own death.

In 1867 a new bye-law required all dogs in Edinburgh to be licensed; unlicensed dogs were to be destroyed. Sir William Chambers, the Lord Provost of Edinburgh, paid Bobby's licence and presented him with a collar inscribed "Greyfriars Bobby from the Lord Provost 1867 licensed".

Death and legacy

Bobby died in 1872 and was buried just inside the gate of Greyfriars Kirkyard, near John Gray's grave. A year later, a statue and fountain commemorating Bobby was erected at the corner of Candlemaker Row and George IV Bridge, built under the sponsorship of the philanthropist Angela Burdett-Coutts. His headstone reads "Greyfriars Bobby – died 14th January 1872 – aged 16 years – Let his loyalty and devotion be a lesson to us all".

The story has been adapted in the novel Greyfriars Bobby (1912) by Eleanor Atkinson and in films including the 1961 Walt Disney production Greyfriars Bobby: The True Story of a Dog and the 2005 film The Adventures of Greyfriars Bobby.

Historical accuracy

Later research has questioned details of the popular story. In 2011, Jan Bondeson published research arguing that Bobby was likely a stray dog encouraged by the graveyard's curator and local tradespeople, and that there may in fact have been two different dogs involved. The story nonetheless remains a beloved part of Edinburgh folklore.
