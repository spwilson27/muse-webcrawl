Young diagram

In mathematics, a Young diagram (also called a Ferrers diagram, particularly in English-language usage) is a finite collection of boxes arranged in left-justified rows, with the row lengths in non-increasing order. A Young diagram is determined by a partition of a positive integer n: the number of boxes in the i-th row is the i-th part of the partition. Young diagrams are named after the British mathematician Alfred Young, who introduced them in 1900 in the study of the representation theory of the symmetric group.

Definition

A partition of n is a weakly decreasing sequence λ = (λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ … ≥ λk) of positive integers summing to n. Its Young diagram has λi boxes in row i. The conjugate (or transpose) partition λ′ is obtained by reflecting the diagram across its main diagonal, interchanging rows and columns. Diagrams are drawn in "English notation" with the longest row at the top, or "French notation" with the longest row at the bottom, as in a Cartesian graph.

Young tableaux

Filling the boxes of a Young diagram with numbers yields a Young tableau. A standard Young tableau fills the diagram with the numbers 1 through n, each used once, increasing across rows and down columns. The number of standard Young tableaux of a given shape is given by the celebrated hook-length formula of Frame, Robinson and Thrall, which computes the dimension of the corresponding irreducible representation of the symmetric group Sn. Semistandard tableaux allow repeated entries and underlie the theory of Schur polynomials.

Applications

Young diagrams index the irreducible representations of the symmetric groups and of the general linear groups, and appear in combinatorics through the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence, which bijects permutations with pairs of standard tableaux of the same shape. They also arise in algebraic geometry (Schubert calculus), in the study of symmetric functions, and in physics, where they classify tensor symmetries of particles.
