Potential energy surface

A potential energy surface (PES) describes the energy of a system, especially a collection of atoms, in terms of certain parameters, normally the positions of the atoms. The surface might define the energy as a function of one or more coordinates; if there is only one coordinate, the surface is called a potential energy curve or energy profile. Under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, the PES for a molecule is the electronic energy (plus nuclear repulsion) as a function of nuclear geometry, and it governs the molecule's structure, vibrations and reactions.

Concept

For a system of N atoms the PES is a function of 3N − 6 internal coordinates (3N − 5 for linear molecules), so except for diatomic molecules it is a hypersurface in many dimensions. It may be visualized for two chosen coordinates — for example, the two bond lengths involved in an atom-exchange reaction A + BC → AB + C — as a landscape of valleys and passes.

Stationary points

Points where the gradient of the energy vanishes are stationary points. Local minima correspond to stable structures: reactants, products and reaction intermediates; their depths give relative energies and, from the curvature (the Hessian), vibrational frequencies. First-order saddle points — maxima along one direction and minima along all others — correspond to transition states, and the lowest path connecting reactant and product valleys through such a pass is the minimum energy path or intrinsic reaction coordinate. The energy difference between reactants and the transition state gives the activation energy central to transition state theory, developed by Eyring and by Evans and Polanyi using the early H + H2 surfaces of Eyring and Polanyi and of London.

Applications

Potential energy surfaces are computed using quantum chemistry methods (from semi-empirical schemes to coupled-cluster and density functional theory) or fitted analytically to empirical force fields for molecular dynamics simulations. They are indispensable in computational chemistry for predicting geometries, spectra, reaction mechanisms and rates, in surface science for adsorption and catalysis, and in protein folding, where the surface is often pictured as a funnel-shaped energy landscape.
