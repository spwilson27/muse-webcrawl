Organ-on-a-chip

An organ-on-a-chip (OOC) is a multi-channel 3-D microfluidic cell culture, integrated circuit (chip) that simulates the activities, mechanics and physiological response of an entire organ or an organ system. It constitutes the subject matter of significant biomedical engineering research, more precisely in bio-MEMS. The convergence of lab-on-a-chip (LOC) and cell biology has permitted the study of human physiology in an organ-specific context. By acting as a more sophisticated in vitro approximation of complex tissues compared to conventional cell culture, they provide the potential as an alternative to animal models for drug development and toxin testing.

Design

Organ chips are typically fabricated from transparent polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) using soft lithography. Microchannels lined with living human cells are perfused with culture medium, and mechanical cues—stretching, peristalsis-like motion, fluid shear—mimic the organ's physical microenvironment. Sensors integrated into the chip can monitor oxygen, pH, barrier integrity (via transepithelial electrical resistance) and metabolite production in real time.

Types

Models have been developed for the lung (the first widely publicised device, from the Wyss Institute in 2010, reproduced breathing motions and pulmonary edema responses), gut, liver, kidney, heart, blood–brain barrier, skin, bone marrow and tumours. Linking several organ chips together produces "body-on-a-chip" (multi-organ) systems used to model inter-organ metabolism and systemic toxicity.

Applications

Applications include drug efficacy and toxicity screening, disease modelling (including infection and rare genetic diseases using patient-derived cells), personalised medicine, and reducing reliance on animal testing. Regulatory interest grew after the US FDA Modernization Act 2.0 (2022) removed the blanket requirement for animal testing before human trials, opening the door to qualified chip-based data.

Limitations

Challenges include standardisation and reproducibility, scaling cell numbers to physiological relevance, sourcing consistent human cells, bubble formation and absorption of hydrophobic drugs into PDMS, and the difficulty of capturing whole-organism complexity such as endocrine and immune interactions.
