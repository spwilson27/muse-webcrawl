Airport security

Airport security refers to the techniques, measures and personnel employed to protect airports, passengers, staff, aircraft and property from accidental or malicious harm, crime, terrorism and other threats. Aviation security is a combination of measures and human and material resources in place to safeguard civil aviation against acts of unlawful interference, in the terminology of the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), which sets global standards in Annex 17 to the Chicago Convention.

History

Early airport security was minimal; passengers could often walk directly to the gate. A wave of aircraft hijackings in the late 1960s and early 1970s — including the hijackings to Cuba and the Dawson's Field hijackings of 1970 — led governments to mandate passenger screening. In 1973 the United States required screening of all passengers and carry-on baggage. The 1988 bombing of Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie prompted stricter checked-baggage screening. The September 11 attacks in 2001 transformed aviation security worldwide, leading to the creation of the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) in the United States, reinforced cockpit doors, air marshals and far more intensive screening.

Screening

Passengers typically pass through a metal detector or a full-body scanner (millimetre-wave or backscatter X-ray), while cabin baggage is examined by X-ray machines, often with computed tomography (CT) scanners in newer installations. Liquids, aerosols and gels in hand baggage have been restricted to containers of 100 ml since foiled plots in 2006. Checked baggage is screened by explosive detection systems, and in some jurisdictions matched to boarded passengers so that a bag cannot fly unaccompanied.

Access control and perimeter security

Airside areas are restricted to authorised staff who display airport identification badges issued after background checks. Perimeter fencing, patrols, CCTV and intrusion-detection systems guard against unauthorised entry, while vehicle checkpoints control deliveries. Police, and in some countries military personnel, patrol terminals; behaviour-detection and canine explosive-detection teams are also used.

International regulation

ICAO Annex 17 requires each contracting state to establish a national civil aviation security programme. Regional bodies such as the European Civil Aviation Conference harmonise measures, and mutual recognition agreements allow "one-stop security" so transfer passengers need not be rescreened. Debates continue over the cost, privacy and effectiveness of screening, and over risk-based approaches such as trusted-traveller programmes.
