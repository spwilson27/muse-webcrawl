M. King Hubbert

Marion King Hubbert (October 5, 1903 – October 11, 1989) was an American geologist and geophysicist. He worked at the Shell Oil Company research laboratory in Houston, Texas, and later for the United States Geological Survey. Hubbert is best known for the Hubbert peak theory, which predicts that for any given geographical area the rate of petroleum production follows a bell-shaped curve — a prediction he used in 1956 to forecast, correctly, that oil production in the contiguous United States would peak around 1970.

Early life and career

Hubbert was born in San Saba, Texas, and studied geology, mathematics and physics at the University of Chicago, receiving his PhD in 1937 with a dissertation on the flow of groundwater. He taught at Columbia University before joining Shell in 1943, where he remained until 1964, when he became a research geophysicist with the US Geological Survey, a post he held until 1976. He was also a professor at Stanford University and the University of California, Berkeley.

Hubbert made important contributions in several areas of the earth sciences. He demonstrated mathematically that rock in the Earth's crust behaves both elastically and plastically, developed theory governing the flow of underground fluids, and gave a physical explanation of hydraulic fracturing pressures. His work on stress in the crust helped explain faulting and the possibility that fluid injection could trigger earthquakes — a conclusion he later applied to the Denver earthquakes of the 1960s.

Peak oil prediction

In a 1956 paper presented to the American Petroleum Institute, Hubbert modelled US petroleum reserves and production and predicted that production in the lower 48 states would peak between 1965 and 1971. US production did in fact peak in 1970 and declined thereafter for decades, vindicating the analysis; the cumulative-production curve he used became known as the Hubbert curve, and the approach is the basis of modern "peak oil" analysis. Hubbert also estimated that world oil production would peak around the year 2000. He was a prominent member of the Technocracy movement in the 1930s, serving as education director of Technocracy Incorporated.

Honors

Hubbert was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, served as president of the Geological Society of America in 1962, and received the Penrose Medal, the Geological Society's highest honour, in 1973.
