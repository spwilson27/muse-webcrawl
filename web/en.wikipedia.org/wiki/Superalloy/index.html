Superalloy

A superalloy, or high-performance alloy, is an alloy with the ability to operate at a high fraction of its melting point — typically above 0.7 of the absolute melting temperature — while retaining mechanical strength, resistance to creep deformation, surface stability, and resistance to corrosion and oxidation. Superalloys are most commonly based on nickel, cobalt, or iron, with substantial additions of elements such as chromium, aluminium, titanium, and refractory metals.

Development and structure

Superalloys were developed from the 1940s onward, driven by the demands of jet engine turbine blades. Nickel-based superalloys derive much of their strength from an ordered intermetallic precipitate phase, gamma prime (γ′), dispersed in a face-centred cubic matrix. Successive generations of alloys for single-crystal turbine blades added increasing amounts of rhenium and ruthenium to raise temperature capability.

Processing

Depending on the application, superalloys are produced by casting (including directionally solidified and single-crystal investment casting), wrought processing, or powder metallurgy. Protective thermal barrier coatings are routinely applied to components in the hottest sections of gas turbines.

Applications

The principal uses are in aero-engines and industrial gas turbines, rocket engines, nuclear reactors, chemical processing plant, and other environments combining high temperature, stress, and corrosiveness. Well-known families include Inconel, Hastelloy, Waspaloy, and the Rene alloys.
