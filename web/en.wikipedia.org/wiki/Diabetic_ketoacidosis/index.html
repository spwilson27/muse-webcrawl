Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a potentially life-threatening complication of diabetes mellitus, occurring mainly in people with type 1 diabetes. Lack of insulin causes blood sugar to rise while the body breaks down fat, producing acidic ketone bodies that accumulate and lower the blood pH.

Signs and symptoms

Features include excessive thirst and urination, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, rapid deep breathing (Kussmaul breathing) as compensation for the acidosis, and a characteristic fruity (pear-drop/acetone) smell on the breath. Without treatment, drowsiness, coma and death follow. Common precipitants are infections, missed insulin doses (including insulin pump failure), and newly diagnosed, previously unrecognised type 1 diabetes.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on the triad of hyperglycaemia, ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis with reduced bicarbonate; blood potassium is typically normal or high even when total body potassium is depleted. Management follows a standard protocol: intravenous fluids, a fixed-rate insulin infusion, careful potassium replacement and monitoring, and treatment of the precipitating cause. A feared complication, particularly in children, is cerebral oedema, which requires urgent osmotic therapy. The SGLT2 inhibitor class of glucose-lowering drugs can trigger DKA at near-normal glucose levels ("euglycaemic DKA").
