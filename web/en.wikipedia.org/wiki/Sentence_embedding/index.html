Sentence embedding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In natural language processing, a sentence embedding is a representation of a sentence as a vector of real numbers, typically produced by a neural network model, such that sentences with similar meanings are mapped to nearby points in the embedding space. Sentence embeddings extend the idea of word embeddings to whole texts and underpin many modern applications, including semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation, clustering, and text classification.

Approaches

Early methods produced sentence vectors by averaging word embeddings. Dedicated models soon followed: Skip-Thought Vectors (2015) trained an encoder to predict neighbouring sentences; InferSent (2017) used a bidirectional LSTM trained on natural language inference data; and the Universal Sentence Encoder (2018) offered transformer and deep-averaging variants.

The transformer era brought Sentence-BERT (SBERT, 2019), which fine-tunes BERT-style models with siamese or triplet networks so that cosine similarity between output vectors directly reflects semantic similarity. Later approaches use contrastive learning at large scale, and general-purpose text embedding models are offered by many research groups and companies, producing vectors of several hundred to a few thousand dimensions.

Evaluation

Sentence embeddings are commonly evaluated on semantic textual similarity benchmarks such as STS Benchmark and on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB), which covers tasks including retrieval, clustering, classification, and reranking across many languages.
