Commutative property

[Image: Commutativity of addition]

In mathematics, a binary operation is commutative if changing the order of the operands does not change the result. It is a fundamental property of many binary operations, and many mathematical proofs depend on it. Most familiar as the name of the property that says something like "3 + 4 = 4 + 3" or "2 × 5 = 5 × 2", the property can also hold in more advanced settings.

Formally, a binary operation ∗ on a set S is called commutative if x ∗ y = y ∗ x for all x, y in S. An operation that does not satisfy this property is called non-commutative. Addition and multiplication of real numbers are commutative; subtraction and division are not. Matrix multiplication is non-commutative in general, as are composition of functions and the cross product of vectors.

History and etymology

The term derives from the Latin commutare, "to exchange". Implicit use of the property dates to antiquity, in Egyptian and Babylonian arithmetic. Its first explicit statement as a general axiom is credited to François-Joseph Servois, who introduced the word "commutative" in 1814; the property was articulated as an assumption about numbers by authors such as Duncan Gregory and Augustus De Morgan in the nineteenth century.

Examples and uses

Everyday examples include putting on socks (commutative — order does not matter) versus putting on shoes over socks (non-commutative). In algebra, structures built from commutative operations include abelian groups and commutative rings, named after Niels Henrik Abel. In quantum mechanics, operators that do not commute correspond to observables that cannot be simultaneously measured with arbitrary precision, as expressed by the canonical commutation relations.
