Friedreich's ataxia

SpecialtyNeurology, medical genetics
InheritanceAutosomal recessive
GeneFXN (frataxin), chromosome 9
OnsetUsually childhood or adolescence
Named afterNikolaus Friedreich

Expansion of GAA repeats in the FXN gene reduces frataxin production

Friedreich's ataxia (FRDA) is an autosomal-recessive genetic disease that causes progressive damage to the nervous system, resulting in impaired coordination (ataxia), muscle weakness, and often heart disease and diabetes. It is the most common inherited ataxia, affecting roughly 1 in 50,000 people, and is named after the German physician Nikolaus Friedreich, who described it in the 1860s.

Cause

The disease is caused by an expanded GAA trinucleotide repeat in the first intron of the FXN gene on chromosome 9, which suppresses production of frataxin, a mitochondrial protein involved in iron–sulfur cluster assembly. Iron accumulates in mitochondria, generating oxidative damage; the dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord, cerebellum, and heart muscle are especially affected.

Signs and course

Symptoms usually begin between ages 5 and 15 with unsteady gait, followed by clumsiness of the hands, slurred speech, loss of reflexes and position sense, scoliosis, and foot deformities. Many patients develop hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the most common cause of early death. There is no cure; treatment is supportive. In 2023 omaveloxolone became the first drug approved in the United States specifically for Friedreich's ataxia.
