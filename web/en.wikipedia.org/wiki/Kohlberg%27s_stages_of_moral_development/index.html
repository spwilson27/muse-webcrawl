Kohlberg's stages of moral development

Proposed byLawrence Kohlberg
First presented1958 (doctoral dissertation)
Structure3 levels, 6 stages
Built onJean Piaget's theory of moral judgment

Kohlberg's stages of moral development are a theory of how moral reasoning develops across the lifespan, proposed by the American psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg. Extending Jean Piaget's work, Kohlberg presented children and adults with moral dilemmas — most famously the "Heinz dilemma" — and classified their reasoning into six stages grouped in three levels.

The stages

The pre-conventional level (typical of young children) comprises obedience-and-punishment reasoning and self-interest reasoning. The conventional level (most adolescents and adults) comprises conformity to social expectations ("good boy/good girl") and law-and-order reasoning. The post-conventional level, reached by a minority, comprises social-contract reasoning and, at stage six, reasoning from universal ethical principles.

Criticism

Carol Gilligan argued that the theory, derived from an all-male sample, undervalues an ethics of care more common in women's reasoning. Others have questioned the universality of the stages across cultures, the gap between moral reasoning and moral behaviour, and whether stage six is empirically observable. The theory nonetheless remains influential in developmental psychology and moral education.
