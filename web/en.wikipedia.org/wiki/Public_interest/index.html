Public interest

The public interest is the welfare of the general public — the idea that certain outcomes benefit society as a whole rather than a narrow group. The concept, related to the older notions of the common good and res publica, is central to political theory, public administration and law, but its meaning is contested: it may be understood as the aggregation of individual preferences, as the outcome of fair procedures, or as substantive goods (health, security, clean air) that can be identified independently of what people happen to want.

Uses in law and policy

Governments invoke the public interest to justify regulation, compulsory purchase (eminent domain), national security secrecy and restrictions on rights. Courts apply public-interest tests in areas such as freedom of information, where exemptions for privacy or security are weighed against the public interest in disclosure; planning law; and public interest immunity, which replaced Crown privilege. Regulators of broadcasting, charities (which must demonstrate public benefit) and professional bodies also operate statutory public-interest mandates.

Public interest law

"Public interest law" refers to legal practice on behalf of under-represented groups and causes — civil rights, environmental protection, consumer advocacy — conducted by non-profits, legal aid and pro bono lawyers. Public interest litigation became a distinctive feature of Indian constitutional courts, where relaxed standing rules from the late 1970s let any citizen petition on behalf of the disadvantaged.

Criticism

Pluralists and public choice theorists argue there is no single public interest beyond the clash of group interests, and that the label can disguise sectional or bureaucratic advantage. Deliberative democrats reply that public reasoning can transform private preferences into shared judgements, keeping the concept useful if hard to define.
