Insolation

Insolation, also called solar irradiance at the Earth's surface, is the power per unit area of solar radiation received on a surface, typically measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). The word blends "incoming solar radiation." Insolation depends on the sun's elevation, the time of year and latitude, atmospheric conditions such as cloud cover and aerosols, and the orientation and tilt of the receiving surface. It is the primary driver of Earth's weather and climate and the key resource for solar energy technologies.

Variation

At the top of the atmosphere, the irradiance on a surface perpendicular to the sun's rays averages about 1,361 W/m², the solar constant; the planetary average over the whole sphere is about one quarter of this. At the surface, absorption and scattering by the atmosphere reduce the value. Insolation is greatest at solar noon, in summer, and at low latitudes; the highest annual values occur in subtropical deserts such as the Sahara and the Atacama, while cloudy equatorial regions and high latitudes receive less. Long-term variations in insolation distribution, driven by changes in Earth's orbital parameters (Milankovitch cycles), are a major pacing mechanism of ice ages.

Measurement and applications

Ground instruments such as pyranometers measure global irradiance, while satellites including NASA's CERES instruments measure it from space. Insolation data guide the siting and design of photovoltaic and solar-thermal plants, passive solar heating in architecture, and agricultural planning. In climatology, distinguishing direct from diffuse radiation, and accounting for surface albedo, is essential for energy-balance models.
