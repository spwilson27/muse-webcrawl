The Great Patriotic War (Russian: Вели́кая Оте́чественная война́) is a term used in Russia and some other former republics of the Soviet Union to describe the conflict fought during the period from 22 June 1941 to 9 May 1945 along the many fronts of the Eastern Front of World War II, predominantly between the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany and its allies. The name consciously echoes the earlier "Patriotic War" of 1812 against Napoleon.

Usage of the term

The phrase appeared in Pravda the day after the German invasion, Operation Barbarossa, began. In Soviet and Russian usage the Great Patriotic War is treated as a distinct period within the Second World War: the wider war is counted from September 1939 (or 1937 in Asia), and the Soviet–German conflict before June 1941 — the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the invasion of Poland, the Winter War and the annexation of the Baltic states — is discussed separately from the Patriotic War itself.

Course

Germany invaded on 22 June 1941 with some three million troops, the largest invasion force in history, advancing towards Leningrad, Moscow and Ukraine. After catastrophic Soviet losses in 1941, the failure before Moscow, the siege of Leningrad, and the turning-point victory at Stalingrad (1942–43) were followed by the Battle of Kursk and a series of massive offensives — above all Operation Bagration in 1944 — that destroyed Germany's Army Group Centre and carried the Red Army to Berlin. Germany signed its surrender on 8 May 1945 (9 May in Moscow time), commemorated in Russia as Victory Day.

Losses and memory

The Soviet Union suffered roughly 26.6 million deaths, including around 8.7 million military dead and enormous civilian losses from occupation, massacres (including the Holocaust on Soviet territory), starvation and the siege of Leningrad. Victory in the Great Patriotic War remains central to national memory in Russia and Belarus, marked by the 9 May holiday, the Immortal Regiment marches, and memorials such as Mamayev Kurgan in Volgograd. Commemoration has been contested in Ukraine, the Baltic states and elsewhere, where Soviet victory is bound up with subsequent Soviet domination.
