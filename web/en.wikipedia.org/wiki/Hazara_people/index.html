The Hazara people (Persian: هزاره) are an ethnic group native to the Hazarajat region of central Afghanistan, speaking Hazaragi, an eastern variety of the Dari language, itself a dialect of Persian. They are one of the principal ethnic groups of Afghanistan and also form significant communities in Pakistan — notably in Quetta — and in Iran, as well as a growing global diaspora.

Origins

The origins of the Hazara are debated. Their distinct East Asian-influenced physical features and elements of vocabulary and oral tradition are commonly attributed to descent, in part, from Mongol and Turkic soldiers who settled in the region during and after the Mongol invasions of the 13th century under Genghis Khan and his successors. The name "Hazara" is generally derived from the Persian word hezar (هزار), meaning "one thousand," a reference to the military unit of a thousand soldiers in the Mongol army. Over subsequent centuries this population mixed extensively with local Iranic peoples, and genetic studies show a blend of Central Asian, East Asian, and West Eurasian ancestry.

History

For much of their history the Hazara inhabited largely autonomous tribal territories in the central highlands. Their subjugation by the Afghan state under Amir Abdur Rahman Khan in the late 19th century was accompanied by massacres, enslavement, and displacement on a large scale; a substantial portion of the Hazara population was killed or exiled during the campaigns of the 1890s. Through the 20th century the Hazara remained among the most economically and politically marginalized groups in Afghanistan. During the Soviet–Afghan war and the civil war of the 1990s, Hazara political parties such as Hezb-e Wahdat organized armed resistance. The community suffered further massacres under the first Taliban regime, including the killings in Mazar-e Sharif in 1998 and the destruction of the Bamiyan Buddhas in the heart of the Hazarajat in 2001. Since 2001 Hazara have achieved greater educational attainment and political representation, though they have continued to be targeted by sectarian attacks, particularly by Islamic State Khorasan Province, and have faced renewed persecution following the Taliban's return to power in 2021.

Language

Hazaragi is closely related to Dari, the Afghan standard of Persian, and is generally mutually intelligible with it, though it incorporates a number of Mongolic and Turkic loanwords. Most Hazara in Afghanistan are educated in Dari, and many also write in it; Hazaragi is primarily a spoken vernacular, though literary production in the dialect has grown.

Religion and culture

The majority of Hazara are Twelver Shia Muslims, a distinction that has historically set them apart from Afghanistan's Sunni majority and contributed to their persecution. Smaller communities follow Sunni and Ismaili Islam. Cultural life features distinctive music, including the dambura (a long-necked lute), traditional wrestling, buzkashi, and a rich body of oral poetry and proverbs. Hazara cuisine, dress, and festivals such as Nowruz reflect a blend of Central Asian and Persianate traditions.

Diaspora

Waves of refugees and labor migrants have created large Hazara communities in Pakistan (around two million in and around Quetta, many descended from 19th-century arrivals), Iran, and more recently in Australia, Europe, and North America, where they have formed active community and advocacy organizations.
