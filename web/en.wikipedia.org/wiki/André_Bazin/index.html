André Bazin (French: [bazɛ̃]; 18 April 1918 – 11 November 1958) was a renowned and influential French film critic and film theorist. He co-founded the journal Cahiers du Cinéma in 1951 and served as the intellectual mentor of the young critics — François Truffaut, Éric Rohmer, Claude Chabrol, and Jacques Rivette — who became the directors of the French New Wave.

Theory

Bazin's criticism rests on a realist aesthetics. In "The Ontology of the Photographic Image" (1945) he argued that photography's automatic, mechanical genesis gives it a unique credibility — a transfer of reality from the thing to its reproduction. In cinema he championed techniques that preserve the ambiguity and continuity of real space and time: the long take, deep focus, and sequence shots as used by Jean Renoir, Orson Welles, and the Italian neorealists, in opposition to meaning imposed through montage.

Influence

Bazin defended the auteur approach to directors' work and the value of classical Hollywood filmmakers such as Howard Hawks and John Ford. His essays, collected posthumously as What Is Cinema?, remain foundational texts in film studies, and his thought shaped generations of filmmakers and critics worldwide.

Death

Bazin died of leukaemia on 11 November 1958, aged only 40 — on the very day Truffaut began shooting The 400 Blows, which is dedicated to him.
