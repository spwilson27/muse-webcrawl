The alienation effect (German: Verfremdungseffekt, sometimes translated distancing effect) is a theatrical concept developed by the German playwright Bertolt Brecht, designating techniques that prevent the audience from passively identifying with the characters and instead encourage critical, analytical distance from the events on stage.

Techniques

Brecht proposed that actors should demonstrate their characters rather than inhabit them — speaking stage directions aloud, addressing the audience directly, or stepping out of character. Productions used projected titles and placards announcing scenes in advance (destroying suspense), visible lighting rigs and musicians on stage, songs that interrupt and comment on the action, half-curtains, and stark, emblematic sets. The aim was to make the familiar strange, so that social arrangements presented on stage would appear historical and changeable rather than natural and inevitable.

Origins and theory

Brecht elaborated the idea in essays of the 1930s, drawing on the Russian formalist Viktor Shklovsky's concept of ostranenie ("defamiliarisation") and on Chinese acting techniques he witnessed in Mei Lanfang's 1935 Moscow performances. The V-Effekt is a cornerstone of his "epic theatre", opposed to the "dramatic theatre" of illusion, empathy and catharsis descended from Aristotle's Poetics.

Legacy

The alienation effect profoundly influenced twentieth-century theatre, political performance and film — filmmakers such as Jean-Luc Godard adopted its devices — and the term is now used broadly for self-reflexive distancing strategies in any medium.
