Theodor Svedberg

Born30 August 1884, Valbo, Sweden
Died25 February 1971, Kopparberg, Sweden
Known forAnalytical ultracentrifugation; colloid chemistry
AwardsNobel Prize in Chemistry (1926)

Theodor Svedberg (1884–1971), often called “The Svedberg”, was a Swedish chemist whose work on colloids and macromolecules helped establish that proteins are definite molecules rather than vague aggregates. He is best known for developing the analytical ultracentrifuge and for using sedimentation to measure molecular masses. The non-SI unit of sedimentation coefficient, the svedberg (symbol S), equal to 10−13 seconds, is named after him.

Life and career

Svedberg was born at Valbo near Gävle and educated at Uppsala University, where he took his doctorate in 1907 with research on colloidal systems. He spent much of his career at Uppsala, becoming professor of physical chemistry in 1912. Uppsala gave him institutional support to build precision instruments, attract collaborators and create a school of physical chemistry that influenced biochemistry across Europe and the United States.

He also took an interest in radioactivity and later in conservation and the history of science. During the Second World War and after, Svedberg was a prominent public scientific figure in Sweden. He was elected to numerous academies and remained associated with research institutes at Uppsala into old age.

Ultracentrifuge and sedimentation

Colloids presented a measurement problem: their particles were too small for ordinary microscopy yet large enough to scatter light and to behave unlike simple solutes. Svedberg reasoned that a very strong centrifugal field could make macromolecules sediment at measurable rates. With collaborators such as Robin Fåhraeus and others, he developed oil-turbine and later electrically driven ultracentrifuges capable of tens of thousands of revolutions per minute, with optical systems to photograph moving boundaries in the cell.

From the sedimentation coefficient and diffusion coefficient, the Svedberg equation relates molecular mass to buoyant density and frictional behaviour. Applied to haemoglobin and other proteins, the method showed reproducible masses and discrete subunits. This mattered because many biochemists still treated proteins as colloidal associations of indefinite size; sedimentation equilibrium and velocity experiments supplied physical evidence for macromolecular individuality.

Nobel Prize and influence

Svedberg received the 1926 Nobel Prize in Chemistry “for his work on disperse systems”. The citation captured the breadth of his colloid research, but posterity has emphasized the ultracentrifuge as a bridge between physical chemistry and molecular biology. Analytical ultracentrifugation later helped characterize viruses, ribosomes, nucleic acids and synthetic polymers. Ribosomal subunits are still described in Svedberg units—30S, 50S, 70S and 80S—although these values are not additive because sedimentation depends on shape as well as mass.

Personality and legacy

Contemporaries remembered Svedberg as exacting, energetic and mechanically gifted, a laboratory leader who expected instruments to be understood rather than merely operated. His name survives in the svedberg unit, in Svedberg Laboratory at Uppsala, and in the continuing use of sedimentation analysis when mass, shape and association state must be measured in solution.
