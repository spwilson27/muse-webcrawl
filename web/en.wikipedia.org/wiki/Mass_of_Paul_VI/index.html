The Mass of Paul VI, also known as the Ordinary Form of the Roman Rite Mass, and in popular usage the Novus Ordo, is the most commonly used liturgy of the Mass in the Catholic Church. It was promulgated by Pope Paul VI in 1969 following the Second Vatican Council and published in the new Roman Missal. It replaced the Tridentine Mass, the form codified in the Missal of 1570 and last revised in 1962, which is now generally called the Extraordinary Form.

History

The Council's constitution on the liturgy, Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963), called for a general revision of the Mass. A commission (the Consilium) under Archbishop Annibale Bugnini prepared the new Order of Mass, which Paul VI promulgated with the apostolic constitution Missale Romanum on 3 April 1969; it came into use on the First Sunday of Advent, 30 November 1969. Revised editions of the Missal followed in 1975 and 2002 (with a new English translation in 2011).

Features

Compared with the older form, the Mass of Paul VI permits celebration in the vernacular, celebration facing the people (versus populum), an expanded cycle of Scripture readings over three years, a greater role for the congregation's responses and singing, the restoration of the Prayer of the Faithful, and a choice among several Eucharistic Prayers alongside the Roman Canon. The Liturgy of the Word and the Liturgy of the Eucharist form its two principal parts.

Reception

The reform achieved near-universal adoption in the Latin Church but remains debated: critics, including traditionalist groups, have charged it with breaking from tradition, while its defenders present it as the Council's intended renewal. Pope Benedict XVI's Summorum Pontificum (2007) widened permission for the older form; Pope Francis's Traditionis Custodes (2021) restricted it again, affirming the Mass of Paul VI as the unique expression of the Roman Rite's lex orandi.
