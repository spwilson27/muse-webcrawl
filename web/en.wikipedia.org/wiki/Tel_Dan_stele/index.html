Tel Dan stele

MaterialBasalt
Created9th century BCE (c. 870–750 BCE)
Discovered1993–1994, Tel Dan, Israel
Present locationIsrael Museum, Jerusalem
LanguageOld Aramaic

The Tel Dan stele is a fragmentary monumental inscription carved on basalt, discovered in 1993 and 1994 during excavations at Tel Dan in northern Israel. Written in Old Aramaic in the 9th century BCE, it commemorates the victory of an Aramean king — usually identified as Hazael of Aram-Damascus — over the kingdoms of Israel and Judah.

Discovery

The first fragment (Fragment A) was found in July 1993 by surveyor Gila Cook in the excavations directed by Avraham Biran, reused as building material in a wall near the ancient city gate. Two smaller fragments (Fragment B) were recovered in June 1994 and were shown to join the original piece. The reconstructed stele stands about 32 cm tall in its surviving portion; the original monument was considerably larger.

Inscription

The surviving text, written without word spacing typical of the period, describes a conflict in which the author claims to have killed two kings. The most famous phrase is bytdwd, read by most scholars as "House of David" — the earliest extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty. A minority of scholars have proposed alternative readings, but "House of David" remains the mainstream interpretation.

Significance

The stele is a cornerstone in debates over the historicity of the biblical United Monarchy, demonstrating that by the 9th century BCE Judah was known to its neighbours as the "House of David." Together with the Mesha Stele and the Kurkh Monographs, it is one of the most important royal inscriptions from the Iron Age Levant.
