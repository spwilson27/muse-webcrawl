Common Fisheries Policy

The CFP sets rules for managing European fishing fleets and fish stocks.

The Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) is the fisheries policy of the European Union (EU). It sets quotas for which member states are allowed to catch each type of fish, as well as encouraging the fishing industry by various market interventions. Together with the Common Agricultural Policy, it is one of the EU's longest-standing common policies.

History

The CFP was created by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, though it only took practical shape with regulations adopted in 1970 and a comprehensive system of total allowable catches (TACs) and quotas agreed in 1983. The policy was significantly reformed in 2002 and again in 2013, with the 2013 reform introducing a legal commitment to fish at maximum sustainable yield and a ban on discarding unwanted catch (the "landing obligation").

Mechanisms

  • Total allowable catches and quotas — annual catch limits for commercial stocks, shared between member states on the basis of "relative stability".
  • Equal access — EU vessels generally have equal access to waters of other member states, subject to a coastal derogation within 12 nautical miles.
  • Structural policy — funding through the European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund for fleet modernisation, ports, aquaculture and coastal communities.
  • Control and enforcement — common rules on monitoring, satellite vessel tracking and inspection, coordinated by the European Fisheries Control Agency.

Criticism

The CFP has been criticised for failing to prevent overfishing, for setting quotas above scientific advice, and for the practice of discarding. The 2013 reform was intended to address these problems. Following Brexit, the United Kingdom left the CFP and negotiates access and quotas with the EU annually.
