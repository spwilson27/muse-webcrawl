Pachuco

Pachuco is a Spanish word associated with Mexican-American youth subculture, especially in the border region of El Paso–Ciudad Juárez and in Southern California, and reaching prominence in the 1930s and 1940s. The term originally referred to a distinctive style, attitude and dialect.

Culture

Pachucos were known for wearing the zoot suit — high-waisted, wide-legged, tightly cuffed trousers with a long coat — and for caló, a slang argot mixing Spanish, English and Romani-derived words, sometimes called pachuco slang. The female counterparts were called pachucas. The subculture developed its own music tastes, favouring jump blues and mambo.

Historical context

The pachuco phenomenon is closely tied to the Sleepy Lagoon murder trial of 1942 and the Zoot Suit Riots in Los Angeles in 1943, in which servicemen attacked zoot-suited Mexican-American youths. Later Chicano writers and artists, notably Luis Valdez in the play and film Zoot Suit, reclaimed the pachuco as a symbol of cultural resistance and identity.
