Firefighting

Firefighting is a profession aimed at controlling and extinguishing fire. A person who engages in firefighting is known as a firefighter, fireman, firewoman or firefighter-engineer. Firefighters typically undergo a high degree of technical training, including search and rescue, hazardous materials response, and emergency medical training. This technical training is in addition to the study of fire science, fire behavior, and fire protection engineering.

Firefighting is conducted by fire departments that respond to emergency incidents from fire stations, using fire engines, ladder trucks, and specialized rescue equipment. In addition to fires, modern fire services respond to medical emergencies, vehicle collisions, technical rescues, and hazardous materials incidents.

Goals

The traditional goals of firefighting are summarized as life safety, incident stabilization, and property conservation. Firefighters use water, fire retardants, and specialized extinguishing agents; tactics include direct attack on the burning material, exposure protection, ventilation, salvage, and overhaul.

History

Organized firefighting began in ancient Rome under Augustus with the Corps of Vigiles. Bucket brigades and hand-pumped engines were used in early modern cities. In 1678, in Boston, the first paid fire department in North America was established; in 1853, Cincinnati created the first fully paid professional department in the United States. Steam pumpers, motorized apparatus, self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA), and thermal imaging cameras progressively transformed the profession in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Hazards

Firefighting is one of the most dangerous occupations. Hazards include exposure to extreme heat, smoke and toxic gases (including carbon monoxide and hydrogen cyanide), building collapse, and elevated rates of cardiovascular events and occupational cancers, leading to presumptive disability laws in many jurisdictions.
