Taiwanese opera

Native name歌仔戲 (koa-á-hì)
OriginYilan, Taiwan, early 20th century
LanguageTaiwanese Hokkien
InfluencesFolk songs, Gaojia opera, Peking opera

Taiwanese opera (Chinese: 歌仔戲; Pe̍h-ōe-jī: koa-á-hì) is a form of traditional drama originating in Taiwan, and the only form of Han Chinese theatre known to have originated in Taiwan itself rather than on the mainland. Performances are sung and spoken in Taiwanese Hokkien. The earliest troupe is generally traced to the area of Yilan in the northeast of the island in the first years of the 20th century.

History

Taiwanese opera developed from folk songs and ballad-singing (koa-á) performed in Yilan, combined with costume drama absorbed from mainland forms such as Gaojia opera and later Peking opera. It spread across Taiwan during the Japanese colonial period, moved indoors into theatres in the 1920s and 1930s, and endured wartime suppression when performances in the Taiwanese language were restricted under the Japanisation (kōminka) movement. After 1945 it flourished again, migrating successively to radio, film and television; televised opera in the 1960s–1980s made stars of performers such as Yang Li-hua. Temple-fair outdoor performances remain a living context for troupes today.

Performance

Stories are drawn from Chinese history, folk tales, classical novels and romances; plots emphasise loyalty, filial piety, love and karmic retribution. Music combines traditional folk melodies — including the well-known "seven-word" aria pattern and tunes such as Duma and the "miscellaneous tunes" (zap-ke) — with later borrowings and original compositions. Accompaniment traditionally uses the kezaixian (a coconut-shell fiddle), moon lute, Chinese flute and percussion; modern troupes add Western instruments.

Role types echo those of other Chinese opera: sheng (male), dan (female), jing (painted-face) and chou (clown). Unusually among Chinese operatic forms, female performers have long dominated Taiwanese opera, including in male roles played by women (kunsheng). Costumes are colourful and ornate, and acting uses bold, expressive gestures, water sleeves and stylised movement. Famous performers include Yang Li-hua, Ye Qing, Sun Tsui-feng of the Ming Hwa Yuan troupe, and Tang Mei-yun, whose company has produced large-scale modern productions.

Today

Though its mass audience declined with changing entertainment, Taiwanese opera is supported as intangible cultural heritage, taught in schools, and renewed by innovative companies blending it with modern staging, film and television formats.
