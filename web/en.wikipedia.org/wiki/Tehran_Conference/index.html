Tehran Conference

The Tehran Conference (codenamed EUREKA) was a strategy meeting of the "Big Three" Allied leaders of World War II — Joseph Stalin of the Soviet Union, Franklin D. Roosevelt of the United States, and Winston Churchill of the United Kingdom — held from 28 November to 1 December 1943 at the Soviet embassy in Tehran, Iran. It was the first of the wartime conferences to bring all three leaders together (following the Anglo-American meeting at Casablanca earlier in 1943), and the first occasion on which Roosevelt and Stalin met. The main outcome was the Western Allies' firm commitment to open a second front in north-west Europe in May 1944 (Operation Overlord), coordinated with a Soviet offensive in the east; the leaders also discussed the postwar division of Germany, the future of Poland and Iran, and the idea of a postwar international organisation.

Course of the conference

Stalin had insisted on a venue within reach of his command responsibilities; Roosevelt, who had hoped for Alaska or elsewhere, made the long journey by battleship and aircraft — his first flight as president on such a mission. Roosevelt lodged at the Soviet embassy, ostensibly for security (the Allies publicised a claim of a German assassination plot, Operation Long Jump, whose reality historians still debate) — a guest arrangement that placed him, Soviet listening devices notwithstanding, in daily private contact with Stalin. Churchill arrived from the Cairo Conference with Chiang Kai-shek, where the Pacific war had been discussed.

The military debate pitted Churchill's Mediterranean preferences — continued operations in Italy and possible ventures toward the Aegean and the Balkans — against the American and Soviet insistence on Overlord as the supreme operation. Stalin, who had mocked the delays of 1942 and 1943, pressed brutally for a date and a named commander; the conference fixed Overlord for May 1944, with a supporting landing in southern France (Anvil, later Dragoon), and Stalin promised a major Soviet offensive at the same time — fulfilled in June 1944 by Operation Bagration, which destroyed German Army Group Centre. At a ceremonial high point, Churchill presented Stalin with the specially forged Sword of Stalingrad, a gift from King George VI to the city; Stalin kissed the scabbard and handed it to Voroshilov, who promptly fumbled and dropped it.

Political discussions

On postwar arrangements Tehran was deliberately imprecise but consequential. The dismemberment of Germany into several states was discussed favourably without a decision. Poland's future was sketched in the form it would take at Yalta: the Curzon Line in the east, compensation at Germany's expense in the west — Churchill illustrated the shifting of Poland westward with matchsticks on the table. Turkey was pressed to enter the war (it declared war on Germany only in February 1945), Finland's position was touched on, and the three signed a Declaration on Iran guaranteeing its independence and territorial integrity and promising economic aid. Roosevelt also unfolded his idea of a postwar "United Nations" and of four policemen — the US, USSR, UK and China — to Stalin, who received it favourably. The atmosphere, on the surface, remained cordial, though Churchill left with misgivings about the Soviet Union's postwar intentions that he would voice more openly as the war ended.

Legacy

Tehran is generally regarded as the high-water mark of Big Three cooperation: it settled the strategy that won the war in Europe and sketched the postwar map that Yalta and Potsdam would fill in. Its informality — decisions taken by three men over dinners, with far-reaching consequences for nations not represented — has also made it, like Yalta, a byword in later debates about the origins of the Cold War.
