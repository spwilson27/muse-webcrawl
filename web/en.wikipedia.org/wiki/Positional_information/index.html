Positional information

Positional information is a concept in developmental biology: the idea that cells in a developing embryo or tissue acquire information about their position relative to other cells, and then interpret that information to develop in a way appropriate to their location. The concept was formalised by Lewis Wolpert in 1969 and is one of the foundational ideas of modern developmental biology.

The French flag model

Wolpert illustrated the idea with the "French flag model": imagine a line of cells that can become blue, white or red. A signal molecule — a morphogen — is produced at one end and diffuses away, forming a concentration gradient. Cells read the local concentration: above a high threshold they become blue, at intermediate concentrations white, and at low concentrations red, producing a tricoloured pattern like the French flag. The same gradient system can be rescaled to different sizes, which Wolpert used to explain the remarkable size-invariance of embryonic patterning.

Morphogen gradients

Several real morphogens have since been identified. In the early Drosophila embryo, the Bicoid protein gradient patterns the head-to-tail axis. In vertebrate limb buds, Sonic hedgehog (Shh) secreted from the zone of polarizing activity specifies digit identity, and retinoic acid and Wnt and BMP signals pattern other axes. Cells respond to morphogen concentrations through thresholds of gene expression, often involving cross-repressing transcription factors that sharpen boundaries.

Relation to other mechanisms

Positional information contrasts with self-organising mechanisms such as Turing reaction–diffusion patterns, proposed by Alan Turing in 1952, which generate pattern without a pre-existing coordinate system. Modern research generally combines both viewpoints: gradients supply global coordinates, while local interactions, cell sorting, and mechanical signals refine the pattern.
