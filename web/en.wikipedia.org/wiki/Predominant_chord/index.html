Predominant chord

In Western tonal music theory, a predominant chord (also called a subdominant chord or pre-dominant) is any chord that normally precedes a dominant chord. Predominants extend the prolongation of tonic harmony and increase the tension that the dominant resolves.

Common predominants

The most common predominant chords are the subdominant (IV in major, iv in minor) and the supertonic (ii, most often in first inversion or as ii6, and iiø7 in minor). Other chords with predominant function include the Neapolitan chord (♭II6), augmented sixth chords (Italian, French and German), the common-tone diminished seventh chord, and borrowed chords such as the minor subdominant in a major key.

Function

The standard phrase model of classical music moves from tonic to predominant to dominant to tonic (T–PD–D–T). Predominant chords characteristically contain scale degrees 4 and 6, which descend by step to the leading tone and dominant scale degree in the dominant chord, creating the characteristic melodic motion toward cadence. A cadence whose predominant moves directly to tonic without a dominant is the plagal cadence.
