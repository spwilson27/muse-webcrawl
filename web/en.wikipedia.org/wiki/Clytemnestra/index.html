In Greek mythology, Clytemnestra was the wife of Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, and the half-sister of Helen of Troy. She is famed for murdering her husband on his return from the Trojan War, and for being killed in turn by her son Orestes — the cycle of vengeance at the heart of Aeschylus' Oresteia.

Myth

Before the Trojan War, Agamemnon sacrificed their daughter Iphigenia at Aulis to obtain favourable winds for the Greek fleet. During Agamemnon's ten-year absence, Clytemnestra ruled Mycenae and took Aegisthus, Agamemnon's cousin, as her lover. When Agamemnon returned with the captive Cassandra, Clytemnestra killed them both — in most versions with an axe or dagger, after entangling Agamemnon in a robe or net in his bath. Cassandra foresaw the murders but, cursed by Apollo, was not believed.

Death and legacy

Years later, her son Orestes, urged by Apollo and his sister Electra, returned and killed Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, then was pursued by the Furies until acquitted at Athens. The figure of Clytemnestra has been interpreted both as a monstrous adulteress and as a mother avenging her murdered daughter; modern retellings often stress the latter.
