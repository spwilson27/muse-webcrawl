The Richelieu River (French: Rivière Richelieu) is a river of Quebec, Canada, and a major tributary of the St. Lawrence River. It flows northward about 124 kilometres from Lake Champlain, on the border of New York and Vermont, to Sorel-Tracy, where it enters the St. Lawrence at Lake Saint Pierre. For centuries it formed one of the principal travel and invasion corridors between the St. Lawrence valley and the Hudson River watershed.

Geography

The river's drainage basin covers about 23,720 km², most of it in the United States within the Lake Champlain watershed. After leaving Lake Champlain near Rouses Point, the Richelieu descends gently through Quebec, passing Île aux Noix, Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu, and the Chambly basin — where the Chambly Canal (opened 1843) bypasses a stretch of rapids — then Beloeil, Saint-Charles-sur-Richelieu and Saint-Denis-sur-Richelieu before reaching Sorel-Tracy. Its principal tributaries include the Hurons, L'Acadie and Lacolle rivers.

History

The river was known to Indigenous peoples for millennia and to Europeans from 1609, when Samuel de Champlain travelled it with Algonquin and Huron allies and fought a battle with Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) forces near its source; Champlain called it the "Iroquois River", and it was later named for Cardinal Richelieu. During the French regime it was guarded by a chain of forts — Fort Richelieu (1642), Fort Saint-Louis (Chambly, 1665), Fort Sainte-Thérèse and Fort Saint-Jean. In the American Revolutionary War and the War of 1812 the valley was a contested invasion route, and Fort Lennox on Île aux Noix defended the frontier. During the Lower Canada Rebellion of 1837, battles were fought at Saint-Denis (a Patriote victory) and Saint-Charles (a defeat). The Chambly Canal made the river part of a commercial waterway linking Montreal with the Hudson and New York City that operated until the mid-20th century; the canal is now a National Historic Site used by pleasure craft.

Environment and flooding

The Richelieu supports a diverse fishery and the endangered copper redhorse, found almost nowhere else. The river is prone to spring flooding; the 2011 Lake Champlain and Richelieu River floods, driven by record lake levels and wind setup, inundated thousands of homes in the Montérégie region for weeks and caused extensive damage on both sides of the border.
