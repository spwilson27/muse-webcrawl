Population bottleneck

A population bottleneck or genetic bottleneck is a sharp reduction in the size of a population due to environmental events such as famines, earthquakes, floods, fires, disease, or drought, or human activities such as genocide, habitat destruction, or deliberate slaughter. Such events can reduce the variation in the gene pool of a population; thereafter, a smaller population, with a correspondingly smaller genetic diversity, remains to pass on genes to future generations. Genetic diversity remains lowered even after the population recovers in numbers, and can be restored only through mutation or gene flow from another population.

Effects

A bottleneck reduces the effective population size, often far below the census size, because not all survivors contribute equally to the next generation. Rare alleles are disproportionately lost, while others may rise to high frequency by chance (genetic drift). Consequences include:

  • Loss of genetic variation — fewer alleles and lower heterozygosity, measurable with molecular markers.
  • Inbreeding depression — survivors mate with relatives, exposing deleterious recessive alleles and reducing fertility and survival.
  • Fixation of harmful alleles — drift in small populations can fix mildly deleterious mutations that selection would purge in large ones.
  • Reduced adaptive potential — less variation means less raw material for selection under changing conditions or new diseases.

Examples in nature

Northern elephant seal

Hunted to an estimated 20–100 individuals by the 1880s, northern elephant seals have recovered to over 200,000 animals but show extremely low genetic diversity compared with the less-hunted southern species.

Cheetah

Cheetahs have famously low genetic variation, attributed to a bottleneck roughly 10,000–12,000 years ago (and possibly an older one), leaving them unusually susceptible to disease and with high rates of abnormal sperm.

Other examples

American bison were reduced from tens of millions to a few hundred by 1884. The Mauritius kestrel fell to four known individuals in 1974. European bison descend from only 12 zoo survivors. Island endemics such as the Chatham Island black robin, reduced to five birds including a single fertile female, illustrate recovery from extreme bottlenecks. Human populations have also experienced bottlenecks: the out-of-Africa migration reduced diversity in non-African populations, isolated groups such as Ashkenazi Jews and Finns show founder-related disease patterns, and one contested hypothesis links a drastic bottleneck about 70,000 years ago to the Toba supervolcano eruption.

Founder effect

A related phenomenon, the founder effect, occurs when a new population is established by a small number of colonists carrying only a fraction of the parent population's variation. Both processes are central to conservation genetics, where managers seek to preserve diversity in endangered species through studbooks, habitat corridors, genetic rescue, and cryopreservation.

Conservation implications

Population geneticists use bottleneck signatures — for example, an excess of heterozygosity relative to allele number — to detect past crashes. The "50/500 rule" of thumb (an effective size of 50 to avoid short-term inbreeding depression, 500 for long-term adaptability) guides minimum viable population targets in conservation planning.
