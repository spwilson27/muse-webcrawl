Transit photometry

Transit photometry is a method of detecting exoplanets (and eclipsing binary stars) by measuring the slight dimming of a star as a planet passes — transits — in front of it. The depth of the dip in the star's light curve is proportional to the ratio of the areas of the planet and the star, so the method directly yields the planet's radius; combined with radial-velocity measurements giving the mass, it reveals the planet's density and bulk composition.

Method

A transit repeats with the planet's orbital period, allowing confirmation and precise timing; the shape and duration of the light curve also constrain the orbital inclination and the star's density. The probability that a given planetary system is aligned for transits falls with orbital distance (roughly 0.5% for an Earth-like orbit), so surveys monitor very large numbers of stars. During transit, starlight filtering through the planet's atmosphere can be analysed spectroscopically to detect atmospheric constituents (transmission spectroscopy), and the brightening after transit can reveal the planet's own thermal emission at secondary eclipse.

Surveys and results

Ground-based surveys such as SuperWASP and HATNet found the first transiting planets in quantity, following the landmark 1999–2000 detections for HD 209458 b. Space missions — CoRoT, NASA's Kepler and K2, and TESS — made the method the most productive planet-finding technique by number of confirmed discoveries; Kepler alone confirmed more than 2,600 planets and established that planets, including small rocky ones, are common. Its census of multi-planet systems transformed understanding of planetary system architectures.
