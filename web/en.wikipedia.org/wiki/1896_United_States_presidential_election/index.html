The 1896 United States presidential election was the 28th quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, November 3, 1896. Former Governor William McKinley, the Republican nominee, defeated former Representative William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic nominee. The campaign, unfolding in the aftermath of the Panic of 1893, is widely regarded as one of the most dramatic and decisive in American history, and is often cited as the starting point of the Fourth Party System.

Background

The election centred on economic issues, above all the debate between the gold standard and "free silver" — bimetallism at a ratio of 16 to 1 — as the basis of the currency. The incumbent Democratic president, Grover Cleveland, had defended the gold standard during the depression, splitting his party. Bryan's electrifying "Cross of Gold" speech at the 1896 Democratic National Convention in Chicago won him the nomination at age 36, making him the youngest major-party presidential nominee in American history.

Campaign

Bryan undertook an unprecedented national speaking tour, travelling some 18,000 miles and speaking to millions, while McKinley conducted a "front porch campaign" from his home in Canton, Ohio, receiving organised delegations. McKinley's campaign manager Mark Hanna raised an enormous war chest from business interests, far outspending the Democrats. The Populist Party also endorsed Bryan (with its own vice-presidential nominee, Tom Watson), forming a fusion campaign.

McKinley carried the industrial Northeast and Midwest, becoming the first Republican to win a majority of the popular vote since Ulysses S. Grant in 1872. Bryan swept the South and most of the West. The realigning election inaugurated a period of Republican dominance that lasted until 1932.
