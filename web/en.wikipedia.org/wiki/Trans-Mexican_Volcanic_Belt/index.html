Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt

The Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt (Spanish: Eje Volcánico Transversal), also known as the Sierra Nevada of Mexico, is an active volcanic arc that stretches roughly 1,000 kilometres across south-central Mexico, from the Pacific coast to the Gulf of Mexico. It results from the subduction of the Cocos and Rivera plates beneath the North American Plate.

Geography

The belt contains many of Mexico's highest peaks and most famous volcanoes, including Pico de Orizaba (the country's highest mountain), Popocatépetl, Iztaccíhuatl, Nevado de Toluca, Parícutin, and Colima. Mexico City lies within the belt's central sector, in the Valley of Mexico. Parícutin, which erupted from a cornfield in 1943 and grew into a new cinder cone, is one of the few volcanoes whose birth was witnessed and documented by scientists.

Geology

Unusually, the arc is not parallel to the Middle America Trench where subduction occurs, an obliquity attributed to the geometry and dip of the subducting Cocos slab. The belt's volcanism began in the Miocene and continues today; Popocatépetl and Colima remain among North America's most active volcanoes.
