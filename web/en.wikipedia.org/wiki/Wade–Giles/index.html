Wade–Giles

Type: Romanisation system for Mandarin Chinese

Creators: Thomas Wade (1867); modified by Herbert Giles (1892)

Status: Largely superseded by Hanyu Pinyin

Wade–Giles (sometimes abbreviated Wade) is a romanisation system for Mandarin Chinese. It was devised by the British diplomat and sinologist Thomas Francis Wade, who published the first Chinese–English dictionary and a syllabary in 1867, and was modified by Herbert Allen Giles in his Chinese–English Dictionary of 1892. For most of the 20th century it was the standard system for transcribing Chinese in the English-speaking world.

Features

The system distinguishes aspirated and unaspirated consonants with apostrophes — for example, Wade–Giles p corresponds to pinyin b, while Wade–Giles p' corresponds to pinyin p — a feature often dropped in popular usage, causing confusion. It uses superscript numerals to mark tones (e.g. ma1, ma2, ma3, ma4), hyphens to join syllables within a word, and the letters ü and ê for certain vowels. Familiar Wade–Giles spellings in English include Peking (Beijing), Mao Tse-tung (Mao Zedong), Taoism (Daoism), kung fu (gōngfu) and tai chi (tàijí).

Decline

Wade–Giles was displaced by Hanyu Pinyin, developed in the People's Republic of China in the 1950s and adopted as the ISO standard in 1982 and by the United Nations and most English-language media thereafter. It survives in Taiwan in some personal and place names, in established English words of Chinese origin, and in older scholarly literature.
