Eastern gray squirrel

Scientific nameSciurus carolinensis
FamilySciuridae
Length38–52 cm including tail
Native rangeEastern and midwestern North America

The eastern gray squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis), also known outside North America simply as the grey squirrel, is a tree squirrel native to eastern North America. It is one of the most familiar mammals of North American suburbs and city parks and an important regenerator of forests, because it buries seeds and nuts — a habit called scatter-hoarding — many of which are never retrieved and go on to germinate.

Description and behaviour

The species takes its name from the predominantly grey fur of its back, though the belly is white and melanistic (black) individuals are common in parts of the range. It builds leaf nests, called dreys, in the forks of trees and also dens in tree cavities, where litters of two to four young are raised, usually twice a year. Eastern gray squirrels feed on tree bark, buds, flowers, berries, and especially acorns, hickory nuts and walnuts.

Introduced populations

Deliberately introduced to Britain, Ireland, Italy and South Africa, the grey squirrel is regarded as an invasive species in Europe, where it outcompetes the native red squirrel and carries squirrel poxvirus, which is lethal to reds but usually harmless to greys. In the United Kingdom its spread has driven major declines of the red squirrel, and control measures and conservation refuges for reds remain controversial topics.
