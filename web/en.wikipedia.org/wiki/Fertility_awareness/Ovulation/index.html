Ovulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ovulation is the release of an egg (oocyte) from an ovary. In humans it occurs about midway through the menstrual cycle, roughly 14 days before the start of the next menstrual period, when a mature ovarian follicle ruptures and discharges the oocyte into the fallopian tube, where it may be fertilised by sperm.

Process

During the follicular phase, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) stimulates a group of follicles to grow; usually one becomes dominant and secretes increasing oestrogen. When oestrogen exceeds a threshold, its feedback on the pituitary switches from negative to positive, provoking a surge of luteinising hormone (LH). The LH surge triggers final maturation of the oocyte, weakening of the follicle wall, and ovulation some 24–36 hours later. The ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone during the luteal phase.

Signs and detection

Signs associated with approaching ovulation include clear, stretchy cervical mucus resembling raw egg white, a slight rise in basal body temperature after the event, changes in cervical position, and sometimes unilateral pelvic pain (mittelschmerz). Ovulation predictor kits detect the urinary LH surge; ultrasound can directly visualise follicular collapse.

Fertile window

The oocyte remains viable for about 12–24 hours, while sperm can survive up to about five days in fertile cervical mucus; together these define the roughly six-day fertile window ending on the day of ovulation — the biological foundation of fertility awareness methods and of conception timing.

Disorders

Failure to ovulate (anovulation) is a common cause of infertility and may result from polycystic ovary syndrome, hypothalamic dysfunction, thyroid disease or hyperprolactinaemia. Ovulation can be induced medically with agents such as clomifene or letrozole.
