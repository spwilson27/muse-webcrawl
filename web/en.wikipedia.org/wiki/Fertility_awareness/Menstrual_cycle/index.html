Menstrual cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The menstrual cycle is the regular natural change that occurs in the female reproductive system (specifically the uterus and ovaries) that makes pregnancy possible. The cycle is required for the production of oocytes and for the preparation of the uterus for pregnancy. It is counted from the first day of menstrual bleeding (day 1) to the day before the next bleed; the average length is about 28 days, with normal cycles ranging roughly from 21 to 35 days in adults.

Phases

The ovarian cycle comprises the follicular phase, in which follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) recruits a cohort of follicles and rising oestrogen rebuilds the endometrium; ovulation, triggered by a surge of luteinising hormone (LH) around mid-cycle, releasing a mature oocyte; and the luteal phase, in which the ruptured follicle forms the corpus luteum, secreting progesterone that prepares the endometrium for implantation.

The uterine cycle is described as menstruation, the proliferative phase and the secretory phase. If no implantation occurs, hormone levels fall, the endometrial lining is shed as menstrual blood, and a new cycle begins.

Hormonal control

The cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus drives pituitary release of FSH and LH, which in turn direct ovarian production of oestrogen and progesterone, whose feedback modulates the axis.

Clinical aspects

Cycles begin at menarche (average age about 12) and continue until menopause (around 51). Disorders include amenorrhea (absent periods), dysmenorrhea (painful periods), menorrhagia (heavy bleeding) and premenstrual syndrome. Tracking the cycle is fundamental to fertility awareness methods and to reproductive medicine.
