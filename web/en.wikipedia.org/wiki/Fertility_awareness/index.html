Fertility awareness

Fertility awareness (FA) is a set of practices used to determine the fertile and infertile phases of the menstrual cycle from primary fertility signs, principally basal body temperature, cervical mucus, and (in some methods) cervical position and calendar calculations. Fertility awareness-based methods (FABMs) can be used either to avoid pregnancy — in which case they are also called natural family planning (NFP) when used within a religious framework — or to increase the chance of conception by timing intercourse to the fertile window.

Fertile window

An ovum survives about 12–24 hours after ovulation, while sperm can survive up to about five days in fertile-quality cervical mucus, giving a fertile window of roughly six days per cycle. Pregnancy is impossible outside this window, which is the physiological basis for all fertility awareness methods: identify when the window opens and closes and behaviour can be adjusted accordingly.

Primary fertility signs

Basal body temperature (BBT), measured on waking, rises by about 0.2–0.5 °C after ovulation under the influence of progesterone and stays elevated until menstruation, confirming ovulation retrospectively. Cervical mucus changes prospectively: as oestrogen rises before ovulation it becomes clear, stretchy and slippery (resembling raw egg white), then turns scant and sticky afterwards. Cervical position also changes, becoming higher, softer and more open near ovulation. Symptothermal methods cross-check at least two signs; mucus-only methods include the Billings ovulation method and the Creighton Model; calendar-based methods include the Standard Days method, usable for cycles of 26–32 days.

Effectiveness

With perfect use, modern symptothermal methods report first-year pregnancy rates under 1–2 per 100 women; typical-use rates for FABMs as a group are considerably higher (often cited around 12–24 per 100 women per year across older studies), reflecting the teaching, motivation and partner cooperation the methods require. Effectiveness depends heavily on correct instruction and on abstaining or using barrier methods during the identified fertile window.

History and acceptance

The temperature method grew out of early-20th-century discoveries linking temperature shifts to ovulation; the mucus method was developed by John and Evelyn Billings in Australia in the 1950s–60s. The Catholic Church permits natural family planning while prohibiting artificial contraception, which has driven development and teaching of these methods; secular interest has grown with period-tracking and fertility apps, some of which have been cleared by regulators as contraceptive software.
