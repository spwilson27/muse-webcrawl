Mini–Mental State Examination

The Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE) or Folstein test is a 30-point questionnaire used extensively in clinical and research settings to measure cognitive impairment. It was introduced by Marshal Folstein and colleagues in 1975 as a brief, standardized method of grading patients' cognitive state, and it is commonly used in screening for dementia.

Administration and content

The MMSE takes about five to ten minutes to administer. Its items sample several cognitive domains: orientation to time and place (10 points), registration of three words (3), attention and calculation, typically counting backwards by sevens or spelling a word backwards (5), recall of the three words (3), language tasks including naming, repetition, following a three-stage command, reading and writing (8), and copying a visual design of intersecting pentagons (1).

Interpretation

Scores of 24 or above (out of 30) are conventionally regarded as normal, while lower scores are often graded as mild (19–23), moderate (10–18) or severe (≤9) impairment. Scores are influenced by age and educational attainment, so norms adjusted for these factors are recommended. The test has good reliability but limited sensitivity for mild cognitive impairment and frontal/executive dysfunction, and it shows ceiling effects in highly educated individuals and floor effects in severe dementia.

Use and licensing

The MMSE became one of the most widely used cognitive screening instruments in medicine and a standard outcome measure in clinical trials of anti-dementia drugs. Because the test is copyrighted and commercially licensed through Psychological Assessment Resources, some clinicians have shifted to alternatives such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).
