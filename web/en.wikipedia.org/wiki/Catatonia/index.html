Catatonia is a neuropsychiatric syndrome characterised by abnormalities of movement and behaviour arising from a disturbed mental state. It is most commonly associated with mood disorders such as bipolar disorder and major depression, but also occurs in schizophrenia and a range of medical conditions including autoimmune encephalitis.

Signs and symptoms

Classic features include stupor, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerisms, stereotypies, waxy flexibility (in which the patient's limbs remain in positions into which they are placed), echolalia and echopraxia. An excited form, marked by purposeless and frenzied motor activity, contrasts with the more common retarded or stuporous form. A life-threatening variant, malignant catatonia, involves fever and autonomic instability.

History

The syndrome was first described in 1874 by the German psychiatrist Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum, who regarded it as a distinct disease with a cyclical course. Emil Kraepelin later folded catatonia into dementia praecox (schizophrenia), an association that dominated classification for much of the twentieth century and contributed to under-recognition of catatonia in mood disorders. Modern classifications, including the DSM-5, treat catatonia as a specifier that can accompany several diagnoses or arise from another medical condition.

Treatment

Catatonia characteristically responds to benzodiazepines such as lorazepam, sometimes dramatically within hours. Electroconvulsive therapy is highly effective, particularly in cases resistant to medication or in malignant catatonia. Antipsychotic drugs are generally avoided during acute catatonia because they may worsen the syndrome or precipitate neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
