Spelling bee

A spelling bee is a competition in which contestants are asked to spell a broad selection of words, usually with a varying degree of difficulty. The concept originated in the United States, where spelling contests — called "bees" after the old sense of a communal gathering for work — became a staple of school life in the nineteenth century, encouraged by Noah Webster's spelling books.

Format

Competitions are typically single-elimination: a pronouncer reads a word, often supplying its definition, language of origin, and a sentence on request; the contestant spells it aloud and is eliminated on an error, traditionally signalled by the ringing of a bell. Rounds continue with progressively harder words until a champion remains.

National competition

The most prominent event is the Scripps National Spelling Bee, first held in 1925 and based in Washington, D.C. Winners qualify through school and regional bees and must not have passed the eighth grade or turned fifteen. The Bee has crowned famous winning words such as gladiolus (1925), logorrhea, Laodicean (2009), knaidel (2013), and murmuration; in 2019, eight contestants were declared co-champions after twenty rounds. Spelling bees are also held in many other English-speaking countries and have been adapted for other languages, though languages with transparent orthographies pose less challenge.

Culture

Top competitors study word roots, etymology, and language patterns from resources such as Merriam-Webster's dictionary. The phenomenon has inspired documentaries and films, including Spellbound (2002) and Akeelah and the Bee (2006), and since the 1990s a large proportion of finalists and champions have been Indian-American students, a trend widely noted in the press.
