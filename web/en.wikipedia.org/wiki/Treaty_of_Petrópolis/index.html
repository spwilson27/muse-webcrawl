Treaty of Petrópolis

Treaty of Petrópolis
Signed17 November 1903, Petrópolis, Brazil
PartiesBrazil and Bolivia
SubjectPurchase of Acre; Brazil–Bolivia boundary
Consideration£2,000,000; a small territorial exchange; construction of the Madeira–Mamoré Railway
Brazilian negotiatorsBaron of Rio Branco; Joaquim Francisco de Assis Brasil
Bolivian negotiatorsFernando Guachalla; Claudio Pinilla

The Treaty of Petrópolis, signed on 17 November 1903 in the Brazilian city of Petrópolis, ended the dispute between Brazil and Bolivia over the rubber-rich territory of Acre. By its terms Bolivia ceded to Brazil some 191,000 km² of Amazonian territory — the future Brazilian state of Acre — in exchange for two million pounds sterling, a small cession of Brazilian land in Mato Grosso, Bolivian navigation rights on Brazilian rivers, and a Brazilian commitment to build a railway around the rapids of the Madeira River. It remains the largest territorial acquisition in Brazilian history achieved by negotiation, and the masterpiece of Brazil's foreign minister, the Baron of Rio Branco.

Background

Although the Treaty of Ayacucho (1867) had drawn the Brazil–Bolivia boundary so as to leave the Acre region in Bolivia, the rubber boom from the 1870s drew tens of thousands of Brazilian tappers up the Purus and Acre rivers, and by the 1890s the territory's population, trade, and customs revenue were effectively Brazilian. Bolivia's belated assertion of sovereignty — a customs post at Puerto Alonso (1899) and, more alarmingly for Brazil, the 1901 concession of Acre's administration to the Bolivian Syndicate, an Anglo-American company with quasi-governmental powers — triggered revolts by the Brazilian settlers: first the quixotic Republic of Acre of the Spaniard Luis Gálvez (1899–1900), then the organized Acrean Revolution of 1902–1903 under José Plácido de Castro, who defeated the Bolivian garrisons at Xapuri and Puerto Alonso. With Bolivian forces expelled and a costly war in the offing, both governments turned to diplomacy. Brazil first bought out the Bolivian Syndicate's concession, compensating it for surrendering its rights, then opened formal negotiations.

Terms

  • Bolivia ceded the Acre territory to Brazil, and the boundary was redrawn along lines substantially matching Brazil's proposals, with a small reciprocal cession of Brazilian territory between the Madeira and Abunã rivers in Mato Grosso.
  • Brazil agreed to pay Bolivia £2,000,000 sterling in two installments.
  • Brazil undertook to build, within four years, the Madeira–Mamoré Railway from Porto Velho to Guajará-Mirim, bypassing the impassable rapids of the Madeira, so that Bolivian trade from the Beni and Mamoré could reach the Amazon and the Atlantic. (Completed in 1912 at enormous cost in money and workers' lives, the "Devil's Railway" was largely obsolete on arrival, as the rubber boom collapsed.)
  • Bolivia received free transit and navigation rights for its commerce on the Amazon system.

Aftermath

The Brazilian Congress ratified the treaty amid celebration of Rio Branco's diplomacy; Acre was organized as a federal territory in 1904 and became a state in 1962. Bolivia's loss of Acre — its only Amazonian rubber province — remained a sore point in Bolivian national memory, though historians note that Bolivia, unable to hold the territory against its Brazilian population, converted an unenforceable claim into cash, territory nearer its heartland, and a railway outlet to the Atlantic. The treaty also set the pattern of Rio Branco's boundary diplomacy: negotiated settlements, backed by demographic and geographic facts on the ground, that fixed nearly all of Brazil's modern frontiers within a decade.
