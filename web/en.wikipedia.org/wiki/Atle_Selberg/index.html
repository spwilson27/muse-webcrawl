Atle Selberg (14 June 1917 – 6 August 2007) was a Norwegian mathematician known for his work in analytic number theory and the theory of automorphic forms. He is regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of the 20th century.

Work

During the Second World War, working in isolation in occupied Norway, Selberg proved fundamental results about the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, showing that a positive proportion of zeros lie on the critical line. In 1948–49 he produced, independently alongside Paul Erdős, an elementary proof of the prime number theorem — an achievement that brought him the Fields Medal in 1950.

His name is attached to the Selberg sieve, the Selberg trace formula, the Selberg zeta function, the Selberg integral and the Rankin–Selberg method. The trace formula, relating spectral data of automorphic forms to geometric data, became one of the most powerful tools in modern number theory.

Life

Selberg was inspired as a schoolboy by the collected works of Srinivasa Ramanujan. He took his doctorate at the University of Oslo in 1943, emigrated to the United States in 1947, and spent the rest of his career at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he remained until his death in 2007.
