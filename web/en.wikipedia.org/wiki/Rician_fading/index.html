Rician fading

Rician fading or Ricean fading is a statistical model for radio signal fading in which a dominant, steady component — typically a line-of-sight path — is present together with many weaker scattered multipath components. The received signal amplitude then follows a Rice distribution, named after Stephen O. Rice, who pioneered the statistical theory of noise and fading in the 1940s.

The model

In a Rician channel, the received signal consists of a strong direct path plus numerous reflected and scattered signals whose phases vary randomly. The ratio of the power in the dominant component to the power in the scattered components is called the Rician K-factor. When K is large (strong line of sight), fading is mild; as K approaches zero, the line-of-sight component disappears and the model reduces to the more severe Rayleigh fading case.

Applications

The Rician model is widely used in wireless communications engineering to characterise mobile radio channels, satellite links and indoor wireless LAN propagation, where a partial line-of-sight path often exists. It is closely related to the Nakagami-m distribution, which can approximate Rician behaviour. System designers use the K-factor to predict error rates and outage probabilities and to size fade margins.
