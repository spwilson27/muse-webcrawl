Suffering Servant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Suffering Servant passages appear in the Book of Isaiah.

The Suffering Servant is a figure described in the Book of Isaiah of the Hebrew Bible, chiefly in the four passages known as the Servant Songs (Isaiah 42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–7; 52:13–53:12). The servant is chosen by God, suffers affliction and rejection, and ultimately is vindicated; in the climactic fourth song he bears the sins of many and is "wounded for our transgressions".

The Servant Songs

The songs occur within "Deutero-Isaiah" (chapters 40–55), generally dated to the period of the Babylonian exile in the 6th century BC. The most famous passage reads:

He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief... Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities. (Isaiah 53:3–5)

Interpretations

The identity of the servant has been debated for centuries. Jewish interpretation has most often understood the servant collectively as the nation of Israel, suffering among the nations but destined for vindication; some rabbinic sources also applied the passages to Moses, to the righteous remnant, or to a future Messiah. Christian interpretation, from the New Testament onward, has understood the Suffering Servant as a prophecy of Jesus Christ, whose crucifixion was seen as the fulfilment of Isaiah 53; the Ethiopian eunuch's question in Acts 8 — "of whom does the prophet say this?" — is answered with Jesus. Modern critical scholars variously identify the servant with Israel, with the prophet himself, or with an individual figure such as the exiled king Jehoiachin, or treat the songs as an idealised portrait applied to different referents at different stages of editing.

Influence

The imagery of the suffering servant has deeply influenced Jewish and Christian theology, concepts of vicarious atonement, and Western literature and music, including Handel's Messiah.
