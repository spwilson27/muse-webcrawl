Chinatown, Manhattan

Chinatown, Manhattan (simplified Chinese: 曼哈顿华埠) is a neighbourhood in Lower Manhattan, New York City, forming the largest Chinatown in the United States and one of the largest Chinese enclaves outside Asia. Centred on Canal Street and its intersecting streets, it is home to a large Chinese American population and a dense concentration of restaurants, markets, and shops.

History

Chinese immigrants began settling around Mott Street in the 1870s, many arriving from the American West, where they had built railroads and worked in the California gold fields, and driven east in part by anti-Chinese violence. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 sharply restricted new immigration, and the community remained a "bachelor society" of mostly men for decades. Repeal of exclusion in 1943 and the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 brought waves of new arrivals, and Chinatown expanded into the Lower East Side and beyond, while new Chinese communities later grew in Flushing, Queens, and Sunset Park, Brooklyn.

Economy and culture

The neighbourhood's economy was long anchored by garment factories, restaurants, and import businesses along Canal, Mott, Pell and Doyers streets. Doyers Street's sharp bend earned it the nickname "the Bloody Angle" during tong wars of the early 20th century. Today, Chinatown draws visitors for its food — from dim sum palaces to hand-pulled noodle shops — and its Lunar New Year parade is one of the city's major annual festivals. Landmarks include the Kim Lau Memorial Arch at Chatham Square, honouring Chinese American war dead, and Columbus Park, a centre of daily mahjong and tai chi.
