The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (NREGA) is an Indian social security and labour law that guarantees the "right to work" by providing at least 100 days of wage employment in a financial year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. It was renamed the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) on 2 October 2009. The programme it created is the largest public employment scheme of its kind in the world.

History

The act drew on earlier employment assurance schemes, notably Maharashtra's Employment Guarantee Scheme of the 1970s. Passed by the United Progressive Alliance government, it came into force on 2 February 2006 in 200 selected districts and was extended to all rural districts of India by April 2008. The law made employment a legal entitlement: if work is not provided within 15 days of application, the state must pay an unemployment allowance.

Provisions

  • Adult members of a rural household may apply for work through their gram panchayat and receive a job card.
  • Work must generally be provided within 5 kilometres of the village; wages are paid at the state-notified rate, with equal wages for men and women.
  • At least one-third of beneficiaries must be women; in practice women have accounted for around half of all person-days generated.
  • Works focus on water conservation, drought proofing, irrigation, land development, and rural roads.
  • Social audits by village assemblies and public disclosure of muster rolls are mandatory transparency measures.

Impact and criticism

Studies have credited MGNREGA with reducing rural poverty, raising agricultural wage rates, curbing distress migration, and empowering women through independent earnings, with payments increasingly routed through bank and post-office accounts. Critics point to delayed wage payments, uneven asset quality, leakage through ghost workers and falsified rolls, and wide variation in implementation between states. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, demand for work under the scheme surged to record levels as migrant workers returned to their villages, and the government raised its budget allocation substantially.
