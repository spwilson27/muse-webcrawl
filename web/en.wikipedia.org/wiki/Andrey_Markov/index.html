Andrey Andreyevich Markov (14 June 1856 – 20 July 1922) was a Russian mathematician best known for his work on stochastic processes. His research on sequences of dependent random variables gave rise to the theory of Markov chains and Markov processes, which now underpin fields from statistical physics and queueing theory to computational linguistics and machine learning. He also proved results in number theory, analysis and the theory of continued fractions.

Life and career

Markov was born in Ryazan and studied at St. Petersburg University, where he was a student of Pafnuty Chebyshev. He graduated in 1878, won a gold medal for work on differential equations, and completed his master's thesis on continued fractions in 1880. He taught at St. Petersburg University for most of his career, becoming a professor in 1894 and a member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences.

Markov was outspoken on political matters. In 1912 he protested the Russian Orthodox Church's excommunication of Leo Tolstoy by requesting his own excommunication; in 1913 he pointedly celebrated the 200th anniversary of the law of large numbers rather than the Romanov dynasty's tercentenary.

Markov chains

Markov began studying chains of dependent variables around 1906, initially to extend the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem beyond independent variables. A sequence in which the probability of each state depends only on the immediately preceding state — the Markov property — became the model bearing his name. In 1913 he famously applied the method to the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, counting vowel–consonant sequences and demonstrating that the letters behave as a dependent chain, one of the earliest statistical analyses of a text.

Other work

Markov's earlier research included a proof (with his brother Vladimir, who died young) of results on polynomials — the Markov brothers' inequality bounds the derivative of a polynomial in terms of its maximum value. His textbook Calculus of Probabilities (1900) was influential in Russia. His son, Andrey Markov Jr., became a noted mathematician and logician.
