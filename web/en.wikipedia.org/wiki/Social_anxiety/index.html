Social anxiety

Social anxiety is the anxiety, fear and discomfort experienced in social situations, involving concern about being judged, evaluated negatively, or rejected by others. It exists on a continuum from ordinary shyness and performance nervousness to the clinically significant condition known as social anxiety disorder (SAD), also called social phobia.

Characteristics

People experiencing social anxiety worry about embarrassing or humiliating themselves in situations such as meeting new people, public speaking, eating or drinking in public, using public restrooms, or being the centre of attention. Anticipatory anxiety may begin days or weeks before an event, and afterwards the person may ruminate over perceived mistakes — a pattern termed post-event processing. Physical symptoms can include blushing, sweating, trembling, rapid heartbeat, nausea and difficulty speaking.

Social anxiety disorder

When fear and avoidance become marked, persistent (typically for six months or more) and interfere with work, school or relationships, the diagnosis of social anxiety disorder applies. It is one of the most common anxiety disorders, with lifetime prevalence estimates around 7–13% in Western populations, and typical onset in adolescence. The disorder is frequently comorbid with depression and substance use, sometimes because people self-medicate social discomfort with alcohol.

Causes

Social anxiety arises from a combination of genetic temperament (behavioural inhibition in childhood), neurobiological factors involving the amygdala and serotonergic systems, learned experiences such as bullying or humiliating events, and cultural norms. Cognitive models emphasize biased attention toward threatening social cues, overestimation of the likelihood and cost of negative evaluation, and safety behaviours (such as avoiding eye contact) that maintain the fear.

Treatment

First-line treatments include cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), particularly exposure-based and group CBT, which teaches patients to test feared predictions in real social situations. Medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are also effective; beta blockers are sometimes used for performance-only anxiety. Untreated social anxiety disorder tends to follow a chronic course, but treatment outcomes are generally favourable.
