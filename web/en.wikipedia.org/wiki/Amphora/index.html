Amphora

An amphora (Greek: ἀμφορεύς, amphoreús; plural amphorae or amphoras) is a type of ceramic vessel with two vertical handles and a neck narrower than the body, used from prehistoric times through the ancient Mediterranean world for the transport and storage of liquid and dry goods — above all wine and olive oil, but also grain, olives, honey and fish sauce (garum). The name refers to the two handles by which it could be carried on both sides.

Forms and use

Transport amphorae typically had a pointed or button toe, allowing them to be stacked tightly in ships' holds, set in sand, or leaned in racks, and a narrow neck sealable with cork, clay or resin — wine amphorae were often lined with pine pitch. Shapes were standardised by producing city: distinctive forms from Chios, Thasos, Rhodes, and later Roman types such as the Dressel 1 and Dressel 20 (for Baetican olive oil) let archaeologists date and source finds precisely. Stamps on handles can name the potter, magistrate or estate. Separate from transport types were fine painted neck amphorae and the Panathenaic prize amphorae, filled with sacred olive oil and awarded to victors at the Panathenaic Games in Athens.

Archaeological importance

Because they survive in enormous numbers — Rome's Monte Testaccio is an artificial hill composed of an estimated 53 million discarded oil amphora fragments — amphorae are among the most informative artefacts for reconstructing ancient trade networks, diet and economy. The amphora also served as a Roman unit of volume, the amphora quadrantal of about 26 litres.
