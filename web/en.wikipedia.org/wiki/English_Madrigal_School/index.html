English Madrigal School

The English Madrigal School was the intense flowering of the musical madrigal in England, mostly from 1588 to 1627, contemporary with the final decades of the English Renaissance. Madrigals — secular part-songs for unaccompanied voices, setting short poems with expressive word-painting — had been perfected in Italy, and the English school adapted the form to English texts and English musical tastes, favouring lighter, rhythmically lively types such as the ballett and the ayre alongside more serious madrigals proper.

Nicholas Yonge's Musica transalpina (1588) launched the English madrigal vogue.

Origins

The immediate spark was Nicholas Yonge's Musica transalpina (1588), a collection of Italian madrigals by Marenzio, Lassus and others fitted with English translations, which proved enormously popular with amateur singers in Elizabethan households. William Byrd's Psalmes, Sonets and Songs (also 1588) provided native exemplars. Thomas Morley, the school's chief theorist and entrepreneur, published canzonets, balletts (with their characteristic "fa-la-la" refrains, modelled on Gastoldi) and madrigals through the 1590s, and codified the genres in his Plaine and Easie Introduction to Practicall Musicke (1597).

Major composers and works

Morley's anthology The Triumphs of Oriana (1601), in which 23 composers contributed madrigals honouring Elizabeth I ("Oriana"), marked the school's zenith. Thomas Weelkes (1576–1623) wrote the most harmonically daring English madrigals, such as "As Vesta was from Latmos hill descending" (for the Triumphs) and "O care, thou wilt despatch me", with vivid chromatic word-painting. John Wilbye (1574–1638), in two books (1598, 1609), achieved a more intimate, expressive style in pieces like "Draw on, sweet night" and "Flora gave me fairest flowers". Orlando Gibbons's First Set of Madrigals and Motets (1612) contains "The Silver Swan", perhaps the most famous English madrigal of all. Other notable figures include John Ward, Thomas Tomkins, John Farmer ("Fair Phyllis I saw sitting all alone") and Michael East.

Decline

After about 1613 publication of madrigals slowed sharply, as taste shifted toward the lute ayre, the masque and, eventually, declamatory song in the Italian manner. The last significant collection of the school appeared in the 1620s. The madrigal's amateur singing culture survived in catches and glees, and the works themselves were rediscovered by the nineteenth-century Musical Antiquarian Society and the twentieth-century early-music revival.
