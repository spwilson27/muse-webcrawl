Continental shelf

A continental shelf is a portion of a continent that is submerged under an area of relatively shallow water known as a shelf sea. Much of the shelf was exposed during glacial periods and interglacial periods. The shelf surrounding an island is known as an insular shelf. The continental margin, between the shelf edge and the abyssal plain, consists of the steep continental slope followed by the flatter continental rise.

Geography

The shelf usually ends at a point of increasing slope called the shelf break. Water depths over the shelf average about 60 metres; the shelf itself varies enormously in width — from a few kilometres off mountainous coasts such as western South America to over 1,000 kilometres in the Arctic off Siberia (the Siberian Shelf, the world's widest). The global average width is about 65 kilometres. During the last glacial maximum, when sea level was about 120 metres lower, broad shelves such as the Sunda Shelf and the Bering land bridge were dry land.

Ecology and resources

Shelf seas are the most biologically productive parts of the ocean: sunlight penetrates the shallow water column, and nutrients well up at the shelf break, supporting major fisheries such as those of the North Sea, the Grand Banks and the Patagonian Shelf. Continental shelves also host most offshore oil and gas production, including fields in the Gulf of Mexico, the North Sea and offshore Brazil, as well as sand and gravel deposits and prospective sites for offshore wind farms.

Legal definition

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, a coastal state has sovereign rights over the continental shelf for exploring and exploiting its natural resources. The legal shelf extends to at least 200 nautical miles from shore, and may extend further — up to 350 nautical miles or beyond under geological criteria — where the submerged prolongation of the landmass reaches further; such extended shelf claims are assessed by the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf. Extended-shelf disputes, notably in the Arctic and the South China Sea, are a significant subject of international politics.
