The Mediterranean monk seal (Monachus monachus) is a monk seal belonging to the family Phocidae (the earless seals). It is one of the rarest marine mammals in the world, with a population estimated at fewer than 1,000 individuals, distributed in small subpopulations in the Mediterranean Sea and the eastern Atlantic Ocean, including around the Cabo Blanco peninsula in Mauritania/Western Sahara and the Desertas Islands of Madeira.

Description

Adults average about 2.4 metres in length and weigh roughly 230–280 kg, males being slightly larger than females. The coat is brown to grey above and paler below, often with a light belly patch; pups are born with a black coat and a white or yellowish belly patch. Monk seals feed on fish, octopus, squid, and lobsters, diving along rocky coasts and resting in sea caves, where pups are also born — a behaviour that makes them highly sensitive to human disturbance of coastal caves.

Distribution and history

Once widespread throughout the Mediterranean, the Black Sea, and the Atlantic coasts of north-west Africa, the species was hunted since antiquity for its skin, oil, and meat, and was later persecuted by fishermen as a competitor. By the late 20th century it had vanished from most of its range, surviving in Greece, Turkey, Cyprus, Croatia, and a few Atlantic sites. The largest single colony, at Cabo Blanco, suffered a mass mortality event in 1997 that killed roughly two-thirds of its animals.

Conservation

Protected under the Bonn and Barcelona Conventions and national laws, the species has slowly recovered: colonies in Greece and at Cabo Blanco have grown, and seals have reappeared in parts of Italy, Lebanon, Israel, and elsewhere. In 2023 the IUCN improved its status from Endangered to Vulnerable, reflecting a genuine but fragile recovery. Marine protected areas such as the National Marine Park of Alonnisos Northern Sporades in Greece and dedicated rescue and rehabilitation centres support its conservation.
