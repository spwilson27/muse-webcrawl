The Jupiter trojans, commonly called Trojan asteroids, are a large group of asteroids that share the orbit of Jupiter around the Sun, clustered around the planet's two stable Lagrange points L4 (60° ahead of Jupiter) and L5 (60° behind), in a 1:1 orbital resonance with the planet.

Discovery and naming

The first trojan, 588 Achilles, was discovered by German astronomer Max Wolf in 1906. By convention, asteroids at L4 (the "Greek camp") are named after Greek heroes of the Trojan War and those at L5 (the "Trojan camp") after Trojan heroes, as recounted in the Iliad; the two camps were accidentally "contaminated" early on by 617 Patroclus (in the Trojan camp) and 624 Hektor (in the Greek camp), the "two spies".

Numbers and composition

More than a million Jupiter trojans larger than 1 km are estimated to exist — comparable to the main asteroid belt — of which over 10,000 have been catalogued. They are dark, reddish, spectrally featureless bodies, mostly classified as D-type asteroids with some P- and C-types, covered in organic-rich material resembling cometary nuclei. Their density is low, suggesting porous interiors of rock and ice.

Origin

Current models hold that the trojans were captured into Jupiter's co-orbital zones during the migration of the giant planets early in Solar System history (the Nice model), so they may be samples of the primordial planetesimal population from which the outer planets formed — including material more like Kuiper Belt objects than main-belt asteroids.

Exploration

NASA's Lucy spacecraft, launched in October 2021, is the first mission to the trojans; it will fly by several L4 and L5 asteroids, ending with the binary pair Patroclus–Menoetius in 2033. Lucy also discovered a satellite around the main-belt asteroid Dinkinesh during a 2023 flyby.
