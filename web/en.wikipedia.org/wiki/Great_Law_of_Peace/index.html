The Great Law of Peace (Mohawk: Gayanashagowa) is the oral constitution of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), under which the disparate, frequently warring nations of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca were united. According to tradition, it was delivered by the prophet known as the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida) and his spokesman Hiawatha, with the sanction of the clan mother Jigonhsasee, sometimes called the Mother of Nations. Proposed dates for the founding of the confederacy vary widely, from the 12th to the 15th century; a frequently cited date is 1142, associated in some interpretations with a solar eclipse.

Contents and structure

The Great Law establishes the Grand Council of fifty rotiyaner (chiefs, often rendered "lords" or "sachems") appointed by clan mothers, the titles being hereditary through matrilineal clans. The Onondaga keep the council fire and the wampum records. Its provisions govern condolence and installation of chiefs, decision-making by consensus among the nations, the roles of clan mothers (who could nominate and depose chiefs), war and peace, and the "burying of weapons" beneath the Great Tree of Peace — an eastern white pine whose white roots extend to the four directions for other nations to follow and join. The union is symbolized by the bundle of five arrows that cannot be broken together.

Record and influence debate

The law was preserved in wampum belts and recitation; written versions were compiled in the 19th and early 20th centuries by Haudenosaunee knowledge-keepers and collaborators such as Seth Newhouse, Arthur C. Parker, and John Arthur Gibson. A debated scholarly and popular thesis holds that the confederacy influenced the framers of the United States Constitution; in 1988 the US Congress passed a concurrent resolution acknowledging the Iroquois Confederacy's contribution to American political thought, a claim many historians treat with caution while affirming Benjamin Franklin's documented familiarity with the confederacy's councils.
