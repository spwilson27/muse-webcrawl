Phineas P. Gage (1823–1860) was an American railroad construction foreman remembered for his improbable survival of an accident in which a large iron rod was driven completely through his head, destroying much of his brain's left frontal lobe. The case became a foundational example in neurology and psychology of the relation between the frontal lobes and personality.

The accident

On 13 September 1848, near Cavendish, Vermont, Gage was packing explosives into rock with a tamping iron — a rod 3 feet 7 inches long and 1.25 inches in diameter — when the charge detonated prematurely. The rod shot through his cheek and skull and landed some 80 feet away. Astonishingly, Gage remained conscious, walked to an oxcart, and was treated by Dr John Martyn Harlow, under whose care he recovered physically over the following months.

Personality changes and later life

Harlow and Gage's employers reported that after the accident Gage was "no longer Gage": the previously capable, well-liked foreman became fitful, irreverent, profane and unreliable, unable to hold his old job. Modern scholarship has debated how exaggerated these accounts became in retelling; Gage later worked as a stagecoach driver in Chile for several years, a structured occupation suggesting substantial social recovery. He returned to the United States in 1859 as his health failed, and died in 1860 after a series of epileptic seizures.

Scientific legacy

Gage's skull and tamping iron are preserved at the Warren Anatomical Museum of Harvard Medical School. The case influenced nineteenth-century debates on cerebral localisation and phrenology, and it remains a standard textbook example — studied by neurologists including Antonio Damasio — of frontal-lobe function in personality, planning and social conduct.
