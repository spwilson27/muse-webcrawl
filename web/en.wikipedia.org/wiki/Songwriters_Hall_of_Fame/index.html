Songwriters Hall of Fame

The Songwriters Hall of Fame (SHOF) is an American institution dedicated to recognising the work of composers and lyricists who create music for the popular music industry. It was founded in 1969 by songwriter Johnny Mercer and publishers Abe Olman and Howie Richmond, and is administered by the National Academy of Popular Music.

History and mission

Mercer lamented that songwriters received little public recognition compared with performers; SHOF was created to honour writers of the popular songbook and to educate the public through archives, scholarships and showcases. The first inductions in 1969–1971 honoured dozens of Tin Pan Alley and standards writers, including Irving Berlin, George Gershwin and Cole Porter.

Inductions and awards

Songwriters become eligible 20 years after their first significant commercial release. Inductees span standards, Broadway, country, R&B and rock: notable members include Bob Dylan, Carole King, Paul McCartney, Dolly Parton, Bruce Springsteen and the writers of standards such as "Son of a Preacher Man"'s generation of Memphis and Brill Building craftsmen. Special awards include the Johnny Mercer Award (for a body of work of exceptional quality), the Sammy Cahn Lifetime Achievement Award, the Hal David Starlight Award for young writers and the Towering Song Award.

Gallery and archives

A physical gallery opened at the Grammy Museum in Los Angeles in 2010, and an online museum presents songwriter biographies, discographies and oral histories. SHOF also runs songwriting workshops and the SongHall university network.
