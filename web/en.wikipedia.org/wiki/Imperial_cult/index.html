Imperial cult

Temples to deified emperors were built across the Roman world

An imperial cult is a form of state religion in which an emperor or a dynasty of emperors (or rulers of another title) are worshipped as demigods or gods. In the polytheistic ancient world, emperors were often deified during their lifetimes (living cult) or after death, and temples, priests, sacrifices, and festivals were devoted to them.

Roman imperial cult

The best-known example is the cult of the Roman emperors. Its roots lay in the Roman veneration of the genius of the paterfamilias, Hellenistic ruler cults, and the deification of Julius Caesar after his assassination in 44 BC. Augustus carefully promoted worship of his genius and of Roma in the provinces while avoiding direct claims of godhood in Rome during his lifetime. Deceased emperors judged worthy were formally deified (divus) by decree of the Senate. Participation in the imperial cult became a test of loyalty to the empire, and refusal to sacrifice to the emperor's image was one cause of the persecution of early Christians.

Other examples

The pharaohs of ancient Egypt were regarded as gods incarnate. In imperial China, the emperor ruled with the Mandate of Heaven and performed state sacrifices, though he was not typically worshipped as a god. In Japan, the emperor was traditionally considered a descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu, and State Shinto promoted reverence for the emperor until 1945, when Emperor Hirohito publicly renounced the concept of his divinity.
