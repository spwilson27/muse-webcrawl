Intestinal villus


System: Digestive system
Location: Small intestine

Intestinal villi (singular: villus) are small, finger-like projections that extend into the lumen of the small intestine. Each villus is between about 0.5 and 1.6 mm long in humans and is covered with still smaller projections called microvilli, which together form the brush border. The villi vastly increase the internal surface area of the intestinal wall, greatly enhancing the absorption of nutrients.

Structure

Each villus is covered by enterocytes and goblet cells over a core of connective tissue (the lamina propria) containing a capillary network, a central lacteal (lymphatic vessel), smooth muscle fibres, nerves, and immune cells. Nutrients such as amino acids and sugars pass into the capillaries and on to the portal vein, while absorbed fats enter the lacteal as chyle. Between the villi lie the crypts of Lieberkühn, whose stem cells continually renew the epithelium every few days.

Function

Together with the circular folds (plicae circulares) and microvilli, the villi multiply the absorptive surface of the small intestine many-fold. Damage to the villi — notably the villous atrophy of coeliac disease — reduces absorption and causes malabsorption syndromes.
