Surface (topology)

In topology, a surface is a two-dimensional manifold: a topological space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open disk in the Euclidean plane (or, for surfaces with boundary, to a half-disk). Familiar examples include the sphere, the torus, the cylinder, the Möbius strip and the Klein bottle.

Classification

The classification theorem for surfaces states that every closed (compact, boundaryless) surface is homeomorphic to exactly one of the following: the sphere; a connected sum of g tori (the orientable surfaces, where g is the genus, the number of "handles"); or a connected sum of k real projective planes (the non-orientable surfaces). Surfaces with boundary are obtained by removing open disks from these. The fundamental invariant distinguishing them is the Euler characteristic,

χ = 2 − 2g  (orientable),    χ = 2 − k  (non-orientable),

together with orientability and the number of boundary components. Historically the theorem grew out of work by Möbius, Jordan, von Dyck and others in the 19th century; rigorous proofs were completed in the early 20th century.

Surfaces in geometry

By the uniformisation theorem, every surface carries a metric of constant curvature: positive (spherical), zero (flat, as on the torus) or negative (hyperbolic, for all higher-genus orientable surfaces). Surfaces are studied through their fundamental groups, mapping class groups, and embeddings in three-space; a closed orientable surface of genus g embeds in ℝ³, while the Klein bottle and projective plane require self-intersection in ℝ³ and embed smoothly only in ℝ⁴.
