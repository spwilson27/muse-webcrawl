Homo sacer (Latin for "sacred man") is a figure of ancient Roman law: a person who has been banned by the community may be killed by anyone with impunity, yet may not be sacrificed in a religious ritual. The term gained wide currency in political philosophy through the Italian philosopher Giorgio Agamben's book Homo Sacer: Sovereign Power and Bare Life (1995; English translation 1998), the first volume of a long-running series of the same name.

Agamben's argument

Agamben takes the homo sacer as the paradigm of "bare life" (zoē, mere biological existence, as opposed to bios, the qualified life of the citizen). Drawing on Carl Schmitt's definition of the sovereign as "he who decides on the state of exception", and on Michel Foucault's concept of biopolitics, Agamben argues that sovereign power is founded on the capacity to declare a state of exception in which law suspends itself and life is exposed to violence without legal protection. For Agamben, this structure is not an aberration but the hidden foundation of Western politics, and its extreme manifestation is the concentration camp, which he calls the "nomos of the modern".

Reception

The book became one of the most influential works of continental political theory of its generation, widely discussed in philosophy, law, anthropology and refugee studies. Later volumes in the series include State of Exception (2003) and The Kingdom and the Glory (2007). Critics have questioned Agamben's reading of the Roman sources, his treatment of the camps as a political paradigm, and the applicability of the state of exception to contemporary measures such as anti-terrorism law and responses to the COVID-19 pandemic, on which Agamben also wrote controversially.
