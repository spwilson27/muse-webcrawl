Dental caries

Dental caries, also known as tooth decay or cavities, is the breakdown of teeth due to acids produced by bacteria. The cavities may be a number of different colours from yellow to black. Symptoms may include pain and difficulty with eating; complications may include inflammation of the tissue around the tooth, tooth loss, and infection or abscess formation. Dental caries is one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, affecting some 2.5 billion people in their permanent teeth according to the World Health Organization.

Cause and progression

Caries results from the interaction of cariogenic bacteria in dental plaque — notably mutans streptococci and lactobacilli — with fermentable carbohydrates. Bacterial metabolism produces acid that lowers plaque pH; below the critical pH (~5.5), enamel demineralizes. Early lesions appear as chalky white spots and may be reversed by remineralization with fluoride and saliva. If demineralization outpaces repair, the enamel surface breaks down into a true cavity; decay then spreads through dentin toward the pulp, producing pulpitis, necrosis, and periapical infection.

Treatment and prevention

Management ranges from remineralizing therapy and sealants for early lesions to restorations (fillings), crowns, root canal treatment, and extraction for advanced destruction. Silver diamine fluoride can arrest lesions non-surgically. Prevention rests on twice-daily brushing with fluoride toothpaste, interdental cleaning, limiting the frequency of sugar consumption, community water fluoridation where adopted, and regular dental care. The global economic burden of caries and other oral diseases runs to hundreds of billions of dollars annually.
