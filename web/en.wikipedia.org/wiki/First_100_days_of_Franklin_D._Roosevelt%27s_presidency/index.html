First 100 days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency

First 100 days of FDR's presidency Period: 4 March – 16 June 1933
President: Franklin D. Roosevelt
Context: Great Depression; the New Deal
Key measures: Emergency Banking Act, CCC, AAA, TVA, NIRA, Glass–Steagall Act
Legacy: origin of the "first hundred days" benchmark for presidents

The first 100 days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency, beginning with his inauguration on 4 March 1933, saw an unprecedented burst of legislation as the United States grappled with the Great Depression. Roosevelt called Congress into special session and signed fifteen major bills, launching the New Deal and setting the benchmark by which the early productivity of later presidents is often measured.

Banking crisis

Roosevelt took office amid a nationwide banking panic. He proclaimed a four-day national bank holiday and pushed through the Emergency Banking Act on 9 March, providing for federal inspection and reopening of solvent banks. His first "fireside chat" on 12 March reassured the public, and deposits flowed back when banks reopened. In June the Glass–Steagall Act separated commercial and investment banking and created federal deposit insurance.

Relief and recovery

Major measures included the Civilian Conservation Corps, which employed young men on conservation projects; the Federal Emergency Relief Act; the Agricultural Adjustment Act, which paid farmers to reduce production; the Tennessee Valley Authority for regional development and electrification; and the National Industrial Recovery Act, establishing the National Recovery Administration and the Public Works Administration.

Legacy

The session closed on 16 June 1933. The scale and speed of the programme transformed expectations of the federal government and entrenched the media convention of judging new presidents by their first hundred days.
