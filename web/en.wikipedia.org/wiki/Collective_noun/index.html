Collective noun

In linguistics, a collective noun is a word referring to a collection of things taken as a whole. Examples in English include committee, family, team, flock, and herd. Most collective nouns are ordinary nouns used for a group; but English also has a rich stock of specialised "terms of venery" for animal groups, such as a pride of lions, a murder of crows, an exaltation of larks, and a parliament of owls.

Agreement

Collective nouns raise a well-known question of grammatical agreement: whether they take a singular or plural verb. British English commonly allows plural agreement when the group is viewed as individuals ("the team are arguing among themselves"), while American English generally prefers singular agreement ("the team is winning"), reserving plural pronouns where the sense demands them.

Terms of venery

Many animal group names derive from the hunting terminology of the fifteenth century, recorded in works such as the Book of Saint Albans (1486) attributed to Dame Juliana Berners. Some of these terms were deliberately fanciful or humorous even then, and the tradition of inventing new ones — for professions and modern objects as well as animals — continues as a linguistic game. In modern usage, many listed terms are rarely used by specialists, who more often say simply "group", though a few, like school of fish and pack of wolves, are standard.
