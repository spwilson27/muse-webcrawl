A strip of 16 mm film

16 mm film is a historically popular and economical gauge of film stock developed by Eastman Kodak and released to the market in 1923. The name refers to the width of the film stock, 16 millimetres. Because it used acetate ("safety") film rather than flammable nitrate, and because its cameras and projectors were inexpensive and portable, 16 mm became the standard gauge for amateur, educational, and industrial filmmaking, and later a major format for television news and independent feature production.

History

Kodak introduced 16 mm in 1923 together with the Cine-Kodak camera and Kodascope projectors, marketing it to amateur filmmakers as a safe alternative to 35 mm nitrate. During the Second World War the format was used extensively for military training, reconnaissance, and newsreel documentation. After the war it spread through schools, churches, and businesses, and became the workhorse of television news gathering until videotape displaced it in the 1970s and 1980s.

Technical specifications

Standard 16 mm film has sprocket holes along one or both edges and a picture area of about 10.26 by 7.49 mm, with an aspect ratio near 1.37:1. Sound films carry an optical or magnetic soundtrack along the edge opposite a single row of sprockets. Super 16, introduced in the 1970s, extends the image into the soundtrack area for a wider frame suited to blow-up to 35 mm. The format runs at 40 frames per foot, so a 100-foot roll provides about 2 minutes 47 seconds at 24 frames per second.

Modern use

Although largely superseded by digital production, 16 mm remains in use by independent and art filmmakers for its distinctive grain and texture, by archivists, and in film schools. Kodak continues to manufacture 16 mm stocks, and the format has seen a modest revival in music videos and independent features.
