Presbyterian polity

Presbyterian polity is a method of church governance ("ecclesiastical polity") typified by the rule of assemblies of presbyters, or elders. Each local church is governed by a body of elected elders usually called the session or consistory, and decisions are made by officers rather than by the laity as a whole (congregational polity) or by a hierarchy of bishops (episcopal polity). Presbyterian polity is characteristic of Presbyterian and continental Reformed churches.

Principles

The system rests on theological convictions drawn from the New Testament, in which governing elders (presbyteroi) appear alongside overseers, and from the practice of councils such as that in Acts 15. Key principles are the parity of ministers — no minister outranks another in office — and the accountability of each assembly to wider assemblies, so that decisions of lower "courts" can be reviewed by higher ones.

Structure

Governance proceeds through graded courts:

  • Session (or kirk session/consistory): the teaching elder(s) (minister(s)) and ruling elders of a single congregation, moderated by the minister. It admits members, exercises discipline and oversees congregational life.
  • Presbytery: the ministers and representative ruling elders of all congregations in a district. It ordains, installs and oversees ministers, and oversees congregations.
  • Synod: in larger denominations, a regional assembly between presbytery and the general assembly.
  • General assembly: the highest court, representing the whole denomination, which sets standards of doctrine and worship and hears appeals.

Offices

Presbyterian churches recognise ordained offices of minister (teaching elder), ruling elder and deacon — sometimes described as two or three offices depending on tradition. Elders are ordinarily elected by the congregation and ordained; teaching and ruling elders sit together as equals in the courts of the church.

History

Presbyterian government was worked out during the Reformation, above all by John Calvin in Geneva (Ecclesiastical Ordinances, 1541) and in the writings of Theodore Beza. John Knox and Andrew Melville carried it to Scotland, where the First and Second Books of Discipline (1560, 1578) laid its foundations and the Church of Scotland adopted it definitively. It spread with Reformed churches to France, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Ireland, North America and, through missions, worldwide. In England it was championed by the Westminster Assembly of the 1640s but never established nationally.

Comparison

Presbyterian polity is a middle way between episcopacy and congregationalism: churches are neither independent of one another nor ruled by bishops, but connected through representative assemblies in which ministers and elders share authority.
