Thymus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The thymus is a specialised primary lymphoid organ of the immune system, located in the upper chest behind the sternum and in front of the heart. Within it, immature T cells (T lymphocytes) mature and are selected so that they can attack foreign material without attacking the body's own tissues.

Development and function

The thymus is most active in childhood and reaches its largest size around puberty; thereafter it gradually shrinks and is replaced by fat (involution), though it continues producing some T cells in adult life. In the thymus, developing T cells are positively selected for recognising the body's MHC molecules and negatively selected — destroyed — if they react strongly against self antigens, establishing central tolerance.

Clinical significance

Congenital absence of the thymus (as in DiGeorge syndrome) causes severe immunodeficiency. The thymus is linked to the autoimmune disease myasthenia gravis, in which it is often enlarged or contains tumours (thymomas), and surgical removal is a recognised treatment.
