IG Farben Trial

The IG Farben Trial (United States of America v. Carl Krauch, et al.) was one of the twelve trials for war crimes that the United States authorities held in their occupation zone in Germany in Nuremberg after the end of World War II, collectively known as the Subsequent Nuremberg trials. It was held from 14 August 1947 to 30 July 1948.

Indictment

Twenty-four executives of the German chemical conglomerate IG Farben were charged with crimes against peace (waging wars of aggression), war crimes and crimes against humanity, mass murder and enslavement, spoliation and plunder of occupied countries, and membership in a criminal organization (the SS). The company had built a vast synthetic fuel and rubber plant at Monowitz, adjacent to the Auschwitz concentration camp, using slave labor from the camp; thousands of prisoners died there. IG Farben also held a stake in the firm that supplied Zyklon B gas to the camps.

Verdict

Thirteen of the defendants were found guilty on some counts and sentenced to prison terms ranging from eighteen months to eight years; the remainder were acquitted. The sentences were criticized by many as lenient, and most of the convicted men were released early in the 1950s, several going on to senior positions in the post-war German chemical industry.

Significance

The trial is a principal historical case on corporate complicity in war crimes and crimes against humanity, and its records documented in detail the relationship between German big business and the Nazi regime.
