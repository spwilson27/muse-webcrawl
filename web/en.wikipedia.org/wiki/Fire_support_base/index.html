Fire support base

Fire support base
TypeTemporary military encampment
PurposeArtillery fire support for infantry operations
Notable eraVietnam War (1965–1973)

A fire support base (FSB, or firebase) is a temporary military encampment widely used during the Vietnam War to provide artillery fire support to infantry operating in areas beyond the normal range of fire support from their own base camps. Because artillery itself needs protection and support, firebases were set up as self-contained positions combining guns, infantry security, communications, logistics and medical facilities.

Design and layout

A typical fire support base was built around a battery of four to six howitzers — commonly 105 mm or 155 mm guns — positioned at the centre, with mortars, a command post, ammunition bunkers and a helicopter landing zone. Defences included perimeter wire, claymore mines, fighting bunkers manned by an infantry company, watchtowers and cleared fields of fire. Bases were positioned so that neighbouring firebases could support each other with overlapping artillery range.

Use

Fire support bases allowed infantry units, inserted by helicopter in the airmobile doctrine of the war, to operate anywhere within range of the guns. Bases were frequently established for a single operation and then abandoned or relocated. Their fixed positions and predictable routines, however, made them targets for sapper attacks, mortar and rocket fire, and occasional ground assaults, as at Fire Support Base Ripcord in 1970 and the overrunning of Fire Base Mary Ann in 1971.

Legacy

The concept evolved into the forward operating bases (FOBs) and combat outposts used by coalition forces in Iraq and Afghanistan, which combine fire support, logistics and force protection in a similar, if larger and more permanent, form.
