Plasticine

Plasticine is a putty-like modelling material made from calcium salts, petroleum jelly, and aliphatic acids. It is used mainly by children for play, and by animators and sculptors as a non-drying modelling medium. Plasticine does not harden in air, remaining malleable so it can be reshaped indefinitely; it cannot be fired like clay, and it softens or melts when heated.

History

Plasticine was invented in 1897 by William Harbutt, an art teacher in Bath, England, who wanted a modelling material for his sculpture students that would not dry out between lessons. He patented the material in 1899 and began commercial production at a factory in Bathampton in 1900. The original formula was grey; coloured versions for children followed, and the product was sold in distinctive striped sticks. Harbutt's company later became part of Flair Leisure Products; production of the original British Plasticine ceased at Bathampton in the 1980s, and the brand has since been manufactured under licence in Thailand and elsewhere.

A similar material, Plastilin, had been invented in Munich in 1880 by Franz Kolb and was sold in Germany; the two products coexisted, and oil-based modelling clays of this family are generically called plastilina in many countries.

Uses

Because it never sets, plasticine is the classic material of clay animation ("Claymation"), in which figures are slightly repositioned between photographed frames; Aardman Animations' Wallace and Gromit films popularised the technique. Sculptors use it for studies and maquettes, engineers for prototypes, forensic and industrial users for impression work, and it is also used to plug and seal, to make dams for electroplating, and as a reusable adhesive-free filler. Its softness varies with temperature, and colours can be kneaded together, though mixed colours eventually tend toward brown-grey.

Composition and safety

The material consists mainly of a powdered mineral filler (traditionally calcium carbonate or gypsum) bound with petroleum jelly and fatty acids such as stearic acid, plus pigments. It is regarded as non-toxic and is approved for children's use, though it is not intended to be eaten and cannot be sterilised by baking.
