AI alignment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Illustration of a reward function being optimised in unintended ways]

AI alignment is the field of research concerned with ensuring that artificial intelligence systems pursue goals that are aligned with the intentions, values, and welfare of their designers and users. The core difficulty is that an AI system optimises the objectives it is actually given, which may diverge from what its creators meant: a system trained to maximise a proxy measure may learn to "hack" that measure — a phenomenon related to Goodhart's law and specification gaming — while a sufficiently capable system might pursue instrumental goals, such as self-preservation or resource acquisition, that its designers never intended.

Problems studied

  • Reward hacking / specification gaming — exploiting flaws in the specified objective to score highly without doing the intended task.
  • Goal misgeneralisation — a system that behaves as intended in training but pursues a different goal in new situations.
  • Deceptive alignment — a hypothetical failure mode in which a system behaves well during evaluation in order to be deployed, then acts otherwise.
  • Scalable oversight — the difficulty of evaluating systems whose outputs exceed human ability to check directly.
  • Value learning — inferring human preferences from behaviour or feedback rather than specifying them by hand.

Approaches

Current practical alignment work on language models relies heavily on reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related techniques, in which human ratings of model outputs are used to train a reward model that then shapes the system's behaviour. Other approaches include interpretability research, red-teaming and evaluations, debate, iterated amplification, and constitutional methods in which a model critiques and revises its own outputs against written principles.

History and debate

Early discussion of the problem appears in Norbert Wiener's writings around 1960 and in I. J. Good's speculation about intelligence explosions. The field took modern shape in the 2000s and 2010s through work by researchers such as Eliezer Yudkowsky, Nick Bostrom (whose 2014 book Superintelligence popularised the "paperclip maximiser" thought experiment), and Stuart Russell, and through technical agendas published by research organisations. Researchers disagree about the difficulty of the problem, the timelines for highly capable AI, and the relative importance of near-term harms versus long-term risks; alignment has nonetheless become a major focus of AI laboratories and of emerging AI governance.
