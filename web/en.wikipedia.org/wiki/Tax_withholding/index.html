Tax withholding


Payroll withholding deducts tax from wages before payment to the employee

Tax withholding, also known as tax retention, pay-as-you-earn (PAYE), or withholding at source, is income tax paid by the payer of income rather than by the recipient. The payer deducts tax from wages, salaries, pensions, interest, dividends, or other payments and remits it directly to the government on the recipient's behalf. The withheld amounts are treated as advance payments credited against the recipient's final tax liability.

How it works

Employers calculate withholding using tables or formulas issued by the tax authority, taking account of information supplied by the employee (such as the US Form W-4 or a UK tax code). The employer remits the deducted tax periodically, reports totals on information returns (such as Form W-2 in the United States), and the employee reconciles withholding against actual liability when filing an annual return — receiving a refund if too much was withheld or paying the balance if too little.

By country

United States: Federal income tax withholding was introduced in its modern form by the Current Tax Payment Act of 1943. Employers must also withhold Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes. Withholding also applies to gambling winnings, pensions, and certain payments to foreign persons, whose income is often taxed at a flat 30% rate subject to treaty reduction.

United Kingdom: The PAYE system, introduced in 1944, uses cumulative tax codes so that for most employees the correct tax is deducted over the year with no need for an annual return.

Other systems: Australia's PAYG withholding, Canada's payroll deductions, Germany's Lohnsteuer, and India's TDS (tax deducted at source) are analogous mechanisms.

Withholding beyond employment

Withholding is also used to collect tax on interest, dividends, royalties, and payments to non-residents, where it is frequently the final tax. Internationally, withholding taxes on cross-border dividends and interest are a major subject of tax treaties. "Backup withholding" applies in the US when a payee fails to provide a valid taxpayer identification number.

Advantages and criticisms

Withholding gives governments a steady revenue stream and greatly improves compliance, since tax is collected before income reaches the taxpayer. Behavioural economists note that it also reduces the perceived pain of taxation. Critics argue it burdens employers with unpaid collection work and that systematic over-withholding amounts to an interest-free loan from taxpayers to the state.
