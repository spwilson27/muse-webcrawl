Constructivist architecture

Constructivist architecture was an architectural movement in the Soviet Union during the 1920s and early 1930s, the architectural wing of the broader Constructivist art movement. It combined advanced structural engineering with an avant-garde artistic commitment to building a new socialist society.

History

After the Russian Revolution, architects such as the Vesnin brothers, Konstantin Melnikov, Moisei Ginzburg and El Lissitzky sought an architecture for the new society: workers' clubs, communal housing (dom-kommuna), factories and power stations. The OSA Group (founded 1925) published the journal SA (Sovremennaya Arkhitektura).

Characteristics

Constructivist buildings expose their structure, use industrial materials (steel, glass, reinforced concrete), and favor dynamic, asymmetrical compositions — intersecting cylinders, cantilevered volumes and ribbon windows.

Key architects and buildings

  • Melnikov House, Moscow (1927–29) — two interlocking cylinders with hexagonal windows.
  • Narkomfin Building, Moscow (1928–30, Ginzburg & Milinis) — communal housing prototype.
  • Rusakov Workers' Club, Moscow (1927–29, Melnikov).
  • Zuev Workers' Club, Moscow (1926–28, Golosov).

Decline and legacy

By 1932 the movement was suppressed in favor of Stalinist neoclassicism. Constructivism influenced Deconstructivism, high-tech architecture and the International Style.
