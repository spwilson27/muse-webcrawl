Epoche

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Epoché (Ancient Greek: ἐποχή, epokhḗ, "suspension" or "cessation") is an ancient Greek philosophical term describing the suspension of judgment about the real nature of things. In the skeptical tradition, epoché is the state of withholding assent to any non-evident proposition, adopted in order to attain ataraxia — freedom from disturbance. The term was revived in the twentieth century by Edmund Husserl, for whom the epoché became the foundational methodological operation of phenomenology.

Epoché: the balance of judgment held in suspension.

Skepticism

The concept is central to Pyrrhonism, the school founded by Pyrrho of Elis. As reported by Sextus Empiricus in the Outlines of Pyrrhonism, the skeptic, confronted with opposing arguments of equal strength (isostheneia) on any matter, suspends judgment — and finds, unexpectedly, that tranquility follows "as a shadow follows the body". Epoché thus differs from dogmatic denial: the skeptic does not assert that truth is unattainable, but simply declines to assent, living by appearances and customs. The Academic skeptics of Plato's Academy also employed epoché, though their position was often interpreted as the (dogmatic) claim that nothing can be known.

Husserl and phenomenology

Husserl adopted the term for his own method, most fully in Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology (1913). The phenomenological epoché "brackets" or "puts out of action" the natural attitude — our everyday, unquestioned belief in the existence of the world — without denying that the world exists. By suspending judgments about existence, the phenomenologist can study phenomena purely as they are given to consciousness, together with the intentional acts in which they are given. The epoché is the first step of the phenomenological reduction, which Husserl regarded as the gateway to phenomenology as a rigorous science of consciousness. Later phenomenologists, including Merleau-Ponty — who remarked that the most important lesson of the reduction is the impossibility of a complete reduction — adapted the operation to their own ends.

Other uses

In Stoic writing the term could describe suspension more generally, and it passed into later philosophical usage wherever judgment is deliberately withheld. It should not be confused with epoch, the chronological term for a reference point in time, though the two words share the same Greek root.
