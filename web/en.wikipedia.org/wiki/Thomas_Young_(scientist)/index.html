Thomas Young FRS (13 June 1773 – 10 May 1829) was an English physician and polymath who made notable contributions to the fields of vision, light, solid mechanics, energy, physiology, language, musical harmony, and Egyptology. He was instrumental in the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs, specifically the Rosetta Stone.

Young has been described as "the last man who knew everything". His work influenced that of William Herschel, Hermann von Helmholtz, James Clerk Maxwell, and Albert Einstein. He is credited with establishing the wave theory of light, in opposition to the corpuscular theory of Isaac Newton.

Biography

Young was born in Milverton, Somerset, into a Quaker family in 1773, the eldest of ten children. He was a child prodigy, learning to read at the age of two; by the age of six he had read through the Bible twice and could recite poetry in Latin. He studied medicine in London, Edinburgh, and Göttingen, and set up a medical practice in London in 1799, though he was not especially successful as a physician.

In 1801 Young was appointed professor of natural philosophy at the Royal Institution. In 1804 he resigned the professorship to avoid interfering with his medical practice. He became foreign secretary of the Royal Society in 1802 and remained in the post for the rest of his life.

Wave theory of light

Young's most famous work is his interference experiments on light. In his 1801 Bakerian lecture and subsequent papers, he presented evidence that light behaves as a wave, most famously through the double-slit experiment, in which light passed through two narrow slits produces an interference pattern of bright and dark bands. This overturned the prevailing corpuscular theory of light held since Newton, though the wave theory only gained general acceptance later in the nineteenth century through the work of Augustin-Jean Fresnel.

Young's modulus and elasticity

In 1807 Young introduced the concept now known as Young's modulus, a measure of the stiffness of a solid material defined as the ratio of stress to strain. He also contributed to the theory of surface tension, and the Young–Laplace equation describes the pressure difference across the interface of a fluid.

Vision and colour

Young proposed that the retina responds to three principal colours — the trichromatic theory of colour vision later developed by Helmholtz as the Young–Helmholtz theory. He also explained how the eye accommodates to different distances by changing the curvature of the lens, and was the first to describe astigmatism.

Egyptology

Young was one of the first to attempt to decipher the Egyptian hieroglyphs. Working on the Rosetta Stone and the Demotic script, by 1819 he had correctly identified several hieroglyphic signs and the names of Ptolemy and Berenice. His work laid the groundwork for Jean-François Champollion's full decipherment in the 1820s, and the two men later disputed priority.

The word "energy"

Young was the first to use the word energy in its modern physical sense, in his 1807 lectures, replacing the older term vis viva.
