Cartel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cartel is a group of independent market participants who collude to fix prices, limit output or divide markets, maintaining the group's advantage against competitors and consumers. Cartels are the core target of competition (antitrust) law: per se illegal under § 1 of the US Sherman Act (1890), prohibited by Article 101 TFEU in the EU, and policed by fines that have reached into the billions (the European Commission's truck cartel fines of 2016–17, the global LIBOR and foreign-exchange enforcement actions against banks).

Economists since Adam Smith (“people of the same trade seldom meet together... but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public”) have noted cartels' internal instability — each member profits by cheating — which enforcement exploits through leniency programmes rewarding the first defector with immunity. Legally tolerated or sovereign examples blur the category: OPEC+ coordinates state producers outside antitrust's jurisdiction; shipping conferences and airline alliances have held partial immunities; and state-sanctioned crisis cartels operated openly in interwar Germany and Japan. Famous illegal cartels — lysine, vitamins, auto parts, LCD panels — document the practice's global reach and the difficulty of detection estimated in empirical studies of overcharges (median overcharges commonly estimated around 20–25 per cent, with wide variance).
