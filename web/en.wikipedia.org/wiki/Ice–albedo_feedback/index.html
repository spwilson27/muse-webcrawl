Ice–albedo feedback

Ice–albedo feedback is a climate change feedback in which a change in the area of ice caps, glaciers, and sea ice alters the albedo (reflectivity) of the planet, which in turn changes the temperature that caused the ice to change. It is a classic example of a positive feedback loop: warming melts ice, exposing darker surfaces beneath, which absorb more solar radiation and cause further warming and melting.

Mechanism

Snow and ice are among the brightest natural surfaces on Earth, reflecting 50 to 90 percent of incoming sunlight back to space. The surfaces they cover — open ocean, bare rock, tundra, and forest — are much darker; open ocean reflects as little as 6 percent. When ice retreats, the newly exposed surface absorbs far more energy. That absorbed energy warms the surface and the air above it, melting yet more ice, closing the loop.

The feedback works in reverse during cooling: expanding ice raises planetary albedo, reflects more sunlight, and accelerates cooling. This helps explain the rapid growth of ice sheets during the onset of glacial periods and the phenomenon of snowball Earth episodes in deep geological history.

Importance

Ice–albedo feedback is a major contributor to polar amplification, the observation that the Arctic has warmed several times faster than the global average. The decline of Arctic sea ice since the late 1970s has measurably darkened the Arctic Ocean in summer, and satellite analyses estimate that the resulting extra absorbed energy is a significant fraction of the warming effect of recent carbon dioxide increases. The feedback is included in all modern climate models and is one of the faster-responding feedbacks in the climate system, though its overall contribution is smaller than that of water vapour, lapse-rate, and cloud feedbacks on global scales.

Related processes

A related effect concerns snow cover on land: earlier spring snowmelt exposes dark ground and vegetation sooner, adding to seasonal warming. On sea ice, the formation of melt ponds in summer lowers the ice's own albedo, accelerating its decay before open water appears.
