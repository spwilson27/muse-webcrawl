Travesti (theatre)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Travesti is a theatrical term referring to the portrayal of a character in an opera, play, or other performance by a performer of a different sex. The term comes from the Italian travestire ("to disguise"), and the resulting roles are variously called travesti roles, breeches roles, trouser roles (in opera), or principal boy roles (in British pantomime), depending on direction and context.

Forms

Historically, the most common reason for travesti was exclusion: in many periods women were barred from the stage, so female roles were played by men or boys — as in classical Greek theatre, Elizabethan England (where boy actors played Shakespeare's heroines), traditional Japanese kabuki (onnagata), and Chinese opera (dan roles played by men such as Mei Lanfang). Conversely, where women were admitted, male roles taken by women developed their own conventions.

In opera

The operatic trouser role (Hosenrolle) is a male character — usually an adolescent youth — sung by a woman, typically a mezzo-soprano or contralto. Famous examples include Cherubino in Mozart's The Marriage of Figaro, Octavian in Richard Strauss's Der Rosenkavalier, Siebel in Gounod's Faust, and the Composer in Strauss's Ariadne auf Naxos. The convention succeeded the castrato roles of baroque opera: Handel's heroic male leads, written for castrati, are nowadays usually sung by women in travesti or by countertenors.

In pantomime and popular theatre

British pantomime maintains two complementary travesti traditions: the principal boy, the young male hero played by a woman in tights, and the pantomime dame, a comic older woman played by a man. Victorian burlesque and music hall also featured both male impersonators such as Vesta Tilley and female impersonators.

Modern practice

Contemporary directors use cross-gender casting for artistic as well as conventional reasons — all-female Shakespeare productions, gender-swapped revivals, and new works exploring gender identity. The term travesti should not be confused with the Latin American social identity of the same name, which refers to a transfeminine gender identity distinct from theatrical practice.
