Model organism

Model organism
ExamplesE. coli, S. cerevisiae, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, Arabidopsis thaliana, laboratory mouse
Selection criteriaShort generation time, ease of culture, small genome, genetic tractability
PioneersT. H. Morgan (Drosophila), Sydney Brenner (C. elegans)

A model organism is a non-human species that is extensively studied in the laboratory to understand biological processes, on the expectation that discoveries in the model will provide insight into other organisms, including humans. Model organisms are chosen for experimental convenience — short life cycles, cheap maintenance, ease of genetic manipulation — balanced against their relevance to the questions being asked.

Major models

The bacterium Escherichia coli underpinned molecular genetics; the yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe model the eukaryotic cell cycle. The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, used by Thomas Hunt Morgan from 1910, established the chromosomal theory of heredity. The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, promoted by Sydney Brenner, has a fully mapped cell lineage and connectome. The thale cress Arabidopsis thaliana is the principal plant model, while the zebrafish and the laboratory mouse (Mus musculus) serve vertebrate developmental biology and medicine respectively.

Strengths and limitations

Shared ancestry means fundamental mechanisms — DNA replication, cell division, developmental gene networks — are conserved; roughly three quarters of human disease genes have a counterpart in the fly. Yet models can mislead: mice differ from humans in immunity and metabolism, and findings must be validated across systems. Dedicated stock centres and databases (such as FlyBase and WormBase) maintain strains and data for the community.
