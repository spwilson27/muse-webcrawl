Foggia (Italian pronunciation: [ˈfɔddʒa]) is a city and comune of Apulia, in southern Italy, capital of the province of Foggia. In the past it was a major agricultural centre and market town on the Tavoliere plain, the great wheat-growing region sometimes called the "granary of Italy".

History

The area was settled in antiquity, near the ancient town of Arpi. The modern city grew around a medieval settlement whose name plausibly derives from the Latin fovea, a pit — pits dug in the area to store grain. Foggia prospered under the Normans and under Frederick II, who built a palace there in the 13th century and frequented the area for hunting. The city was severely damaged by an earthquake in 1731 and was rebuilt. During the Second World War, its airfields and rail yards made it a target of heavy Allied bombing in 1943.

Main sights

Foggia Cathedral, dedicated to Santa Maria de Fovea, dates to the 12th century and was rebuilt after the 1731 earthquake in Baroque style. The Palazzo Dogana, once the customs house for the transhumant sheep trade, is part of the UNESCO-listed network of transhumance routes. The city also preserves stretches of its old walls and the Frederick II palace arch.
