Ottonian art

Ottonian art is a style in pre-Romanesque German art, covering roughly the period from about 919 to 1024 — the reigns of the Ottonian dynasty of Holy Roman Emperors, from Henry the Fowler to Henry II. It drew on late antique, Carolingian and Byzantine models and expressed the renewed imperial ambitions of the Ottonians (renovatio imperii), centring on the great cathedrals, abbeys and imperial courts of Saxony, the Rhineland and southern Germany.

Manuscript illumination

Ottonian art is best known for its sumptuously illuminated manuscripts, produced in scriptoria such as Reichenau, Trier, Cologne, Regensburg and Echternach. Masterpieces include the Gospels of Otto III, the Bamberg Apocalypse, the Pericopes of Henry II and the Hitda Codex. Their style features figures set against shimmering gold grounds, highly expressive enlarged hands and eyes, and an abstract, hierarchical approach to space quite different from Carolingian naturalism. The "Master of the Registrum Gregorii" at Trier is the best-known individual artist of the age.

Metalwork, ivory and architecture

Imperial patronage also produced celebrated goldsmith work — the Cross of Lothair in Aachen, the Golden Madonna of Essen (the oldest known sculpted figure of the Virgin in the West, c. 980), and the bronze doors and column commissioned by Bishop Bernward for Hildesheim (c. 1015) — together with carved ivories for book covers. In architecture, Ottonian churches such as St Michael's at Hildesheim and the abbey church at Gernrode developed the alternating-support nave and double-ended plans that bridge Carolingian and Romanesque building.
