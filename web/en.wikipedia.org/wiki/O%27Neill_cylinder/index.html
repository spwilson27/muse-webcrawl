O'Neill cylinder

An O'Neill cylinder is a proposed space settlement design consisting of two enormous counter-rotating cylinders, each about 8 kilometres in diameter and 32 kilometres long, whose inner surfaces provide land and simulated gravity through rotation. The concept was introduced by physicist Gerard K. O'Neill in his 1976 book The High Frontier: Human Colonies in Space and is also known as an "Island Three" design.

Design

Each cylinder's interior is divided lengthwise into six panels: alternating land areas and huge windows. Mirrors hinged along the windows reflect sunlight into the habitat, and the angle of the mirrors simulates a day-night cycle. Counter-rotation cancels gyroscopic precession, allowing the colony to keep its mirrors pointed at the Sun. At the proposed radius and a rotation of roughly half a revolution per minute, inhabitants on the inner surface would experience Earth-like gravity. Agriculture would be carried out in separate, smaller rotating cylinders optimised for crops.

Background

O'Neill developed the concept with his students at Princeton beginning in 1969 while seeking large habitats that did not require planetary surfaces. The design drew wide attention through Stewart Brand's publications and NASA summer studies in the 1970s. O'Neill cylinders have since become a standard reference point in discussions of space colonisation and a recurring setting in science fiction.
