Orobanche, commonly known as broomrape, is a genus of over 150 species of parasitic plants in the family Orobanchaceae. They are mostly native to the temperate Northern Hemisphere and are best known as serious pests of agriculture. Broomrapes are holoparasites: they lack chlorophyll, cannot photosynthesise, and depend entirely on a host plant for water and nutrients, attaching to its roots by means of a specialised organ called a haustorium.

Description

Above ground, an Orobanche plant consists of a fleshy, yellowish, brownish, or purplish unbranched stem bearing scale-like leaves and a dense spike of tubular, two-lipped flowers, often in shades of violet, yellow, or brown. A single plant can produce hundreds of thousands of dust-like seeds, each well under a millimetre long, which can persist in the soil for a decade or more.

Life cycle and parasitism

The seeds germinate only when they detect chemical signals — strigolactones — exuded by the roots of a suitable host. The emerging radicle grows toward the host root, forms a haustorium that penetrates the root and connects to the host's vascular system, and develops underground into a tubercle that accumulates resources before sending up the flowering spike. Because nearly all damage occurs before the parasite emerges, infestations are difficult to detect and control in time.

Agricultural impact

Species such as Orobanche crenata (on legumes), O. cumana (on sunflower), O. aegyptiaca and O. ramosa (on tomato, tobacco, and other solanaceous crops), and O. minor (on clover) cause severe yield losses around the Mediterranean, in eastern Europe, and in parts of Asia and Africa. Control strategies include resistant crop varieties, trap crops that stimulate suicidal germination, hand-pulling before seed set, soil solarisation, and carefully timed herbicide applications.
