Zionism is a nationalist movement that emerged in Europe in the late 19th century among Jews, aimed at establishing and maintaining a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. After a long period of Jewish exile and in the context of rising antisemitism in Europe, Zionist thinkers argued that Jewish emancipation and safety required national self-determination. The movement achieved its central political goal with the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, and since then has generally meant support for Israel as a Jewish state.

History

Jewish attachment to the Land of Israel, referred to in the Bible as Zion, has ancient religious roots, and small numbers of Jews returned to the region throughout the centuries of diaspora. Modern Zionism arose in the 19th century, influenced both by European nationalism and by antisemitic persecution, including pogroms in the Russian Empire. Precursors such as Moses Hess and Leon Pinsker argued for Jewish national revival before the movement was organized politically.

The Viennese journalist Theodor Herzl, shaken by the antisemitism surrounding the Dreyfus affair in France, published Der Judenstaat ("The Jewish State") in 1896, arguing that the Jewish question was a national question requiring a political solution. In 1897 he convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel, Switzerland, which adopted the Basel Program and founded the World Zionist Organization.

Streams and development

Zionism developed several strands. Political Zionism, associated with Herzl, pursued international diplomatic recognition of a Jewish homeland. Practical Zionism emphasized immigration and settlement in Palestine, which proceeded in waves (aliyot) from the 1880s onward. Labor Zionism, which combined national goals with socialist ideals, founded institutions such as the kibbutz movement and came to dominate the pre-state Jewish community (the Yishuv). Revisionist Zionism, led by Ze'ev Jabotinsky, took a more maximalist position on territory and a more assertive stance on military self-defense. Cultural Zionism, associated with Ahad Ha'am, prioritized a spiritual and cultural center over immediate statehood.

From the Balfour Declaration to statehood

In 1917 the British government issued the Balfour Declaration, expressing support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," and after the First World War the League of Nations incorporated this aim into the Mandate for Palestine. Jewish immigration grew under the Mandate; after the Second World War and the Holocaust, international support for partition increased. In 1947 the United Nations proposed dividing Palestine into Jewish and Arab states. On 14 May 1948, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the establishment of the State of Israel, immediately followed by war with neighboring Arab states.

After 1948

Since 1948, Zionism has primarily concerned support for Israel's continued existence as a Jewish and democratic state, and has remained a subject of political debate. Supporters regard it as a legitimate national liberation movement of the Jewish people; critics, including Palestinian and Arab movements, view it in the context of Palestinian displacement in 1948 (the Nakba) and the ongoing Israeli–Palestinian conflict. The movement's ideas continue to be discussed and contested in debates over nationalism, self-determination, and the character of the Israeli state.
