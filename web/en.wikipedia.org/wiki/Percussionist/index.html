Percussionist
Occupation: Musician
Instruments: Drums, timpani, mallet instruments, hand percussion
Fields: Orchestral, popular music, jazz, world music

An orchestral percussionist must master dozens of instruments

A percussionist is a musician who plays percussion instruments — instruments sounded by being struck, shaken, scraped, or rubbed. Because the percussion family is by far the largest and most varied of all instrument families, percussionists are typically expected to play many different instruments, from the snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, and timpani to pitched mallet instruments such as the xylophone, marimba, vibraphone, and glockenspiel, as well as tambourines, triangles, and a vast array of "auxiliary" and world percussion.

Roles

In the symphony orchestra, a percussion section of several players (plus a dedicated timpanist) covers parts that may call for dozens of instruments in a single work; composers from Berlioz and Stravinsky onward have vastly expanded the percussionist's palette. In popular music, the role overlaps with that of the drummer, who plays the drum kit, though many bands employ a separate percussionist for congas, bongos, cajón, shakers, and electronic pads alongside the kit drummer. Latin, African, and Afro-Cuban traditions feature percussionists as lead and solo voices, while jazz vibraphonists and drummers helped define whole eras of the music.

Technique and training

Percussionists study matched and traditional grip, rudiments, four-mallet keyboard technique, timpani tuning by ear, and sight-reading across clefs and instrument setups. Conservatoire training covers orchestral excerpts, contemporary solo repertoire, and increasingly electronic and multi-percussion setups, in which a single player performs on an array of instruments arranged around them.
