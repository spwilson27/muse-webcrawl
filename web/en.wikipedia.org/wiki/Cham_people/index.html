The Cham people (Cham: ꨂꨣꩃ, Urang Campa) are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to central and southern Vietnam and Cambodia. They are the descendants of the people of Champa, a collection of Indianized kingdoms that flourished on the coast of what is now central Vietnam from about the 2nd to the 19th century.

History

Champa traded extensively across the South China Sea and absorbed strong Indian cultural influence, adopting Hinduism, Sanskrit inscriptions, and distinctive red-brick temple architecture such as the towers at Mỹ Sơn and Po Nagar. From the 15th century onward, Vietnamese expansion southward (Nam tiến) progressively conquered Champa; the last Cham principality, Panduranga, was annexed in 1832.

Religion and culture

The Cham are religiously diverse. Cham Balamon communities in Vietnam preserve a form of Hinduism, while the Cham Bani practice a localized form of Islam; most Cambodian Cham are Sunni Muslims. Cham communities suffered severely under the Khmer Rouge in Cambodia in the 1970s. Cham culture is noted for matrilineal traditions in some communities, weaving, pottery, and festivals such as Kate and Ramuwan.
