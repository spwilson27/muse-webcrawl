Siege of Mostar

The siege of Mostar took place during the Bosnian War, from 1992 to 1994, in the city of Mostar in Bosnia and Herzegovina. It is commonly divided into two phases: a first siege in 1992, when the city was held jointly by Bosniak (Muslim) and Croat forces against the Yugoslav People's Army (JNA) and then the Army of Republika Srpska (VRS); and a second, longer siege in 1993–1994, when the formerly allied Bosniak Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH) and the Croatian Defence Council (HVO) fought each other in the Croat–Bosniak War, and the HVO besieged the Bosniak-held eastern part of the city.

Background

Mostar, the largest city in Herzegovina, stood on the Neretva River and was famous for the Stari Most (Old Bridge), a 16th-century Ottoman stone bridge. Before the war its population was mixed: Bosniaks, Croats, Serbs and others lived side by side. When Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence in 1992, fighting spread quickly across the country.

First siege (1992)

In April–June 1992, JNA and Bosnian Serb forces attacked Mostar from the surrounding hills. Bosniak and Croat defenders, cooperating at this stage, held the city, and in June 1992 a joint HVO–ARBiH offensive pushed the Serb forces back from most of the urban area. Serb forces nevertheless retained positions in the mountains east of the city and shelled it intermittently.

Second siege (1993–1994)

Relations between the Bosniak-led government in Sarajevo and the Croat leadership, which proclaimed the Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia, collapsed into open war in 1993. In May 1993 the HVO attacked Bosniak areas of Mostar; Bosniak civilians were expelled from the western, Croat-held bank, and the ARBiH-held eastern enclave was encircled and besieged for about nine months. The enclave suffered severe shortages of food, water and medicine, and sustained artillery shelling.

On 9 November 1993, HVO tank fire destroyed the Stari Most, an event that became one of the most widely reported symbols of the war's cultural destruction. HVO commander Slobodan Praljak was later convicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) in connection with crimes committed during the siege, including the bridge's destruction.

Aftermath

The Washington Agreement of March 1994 ended the Croat–Bosniak War and lifted the siege; Mostar was placed under a European Union administration for two years and formally reunified. The Old Bridge was rebuilt using traditional methods and original materials recovered from the river, and reopened in 2004; the Old Bridge area was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2005. The city nevertheless remained politically and socially divided for many years, holding no local elections between 2008 and 2020.
