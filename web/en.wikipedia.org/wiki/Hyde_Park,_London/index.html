Hyde Park is a 350-acre (142-hectare) Royal Park in central London, England. It is the largest of the four Royal Parks that form a chain from Kensington Palace through Kensington Gardens and Hyde Park to Green Park and St James's Park. The park is divided by the Serpentine lake and the Long Water, and is contiguous with Kensington Gardens, from which it is separated by West Carriage Drive.

History

The land was acquired by Henry VIII from Westminster Abbey in 1536 for use as a hunting ground. Charles I created the Ring and opened the park to the public in 1637. In 1851 Hyde Park hosted the Great Exhibition, for which Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace was erected along its southern edge. The park also contains the site of the Tyburn gallows, near modern Marble Arch, London's principal place of execution until 1783.

Features

Hyde Park Corner contains the Wellington Arch and Apsley House. At the north-east corner, Speakers' Corner — established after the Reform Act demonstrations of the 1860s and the Parks Regulation Act 1872 — remains a symbol of free speech, where orators address crowds on Sundays. Other features include the Diana, Princess of Wales Memorial Fountain (2004), the Serpentine Gallery, Rotten Row (a historic bridleway), the Rose Garden, and the Holocaust and 7 July Memorials. The park hosts major concerts, the Winter Wonderland fair, and the triathlon and marathon swimming events of the 2012 Olympic Games.

Protests

The park has a long history of mass demonstration, from the Chartists and the 1866 Reform League riots to the 2003 anti-Iraq War march, one of the largest protests in British history.
