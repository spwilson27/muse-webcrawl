Cult

Cult
A communal religious group, of the kind often labelled a cult
FieldSociology of religion, religious studies
Related termsNew religious movement, sect, denomination
Key conceptsCharismatic authority, anti-cult movement, brainwashing debate

A cult is a group or set of devotional practices centred on a particular figure, object or belief system. In its original scholarly sense the word (from Latin cultus, "worship" or "care") referred neutrally to a system of religious veneration, such as the cult of a saint. In modern popular usage, however, "cult" has acquired a strongly pejorative meaning, typically denoting a group — often a new religious movement — perceived as secretive, authoritarian, exploitative or dangerous, usually under the control of a charismatic leader.

Terminology and debate

Because of the term's negative connotations, most scholars of religion prefer the neutral expression new religious movement. Sociologists since Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch have classified religious organisations along a church–sect continuum; in the typology of Howard Becker, a cult is the loosest and most individualistic form of religious organisation, often centred on a charismatic figure and novel or imported beliefs. Critics argue that "cult" is frequently applied simply to unfamiliar or unpopular religions, while anti-cult activists maintain that some groups cause genuine psychological and financial harm to members.

Characteristics attributed to cults

Popular and scholarly checklists of "cultic" traits commonly include: absolute obedience to a charismatic leader; isolation of members from family and former friends; apocalyptic or exclusive truth claims; control of information and behaviour (sometimes framed through Robert Jay Lifton's criteria of thought reform); financial exploitation; and barriers to leaving the group. The extent to which such groups practise "brainwashing" or coercive persuasion remains scientifically contested.

Notorious cases

Several groups labelled cults became notorious through violence: the Peoples Temple, whose members died in the Jonestown mass murder-suicide of 1978 (918 deaths); the Branch Davidians, whose 1993 standoff at Waco, Texas ended in a fire killing 76 people; the Japanese movement Aum Shinrikyo, responsible for the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack; Heaven's Gate, whose 39 members died by suicide in 1997; and the Manson Family murders of 1969. Such cases heavily shaped public perception of the word.

Anti-cult movement

From the 1970s, an anti-cult movement of concerned parents, former members and "deprogrammers" campaigned against groups they regarded as destructive; some deprogrammings involved forcible abduction and were themselves controversial and sometimes illegal. Government responses have varied, from parliamentary lists of "sects" in some European countries to strong religious-freedom protections in the United States.
