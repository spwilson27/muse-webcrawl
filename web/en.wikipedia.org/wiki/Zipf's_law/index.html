Zipf's law

Zipf's law is an empirical law, formulated using mathematical statistics, named after the American linguist George Kingsley Zipf, who first proposed it. Zipf's law states that given a large sample of words used, the frequency of any word is inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table. So word number n has a frequency proportional to 1/n.

Thus the most frequent word will occur approximately twice as often as the second most frequent word, three times as often as the third most frequent word, etc. For example, in the Brown Corpus of American English text, the word "the" is the most frequently occurring word, and by itself accounts for nearly 7% of all word occurrences (69,971 out of slightly over 1 million). True to Zipf's law, the second-place word "of" accounts for slightly over 3.5% of words (36,411 occurrences), followed by "and" (28,852).

History

The law is named after Zipf, who in 1935 noted that the frequency of the r-th most frequent word in a text is roughly proportional to 1/r. Similar distributions had been observed earlier by the French stenographer Jean-Baptiste Estoup (1916) and by Felix Auerbach for city sizes (1913). Zipf analysed the phenomenon in his 1935 book Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (1949), where he proposed that the distribution reflects a balance between speaker and hearer effort.

Formal definition

Zipf's law is most easily observed by plotting the data on a log–log graph, with the axes being log(rank order) and log(frequency). The data conform to Zipf's law to the extent that the plot is linear. Formally, a discrete distribution following Zipf's law with exponent s and N elements has probability mass function:

f(k; s, N) = (1/ks) / HN,s

where HN,s is the N-th generalized harmonic number.

Occurrence

Zipfian distributions have been observed in many different phenomena: word frequencies in natural language, city sizes, income distributions (where the related Pareto distribution is often used), firm sizes, Internet traffic, earthquake magnitudes, and the frequencies of notes in music. The underlying mechanisms generating such distributions are still debated; proposed explanations include preferential attachment, least-effort optimization, and random typing models such as the "monkey at a typewriter" model described by Benoit Mandelbrot.
