A gill (pronounced /ɡɪl/) is a respiratory organ that many aquatic organisms use to extract dissolved oxygen from water and to excrete carbon dioxide. The gills of some species, such as hermit crabs, have adapted to allow respiration on land provided they are kept moist. The microscopic structure of a gill presents a large surface area to the external environment, maximising gas exchange.

Structure

In fish, gills are located on both sides of the pharynx and consist of gill arches bearing pairs of filaments; each filament carries many thin lamellae rich in capillaries. Water flows over the lamellae in the opposite direction to the blood flow, a countercurrent exchange arrangement that allows efficient extraction of oxygen. In bony fish, the gills are protected by a bony cover called the operculum.

Gas exchange

Branchial respiration relies on the diffusion of oxygen from water, where it is dissolved at relatively low concentration, into the blood. Countercurrent exchange means the blood always encounters water with a higher oxygen concentration, enabling fish to extract up to 80–90% of dissolved oxygen. Gills also play a key role in osmoregulation and the excretion of nitrogenous waste, mainly as ammonia.

Other animals

Many aquatic invertebrates, including molluscs and crustaceans, have feathery gills (ctenidia in molluscs). Amphibian larvae such as tadpoles bear external gills, and some salamanders, like the axolotl, retain them into adulthood (neoteny).
