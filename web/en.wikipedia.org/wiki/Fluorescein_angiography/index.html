Fluorescein angiography

Fluorescein angiography
SynonymsFluorescent angiography, fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA)
PurposeImaging of retinal and choroidal circulation
Contrast agentSodium fluorescein (intravenous)
SpecialtyOphthalmology

Fluorescein angiography (FA) is a medical imaging technique used by ophthalmologists to examine the circulation of the retina and choroid at the back of the eye. A fluorescent dye, sodium fluorescein, is injected into a vein (usually in the arm), and a specialized fundus camera with excitation and barrier filters photographs the dye as it circulates through the ocular blood vessels.

Technique

After the pupils are dilated, control photographs are taken. Fluorescein is then injected intravenously, and as the dye enters the eye—typically within 10 to 15 seconds—a rapid sequence of photographs records the choroidal, arterial, arteriovenous, and venous phases of filling. Later images, taken up to about 10–20 minutes after injection, show dye leakage, pooling, or staining. Fluorescein absorbs blue light (around 465–490 nm) and emits yellow-green light (around 520–530 nm), which the camera records.

Clinical uses

Fluorescein angiography is used to diagnose and guide treatment of retinal vascular diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, retinal vein and artery occlusions, macular edema, and choroidal neovascularization. It reveals areas of capillary non-perfusion, microaneurysms, neovascularization, and breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier.

Adverse effects

The procedure temporarily discolors the skin and urine yellow-orange. Nausea and vomiting occur in a small percentage of patients. Allergic reactions are uncommon, and severe anaphylaxis is rare. A related technique, indocyanine green angiography, is better suited to imaging the choroidal circulation.
