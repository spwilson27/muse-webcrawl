Pallidotomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pallidotomy is a neurosurgical procedure in which a small, targeted lesion is created in the globus pallidus interna (GPi) of the brain in order to relieve the motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease and certain other movement disorders, particularly levodopa-induced dyskinesia.

Procedure

Using stereotactic guidance — traditionally a head frame combined with CT or MRI imaging and microelectrode recording — the surgeon advances a radiofrequency probe through a small burr hole to the posteroventral portion of the GPi. Heating the probe tip destroys a small volume of tissue. The patient is usually awake so that speech, vision, and movement can be monitored during lesioning. In recent years, MRI-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) has been used to perform pallidotomy without an incision.

Indications and effects

Posteroventral pallidotomy is most effective against dyskinesias, rigidity, tremor, and bradykinesia on the side of the body opposite the lesion. It was widely performed in the 1990s after the Swedish neurosurgeon Lauri Laitinen revived and refined the technique, publishing influential results in 1992. Bilateral lesions carry a higher risk of speech and cognitive complications, so bilateral procedures are approached cautiously.

Comparison with deep brain stimulation

Since the 2000s, deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the GPi or subthalamic nucleus has largely replaced pallidotomy in wealthier health systems because it is adjustable and reversible and can be applied bilaterally with fewer adverse effects. Pallidotomy remains an option where DBS hardware, follow-up programming, or cost present barriers, and focused-ultrasound pallidotomy has renewed interest in the approach.

Risks

Complications can include hemorrhage, infection, and damage to neighbouring structures such as the internal capsule or optic tract, causing weakness or visual field defects.
