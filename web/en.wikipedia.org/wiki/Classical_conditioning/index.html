Classical conditioning

Classical conditioning (also Pavlovian or respondent conditioning) is a type of learning in which an organism comes to associate a previously neutral stimulus with a stimulus that naturally evokes a response, so that the neutral stimulus alone begins to evoke a similar response.

Pavlov's experiments

Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov, studying digestion in dogs in the 1890s–1900s, noticed that dogs salivated before food arrived. Pairing a bell (neutral stimulus) with food (unconditioned stimulus) repeatedly caused the bell alone (conditioned stimulus) to elicit salivation (conditioned response).

Processes

Acquisition is the initial learning; extinction occurs when the conditioned stimulus is presented without reinforcement; spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished response after a rest. Generalisation extends the response to similar stimuli, while discrimination narrows it. Higher-order conditioning builds chains of associations.

Applications and theory

Classical conditioning underlies taste aversion, fear learning (as in Watson and Rayner's 1920 'Little Albert' experiment), advertising and exposure therapy for phobias. The Rescorla–Wagner model (1972) formalised learning as driven by prediction error — a principle echoed in modern reward-learning neuroscience and machine learning.
