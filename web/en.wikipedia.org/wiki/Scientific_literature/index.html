Scientific literature

Scientific literature comprises the body of written publications reporting original empirical and theoretical work in the natural and social sciences, as well as scholarly reviews synthesising that work. Its principal genres are the journal article, the conference proceeding, the scholarly monograph and the review article; the system of academic publishing through which it circulates developed over the 17th to 19th centuries.

History

The first scientific journals, the Journal des sçavans and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, both appeared in 1665. Learned societies initially dominated scientific publishing, which commercialised in the 19th and 20th centuries. The 20th century saw explosive growth in the number of journals and papers, formalisation of peer review after the Second World War, and, from the 1990s, the transition to electronic publishing, preprint servers such as arXiv (founded 1991), and the open access movement.

Types of publication

  • Research articles report original results, conventionally structured as introduction, methods, results and discussion (IMRaD).
  • Review articles summarise and evaluate a field; systematic reviews and meta-analyses follow explicit protocols.
  • Conference proceedings are the primary venue in fields such as computer science.
  • Monographs and edited volumes present book-length scholarship, especially in the humanities and social sciences.
  • Grey literature includes preprints, theses, technical reports and datasets not issued by commercial publishers.

Peer review and metrics

Most scientific literature is filtered by peer review, in which independent experts assess manuscripts before publication. Bibliometric indicators such as citation counts, the h-index and journal impact factors are used — controversially — to evaluate literature and researchers. Critics discuss publication bias, the replication crisis, predatory journals and "paper mills" as threats to the literature's reliability.

Access

Traditionally financed by subscriptions, the literature is increasingly available through open access models in which authors or funders pay article processing charges or institutions adopt "transformative agreements". Indexing services such as Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed and Google Scholar organise access to the corpus.
