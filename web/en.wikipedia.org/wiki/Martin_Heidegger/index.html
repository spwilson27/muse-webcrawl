Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) was a German philosopher best known for Being and Time (Sein und Zeit, 1927), one of the most influential philosophical works of the 20th century. A student and assistant of Edmund Husserl at Freiburg, he succeeded to Husserl's chair in 1928.

Thought

Being and Time reopens the question of Being (Sein) through an analysis of Dasein — the human being characterised by an understanding of Being. Its analyses of being-toward-death, anxiety, authenticity, care and temporality reshaped existentialism, hermeneutics and theology. Later work (the "turn", Kehre) turned to language ("language is the house of Being"), poetry, art and a critique of modern technology as "enframing" (Gestell). His prose is famously dense and neologistic.

Nazism controversy

Heidegger joined the Nazi Party in 1933 and served as rector of Freiburg University, delivering an infamous rectoral address aligning the university with the regime. He never joined the party's inner circle and later claimed inner resistance, but he made no public reckoning with the Holocaust, and the publication of his private Black Notebooks (from 2014) revealed antisemitic passages that intensified debate about the relationship between his philosophy and his politics. Postwar he was banned from teaching for several years before being reinstated.

Influence

His students and readers included Hannah Arendt, Hans-Georg Gadamer, Jean-Paul Sartre, Emmanuel Levinas and Jacques Derrida. Heidegger remains both central and contested: a reference point for continental philosophy, literary theory and architecture, and a standing case study in intellectual responsibility.
