Kurgan

A kurgan is a type of tumulus constructed over a grave, often characterised by containing a single human body and grave goods, various materials — stones, timber and earth — heaped over it. Originating from the steppes of Central Asia and Eastern Europe, kurgans were built throughout the Bronze Age, Iron Age and into the medieval period by cultures including the Yamnaya, Scythians, Sarmatians, Huns and Turkic peoples. The word derives from Turkic kurğan, meaning a fortress or burial mound.

Archaeology

Kurgans typically consist of a central burial chamber — sometimes a wooden or stone structure sunk into a pit — covered by a large earthen mound, often ringed by a stone enclosure (kromlech) or ditches. Elite burials, such as the royal Scythian kurgans of the Pontic steppe (Kul-Oba, Chertomlyk, Solokha) and the frozen tombs of Pazyryk in the Altai Mountains, contained rich grave goods: gold ornaments, weapons, horse burials and wagons. The exceptionally well-preserved Pazyryk finds, protected by permafrost, include the world's oldest surviving pile carpet.

The Kurgan hypothesis, formulated by Marija Gimbutas in the 1950s, proposes that the Proto-Indo-European homeland lay in the Pontic–Caspian steppe among the kurgan-building Yamnaya culture of the 4th millennium BC, whose expansions carried Indo-European languages into Europe and Asia — a view substantially supported by later archaeogenetic research. The city of Kurgan in Russia, founded near an ancient burial mound, takes its name from the term.
