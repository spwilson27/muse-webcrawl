Wind tunnel

A wind tunnel is a facility in which air is moved past a stationary test object — such as an aircraft model, automobile, building, or bridge section — to study the effects of airflow on the object. Wind tunnels are used in aerodynamic research to measure lift, drag, pressure distribution, and flow patterns, and to validate computational fluid dynamics simulations.

Types

Wind tunnels are classified by the speed range they produce: subsonic, transonic, supersonic, and hypersonic. In an open-circuit tunnel, air is drawn in from the surroundings and exhausted after passing through the test section; in a closed-circuit (return-flow) tunnel, the same air recirculates through a loop, which improves flow quality and energy efficiency. Smoke, tufts, laser velocimetry, and pressure-sensitive paint are used to visualize and measure the flow.

History

The first enclosed wind tunnels were built in the late nineteenth century; Francis Herbert Wenham designed one in 1871, and the Wright brothers used a small tunnel in 1901 to test airfoil shapes before their first powered flight. Large national facilities, such as those at NASA's Ames and Langley research centers, have supported aircraft and spacecraft development ever since.

Other applications

Beyond aerospace, wind tunnels test automotive aerodynamics and fuel efficiency, building and bridge wind loads, pollutant dispersion, sports equipment, and even athletes such as ski jumpers and cyclists. Vertical wind tunnels are also used for indoor skydiving and military freefall training.
