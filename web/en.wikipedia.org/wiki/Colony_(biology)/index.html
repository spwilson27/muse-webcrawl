Colony (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A honeybee hive is a colony of eusocial insects.

In biology, a colony is composed of two or more conspecific individuals living in close association with, or connected to, one another, usually to the mutual benefit of all members. Such associations can be found in many groups of organisms, from bacteria to mammals.

Types of colonies

Microbial colonies form when individual cells of bacteria, fungi, or other microorganisms grow together, often arising from a single parent cell; the discrete colonies that appear on an agar plate in microbiology are used to isolate pure cultures. Colonial organisms such as corals, bryozoans, and siphonophores consist of many connected, often specialised units (zooids) that function together as a single organism. Social colonies are formed by eusocial insects — ants, termites, many bees and wasps — in which a reproductive division of labour exists between queens and sterile workers. Other animal colonies include seabird colonies, bat roosts, and prairie dog towns, where individuals live together for protection, warmth, or cooperative breeding.

Coloniality and evolution

The evolution of colonial and multicellular organisation is a major transition in the history of life: colonies of cells with division of labour bridge the gap between unicellular organisms and true multicellular bodies. Colonial species such as the Portuguese man o' war blur the boundary between a colony and a single organism.
