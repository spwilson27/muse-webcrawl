Nguyen dynasty

The Nguyen dynasty (Vietnamese: Nhà Nguyễn; chữ Hán: 家阮) was the last Vietnamese dynasty, which ruled the unified state of Vietnam — first as an independent empire and later as a French protectorate — from 1802 until 1945. It was founded by Nguyen Phuc Anh, a descendant of the Nguyen lords who had governed southern Vietnam, after he defeated the Tay Son dynasty and took the reign name Gia Long.

History

Gia Long unified the country for the first time in centuries under the name Vietnam (1804), building the great citadel at Hue, which remained the capital. His successor Minh Mang (reigned 1820–1841) pursued conservative Confucian reforms, centralised administration and expanded Vietnamese influence into Cambodia and Laos, at times provoking conflict with Siam.

During the mid-19th century the dynasty faced mounting French pressure. French forces seized Saigon in 1859 and, after campaigns in 1883–1884, Vietnam became a French protectorate under the Treaties of Hue, split administratively into Cochinchina (a colony), Annam and Tonkin (protectorates). The emperors thereafter reigned as largely ceremonial figures, though several — Ham Nghi, Thanh Thai and Duy Tan — resisted and were exiled.

End of the dynasty

Emperor Bao Dai, the last ruler, abdicated in August 1945 after the August Revolution, formally ending 143 years of Nguyen rule. The dynasty is remembered for the architecture of Hue (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), its elaborate court ritual, and its role in shaping modern Vietnamese national identity.

List of emperors

Gia Long (1802–1820), Minh Mang (1820–1841), Thieu Tri (1841–1847), Tu Duc (1847–1883), Duc Duc, Hiep Hoa, Kien Phuc, Ham Nghi, Dong Khanh, Thanh Thai, Duy Tan, Khai Dinh, and Bao Dai (1926–1945).
