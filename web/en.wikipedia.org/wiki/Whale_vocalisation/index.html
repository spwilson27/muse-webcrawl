Whale vocalisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Whale vocalisation refers to the sounds made by cetaceans — whales, dolphins and porpoises — for communication and, in toothed whales, for echolocation. The word "song" is used in particular for the patterned, repeating vocalisations of some baleen whales, most famously the humpback whale, whose complex songs can last for hours and are sung by males during the breeding season.

Production and types of sound

Toothed whales (odontocetes) produce rapid bursts of clicks for echolocation and whistles and burst-pulse sounds for communication, generated in the nasal passages rather than the larynx; sperm whale clicks are among the loudest sounds made by any animal. Baleen whales (mysticetes) produce lower-frequency moans, calls and songs in the larynx; blue whale calls, among the lowest-frequency animal sounds, can travel enormous distances through deep-ocean sound channels.

Whale song

Recordings of humpback song, popularised by Roger Payne's 1970 album Songs of the Humpback Whale, helped galvanise the Save the Whales movement and the 1982 moratorium on commercial whaling. Within a population, all males sing essentially the same song, which evolves gradually over the season and can spread culturally between populations across ocean basins. The purpose of song is debated, with mate attraction and male-male spacing among the leading hypotheses.

Noise pollution

Rising ocean noise from shipping and sonar masks whale communication and is believed to cause stress and strandings, an increasing focus of research and regulation.
