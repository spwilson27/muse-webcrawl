Formalism (literature)

Formalism is a school of literary criticism and literary theory having mainly to do with structural purposes of a particular text. It is the study of a text without taking into account any outside influence. Formalism rejects (or sometimes simply "brackets") notions of culture or societal influence, authorship, and content, and instead focuses on the work's mode, genre, discourse and forms. Two branches are usually distinguished: Russian formalism, centred in Moscow and St. Petersburg in the 1910s–1920s, and the New Criticism of mid-twentieth-century America, which shared the commitment to close reading of the text itself.

Russian formalism

The Russian formalists — members of the Moscow Linguistic Circle (founded 1915, including Roman Jakobson) and the St. Petersburg Society for the Study of Poetic Language (OPOYAZ, founded 1916, including Viktor Shklovsky, Boris Eichenbaum and Yuri Tynyanov) — sought to define literariness: what makes a verbal work a work of art. Their best-known concept is Shklovsky's defamiliarization (ostranenie, 1917): art makes the familiar strange, disrupting habitual perception so that objects are perceived freshly rather than merely recognised. The formalists also distinguished fabula (the raw chronological story material) from syuzhet (the plot as actually arranged in the text), a distinction foundational for narratology.

New Criticism

In the United States, the New Critics — John Crowe Ransom, Cleanth Brooks, Allen Tate, Robert Penn Warren, and W. K. Wimsatt — practised intensive close reading, treating the poem as an autonomous verbal object whose meaning arises from tensions, paradoxes and ironies within the text. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley's essays on the "intentional fallacy" (1946) and "affective fallacy" (1949) codified the exclusion of authorial intention and reader response from evaluation.

Criticism and influence

Formalist approaches were attacked by Marxist critics (including Trotsky, and later Mikhail Bakhtin's circle, partly from within), by historicists, and later by reader-response and cultural-studies critics for ignoring history and politics. Nevertheless, formalism profoundly influenced structuralism — via Jakobson's move to Prague — and remains the ancestor of much modern narrative theory and stylistics.
