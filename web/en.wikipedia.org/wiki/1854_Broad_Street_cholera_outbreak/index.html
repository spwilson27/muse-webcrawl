1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak

The Broad Street cholera outbreak (or Golden Square outbreak) was a severe outbreak of cholera that occurred in 1854 near Broad Street (now Broadwick Street) in the Soho district of London, England, during the worldwide 1846–1860 cholera pandemic. The outbreak killed 616 people and is best known for the physician John Snow's investigation, which identified a public water pump as the source — a founding episode in the science of epidemiology.

The outbreak

At the end of August 1854, cholera erupted in the crowded streets around Broad Street, where sanitation was rudimentary and cesspools contaminated wells. Within days hundreds of residents fell ill; in the worst streets, more than one in ten inhabitants died. Many fled the district, sharply reducing the population exposed.

Snow's investigation

Dr John Snow, who had theorised that cholera spread through contaminated water rather than "miasma", mapped the deaths — producing his celebrated spot map — and found them clustered around the Broad Street pump. Interviews strengthened the case: workers at a nearby brewery who drank beer rather than water were spared, while a widow in distant Hampstead who had Broad Street water delivered to her home died of the disease. Snow persuaded the parish guardians, with help from the Reverend Henry Whitehead, to remove the pump handle on 8 September. The epidemic, already declining, subsided. Later investigation traced the contamination to a cesspool near the pump, likely fouled by the nappies of an infant who had died of cholera at 40 Broad Street.

Legacy

Snow's work is regarded as a founding event of modern epidemiology and public health, demonstrating the power of mapping, statistical reasoning and intervention. A replica pump and a nearby pub named the John Snow commemorate the site in present-day Broadwick Street.
