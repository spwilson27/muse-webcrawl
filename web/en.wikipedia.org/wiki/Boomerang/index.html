Boomerang

A boomerang is a thrown tool, typically constructed as a flat aerofoil, designed to spin about an axis perpendicular to its flight direction. Returning boomerangs fly in a characteristic elliptical path and come back to the thrower; they were historically used by Aboriginal Australians for hunting, sport, and entertainment, and non-returning throwing sticks of similar form were used as weapons by many cultures, including in ancient Egypt and North America.

History

The oldest surviving Australian boomerang, found at Wyrie Swamp in South Australia, dates to around 10,000 years ago, and rock art depicting boomerangs may be far older. Throwing sticks appear in the tomb of Tutankhamun and were used by Hopi and other peoples for hunting rabbits. In Australia, heavier non-returning boomerangs served as weapons, digging tools, and fire-starters as well as trade goods. The word "boomerang" derives from the Dharug language of the Sydney region and entered English in the early 19th century.

Flight

A returning boomerang's arms are aerofoils. When thrown nearly vertically with rapid spin, the arm moving forward into the apparent wind travels faster through the air than the retreating arm, generating lift that is uneven across the disc of rotation. Gyroscopic precession converts this asymmetric lift into a continuous turn, bending the flight into a circle that — with a correctly made and thrown boomerang — returns to the thrower. Modern sport boomerangs are made from plywood, plastics, and carbon composites and are used in competitions for accuracy, endurance, fast catch, and maximum time aloft.
