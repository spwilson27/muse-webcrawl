The Accipitriformes are an order of birds that includes most of the diurnal birds of prey, including hawks, eagles, vultures, kites, osprey, and the secretarybird — more than 260 species in total. They are distinguished by strongly hooked bills with sharp edges, powerful talons, keen eyesight, and, in most species, a carnivorous diet of vertebrate prey or carrion.

Taxonomy

Traditionally, the diurnal raptors were grouped with the falcons in the order Falconiformes. DNA sequence studies in the early 21st century showed that falcons are more closely related to parrots and passerines than to the other raptors, so the hawks and their relatives were placed in their own order, Accipitriformes, while falcons were retained in a narrowed Falconiformes.

The families usually recognized are:

  • Accipitridae — hawks, eagles, kites, harriers, Old World vultures, and allies; by far the largest family.
  • Pandionidae — the osprey.
  • Sagittariidae — the secretarybird of sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Cathartidae — the New World vultures and condors, included in the order by some authorities and placed in their own order Cathartiformes by others.

Characteristics

Accipitriform birds range in size from the tiny pearl kite, weighing about 100 g, to the Andean condor, which can exceed 14 kg with a wingspan over 3 metres. Females are typically larger than males (reversed sexual dimorphism), most markedly in species that hunt agile birds. Plumage is usually brown, grey, black, or white, often with barred or streaked underparts. Vision is exceptionally acute, aided by a large number of photoreceptors and a deep central fovea.

Distribution and ecology

The order is found on every continent except Antarctica and in nearly all terrestrial habitats, from tundra to tropical forest. Many species are migratory, funnelling along coastlines and mountain ridges. Several Old World vultures have declined catastrophically in South Asia and Africa owing to poisoning by veterinary drugs and deliberate persecution.
