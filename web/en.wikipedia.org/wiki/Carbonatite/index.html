Carbonatite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carbonatites are igneous rocks — intrusive or extrusive — defined by a mineralogical composition consisting of greater than 50% carbonate minerals. They are rare and peculiar rocks, formed from mantle-derived magmas exceptionally rich in carbon dioxide, and they are of major economic interest as sources of niobium, rare-earth elements, phosphate and other commodities.

Occurrence and formation

Only about 600 carbonatite complexes are known worldwide. They occur exclusively in continental settings, often in rift valleys and stable cratons, and are commonly associated with alkaline silicate rocks such as nepheline syenites and kimberlites. Intrusive bodies may take the form of plugs, dykes, sills and ring complexes, and their intrusion fenitises (alkali-metasomatises) the surrounding country rock. Famous examples include Palabora in South Africa, Mountain Pass in California, Bayan Obo in China (the world's largest rare-earth deposit) and Araxá in Brazil.

Carbonatite lava

Almost all carbonatites are intrusive; the sole known active carbonatite volcano is Ol Doinyo Lengai in Tanzania, which erupts sodium-rich natrocarbonatite lava at unusually low temperatures of about 500–600 °C — cool enough to appear black in daylight rather than glowing, turning white as its minerals hydrate in air.

Classification and economic importance

Carbonatites are named by their dominant carbonate: calcite-carbonatite (sövite when coarse-grained), dolomite-carbonatite (beforsite), ferrocarbonatite and natrocarbonatite. Because they concentrate elements that are incompatible in silicate magmas, carbonatites supply most of the world's niobium and a large share of its rare earths, as well as apatite (phosphate), vermiculite, fluorite, copper and barite.
