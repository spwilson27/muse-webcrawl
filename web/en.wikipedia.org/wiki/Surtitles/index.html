Surtitles

Surtitles, also known as supertitles, are translated or transcribed lyrics and dialogue projected above a stage or displayed on a screen, commonly used in opera and theatre to help audiences understand works performed in a foreign language. The term "surtitles" derives from the French sur, meaning "on" or "above", by analogy with subtitles in film, which appear below the picture.

History

Surtitles were first used in 1983 by the Canadian Opera Company for its production of Richard Strauss's Elektra, using a slide projection system developed by the company's artistic director, Lotfi Mansouri. The practice spread rapidly through opera houses in the following decade, initially amid controversy: critics argued that reading titles distracted from the music and staging, while supporters credited them with making opera accessible to new audiences and with increasing attendance.

Technology

Early systems used projected slides; most houses now use LED or LCD displays mounted above the proscenium, electronic libretto systems with small screens on the backs of seats, or mobile applications. Seatback systems, pioneered at the Metropolitan Opera in New York in 1995 under the name "Met Titles", allow each audience member to choose among several languages or switch the display off.

Use beyond opera

Surtitles are also used in spoken theatre, both for translation and as an accessibility aid for deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences, and have been adopted in some cinemas and concert halls for sung texts such as oratorios and song cycles.
