Creek War

The Creek War (1813–1814), also known as the Red Stick War and the Creek Civil War, was a regional war fought between opposing Native American factions, European empires, and the United States in the early 19th century in the modern-day U.S. states of Alabama and Georgia.

The war began as a civil conflict within the Creek (Muscogee) nation, between the traditionalist Red Stick faction and the Creek National Council. It became intertwined with the broader War of 1812, as the United States, Britain, and Spain all became involved.

Background

Creek leaders who resisted American expansion and cultural assimilation, inspired in part by the Shawnee leader Tecumseh, formed the Red Sticks. Tensions escalated after the Battle of Burnt Corn in July 1813 and the Fort Mims massacre of August 1813, in which Red Sticks killed hundreds of settlers and allied Creeks.

Campaigns

American forces under Andrew Jackson, including Tennessee militia and Cherokee and Lower Creek allies, campaigned through the Creek homeland. The decisive engagement was the Battle of Horseshoe Bend in March 1814, where Jackson's forces destroyed the main Red Stick army.

Aftermath

The war ended with the Treaty of Fort Jackson (1814), which forced the Creek nation — including Jackson's Creek allies — to cede some 23 million acres of land in present-day Alabama and Georgia to the United States. The defeat hastened Creek removal westward in the following decades.
