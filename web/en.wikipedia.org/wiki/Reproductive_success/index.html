Reproductive success

Reproductive success is the passing on of genes to the next generation in a way that those genes can also be passed on to subsequent generations. In practice it is measured as the number of offspring an individual produces that themselves survive to reproductive age. It is the operational basis of fitness in evolutionary biology: traits that raise an organism's reproductive success tend to spread through a population.

Measurement

Field studies typically quantify lifetime reproductive success (LRS) — the total number of offspring, or of offspring surviving to independence or breeding, produced over an individual's life. Long-term studies of marked populations, such as red deer on Rum, Soay sheep on St Kilda, and great tits in Wytham Wood, have shown that LRS varies enormously among individuals: many die without breeding, while a few produce most of the next generation. This variance is the raw material of natural and sexual selection.

Determinants

Reproductive success depends on survival to breeding age, mating success, fecundity per attempt, and the survival of the offspring themselves. In sexually reproducing species it is frequently far more variable in males than in females (Bateman's principle), which underlies much of sexual selection theory, though the strength of the pattern varies across taxa.

Human reproductive success

In behavioural ecology and evolutionary anthropology, reproductive success has been used to test predictions about mating systems, inheritance, and parental investment in human societies, though critics caution that in modern societies wealth and status do not map simply onto numbers of descendants.
