Swamp gas

Swamp gas, also called marsh gas, is a mixture of gases produced by the anaerobic decomposition of organic matter in wetlands, bogs, and marshes. Its principal component is methane, with carbon dioxide, nitrogen, hydrogen sulphide, and traces of phosphine and diphosphane. The same gas forms in landfill sites, rice paddies, and the digestive tracts of ruminants, and is harvested deliberately as biogas.

Formation

In waterlogged, oxygen-poor sediments, bacteria break down dead plant material through fermentation, and methanogenic archaea convert the products into methane and carbon dioxide. Disturbing the sediment — by a footstep or a pole — releases streams of bubbles that can be collected and ignited.

Will-o'-the-wisp

Swamp gas is the leading scientific explanation for the will-o'-the-wisp (ignis fatuus), the flickering blue flames of folklore said to hover over marshes and lure travellers astray. Impurities such as phosphine and diphosphane are spontaneously flammable in air and can ignite the methane, producing small, cool flames; chemiluminescence from phosphorus compounds offers another possible mechanism. The phenomenon has rarely been photographed and remains incompletely reproduced in the laboratory.

"Swamp gas" and UFO reports

The term became famous in ufology after the March 1966 Michigan sightings near Dexter and Hillsdale, when U.S. Air Force consultant J. Allen Hynek suggested at a press conference that some of the reported lights might be swamp gas. The remark was widely ridiculed in the press, soured Hynek's relationship with the Air Force's Project Blue Book, and "swamp gas" entered popular culture as a byword for an unconvincing official explanation of UFO reports.

Environmental significance

Wetlands are the largest natural source of atmospheric methane, a potent greenhouse gas, and emissions from swamp gas contribute significantly to the global methane budget — a major concern for climate science and for proposals to manage or restore wetlands.
