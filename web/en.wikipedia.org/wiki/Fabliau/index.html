Fabliau

A fabliau (plural fabliaux) is a comic, often anonymous, tale written by jongleurs in northeast France between about 1150 and 1340. Some 150 French fabliaux survive, typically in octosyllabic couplets.

Characteristics

Fabliaux are usually bawdy, scatological, and satirical, set in a contemporary everyday world of peasants, millers, merchants, priests, and unfaithful wives. Their plots turn on tricks, deceptions, mistaken identity, and cuckoldry, and their humour is often at the expense of clergy, husbands, and the greedy. Unlike the courtly romance, which celebrates aristocratic ideals, the fabliau delights in cleverness and appetite.

Influence

The genre strongly influenced Geoffrey Chaucer, whose "Miller's Tale", "Reeve's Tale", and "Shipman's Tale" in The Canterbury Tales are recognisably English fabliaux, and Giovanni Boccaccio, many of whose tales in the Decameron draw on fabliau plots. Scholars such as Joseph Bédier, who published the classic collection Les Fabliaux (1893), debated the genre's origins and social audience; modern scholarship sees the tales as performed for mixed audiences, including courts.
