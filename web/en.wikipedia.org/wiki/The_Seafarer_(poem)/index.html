Overview

The Seafarer is an Old English poem of 124 lines recorded in the Exeter Book, a tenth-century manuscript. One of the best-known Anglo-Saxon elegies, it is spoken in the voice of an aging sailor who recounts the hardships of life at sea — cold, hunger, storms, and loneliness — yet expresses an irresistible compulsion to return to the ocean.

The poem moves from the seafarer's suffering and longing toward reflections on the transience of earthly glory and, in its later lines, an explicitly Christian meditation on the soul's journey toward heaven. Scholars debate whether the poem is a unified work or combines an originally secular elegy with a religious conclusion, and whether its seafaring is literal or allegorical.

Translations and influence

The poem has been translated many times, most famously in an abridged, free rendering by Ezra Pound (1911), which captures its pounding alliterative rhythms while omitting much of the Christian ending. It remains a staple of Old English literature courses and a touchstone for the elegiac mood of Anglo-Saxon poetry, alongside The Wanderer and The Ruin.
