Hasan-i Sabbah

Hasan-i Sabbah (c. 1050 – 12 June 1124), also known as Hasan I of Alamut, was a religious and political leader who founded the Nizari Isma'ili state in Persia and Syria. A missionary (da'i) of the Isma'ili branch of Shia Islam, he seized the mountain fortress of Alamut in 1090 and built a network of strongholds that resisted the Seljuk Empire for decades. In Western legend he became known as the "Old Man of the Mountain," and his followers were called the Assassins.

Early life

Hasan was born in Qom to a family of Twelver Shia background, and was educated in Rey (Rayy), where he converted to Isma'ilism in his teens. He travelled to Fatimid Cairo around 1078 to further his studies, and after returning to Persia he travelled widely as a missionary, organising support for the Isma'ili cause.

Seizure of Alamut

In 1090 Hasan's followers infiltrated and took the fortress of Alamut in the Alborz mountains, reportedly without bloodshed. From this base he extended Nizari control over a string of castles in Daylam, Quhistan and later Syria. The Nizari state survived through a strategy of targeted killings of political and military leaders — the origin of the word "assassin," derived in popular accounts from the Arabic hashishiyya.

Succession crisis and doctrine

When the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir died in 1094, Hasan supported the succession of his elder son Nizar against the younger al-Musta'li, creating the Nizari–Musta'li schism. Hasan's followers recognised Nizar and his descendants as the rightful imams. Theologically, Hasan is associated with the doctrine of ta'lim (authoritative instruction), expounded in his treatise known as the Four Chapters, which argued that human reason alone cannot attain knowledge of God and requires an authoritative teacher.

Death and legacy

Hasan died at Alamut in 1124 after a short illness and was succeeded by his commander Buzurg-Ummid. The state he founded endured until Alamut fell to the Mongols under Hulagu in 1256. Later legends — including stories of a garden of paradise and drugged devotees, popularised in Europe by Marco Polo — greatly embellished his historical record.
