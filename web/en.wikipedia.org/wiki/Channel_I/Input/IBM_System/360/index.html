IBM System/360

A System/360 installation in the 1960s

The IBM System/360 (S/360) is a family of mainframe computer systems announced by IBM on 7 April 1964. It was the first family of computers designed to cover a complete range of applications, from small to large, with a single architecture—programs written for one model could run on any other, a revolutionary break from IBM's earlier incompatible product lines.

Architecture

System/360 established the 8-bit byte, byte-addressable memory, and 32-bit words as computing standards. Its I/O was handled by channels: selector channels for fast devices and byte-multiplexer channels for many slow devices, executing channel programs of command words independently of the CPU. This channel architecture became the template for IBM mainframe I/O ever since.

Legacy

IBM spent an estimated $5 billion developing System/360—often called "the biggest bet in business history"—and it dominated mainframe computing for decades. Its descendants, through System/370 to today's IBM Z, still run much of the world's banking and airline infrastructure, and much of its instruction set and channel design survives in modern machines.
