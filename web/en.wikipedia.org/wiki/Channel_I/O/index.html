Channel I/O

In computing, channel I/O is a high-performance input/output (I/O) architecture that is implemented in various forms on a number of mainframe computer systems, most notably by IBM. In the channel architecture, a channel is an independent processor that manages the flow of data between main storage and I/O devices, executing its own programs called channel programs, so that the central processing unit (CPU) is freed from the detailed work of controlling devices.

History

The concept of data channels predates System/360: earlier IBM machines such as the IBM 709 and 7090 used data channels to offload I/O from the CPU. With the announcement of System/360 in 1964, IBM generalised the idea across a whole family of compatible machines. Channels became a defining feature of IBM mainframe architecture and persist in modern IBM Z systems, where the channel subsystem connects to devices over FICON (Fibre Channel) links.

How it works

To perform an I/O operation, the CPU executes a Start Subchannel instruction naming a device. The channel subsystem then fetches and executes a channel program — a sequence of channel command words (CCWs) stored in main memory — that controls the device, transfers data, and handles device-specific details such as seeking on a disk or rewinding a tape. When the operation completes, the channel presents an interruption to the CPU. This asynchronous design lets the CPU continue useful work while transfers proceed in parallel.

Channel types

  • Selector channels handle one high-speed device at a time.
  • Multiplexor channels interleave transfers from many slow devices such as terminals and card readers.
  • Block multiplexor channels combine features of both for disks and tapes.

In modern systems these have been replaced by fibre-connected channel paths managed by a channel subsystem with many subchannels.

Comparison with other architectures

Channel I/O is sometimes described as an extreme form of direct memory access (DMA): whereas a typical DMA controller performs a single fixed transfer, a channel processor executes arbitrary programs, chaining commands and reacting to device status without CPU intervention.
