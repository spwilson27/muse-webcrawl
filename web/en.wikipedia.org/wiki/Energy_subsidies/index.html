Energy subsidies

Energy subsidies are government measures that keep prices for energy consumers below market levels, raise prices for producers above market levels, or reduce costs for producers. They include direct transfers, tax breaks, price controls, cheap loans, and publicly funded infrastructure.

Scale and types

The International Energy Agency estimates explicit fossil-fuel consumption subsidies at around US$1 trillion in 2022, a record driven by the global energy crisis. The IMF, counting implicit subsidies — unpriced environmental and health damage — has estimated total fossil-fuel subsidies at about US$7 trillion (7% of global GDP) in 2022. At the same time, governments subsidise renewables and nuclear power through feed-in tariffs, tax credits (such as the US production and investment tax credits), auctions, and research funding.

Arguments

Supporters argue subsidies protect poor households from energy poverty, support infant industries and energy security, and accelerate low-carbon deployment. Critics argue fossil-fuel subsidies encourage wasteful consumption and emissions, disproportionately benefit wealthier households (which consume more fuel), and strain public finances; reform attempts have triggered protests in countries including France (gilets jaunes), Ecuador, Nigeria, and Iran.

Reform efforts

The G20 pledged in 2009 to phase out "inefficient" fossil-fuel subsidies; COP26 (2021) repeated the call. The WTO's disputes and the EU's state-aid rules shape subsidy design in member states. Successful reforms typically pair price increases with targeted cash compensation, as in Indonesia and Iran's 2010 programme.
