Permanent Settlement

Permanent Settlement of Bengal
Introduced: 1793
By: Charles Cornwallis, Governor-General of India
Region: Bengal, Bihar, Orissa
System: Zamindari

The Permanent Settlement, also known as the Permanent Settlement of Bengal, was an agreement between the East India Company and Bengali landlords (zamindars) to fix revenues to be raised from land on a perpetual basis. Introduced in 1793 by Lord Cornwallis, then Governor-General of India, it formed one part of a larger body of legislation known as the Cornwallis Code.

Background

Earlier revenue systems in Bengal, including the farming system introduced under Warren Hastings, produced instability, corruption, and declining collections. Company officials debated whether revenue should be settled with the zamindars, who acted as tax collectors and intermediaries, or directly with the cultivators (ryots). Influenced by physiocratic ideas and by the English model of landed estates, Cornwallis and his advisers, including John Shore, chose the zamindars.

Terms

Under the settlement, zamindars were recognised as hereditary owners of the land so long as they paid the fixed revenue demand to the Company on time. The state's demand was fixed at roughly ten-elevenths of the rental, leaving one-eleventh to the zamindar. If payment was not made by sunset on the due date, the estate could be sold at auction — the so-called "sunset law."

Effects

The settlement created a class of loyal landed magnates and gave the Company a stable, predictable income. However, the fixed demand did not rise with prices or production, so the state's share shrank over time, while many zamindars racked up arrears and lost their estates. Sub-infeudation multiplied layers of intermediaries, and tenant cultivators often faced high rents and insecurity. The system was extended to parts of northern India but contrasted with the ryotwari and mahalwari systems adopted elsewhere.

Legacy

The Permanent Settlement shaped Bengal's agrarian society for over 150 years. Zamindari was abolished after independence, in East Pakistan in 1950 and in West Bengal through land reform legislation in the 1950s.
