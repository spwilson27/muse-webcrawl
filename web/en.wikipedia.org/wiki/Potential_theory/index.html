Potential theory

In mathematics and mathematical physics, potential theory is the study of harmonic functions. The term arises from nineteenth-century physics, in which the fundamental forces of nature could be expressed as gradients of scalar fields called potentials: the gravitational potential, the electrostatic potential, and the velocity potential in fluid dynamics. Because these potentials satisfy Laplace's equation in regions free of sources, the mathematical theory of harmonic functions became the central tool for analysing them.

Definition

A twice continuously differentiable function u defined on a domain in Euclidean space is harmonic if it satisfies Laplace's equation:

Δu = ∂²u/∂x₁² + … + ∂²u/∂xₙ² = 0.

Harmonic functions enjoy strong regularity properties: they are infinitely differentiable, satisfy the mean value property (the value at a point equals the average over any ball or sphere centred there), and obey the maximum principle, attaining their maxima and minima only on the boundary of a domain unless constant.

Historical development

The theory grew out of the work of George Green, Carl Friedrich Gauss, and Lord Kelvin on gravitation and electrostatics. Green's essay of 1828 introduced the Green's function and Green's identities; Gauss gave the mean value property; and later mathematicians — Dirichlet, Riemann, Poincaré, and Fredholm — developed the rigorous solution of the Dirichlet problem, determining a harmonic function from its boundary values.

Subharmonic functions and the Perron method

A central modern tool is the class of subharmonic functions, upper semicontinuous functions satisfying a comparison form of the mean value inequality. Oskar Perron's method constructs the solution of the Dirichlet problem as the supremum of all subharmonic functions lying below the given boundary data, and the study of irregular boundary points led to Wiener's criterion.

Connections

In two dimensions, potential theory is intimately connected with complex analysis: the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are harmonic conjugates. There are also deep links with probability theory, since harmonic functions characterise the expected values of Brownian motion, and with the theory of capacities and fine topology developed by Brelot, Doob, and others.
