Island of stability

In nuclear physics, the island of stability is a predicted set of superheavy isotopes that may have considerably longer half-lives than nearby nuclei. The idea arises from the nuclear shell model: nuclei whose proton or neutron numbers equal certain "magic numbers" — corresponding to completely filled nuclear shells — are especially stable. Calculations since the 1960s have predicted a closed neutron shell at N = 184 and proton shells at Z = 114, 120 or 126, producing an "island" of relatively long-lived superheavy nuclei surrounded by a "sea" of instability.

History

The concept was developed in the 1960s by researchers including William Myers and Władysław Świątecki, and was popularised by Glenn T. Seaborg. The liquid-drop model alone predicts that nuclei much heavier than uranium should fission almost instantly; the shell model explained why shell closures could add enough extra binding energy to resist fission. Early predictions centred on element 114 (flerovium) with 184 neutrons, the isotope 298Fl.

Experimental search

Elements up to 118 (oganesson) have been synthesised at laboratories in Dubna (Russia), Berkeley and Livermore (United States) and Darmstadt (Germany), usually by bombarding actinide targets with calcium-48 or other projectiles. Observed trends are encouraging: half-lives and stability do increase as neutron number approaches 184, with copernicium and flerovium isotopes living far longer than a simple extrapolation would suggest. However, the most neutron-rich known nuclei remain several neutrons short of N = 184, and reaching the centre of the island will likely require new production methods, such as multi-nucleon transfer reactions or neutron-rich radioactive beams. Estimates of half-lives on the island range from minutes or hours to, in optimistic early predictions, millions of years; modern calculations generally suggest much shorter, but still unusually long, values for superheavy nuclei.

Related concepts

An analogous predicted region for still heavier, truly spherical "doubly magic" nuclei exists near Z = 126, and a separate "island" of stability is also predicted for nuclei bound by the strong force in exotic configurations. The search for the island motivates the construction of dedicated facilities such as the SHE Factory in Dubna and informs the naming of flerovium and livermorium, elements in the island's predicted foothills.
