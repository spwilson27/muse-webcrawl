Somali Latin alphabet

The Somali Latin alphabet is the official writing system of the Somali language. It was formally adopted by the government of Somalia in October 1972, when Somali was declared the country's official language and the Latin-based orthography became mandatory for government and education. Developed principally by the Somali linguist Shire Jama Ahmed, the alphabet was chosen after more than a decade of debate over rival indigenous scripts — notably the Osmanya, Borama, and Kaddare alphabets — and the Arabic script.

The alphabet

The alphabet consists of the following letters, in official order:

B, T, J, X, KH, D, R, S, SH, DH, C, G, F, Q, K, L, M, N, W, H, Y, A, E, I, O, U

  • There are no letters p, v, or z in native Somali words.
  • The consonants C and X represent the voiced and voiceless pharyngeal fricatives /ʕ/ and /ħ/ (sounds borrowed conceptually from Arabic), Q is a uvular stop /q/, and ' (apostrophe-like) marks the glottal stop.
  • Digraphs KH /x/, SH /ʃ/, and DH — a voiced retroflex stop /ɖ/ — are treated as unitary sounds.
  • Long vowels are written double: aa, ee, ii, oo, uu. Vowel length is phonemic in Somali.
  • Somali is a tonal language, but tone is not marked in the standard orthography.

History

Somali had been written for centuries in Arabic script and in several invented indigenous alphabets, of which Osmanya (created in the 1920s by Osman Yusuf Kenadid) had the strongest following. After independence in 1960, the Somali Language Committee evaluated scripts for a decade without political agreement — script choice carried clan and religious sensitivities. The military government of Siad Barre cut the knot in 1972 by decree, selecting Shire Jama Ahmed's Latin orthography, judged easiest for printing, typewriting, and mass literacy. A nationwide literacy campaign (1973–75), including the Rural Literacy Campaign of 1974–75 in which teachers and students were sent to the countryside, dramatically raised literacy and established the script.
