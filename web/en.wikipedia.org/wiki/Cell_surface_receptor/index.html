Cell surface receptor

Cell surface receptors (membrane receptors, transmembrane receptors) are receptors embedded in the plasma membrane of cells. They act in cell signalling by receiving (binding to) extracellular molecules such as hormones, neurotransmitters, cytokines and growth factors, and transducing that binding into intracellular responses, allowing cells to communicate with their environment and with each other.

Classes

Three major classes are recognised by their transduction mechanism. Ion channel-linked receptors (ligand-gated ion channels) open a pore on binding ligand, as at chemical synapses. G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), the largest family, have seven transmembrane helices and activate heterotrimeric G proteins; they include receptors for adrenaline, dopamine, light (rhodopsin) and most odours. Enzyme-linked receptors, such as receptor tyrosine kinases (including the insulin and epidermal growth factor receptors), have intrinsic enzymatic activity or associate with enzymes that phosphorylate intracellular signalling proteins.

Mechanism

Ligands that are hydrophilic or too large to cross the membrane bind the receptor's extracellular domain, inducing a conformational change, oligomerisation or enzymatic activation. The resulting intracellular signal is typically amplified by second messengers — cyclic AMP, calcium ions, inositol phosphates — and by phosphorylation cascades, ultimately altering enzyme activity, ion channel behaviour or gene expression.

Medical importance

Cell surface receptors are targets for a large share of modern drugs, including beta blockers, antihistamines and many anticancer antibodies. Mutations that lock receptors in an active state, such as HER2 amplification in breast cancer, can drive disease, while others cause insensitivity syndromes such as androgen insensitivity.
