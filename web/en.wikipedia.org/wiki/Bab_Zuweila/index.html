Bab Zuweila

Bab Zuweila is one of the three remaining gates in the walls of the Old City of Cairo, Egypt. It was completed in 1092 and is one of the finest surviving examples of Fatimid military architecture. The gate was named after the Zuwayla Berber soldiers from Libya who were quartered in the district nearby.

Architecture

The gate is flanked by two semicircular towers and is surmounted by a loggia. Above it rise two slender minarets, added around 1415, which belong to the Mosque of Sultan al-Mu'ayyad next door; visitors can climb them for views over Islamic Cairo. The massive wooden doors of the gate are famous in their own right and were traditionally believed to have healing powers in local folklore.

History

Bab Zuweila was the southern gate of the Fatimid city, built under the vizier Badr al-Jamali. In the Mamluk period it was the site of public executions — the heads of executed criminals and enemies, including those of Mongol envoys and the last Mamluk sultan Tuman Bay II in 1517, were displayed on spikes above the gate. The area outside the gate was a starting point for caravans departing for the Hajj pilgrimage and for trade with the south.

The gate stands at the southern end of al-Muizz Street, the main ceremonial street of medieval Cairo, near the tentmakers' bazaar.
