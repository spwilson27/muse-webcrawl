Fused filament fabrication (FFF) is a 3D printing process that uses a continuous filament of a thermoplastic material, fed from a spool through a heated extruder head and deposited in layers to build up a three-dimensional object. The process was developed by S. Scott Crump, co-founder of Stratasys, and commercialised under the trademarked name fused deposition modeling (FDM).

Process

A digital model is sliced into horizontal layers by slicing software, which generates toolpaths (usually in G-code). The printer heats the filament to just above its melting point in a "hot end" and extrudes it through a fine nozzle — commonly 0.4 mm in diameter — while the head and/or build plate move so that each layer is drawn in turn. Successive layers fuse as they cool. Overhanging geometry may require removable support structures, and enclosed or heated build chambers reduce warping in materials such as ABS.

History and spread

Crump filed the FDM patent in 1989; it expired in 2009. The open-source RepRap project, begun by Adrian Bowyer in 2005, deliberately targeted the same technique under the generic name FFF, and the patent's expiry triggered an explosion of inexpensive desktop printers from companies such as MakerBot, Prusa Research and Creality. FFF is now the most widely used 3D printing technology by number of machines.

Applications and limitations

FFF is used for rapid prototyping, customised products, jigs and fixtures, education and hobbyist fabrication. Its limitations include visible layer lines, anisotropic strength (parts are weakest between layers), slower build speeds than resin processes, and limited resolution compared with stereolithography.
