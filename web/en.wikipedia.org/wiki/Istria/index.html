Istria

The triangular Istrian peninsula, shared by Croatia, Slovenia, and Italy.

Istria (Croatian and Slovene: Istra; Italian: Istria) is the largest peninsula in the Adriatic Sea, situated at the head of the Adriatic between the Gulf of Trieste and the Kvarner Gulf. It is shared by three countries: Croatia, Slovenia, and Italy. Most of the peninsula lies in Croatia's Istria County, whose administrative centre is Pazin and whose largest city is Pula.

Geography

The peninsula covers about 3,476 square kilometres. Its coastal towns — including Rovinj, Poreč, Umag, and Opatija on its eastern flank — are major tourist destinations, while the interior is a rolling landscape of hilltop villages, vineyards, and olive groves, sometimes called "Red Istria" for its terra rossa soil in the south and west, "White Istria" for its limestone mountains, and "Grey Istria" for its flysch hills.

History

Istria was inhabited by the Histri tribe in antiquity before Roman conquest in the 2nd century BC. Pula retains a celebrated Roman amphitheatre. After the fall of Rome it passed through Byzantine, Frankish, and Venetian hands; the coastal towns were long dominated by the Republic of Venice while the interior belonged to the Habsburgs. It became part of Italy after the First World War, and after the Second World War most of Istria was assigned to Yugoslavia, prompting the exodus of much of its Italian population. Since 1991 the bulk of the peninsula has been part of independent Croatia.

Culture

Istria is notably bilingual in places, with Italian holding official status in parts of Istria County. The region is renowned for its cuisine, including truffles from the Motovun forest, olive oil, and Malvasia wines, and for distinctive folk traditions such as the two-part singing style inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage list.
