Viscoelasticity

Viscoelasticity is a material property that combines both viscous and elastic characteristics during deformation. Viscous materials resist shear flow and exhibit strain that increases linearly with time when stress is applied, while elastic materials strain immediately when stretched and return just as quickly to their original state once the stress is removed. Viscoelastic materials display both behaviours: their response depends on the rate and duration of loading.

Background

In the nineteenth century, physicists such as James Clerk Maxwell, Ludwig Boltzmann and Lord Kelvin investigated creep and stress relaxation in glasses, metals and biological materials. Unlike purely elastic solids, which obey Hooke's law, or purely viscous fluids, which obey Newton's law of viscosity, viscoelastic materials exhibit time-dependent strain.

Elastic versus viscoelastic behaviour

A purely elastic material stores all the energy of deformation and returns it on unloading; a viscoelastic material dissipates part of that energy as heat, producing a hysteresis loop in a loading–unloading cycle. Characteristic phenomena include:

  • Creep — increasing strain under constant stress.
  • Stress relaxation — decreasing stress under constant strain.
  • Hysteresis — a phase lag between applied stress and resulting strain.
  • Strain-rate dependence — the material appears stiffer under rapid loading.

Constitutive models

Linear viscoelastic behaviour is commonly represented by mechanical analogues combining springs and dashpots. The Maxwell model places a spring and dashpot in series and captures stress relaxation. The Kelvin–Voigt model places them in parallel and captures creep. The standard linear solid (Zener) model combines both arrangements and describes creep and relaxation simultaneously. Fitting these models to experiment yields relaxation times and spectra used throughout polymer science.

Dynamic properties

Under oscillatory loading, viscoelastic materials are described by a complex modulus with a storage modulus (elastic, in-phase response) and a loss modulus (viscous, out-of-phase response). The ratio of loss to storage modulus, tan δ, measures damping. Time–temperature superposition allows curves measured at different temperatures to be shifted into a single master curve, extending the effective range of frequencies studied.

Applications

Viscoelasticity governs the behaviour of polymers, rubbers, biological tissues, asphalt, and many foods and foams. It is central to rheology, vibration damping design, biomechanics, and the study of geological materials over long time scales.
