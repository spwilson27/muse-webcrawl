Allegoresis

Allegoresis is the practice of interpreting a text — especially an ancient poem, myth or scripture — as an allegory, that is, as carrying a hidden meaning distinct from its literal sense. The term distinguishes the interpretation of a work as allegorical from the composition of an allegory (allegory in the rhetorical sense).

History

Allegoresis originated in 6th-century BCE Greece, when thinkers such as Theagenes of Rhegium defended Homer against charges of impiety by reading the gods' quarrels as figurative representations of natural forces or moral conflicts. The Stoics systematised the method, and Philo of Alexandria applied it to the Hebrew Bible. Early Christian writers, above all Origen, developed multi-level exegesis — literal, moral and spiritual senses — which shaped medieval biblical interpretation and the reading of classical poetry throughout the Middle Ages and Renaissance.

Criticism

Modern critics have questioned whether the hidden meanings allegoresis uncovers were intended by the original authors, and the method is often contrasted with historical-critical approaches that privilege a text's original context. Nevertheless, allegoresis remains important to the history of hermeneutics and to the study of how communities reread canonical texts.
