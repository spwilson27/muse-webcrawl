Information architecture

Information architecture (IA) is the structural design of shared information environments — the organisation, labelling, search, and navigation systems that help people find and manage information in websites, apps, intranets, and other digital and physical spaces. The Information Architecture Institute defined it as "the structural design of shared information environments" and "the art and science of organizing and labeling websites... to support usability and findability."

Components

The standard account, from Lou Rosenfeld and Peter Morville's influential book Information Architecture for the World Wide Web (1998, the "polar bear book"), identifies four interdependent systems: organization systems (how information is categorised and structured, by schemes such as topic, task, or audience, and structures such as hierarchies and databases), labeling systems (the words used for categories and links), navigation systems (menus, breadcrumbs, and other ways of moving through content), and search systems. Good IA balances the needs of users, the nature of the content, and the goals and constraints of the context — the three "circles" of Rosenfeld and Morville's model.

Practice

Information architects use techniques including content inventories and audits, card sorting, tree testing, user research, sitemaps, and wireframes. The term was coined by architect and graphic designer Richard Saul Wurman in the 1970s, who imagined an "architecture of information" for making complex information understandable; it was later adopted by library and information science and by web design, where it became a core discipline of user experience (UX) design.
