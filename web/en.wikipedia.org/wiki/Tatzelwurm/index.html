Tatzelwurm


Artist's impression of the Tatzelwurm, the "claw worm" of Alpine folklore

The Tatzelwurm (from German Tatze, "claw/paw", and Wurm, "worm"), also known as the Stollenwurm or Bergstutzen, is a lizard-like creature of Alpine folklore, reported from the mountainous regions of Austria, Bavaria, Switzerland, and northern Italy. It is one of the most persistently reported cryptids of Europe and is a subject of interest in cryptozoology.

Description

Descriptions of the creature vary but generally agree on a serpentine or stout lizard-like body between roughly 0.5 and 1.5 metres (about 2 to 5 feet) long, a large cat-like head, a short tail, and — its distinguishing feature — a single pair of clawed forelimbs, with the hindquarters tapering limbless like a snake. Some accounts add a second pair of hind legs, bristles along the back, large round eyes, or the ability to leap at attackers. The Tatzelwurm is often said to hiss, whistle, or squeak, and in some tales its breath or bite is poisonous.

History of reports

Sightings have been recorded since at least the 18th century. A well-known early account, from 1779, describes the naturalist Hans Fuchs encountering two of the creatures near Unken in Austria; the shock was said to have contributed to his death, and his family erected a votive painting commemorating the event. Reports continued through the 19th and early 20th centuries, including a widely publicised 1934 Swiss case in which a photographer named Balkin claimed to have taken a picture of the creature — a photograph later widely suspected of being staged using a ceramic model.

The creature features in the works of cryptozoologists such as Bernard Heuvelmans, who grouped it among unexplained European "worm" reports. Suggested mundane explanations include otters, large lizards such as the European legless skink or slow worms, salamanders, and the amphisbaenian known as the Italian worm lizard, though none fully matches the traditional description.

In folklore and culture

In Alpine tradition the Tatzelwurm occupies a similar niche to the dragon or lindworm but on a smaller scale: a dangerous creature of high pastures and rocky slopes, feared by herdsmen and hunters. It remains a popular motif in regional tourism, heraldry, festival costumes, and children's literature in the Alpine countries.
