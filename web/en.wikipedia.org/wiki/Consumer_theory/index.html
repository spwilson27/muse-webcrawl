Consumer theory

Consumer theory
FieldMicroeconomics
Key conceptsUtility, budget constraint, indifference curve, demand
Key figuresWilliam Stanley Jevons, Léon Walras, Vilfredo Pareto, John Hicks

Consumer theory is the branch of microeconomics that relates preferences to consumption expenditures and to consumer demand curves. It analyzes how consumers maximize the desirability of their consumption, as measured by their preferences subject to limitations on their expenditures, by maximizing utility subject to a consumer budget constraint. Factors influencing consumers' evaluation of the utility of goods include income levels, cultural factors, product information, and physio-psychological factors.

The consumer's problem

A consumer's consumption is usually modeled as choosing a bundle of goods (x1, …, xn) to maximize a utility function U(x) subject to the budget constraint p1x1 + … + pnxn ≤ m, where pi is the price of good i and m is income. The solution yields the Marshallian demand functions xi(p, m). Graphically, the optimum lies where the highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the budget line, so that the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio.

Preferences

Standard assumptions on preferences include completeness, transitivity, monotonicity (more is better) and convexity (averages are preferred to extremes). Under these and continuity, preferences can be represented by a utility function (Debreu's theorem). The dual problem — minimizing expenditure to reach a given utility — yields Hicksian (compensated) demand and the expenditure function, linked by the Slutsky equation, which decomposes a price change into substitution and income effects.

Effects of price changes

When the price of a good falls, the substitution effect always moves consumption toward the now-cheaper good, while the income effect may reinforce or oppose it. For a normal good both effects increase quantity demanded; for an inferior good the income effect partly offsets substitution, and in the extreme (theoretical) case of a Giffen good, the income effect dominates and demand rises with price. The theory also underlies the construction of price indices and welfare measures such as consumer surplus, compensating variation and equivalent variation.

Revealed preference

Paul Samuelson's revealed preference approach dispenses with unobservable utility and infers preferences from observed choices, resting on axioms such as the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP). Later developments address intertemporal choice, choice under uncertainty (expected utility), household production (Gary Becker), and the challenges posed by behavioral economics, which documents systematic deviations from the rational-choice model such as loss aversion and hyperbolic discounting.
