Oncovirus

An oncovirus or oncogenic virus is a virus that can cause cancer. The term originated from studies of acutely transforming retroviruses in the 1950s and 1960s, which were found to carry viral oncogenes. It is now estimated that about 15–20% of human cancers worldwide are attributable to infectious agents, principally viruses.

Human oncoviruses

Seven viruses are recognised as causes of human cancers: Epstein–Barr virus (associated with Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma); hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus (hepatocellular carcinoma); human papillomaviruses, especially HPV-16 and HPV-18 (cervical and other anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers); human T-lymphotropic virus 1 (adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma); Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (HHV-8); and Merkel cell polyomavirus (Merkel cell carcinoma).

Mechanisms

Oncoviruses cause cancer through several mechanisms: viral oncogenes can drive uncontrolled cell proliferation (for example HPV's E6 and E7 proteins, which inactivate the tumour suppressors p53 and Rb); viruses may insert their genome near host proto-oncogenes; and chronic infection and inflammation, as with hepatitis viruses, can lead to accumulating cellular damage. Vaccines against hepatitis B and HPV are effective cancer-prevention tools.
