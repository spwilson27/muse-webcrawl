The Salon des Refusés (French for "exhibition of rejects") was an exhibition of works rejected by the jury of the official Paris Salon, held in 1863 and authorised by Emperor Napoleon III. It is regarded as a landmark in the emergence of modern art, because the works shown there — ridiculed by many contemporary critics — included some of the foundational paintings of the avant-garde.

Background

The Paris Salon, run under the auspices of the Académie des Beaux-Arts, was the principal route to artistic success in nineteenth-century France. In 1863 the jury was unusually severe, rejecting some 3,000 of the roughly 5,000 works submitted. The rejected artists protested loudly, and when the complaints reached the emperor, Napoleon III inspected the rejected works and decreed that they be displayed in another part of the Palais de l'Industrie so that the public could judge for itself.

The 1863 exhibition

The exhibition opened on 17 May 1863 and drew large, often mocking crowds. Its most notorious exhibit was Édouard Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (shown as Le Bain), whose depiction of a nude woman picnicking with fully clothed men scandalised visitors. Other exhibits included works by Camille Pissarro, Paul Cézanne, Gustave Courbet and James McNeill Whistler's Symphony in White, No. 1: The White Girl. Critical reaction was largely hostile, but the event demonstrated that an audience existed for art outside academic sanction.

Legacy

Further salons of the refused were held in 1874, 1875 and 1886, and the idea fed directly into the independent exhibitions of the Impressionists from 1874 onward and the Salon des Indépendants (founded 1884), which abolished juries altogether. The phrase "salon des refusés" has since become a general term for exhibitions of rejected or avant-garde work.
