Dominion

A dominion was a self-governing country within the British Empire and later the Commonwealth of Nations that shared the British monarch as head of state. The term came into constitutional use with Canadian Confederation in 1867, when the new federation was styled the Dominion of Canada — a name drawn from Psalm 72: "He shall have dominion also from sea to sea."

Development

Dominion status was subsequently granted to Australia (1901), New Zealand (1907), Newfoundland (1907), South Africa (1910), the Irish Free State (1922) and, at the end of British rule in India, to India and Pakistan (1947) and Ceylon (1948). The Balfour Declaration of 1926 defined the dominions as "autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another in any aspect of their domestic or external affairs, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown". The Statute of Westminster 1931 gave that equality legal effect, granting the dominions full legislative independence.

Decline of the term

After the Second World War the word fell out of use, carrying to some ears an implication of subordination. The London Declaration of 1949 allowed republics to remain in the Commonwealth, and the former dominions became known simply as Commonwealth realms or, for those that became republics (India 1950, Pakistan 1956, South Africa 1961, Ireland 1949), as republics within the Commonwealth. Canada retained "Dominion" in some official uses (e.g. Dominion Day, renamed Canada Day in 1982). Today the concept survives in the sixteen realms that share the monarch, each legally independent under its own crown.
