Recursion

Recursion is a method of solving a computational problem where the solution depends on solutions to smaller instances of the same problem. Recursion solves such problems by using functions that call themselves, breaking the problem down until reaching a base case that can be answered directly.

In computer science

A recursive function is defined in terms of itself. It must have one or more base cases, which return a result without further recursion, and a recursive case, which reduces the input toward a base case. Classic examples include computing factorials, traversing trees, the Tower of Hanoi puzzle, and divide-and-conquer sorting algorithms such as quicksort and mergesort.

Recursion and iteration are expressively equivalent — any recursive program can be rewritten iteratively using an explicit stack — but recursive formulations are often clearer. Excessive recursion depth can exhaust the call stack, causing a stack overflow; tail recursion can be optimised by some compilers into iteration.

In mathematics

Recursive definitions define objects in terms of simpler instances of themselves: the natural numbers are defined by 0 and the successor operation (Peano axioms), and sequences are defined by recurrence relations. Mathematical induction is the corresponding proof technique. Kurt Gödel's primitive recursive functions and the general theory of computability (the μ-recursive functions) formalise the concept.

In language and art

Human languages exhibit recursion in syntax: a noun phrase can contain a relative clause containing another noun phrase, allowing unbounded embedding ("the cat that chased the rat that..."). In art, recursion appears in mise en abyme and the Droste effect, in M. C. Escher's self-referential prints, and in fractal geometry.
