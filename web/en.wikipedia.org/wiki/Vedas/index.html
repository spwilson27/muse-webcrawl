Vedas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A palm-leaf manuscript of Vedic hymns in Devanagari script
A palm-leaf manuscript of Vedic hymns in Devanagari script

The Vedas are a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism. Composed in Vedic Sanskrit between roughly 1500 and 900 BC (in the Punjab region and transmitted orally with extreme precision), they comprise four collections — the Rigveda (hymns), the Samaveda (chants), the Yajurveda (ritual formulas) and the Atharvaveda (spells and practical lore) — each with its Samhita, Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads. Hindus regard them as śruti, 'what is heard' — revelation, authorless and eternal.

Content

The Rigveda's 1,028 hymns address the gods of a sacrificial religion — Indra the thunderer, Agni the fire, Soma the sacred drink — while its late hymns (the Nasadiya Sukta's creation scepticism, the Purusha Sukta's cosmic sacrifice) open philosophy. The Brahmanas codify ritual; the Upanishads (c. 800–200 BC) interiorise it into the doctrines of atman and brahman that shape all later Hindu thought.

Transmission and status

Oral preservation — mnemonic techniques checking every syllable — kept the texts near-frozen for three millennia (UNESCO lists Vedic chanting as intangible heritage). In classical Hinduism the Vedas' authority is the line between orthodoxy (astika) and its others (Buddhism, Jainism), even where practice owes them little — a scriptures' empire of reverence rather than readership.
