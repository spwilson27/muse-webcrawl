Mise-en-scène

Mise-en-scène (French for "placing on stage") is a term from theatre and film criticism describing the arrangement of everything that appears within the frame or on the stage: sets, props, costumes, lighting, and the placement and movement of actors. In film studies it refers to what is in front of the camera and how it is composed, as distinct from cinematography (how it is filmed) and editing (how shots are joined).

Elements

Analyses of mise-en-scène typically consider setting and décor, costume and make-up, lighting (including its quality, direction, and colour), and staging — the movement and positioning of figures in depth within the frame. Directors celebrated for their mise-en-scène, such as Max Ophüls, Kenji Mizoguchi, and Orson Welles, often favour long takes and deep staging that let meaning emerge from composition rather than cutting.

In film criticism

The term became central to film criticism through the French journal Cahiers du Cinéma in the 1950s, whose critics championed mise-en-scène as the essence of directorial authorship in the auteur theory. André Bazin praised realistic mise-en-scène and the long take for preserving the ambiguity and continuity of reality, contrasting it with montage-based styles.
