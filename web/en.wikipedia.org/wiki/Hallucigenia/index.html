Hallucigenia is a genus of extinct lobopodian animals known from Cambrian deposits including the Burgess Shale of Canada and the Chengjiang biota of China. It is famous for its bizarre appearance and for having been reconstructed upside down when first described in detail.

Description

Hallucigenia was a small, worm-like animal, roughly 0.5–5.5 cm long, with seven or eight pairs of slender legs ending in claws and two rows of stout conical spines running down its back. Its head bore a pair of simple eyes and a mouth with radial teeth; a bulbous structure at one end was long mistaken for the head. In 2015, study of exceptionally preserved specimens confirmed the true head and showed the legs ended in paired claws similar to those of modern velvet worms.

History of interpretation

The fossils were originally classified within Canadia. In 1977, Simon Conway Morris erected the genus Hallucigenia, imagining an animal walking on its stiff spines like stilts, with a row of tentacles along its back — a reconstruction so strange it inspired the name, referring to a dreamlike, hallucinatory quality. Re-examination in 1991 showed it had been inverted: the "tentacles" were legs, and the "stilts" were defensive dorsal spines. Hallucigenia is now understood as a member of the lobopodians, related to modern onychophorans (velvet worms) and the wider panarthropod group that includes arthropods and tardigrades.
