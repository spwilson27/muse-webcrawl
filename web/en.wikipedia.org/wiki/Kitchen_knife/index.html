A kitchen knife is a knife intended for use in food preparation. Kitchen knives come in many forms specialised by task, from the versatile chef's knife to small paring knives, and are among the oldest and most essential kitchen tools. Blade profiles, grinds, and materials differ between Western and Japanese traditions.

Types

  • Chef's knife — an all-purpose knife, typically 20 cm (8 in), for chopping, slicing, and dicing.
  • Paring knife — a short knife (8–10 cm) for peeling and detailed work.
  • Bread knife — a serrated blade for crusty loaves and soft produce.
  • Boning knife — a narrow, flexible blade for removing bones.
  • Cleaver — a large rectangular blade for chopping through bone and squash.
  • Santoku — a Japanese all-purpose knife with a sheepsfoot profile and granton edge.
  • Nakiri and usuba — Japanese vegetable knives with straight edges.
  • Yanagiba — a long, single-bevel Japanese knife for slicing sashimi.
  • Utility knife — a mid-size knife between paring and chef's sizes.
  • Carving knife — a long, thin knife for slicing roasts.

Materials and construction

Blades are made from carbon steel (easy to sharpen but prone to rust and discoloration), stainless steel (corrosion-resistant), high-carbon stainless steel, layered "Damascus" laminates, or ceramics such as zirconia, which are extremely hard but brittle. Knives are forged from a single billet or stamped from sheet steel, and may have a full tang extending through the handle. Handle materials include wood, composites, and steel.

Care

A keen edge is maintained by honing with a sharpening steel and restored by grinding on a whetstone or other abrasive. Most kitchen knives should be hand-washed and stored on a magnetic strip or in a block to protect the edge; dull knives are more dangerous than sharp ones because they require more force and slip more easily.
