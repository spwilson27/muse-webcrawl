Simele massacre

The Simele massacre (Syriac: ܦܪܡܬܐ ܕܣܡܐܠܐ, Pramta d'Simele) was a massacre of Assyrian Christians carried out by the Iraqi Army, led by Bakr Sidqi, with Kurdish and Arab irregulars, in August 1933 in and around the town of Simele (Semel) in northern Iraq. Estimates of the dead range from about 600 to several thousand; the total number of Assyrians killed across dozens of villages in the Dohuk and Mosul districts is often estimated around 3,000.

Background

The Assyrians of Hakkari had been expelled from their homeland during the First World War (the Seyfo genocide) and resettled in Iraq under the British Mandate, where many served in the Assyrian Levies. When Iraq became independent in 1932, Assyrian leaders under Patriarch Shimun XIX pressed for an autonomous enclave, fearing for their community without British protection. Tensions escalated into clashes near the Syrian border in early August 1933, which Iraqi nationalist press portrayed as rebellion.

The massacre

Beginning around 8–11 August 1933, army units and irregulars attacked Assyrian villages; at Simele itself hundreds of unarmed men who had gathered under assurances of safety were machine-gunned. Looting and killings spread through the region, and thousands of survivors fled to French Syria. Raphael Lemkin later cited the case among those that moved him to formulate the concept of genocide.

Aftermath and remembrance

The events hardened Arab nationalist sentiment in Iraq — the army was feted on its return to Baghdad — and shattered Assyrian hopes of autonomy; Patriarch Shimun was exiled. Assyrian communities worldwide commemorate the victims on 7 August, Assyrian Martyrs' Day.
