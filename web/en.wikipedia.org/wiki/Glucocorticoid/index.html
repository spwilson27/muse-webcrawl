Glucocorticoid


Cortisol, the main endogenous glucocorticoid in humans.

Glucocorticoids are a class of corticosteroid hormones produced in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex. They are involved in the metabolism of glucose, the regulation of the immune system and the body's response to stress. The name derives from their roles in glucose metabolism, their site of production in the cortex of the adrenal gland, and their steroidal structure.

Physiology

The principal glucocorticoid in humans is cortisol (hydrocortisone); corticosterone predominates in many other species. Secretion is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis: corticotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus stimulates release of ACTH from the anterior pituitary, which in turn stimulates cortisol synthesis. Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm, peaking around waking, and rises sharply in response to stress. Negative feedback by cortisol restrains further ACTH and CRH release.

Mechanism of action

Glucocorticoids diffuse across cell membranes and bind the cytosolic glucocorticoid receptor. The receptor–ligand complex translocates to the nucleus, where it alters gene transcription, either activating genes via glucocorticoid response elements or repressing the activity of transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1. Through these genomic effects, glucocorticoids increase hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogen storage, promote protein catabolism and lipolysis, and suppress inflammation and immune responses by inhibiting cytokine production and immune-cell trafficking.

Pharmacology and clinical use

Synthetic glucocorticoids — including prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone and dexamethasone — are among the most widely prescribed anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drugs. They are used in asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, organ transplantation and many allergic and autoimmune conditions, and as replacement therapy in adrenal insufficiency. Long-term or high-dose use carries substantial adverse effects: hyperglycaemia and steroid-induced diabetes, osteoporosis, hypertension, weight gain with a characteristic central distribution (as in Cushing's syndrome), increased infection risk, mood disturbance and suppression of the HPA axis, so doses are tapered where possible.
