Traffic circle

A traffic circle is a type of circular intersection in which traffic flows around a central island. In most countries, vehicles circulate in one direction only — counterclockwise in right-hand-traffic countries and clockwise where traffic drives on the left. The term is often used interchangeably with roundabout, though traffic engineers in North America distinguish older, larger, higher-speed traffic circles (also called rotaries) from modern roundabouts, which have yield-on-entry control, smaller diameters, and deflection that forces low speeds.

History

Circular junctions appeared in the early 20th century: William H. Eno designed New York's Columbus Circle (1905) and Paris's Place de l'Étoile carried circulating traffic from about 1907. In the United States, rotaries became common on highways from the 1920s to the 1950s, but their high speeds and weaving sections produced accidents and congestion, and many were removed or signalised. Britain's adoption of the priority-to-circulating-traffic (offside priority) rule in 1966, and the mini-roundabout, produced the modern roundabout design that spread internationally from the 1970s onward.

Operation and safety

In a modern roundabout, entering traffic yields to traffic already circulating, and geometry (entry deflection, small inscribed circle) keeps speeds low. Studies by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety and others find that converting conventional intersections to roundabouts reduces fatal and injury crashes substantially — often by 70–80% — because right-angle and head-on collisions are eliminated and speeds are lower, though minor property-damage crashes may increase initially. Roundabouts also reduce delay and fuel consumption where traffic flows are balanced, because vehicles need not stop at red lights.

Notable examples

Famous large traffic circles include the Arc de Triomphe's Place Charles de Gaulle in Paris, with twelve radiating avenues; the Magic Roundabout in Swindon, England, composed of five mini-roundabouts; and numerous monumental circles in Washington, D.C. and Mexico City.

Terminology

Regional terms include rotary (New England), roundabout (most of the English-speaking world), and gyratory (older British usage). Mini-roundabouts, with fully traversable central islands, serve constrained urban junctions.
