Atomic physics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atomic physics is the field of physics that studies atoms as an isolated system of electrons and an atomic nucleus. It is primarily concerned with the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus and the processes by which these arrangements change. The term is sometimes confused with nuclear physics, which studies the nucleus alone, and with atomic energy.

History

The field grew from spectroscopy and the discovery of spectral lines. The Bohr model (1913) explained the hydrogen spectrum with quantised electron orbits, and the development of quantum mechanics in the 1920s — the Schrödinger and Heisenberg formulations — provided the complete theoretical framework, including electron spin, the Pauli exclusion principle and the shell structure underlying the periodic table.

Topics

Atomic physics covers atomic structure and energy levels, transitions and spectral lines, fine and hyperfine structure, atoms in external electric and magnetic fields (Stark and Zeeman effects), collisions, photoionisation, and precision measurements that test fundamental physics. Modern subfields include laser cooling and trapping, Bose–Einstein condensates, cold and ultracold atoms, and atomic clocks — the most precise timekeeping devices known.

Applications

Atomic physics underpins lasers, atomic clocks used in GPS, quantum information science, and spectroscopic techniques across astronomy, chemistry and medicine.
