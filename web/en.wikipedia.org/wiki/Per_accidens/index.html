Per accidens

Per accidens is a Latin expression meaning "by accident" or "through an accident", used in philosophy and logic in contrast to per se ("through itself" or "in itself"). Something is said to be or to happen per accidens when it holds not essentially or necessarily, but in virtue of some incidental (accidental) feature or connection.

The distinction originates in Aristotle's philosophy, notably in the Metaphysics and Posterior Analytics, where scientific knowledge (episteme) is concerned with what belongs to things per se, while what belongs only per accidens cannot be the subject of strict demonstration. The terminology was taken up extensively in medieval scholastic philosophy, particularly in the works of Thomas Aquinas, who for example distinguished between causes per se and causes per accidens in his arguments for the existence of God.

Related uses include "accidental predication", in which a predicate applies to a subject only through an accident, as when we say "the musician is pale".
