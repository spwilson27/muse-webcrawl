Ultra (cryptography)

Ultra was the designation adopted by British military intelligence during the Second World War for signals intelligence obtained by breaking high-level encrypted Axis radio and teleprinter communications, most famously the Enigma cipher used by Nazi Germany. Decrypts produced at Bletchley Park in Buckinghamshire, England, were disseminated under the strictest secrecy to a small circle of commanders; the existence of Ultra remained a state secret until 1974.

Origins

Polish cryptologists of the Cipher Bureau — including Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski — first broke Enigma in the 1930s and shared their methods with Britain and France in 1939. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman and others industrialised the attack with the electromechanical Bombe, while the high-level Lorenz teleprinter traffic (codenamed "Tunny") was broken with the help of the Colossus machines, the world's first programmable electronic computers.

Use and impact

Ultra intelligence influenced the Battle of the Atlantic, the North African campaign, and the preparations for D-Day, where it confirmed that German commanders had accepted the Fortitude deception. Because acting too openly on decrypts risked revealing the source, cover stories were devised for Ultra-guided operations. Historians have estimated that Ultra shortened the war, though by how much — claims range up to two years — remains debated.

Secrecy

All Ultra personnel were bound by the Official Secrets Act for life. The secret was publicly exposed in F. W. Winterbotham's The Ultra Secret (1974), after which the contributions of Bletchley Park and of the Polish pioneers became widely recognised.
