Ethical altruism

Ethical altruism is an ethical doctrine that holds that the moral value of an individual's actions depends solely on the impact on other individuals, regardless of the consequences for the individual itself. It is a form of consequentialism in which the morally right action is the one that best promotes the welfare of others.

Overview

The term "altruism" (French altruisme, from autrui, "others") was coined by Auguste Comte, the founder of positivism, who held that individuals have a moral obligation to renounce self-interest and live for others. Ethical altruism stands in contrast to ethical egoism, which holds that agents should act in their own self-interest, and to ethical theories that balance the interests of self and others, such as many forms of utilitarianism.

Criticism

Critics argue that ethical altruism is excessively demanding, requiring the sacrifice of one's own legitimate projects and well-being whenever doing so would benefit others. Ayn Rand attacked altruism as a doctrine that treats self-sacrifice as the highest virtue, defending instead rational egoism. Philosophers such as Thomas Nagel have attempted to articulate intermediate positions in which the interests of others constrain, but do not entirely override, personal projects. The contemporary effective altruism movement applies the underlying idea — that one should do as much good for others as one reasonably can — using evidence and cost-effectiveness analysis.
