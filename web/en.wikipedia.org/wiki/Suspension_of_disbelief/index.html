Suspension of disbelief

Suspension of disbelief is the avoidance — consciously or otherwise — of critical thinking when engaging with works of fiction, allowing the audience to accept implausible or impossible elements for the sake of enjoyment. The term was coined by the poet and philosopher Samuel Taylor Coleridge in his Biographia Literaria (1817), where he described the "willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith." The concept is fundamental to literature, theatre, film, and games, particularly in fantasy and science fiction.

Audiences willingly suspend disbelief to engage with fantastical fictional worlds.

Origin

Coleridge proposed that if a writer could infuse a "human interest and a semblance of truth" into a fantastic tale, readers would suspend judgment about its implausibility. He contrasted his own supernatural poems in Lyrical Ballads with Wordsworth's poems of ordinary life. Earlier anticipations appear in Horace's Ars Poetica and in Shakespeare, but Coleridge's formulation made the idea canonical.

Criticism and development

J. R. R. Tolkien objected in his essay "On Fairy-Stories" (1939) that suspension of disbelief is a failure state: a successful fantasy creates a "secondary world" that commands secondary belief on its own terms, and disbelief must be suspended only when the storyteller breaks internal consistency. Critics also note that audiences differ in tolerance, that genre conventions set expectations of plausibility, and that the concept borders on related ideas such as immersion, transportation, and the paradox of fiction — why people feel real emotions for characters they know are unreal.
