Self

In philosophy, the self is an individual person's experience of their own existence, the subject of one's own experience of phenomena including perception, emotions, thoughts and actions. The self is the locus of personal identity, and questions about its nature, persistence and relationship to body and mind are central to philosophy of mind, psychology and religion.

Philosophy

René Descartes argued that the self is most fundamentally a thinking thing, known directly through the cogito ("I think, therefore I am"). John Locke grounded personal identity in continuity of consciousness and memory, while David Hume famously reported that introspection reveals only a bundle of perceptions, never a unitary self — a view echoed in the Buddhist doctrine of anattā (non-self). Immanuel Kant distinguished the empirical self, known through inner sense, from the transcendental unity of apperception that makes experience possible.

Psychology

In psychology, the self encompasses self-concept, self-esteem, self-knowledge and self-image. William James distinguished the "I" (the self as knower) from the "Me" (the self as known). Carl Jung used "Self" for the archetype of wholeness uniting conscious and unconscious — see Self in Jungian psychology. Social psychologists study how the self is shaped by reflected appraisals and group membership.

Neuroscience

Research on the bodily self, minimal self and narrative self examines how the brain constructs a sense of ownership, agency and temporal continuity, and how these can break down in disorders such as depersonalisation and schizophrenia.
