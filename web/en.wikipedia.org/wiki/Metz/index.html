Metz (French pronunciation: [mɛs]) is a city in northeastern France, the prefecture of the Moselle department in the Grand Est region, located at the confluence of the Moselle and Seille rivers. With a history spanning some 3,000 years, it was a Celtic oppidum, an important Roman city, the birthplace of the Carolingian dynasty, and a contested border fortress between France and Germany.

History

Known to Rome as Divodurum Mediomatricorum, Metz grew wealthy on trade and wine. It became a bishopric and later a free imperial city of the Holy Roman Empire. France seized the Three Bishoprics in 1552, and the Treaty of Westphalia confirmed Metz as French in 1648. After the Franco-Prussian War, Germany annexed Metz (1871–1918) and ringed it with powerful forts; the city returned to France after the First World War, was annexed again by Nazi Germany in 1940, and liberated in the Battle of Metz in late 1944.

Sights

The Gothic Saint-Étienne Cathedral is famed for its vast stained-glass windows, including works by Marc Chagall. Other landmarks include the Temple Neuf on an island in the Moselle, the German Gate, the Arsenal concert hall, and the Centre Pompidou-Metz, a branch of the Paris museum opened in 2010 under a sweeping timber roof designed by Shigeru Ban.

Economy and transport

Metz is a commercial and administrative centre for Lorraine, close to the Luxembourg and German borders. The Lorraine TGV station and the A31 motorway link it to Paris, Luxembourg, and the Saarland.
