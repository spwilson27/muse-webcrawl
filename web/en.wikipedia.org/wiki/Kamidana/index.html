Kamidana

A kamidana (神棚, "god-shelf" or "kami shelf") is a miniature household Shinto altar found in Japanese homes, offices, shops and martial-arts dōjō. It is the Shinto counterpart of the Buddhist butsudan, and provides a place to venerate the kami daily.

Form

The kamidana is typically a small wooden shrine (miyagata), modelled on shrine architecture and often made of Japanese cypress, mounted on a shelf high on a wall, ideally facing south or east. Inside or before it is placed an ofuda, a paper or wooden talisman obtained from a shrine — most commonly Ise Grand Shrine's Jingū Taima — which serves as the vessel (yorishiro) for the kami. Miniature torii gates, sacred rope (shimenawa), and mirror and jewel ornaments may accompany it.

Worship

Offerings (shinsen) of water, rice, salt and sake, plus evergreen sakaki branches, are placed before the altar, traditionally renewed on the 1st and 15th of each month (and daily for water). Worshippers clap twice and bow in the usual shrine manner. Houses often enshrine protective deities such as the kitchen god (kamado-gami) alongside the Ise amulet, and businesses enshrine Ebisu or Inari; dōjō kamidana are honoured at the start and end of training.

History

Household kami shelves became widespread in the Edo period with the distribution of Ise amulets by travelling onshi priests, and they were actively promoted under State Shinto, when portraits of the Emperor were also enshrined. After 1945 the practice continued as a private custom under the Association of Shinto Shrines.
