Scraper (archaeology)

In prehistoric archaeology, a scraper is a stone tool — typically of flint, chert, or obsidian — characterised by one or more edges retouched into a steeply angled working edge, used for scraping tasks such as cleaning hides, working wood or bone, and processing plant material. Scrapers are among the most common and long-lived tool types, appearing from the Lower Palaeolithic through the Bronze Age and in ethnographic contexts worldwide.

Types

Typologies, notably François Bordes' classification of Middle Palaeolithic (Mousterian) tools, distinguish scrapers by the position and shape of the retouched edge: side scrapers (racloirs) have the edge along a long side of a flake and may be straight, convex, or concave; end scrapers (grattoirs), typical of Upper Palaeolithic assemblages, carry the edge on the short distal end of a flake or blade; there are also transverse, convergent (pointed), double, and thumbnail scrapers. Notched scrapers and denticulates are related forms with serrated edges.

Function and analysis

Microwear (use-wear) analysis, pioneered by Sergei Semenov and developed by Lawrence Keeley, shows that many scrapers were used on animal hides — the classic "hide scraper" hafted or held in the hand — while others worked wood, bone, or antler. Residue analysis and experimental archaeology supplement these inferences. The abundance of scrapers in Mousterian assemblages, and variation in their forms, were central to the Bordes–Binford debate over whether Neanderthal toolkits reflected distinct cultures or different activities.
