Jewish burial

Jewish burial is governed by Jewish law (halakha) and custom, which emphasize prompt burial, simplicity, and respect for the dignity of the dead. Burial in the ground is traditionally required; cremation is forbidden by Orthodox and discouraged by Conservative authorities, though some Reform communities permit it.

Rituals and practices

Traditionally the body is watched over continuously until burial (shemira) and ritually washed and purified (tahara) by the chevra kadisha, the communal burial society, then dressed in simple white shrouds (tachrichim) and placed in a plain wooden coffin. Burial ideally takes place within 24 hours of death. At the funeral, mourners recite the Kaddish and traditionally tear a garment (keriah) as a sign of grief; at the graveside, mourners themselves help fill the grave with earth.

Mourning periods

Burial is followed by shiva, seven days of intense mourning at home, then sheloshim, a thirty-day period, and for a parent a full year of mourning during which Kaddish is recited. The anniversary of the death (yahrzeit) is observed annually thereafter.
