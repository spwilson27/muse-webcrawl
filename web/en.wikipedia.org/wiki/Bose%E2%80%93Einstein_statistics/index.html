Bose–Einstein statistics

Bose–Einstein statistics
Introduced by: Satyendra Nath Bose (1924); Albert Einstein (1924–25)
Applies to: bosons (integer-spin particles)
Contrast: Fermi–Dirac statistics (fermions); Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics (classical)
Predicts: Bose–Einstein condensation

In quantum statistics, Bose–Einstein statistics describe the distribution of identical bosons — particles with integer spin — over the energy states in thermal equilibrium. Unlike fermions, any number of bosons may occupy the same quantum state. The mean occupation number of a state of energy ε is 1 / (e(ε−μ)/kT − 1), where μ is the chemical potential, k is Boltzmann's constant, and T is temperature.

History

Satyendra Nath Bose derived the distribution in 1924 in a new counting of photon states that reproduced Planck's law of black-body radiation without reference to classical electrodynamics. Unable to publish in British journals, Bose sent the paper to Albert Einstein, who translated it into German, arranged its publication, and extended the statistics to massive particles, predicting in the process a new state of matter, the Bose–Einstein condensate.

Applications

Bose–Einstein statistics govern photons (yielding Planck's law), phonons in solids, and bosonic atoms at low temperature. The tendency of bosons to accumulate in the ground state underlies lasers, superfluidity, and superconductivity (through bosonic Cooper pairs). At high temperatures, where occupation numbers are small, the distribution approaches the classical Maxwell–Boltzmann limit.
