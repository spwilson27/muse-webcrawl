Gaspard Monge

Born9 May 1746, Beaune, France
Died28 July 1818, Paris, France
FieldsMathematics, engineering, physics
Known forDescriptive geometry; Monge–Ampère equation; metric system

Gaspard Monge, Comte de Péluse (9 May 1746 – 28 July 1818) was a French mathematician, commonly considered the father of descriptive geometry and of differential geometry in its modern form, and a leading figure of the French Revolution's scientific mobilisation. His name is one of the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.

Early career

Monge was educated at Beaune and became a draughtsman and teacher at the royal engineering school at Mézières, where, still a teenager, he solved a fortress-design problem so elegantly that his method — descriptive geometry, the systematic projection of three-dimensional objects onto planes — was classified as a military secret for years. He became a professor at Mézières and later taught in Paris, entering the Academy of Sciences in 1780.

Revolution and institutions

An enthusiastic revolutionary, Monge served as Minister of the Marine (1792–93), helped organise the Republic's arms production, and played a central role in the reform of weights and measures that produced the metric system. He was a co-founder of the École Polytechnique (1794), where his lectures on descriptive geometry, published as Géométrie descriptive (1799), shaped engineering education worldwide. He also helped found the École Normale.

Egypt and later life

Monge accompanied Napoleon on the Egyptian expedition of 1798, becoming president of the Institut d'Égypte and helping to survey and document the country. Napoleon made him a count and senator. After the Bourbon Restoration he was stripped of his honours and expelled from the Institute; he died in Paris in 1818. In 1989 his remains were transferred to the Panthéon.

Mathematics

Monge's Application de l'analyse à la géométrie (1795) founded the analytic differential geometry of curves and surfaces. His name survives in the Monge–Ampère equation of nonlinear partial differential equations, Monge cones and Monge patches in differential geometry, and the theory of optimal transport, which grew from his 1781 memoir on moving earth (déblais et remblais).
