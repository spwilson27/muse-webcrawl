Sale and leaseback

A sale and leaseback, or simply leaseback, is a financial transaction in which the owner of an asset — typically real estate, aircraft, ships or industrial equipment — sells it to a buyer and simultaneously leases it back for an agreed term, continuing to use it while releasing the capital tied up in it. The seller-lessee gains liquidity without interrupting operations; the buyer-lessor gains a long-term rental income stream backed by the asset.

Mechanics and motives

Deals are usually structured as long leases (15–25 years in property) at rents linked to the purchase price and often indexed to inflation, frequently "triple-net", with the tenant responsible for insurance, taxes and maintenance. Companies use leasebacks to raise cash, reduce debt, fund expansion, or focus management on their core business rather than property ownership. Retail chains, airlines, hotel groups and supermarkets are heavy users; a retailer may sell its freehold stores to an institutional investor and trade on as tenant.

Accounting and risks

Under older accounting rules leasebacks could move obligations off the balance sheet; IFRS 16 and ASC 842 now require most leases to be recognised as right-of-use assets and lease liabilities, and restrict recognising gains on sale where the leaseback shows the sale was not genuine. Risks for the tenant include loss of control of a strategic property, exposure to rent reviews, and dependence on the landlord's solvency — and, if the company fails, the loss of the asset itself. For investors, value depends on the tenant's creditworthiness and the property's alternative-use value.
