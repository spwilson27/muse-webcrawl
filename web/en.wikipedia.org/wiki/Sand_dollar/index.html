Sand dollar

Sand dollars are flat, burrowing sea urchins belonging to the order Clypeasteroida. They live in shallow sandy or muddy seabeds in temperate and tropical seas. Their round, flattened skeletons (tests), often found washed up on beaches, display a distinctive five-petalled pattern.

Anatomy

A living sand dollar is covered in short, fine spines coated with cilia, which give it a velvety, often purple, brown or grey appearance. The spines move food particles along grooves toward the mouth on the underside. Inside the body, a hard jaw apparatus called Aristotle's lantern grinds food. The rigid test is made of fused calcium carbonate plates.

Ecology

Sand dollars feed on detritus, algae and tiny organisms in the sediment. Some species stand partly upright in the sand in dense beds, straining food from currents. They are preyed on by fish, sea stars and gulls. The bleached white tests found on beaches are the skeletons left after the animal dies.

In culture

The petal pattern on the test is sometimes associated in folklore with the legend of the sand dollar, linking its markings to the life of Christ, and the five doves supposedly released when the centre is broken open.
