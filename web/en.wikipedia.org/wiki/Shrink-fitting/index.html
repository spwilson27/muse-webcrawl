Shrink-fitting

Shrink-fitting is an interference fit technique in mechanical engineering in which two parts are joined by heating the outer (female) part so that it expands, assembling it over the inner (male) part, and allowing it to cool and contract, forming a tight, strong joint through friction. The converse method—cooling the inner part (for example with liquid nitrogen or dry ice) so it contracts before assembly—is sometimes called freeze-fitting.

Applications

Shrink fits are used where a joint must transmit high torque or withstand heavy loads without fasteners or keys: railway wheel tires shrunk onto wheel centers, gear rings on shafts, bearing races, crankshaft counterweights, and the hoops historically used on cannon barrels and wooden wheels (the wagon-wheel tire). Induction heating, oil baths, and ovens are common heating methods.

Design

The required temperature change depends on the coefficient of thermal expansion of the materials and the specified interference, which is usually a small fraction of the shaft diameter. Excessive interference can yield or crack the outer part, so stresses are calculated using thick-cylinder (Lamé) equations.
