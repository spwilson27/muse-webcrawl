8VSB is an eight-level vestigial sideband modulation method. It is the standard transmission method for over-the-air digital television in the ATSC system used in North America, Mexico, South Korea, and a few other countries. Developed by Zenith Electronics and adopted by the ATSC in the mid-1990s, it carries about 19.39 megabits per second of useful data in a 6 MHz television channel.

Technical description

8VSB encodes three bits per symbol in a single-carrier amplitude-modulated signal with eight discrete levels. The signal is filtered with a root-raised-cosine vestigial sideband filter, and includes a small pilot tone at the lower band edge to aid receiver synchronization. Forward error correction uses Reed–Solomon coding, trellis coding, and interleaving. The related 16VSB mode doubles the data rate to 38.78 Mbit/s but is far less robust and was intended for cable; in practice cable in North America uses QAM instead.

Reception and criticism

Early 8VSB receivers struggled with multipath interference, particularly with indoor antennas, leading some broadcasters to argue for the multicarrier COFDM scheme used in the European DVB-T standard. Successive generations of demodulator chips greatly improved equalization and multipath handling, and the FCC retained 8VSB. The newer ATSC 3.0 standard abandons 8VSB in favor of OFDM.
