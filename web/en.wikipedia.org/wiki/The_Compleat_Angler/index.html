The Compleat Angler, or the Contemplative Man's Recreation is a book by Izaak Walton, first published in 1653 by Richard Marriot in London. It is a celebration of the art and spirit of fishing in verse and prose, presented as a dialogue between Piscator (the fisherman), Venator (the hunter), and Auceps (the falconer), and is one of the most famous and frequently reprinted books in English literature.

Walton continued to add to the work throughout his life; the fifth edition, issued in 1676, included a second part on fly fishing written by his friend and adopted son Charles Cotton. The book combines practical instruction on tackle, baits, and the habits of fish with poems, songs, recipes, and reflections on the contemplative virtues of the rural life.

Context and content

Walton, a London ironmonger who retired to the countryside, wrote the book after the English Civil War. Its peaceful celebration of nature and friendship is often read against the turmoil of that conflict. The narrative follows Piscator as he travels along the River Lea and later, in Cotton's part, the River Dove in Derbyshire, instructing his companions in the catching of trout, pike, chub, and other fish.

Legacy

The book has never been out of fashion among anglers and is often said to be one of the most reprinted books in the English language after the Bible and the works of Shakespeare. Hundreds of editions have appeared. Its famous sentiments — "Good company and good discourse are the very sinews of virtue" and Walton's injunction to "study to be quiet" — are widely quoted, and the book gave its name to countless angling clubs, pubs, and tackle shops.
