Venison

Venison is the meat of a deer. The term originally derived from the Latin venari ("to hunt") and in medieval usage referred to the meat of any hunted game animal — including hare, boar and other beasts of the chase — but in modern usage it is restricted to deer, including species such as red deer, fallow deer, roe deer, whitetail deer and reindeer. Venison may be obtained from wild hunting or deer farming.

Characteristics and nutrition

Venison is typically darker, finer-grained and lower in fat than beef or pork from grain-fed animals, with a flavour often described as rich or "gamey", influenced by the animal's diet of acorns, sagebrush, herbs and forage. It is high in protein, iron and B vitamins and relatively low in saturated fat, though its leanness makes it prone to drying out and it is usually cooked rare or medium-rare, or braised or minced with added fat. Chronic wasting disease in deer populations has raised food-safety concerns in affected regions.

Production and trade

New Zealand is the world's largest producer and exporter of farmed venison, raising red deer; farmed production also exists in Europe and North America, where white-tailed deer are farmed in some jurisdictions though sale of wild-shot deer is restricted in others. In the United Kingdom and continental Europe, a substantial share of venison comes from wild culling of deer populations to manage woodland and farmland damage. Cuts mirror those of beef — loin, haunch (leg), saddle and shoulder — and the meat is used for roasts, steaks, sausages, charcuterie and traditional dishes such as German Rehbraten and venison pie.

Cultural notes

Deer hunting and the serving of venison have long carried associations of aristocratic privilege: in medieval England deer in royal forests were reserved to the Crown, and poaching them was severely punished. Venison remains a feature of autumnal and festive cooking in many northern countries.
