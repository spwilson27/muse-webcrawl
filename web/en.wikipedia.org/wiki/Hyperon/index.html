Hyperon

In particle physics, a hyperon is any baryon containing one or more strange quarks but no charm, bottom or top quarks. Hyperons are heavier than the ordinary nucleons (protons and neutrons) and decay rapidly via the weak interaction, typically within a fraction of a nanosecond.

Types

The principal hyperons are the Lambda (Λ, one strange quark), the three Sigmas (Σ), the two Cascades (Ξ, two strange quarks) and the Omega (Ω⁻, three strange quarks). Their discovery in cosmic rays and early accelerators during the late 1940s and 1950s revealed particles produced strongly but decaying slowly — the puzzle that led Murray Gell-Mann and others to introduce the quantum number called "strangeness", conserved in strong interactions but violated in weak decays.

Role in the quark model

Hyperons were essential evidence for the Eightfold Way classification and the quark model: the prediction and 1964 discovery of the Ω⁻, with exactly the expected mass, was a landmark triumph. Hyperons also matter in astrophysics, since they may appear in the dense cores of neutron stars, softening the equation of state — the "hyperon puzzle" in reconciling this with the existence of very massive neutron stars. Weak decays of hyperons, such as Λ → pπ⁻, exhibit parity violation, and "hypernuclei" containing a bound hyperon are studied in nuclear physics.
