Veneration

Veneration (Latin: veneratio; Greek: proskynesis) is the act of honouring a saint, a relic, an icon, or a revered person or object with deep respect, reverence and devotion. In Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and some Anglican theology, veneration is carefully distinguished from latria, the worship due to God alone; honour given to saints is termed dulia, and the special honour given to the Virgin Mary is termed hyperdulia.

Veneration of saints

The veneration of saints developed in the early Church around the tombs of martyrs, whose anniversaries were celebrated with prayer and the Eucharist. Believers asked saints to intercede with God on their behalf, much as one might ask a living friend to pray for them. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) affirmed the veneration of icons, teaching that the honour shown to an image passes to the person depicted.

Relics and pilgrimage

Physical remains of saints (relics) and objects associated with them became focal points of veneration, housed in reliquaries and altar stones and carried in procession. Major shrines drew pilgrimages from across Christendom. Protestant reformers largely rejected the veneration of saints and relics as idolatrous, while Catholic and Orthodox practice retained it; the Council of Trent reaffirmed it in the sixteenth century.

Other traditions

Analogous practices exist worldwide: the veneration of ancestors in East and Southeast Asian religions, reverence for gurus and holy persons in Hinduism, devotion at Sufi saints' tombs in Islam, and honour paid to arhats and bodhisattvas in Buddhism.
