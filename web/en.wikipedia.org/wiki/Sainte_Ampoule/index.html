Sainte Ampoule

The Sainte Ampoule ("Holy Ampulla") was a small glass vial containing the holy oil (chrism) used to anoint the kings of France at their coronations in Reims Cathedral. According to legend, the vial was brought from heaven by a dove at the baptism of Clovis I, the first Catholic king of the Franks, around 496 AD, giving the French monarchy a uniquely sacred character.

The Sainte Ampoule, containing the chrism for anointing French kings.

Legend and use

The legend is first recorded in the 9th century by Archbishop Hincmar of Reims. At Clovis's baptism by Saint Remigius (Remi), the chrism was said to have been forgotten, whereupon a dove descended bearing a vial of oil in its beak. The ampoule was thereafter kept at the Abbey of Saint-Remi in Reims and used in the coronation rite: a small particle of its balm was mixed with chrism and applied to the king's head, breast, shoulders, and joints. Nearly every French king from the medieval period onward was anointed with oil from the vial.

Destruction and survival

During the French Revolution, the Sainte Ampoule was publicly smashed on 7 October 1793 by the commissioner Philippe Rühl in Reims as a symbol of the old regime. Fragments of the vial and some of its contents were secretly preserved by witnesses, and the surviving remains were used at the coronation of Charles X in 1825 — the last use of the rite. The remnants are preserved today in a 19th-century reliquary in Reims.
