Artificial sweetener

Sugar substitutes / artificial sweeteners
Examples: Aspartame, sucralose, saccharin, acesulfame K, stevia (natural), xylitol (sugar alcohol)
Uses: Diet drinks, tabletop sweeteners, sugar-free foods
First discovered: Saccharin (1879)

Artificial sweeteners are synthetic sugar substitutes — food additives that provide sweetness with few or no calories. Many are far sweeter than sucrose: saccharin is roughly 300–500 times sweeter, sucralose about 600 times, and neotame several thousand times, so only tiny amounts are needed.

Common sweeteners

Saccharin (discovered 1879) was the first; aspartame (1965, about 200× sugar) is used in diet sodas but breaks down with heat and is unsuitable for people with phenylketonuria; sucralose (1976) is heat-stable and widely used in drinks and baking; acesulfame potassium is often blended with others. Stevia extracts and monk fruit are plant-derived "natural" high-intensity sweeteners, while sugar alcohols such as xylitol and erythritol provide bulk sweetness with fewer calories.

Uses and regulation

Sweeteners are regulated as food additives with acceptable daily intake (ADI) values set by bodies such as the FDA, EFSA, and JECFA. They are used in soft drinks, yoghurts, chewing gum, and products for people managing diabetes or weight.

Health debate

Decades of studies and regulatory reviews have generally found approved sweeteners safe within ADIs, though research continues — the WHO's 2023 guideline advised against using non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, citing limited long-term benefit, and IARC classified aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) in 2023 while JECFA maintained its ADI.
