The Calling of an Engineer


The Iron Ring, presented at the Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer

The Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer (French: Rite d'engagement de l'ingénieur) is a private ceremony in Canada at which graduating and licensed engineers are invited to attend an "obligation ceremony", written by Rudyard Kipling in 1922, with the text provided at the request of Professor H. E. T. Haultain of the University of Toronto. Participants take an obligation to uphold the standards and ethics of the profession and receive an Iron Ring to wear on the little finger of the working hand as a reminder of that obligation.

History

Haultain proposed in 1922 that engineers take an ethical oath similar to the Hippocratic Oath of medicine. He asked the British poet Rudyard Kipling, who had written extensively about engineers, to compose the ceremony. The first ceremony was held in Toronto on 25 April 1925. The ritual is administered by the Corporation of the Seven Wardens, named for the first wardens, who were past presidents of the Engineering Institute of Canada.

The ceremony

The ritual is closed to the public and to the press; only candidates and ring-holding engineers may attend. The Obligation is a solemn undertaking, not an oath or a religious ceremony. The iron ring — originally claimed in popular lore to be made from the steel of the collapsed first Quebec Bridge, a claim the Corporation treats as myth rather than fact — symbolises the engineer's responsibility, humility, and the consequences of error.

Influence

The Canadian ritual inspired the later American Order of the Engineer (founded 1970), in which participants accept an "Obligation of an Engineer" and wear a stainless steel ring. Similar ceremonies have been adopted in some other countries, but the Iron Ring remains distinctively Canadian.
