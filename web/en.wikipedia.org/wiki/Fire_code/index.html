Fire code

A fire code (also fire prevention code or fire safety code) is a set of rules prescribing how buildings and other premises must be designed, maintained and operated in order to preserve life and property from fire and related hazards. Fire codes address the storage and handling of flammable materials, the means of egress from buildings, fire detection and suppression systems, and the occupancy limits of spaces.

Content

Typical provisions include requirements for fire alarms and smoke detectors, sprinkler systems, fire extinguishers, emergency lighting, unobstructed and clearly marked exits, fire-resistance ratings of walls and doors, and restrictions on the storage of hazardous substances. Codes also regulate activities such as open burning, fireworks and the use of temporary heating equipment, and they set standards for inspections and for the training of staff in evacuation procedures.

Model codes

In the United States, the principal model fire codes are the International Fire Code, published by the International Code Council, and NFPA 1, Fire Code, published by the National Fire Protection Association, used together with NFPA 101, the Life Safety Code. States and municipalities adopt these model codes, often with local amendments, and enforce them through fire marshals and building officials. The United Kingdom regulates fire safety primarily through the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which requires responsible persons to carry out fire risk assessments.

History

Fire regulations date back to antiquity, but modern fire codes developed in response to catastrophic urban fires and disasters, such as the Great Fire of London (1666), the Iroquois Theatre fire (1903), the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire (1911) and the Cocoanut Grove fire (1942). Each disaster exposed failures — locked exits, flammable decorations, overcrowding — that were subsequently addressed in code provisions, a pattern often described by the saying that fire codes are "written in blood."

Enforcement

Compliance is typically verified at the design and construction stage through building permits and inspections, and afterwards through periodic inspections by the local fire authority. Violations can lead to orders to remedy, fines, closure of premises, or criminal liability in the event of injury or death.
