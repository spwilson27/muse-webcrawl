Entoptic phenomenon

Entoptic phenomena (from Greek entós, "within", and optikós, "relating to sight") are visual effects whose source is the eye itself. In other words, the images originate from within the observer's own eye rather than from external objects. Entoptic images have a physical basis in the structure of the eye and differ between individuals.

Examples

Well-known entoptic phenomena include:

  • Floaters (muscae volitantes) — shadow images of structures in the vitreous humour, seen as drifting specks or threads against a bright background.
  • Blue field entoptic phenomenon — tiny bright dots moving along squiggly lines in the visual field when looking at bright blue light, caused by white blood cells in the capillaries of the retina.
  • Haidinger's brush — a faint yellowish bow-tie pattern visible when viewing polarised light, arising from the dichroism of macular pigments.
  • Purkinje images — reflections of objects from the surfaces of the lens and cornea.
  • Phosphenes — the perception of light without light entering the eye, for example from pressure on the eyeball or electrical stimulation.
  • The Purkinje tree, the shadow of the retinal blood vessels, which can be made visible by unusual illumination of the eye.

Explanation

Under normal conditions the visual system discounts stable images formed by structures of the eye itself, so entoptic effects are usually unobtrusive. They become apparent when viewing conditions change the angle, intensity, or wavelength of illumination, or when the observer looks at a uniform bright field such as a clear sky.
