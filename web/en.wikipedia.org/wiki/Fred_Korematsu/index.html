Fred Toyosaburo Korematsu (January 30, 1919 – March 30, 2005) was an American civil rights activist who resisted the internment of Japanese Americans during World War II. His challenge to Executive Order 9066 reached the Supreme Court as Korematsu v. United States (1944), which controversially upheld the exclusion of Japanese Americans from the West Coast.

Defiance and trial

Korematsu, a welder born in Oakland to Japanese immigrant parents, refused to report for relocation in 1942, underwent minor surgery to alter his appearance, and was arrested in San Leandro, California. The ACLU took his case as a test of the exclusion orders. He was convicted of violating the military exclusion order, and the conviction was affirmed by the Ninth Circuit and then by the Supreme Court in a 6–3 decision on 18 December 1944, which held the exclusion a military necessity.

Vindication

In the early 1980s, researcher Peter Irons and Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga uncovered documents showing that the government had suppressed and altered evidence contradicting the claim of military necessity. On the basis of this evidence, a federal court vacated Korematsu's conviction in 1983 on a writ of coram nobis. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 formally apologised for the internment and paid reparations to survivors.

Later life and honours

Korematsu remained an activist, speaking on civil liberties and, after the September 11 attacks, warning against similar treatment of Arab and Muslim Americans; he filed amicus briefs in Guantánamo detention cases. President Bill Clinton awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998. In 2010 California established Fred Korematsu Day (30 January), the first statewide day named for an Asian American. The Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Trump v. Hawaii expressly repudiated the 1944 ruling as "gravely wrong".
