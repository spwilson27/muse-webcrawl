Dickinsonia is a genus of extinct organism of the Ediacaran biota, living in the late Ediacaran period, roughly 567 to 550 million years ago. Its fossils, found principally in the Flinders Ranges of South Australia and near the White Sea in Russia, are flat, oval, ribbed impressions ranging from a few millimetres to nearly a metre and a half long, with a body divided into quilted segments (isomers) arranged with glide reflection symmetry about a central groove.

Discovery and interpretation

The genus was described in 1947 by Reg Sprigg, discoverer of the Ediacara fossils, and named for Ben Dickinson, a South Australian geological official. Its affinities were debated for seventy years: it was variously interpreted as a jellyfish, a worm, a fungus, a lichen, a giant protist, or a member of an extinct kingdom (the "Vendobionta"). Trace-like impressions suggest it moved and fed on microbial mats, absorbing nutrients through its underside.

Animal affinity

In 2018, analysis of exceptionally preserved White Sea specimens reported the biomarker cholesteroids — compounds associated with animals — in Dickinsonia fossils, strongly supporting its identification as one of the earliest known animals, though its exact placement (variously near sponges, cnidarians, or the base of the bilaterians) remains unsettled. Growth studies show it added new segments at one end as it grew, reaching an adult size and then largely maintaining it.
