Faraday's law of induction

Faraday's law of induction (or simply Faraday's law) is a basic law of electromagnetism predicting how a magnetic field interacts with an electric circuit to produce an electromotive force (emf) — a phenomenon known as electromagnetic induction. It is the fundamental operating principle of transformers, inductors and many electric generators.

Statement

The law states that the induced emf in any closed circuit is equal to the negative of the time rate of change of the magnetic flux through the circuit:

ε = − dΦB / dt

where ε is the electromotive force and ΦB is the magnetic flux. The negative sign, expressing Lenz's law, indicates that the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it. For a coil of N tightly wound turns, the emf is multiplied by N.

History

The law was discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831, based on experiments showing that a changing magnetic field — produced by moving a magnet near a coil or switching a neighbouring current on and off — induced a current. Joseph Henry made similar observations independently in America. James Clerk Maxwell later incorporated Faraday's law as one of Maxwell's equations, in differential form relating the curl of the electric field to the time derivative of the magnetic field.

Applications

Electromagnetic induction underlies electric power generation (rotating coils in generators), transformers that step voltages up and down, induction motors, electric guitar pickups, induction cooking, and magnetic recording. The "flux rule" formulation can fail in certain moving-circuit configurations (the Faraday paradox), which are resolved by the full Maxwell–Faraday equation and Lorentz force considerations.
