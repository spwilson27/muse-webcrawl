Ama (diving)

Ama (Japanese: 海女, "sea women") are Japanese divers famous for collecting pearls, though traditionally they harvested seafood — abalone, turban shells, seaweed, and oysters — by freediving without breathing apparatus. Most ama are women, and the tradition is documented in Japanese sources over two thousand years old. The best-known ama regions are the Shima Peninsula in Mie Prefecture and the coast of Chiba; a related tradition exists in Korea, where divers are called haenyeo.

Practice

Ama dive in groups supervised by a boatman, traditionally a male relative, who tends the safety rope and boat. Divers make repeated dives to depths of roughly 5 to 25 metres, surfacing with a characteristic whistling breath called isobue, which helps regulate breathing. Dives are organised into shifts called funado or shore-based kachido depending on whether a boat is used.

History

Mentions of ama appear in early Japanese texts including the Man'yōshū. In the late 19th and 20th centuries, ama became central to the cultured-pearl industry of Mikimoto Kōkichi, who employed them to tend pearl oysters. Since the mid-20th century the number of ama has fallen sharply — from well over ten thousand after the war to a small fraction of that today — owing to declining shellfish stocks, restrictive quotas intended to prevent overharvesting, and younger generations choosing other work.

Culture

Ama culture, tools, and rites are preserved in museums at Toba and Shima. The women traditionally wear white to ward off sharks and for good fortune, and shore huts called amagoya serve as rest houses where divers warm themselves around a fire between shifts.
