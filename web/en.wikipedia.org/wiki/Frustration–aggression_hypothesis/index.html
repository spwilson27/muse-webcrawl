Frustration–aggression hypothesis

The frustration–aggression hypothesis is a theory of aggression proposed in 1939 by psychologists John Dollard, Leonard Doob, Neal Miller, O. H. Mowrer and Robert Sears in their monograph Frustration and Aggression. In its original, strong form, the hypothesis stated two propositions: aggression is always a consequence of frustration, and frustration always leads to some form of aggression. Frustration was defined as the blocking of goal-directed behaviour.

Development and criticism

The theory drew on psychoanalytic ideas and behaviourism, and suggested that when direct aggression against the source of frustration is inhibited — for example by fear of punishment — aggression may be displaced onto other targets, including scapegoats. The hypothesis was applied to phenomena such as the relationship between economic hardship and lynching in the American South.

The strong form of the theory was soon qualified. Miller (1941) acknowledged that frustration produces an instigation to aggression but also instigations to other responses, and aggression need not follow. Critics noted that aggression can occur without frustration, for example as learned instrumental behaviour. In a widely cited reformulation, Leonard Berkowitz proposed that frustration produces negative affect, which in turn creates a readiness for aggression; aggressive cues and appraisal processes determine whether aggression actually occurs. This revised model became part of the broader cognitive-neoassociation theory of aggression.
