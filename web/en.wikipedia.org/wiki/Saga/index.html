Saga

A saga is a prose narrative, most famously the medieval Icelandic and lesser-known Norwegian prose tales of kings, heroes, saints, bishops, and ordinary Icelandic farmers. The word saga (plural sögur) comes from Old Norse and relates to the verb segja, "to say, tell" — a saga is thus something told, a story or a history. Sagas were written mostly in Iceland in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, though their subject matter often reaches back to the settlement of Iceland (c. 870–930) and the Viking Age.

Classification

Scholars conventionally divide the saga corpus into several genres:

  • Kings' sagas (konungasögur) — lives of Norwegian and Danish kings, such as Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla.
  • Sagas of Icelanders (Íslendingasögur) — family sagas about Icelandic farmers and chieftains of the tenth and early eleventh centuries, including Njáls saga, Egils saga, and Laxdæla saga.
  • Contemporary sagas (samtíðarsögur) — accounts of twelfth- and thirteenth-century Iceland, collected in Sturlunga saga.
  • Legendary sagas (fornaldarsögur) — tales of the heroic age before Iceland's settlement, such as the Völsunga saga.
  • Chivalric sagas (riddarasögur) — translations and adaptations of French romances.
  • Saints' and bishops' sagas — hagiographic and ecclesiastical narratives.

Style and historicity

Saga style is famously laconic and objective in tone: events are presented with minimal authorial commentary, long genealogies anchor the narrative, and dialogue carries much of the drama. The so-called "saga age" heroes are characterised by honour, feud, and fate. How far the family sagas preserve genuine oral tradition versus thirteenth-century literary invention has been debated since the nineteenth century; the "bookprose" theory of Sigurður Nordal emphasised individual authorship, against the older "freeprose" view of oral transmission.

Manuscripts and legacy

Sagas survive in vellum manuscripts such as Möðruvallabók and in later paper copies. They remain central to Icelandic national identity and have influenced modern fantasy literature: J. R. R. Tolkien and William Morris drew heavily on them. In modern English usage, "saga" has broadened to mean any long, involved story or series of events — hence its use for modern family chronicles and film franchises.
