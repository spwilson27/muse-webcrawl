The Grapes of Wrath

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Joad family's overloaded Hudson truck on Route 66, piled with mattresses and household goods, crossing the Oklahoma plains]

The Grapes of Wrath is a novel by John Steinbeck, published in 1939, the defining American novel of the Great Depression. It follows the Joad family, tenant farmers driven from Oklahoma by drought, mechanisation and the banks, on their migration along Route 66 to the promised land of California — where they find not Eden but surplus labour, starvation wages and violent strikebreaking.

Alternating the Joads' story with short intercalary chapters — on tractors, highways, used-car lots and the economics of plenty destroyed — the novel builds from documentary into something biblical in scale. It won the National Book Award and the Pulitzer Prize, was filmed by John Ford in 1940, and was publicly burned in Kern County, California, where much of it is set.

Origins and writing

Steinbeck reported on migrant camps for the San Francisco News in 1936 (the articles became The Harvest Gypsies) and travelled with FSA camp manager Tom Collins, to whom the book is jointly dedicated. He wrote the novel in about five months of ferocious concentration in 1938, aiming, as he put it in his journal, to rip a reader's nerves to rags. Its title comes from the 'Battle Hymn of the Republic', its ending — Rose of Sharon nursing a starving stranger — from its fusion of documentary and parable.

Reception

The book was the best-seller of 1939, was attacked as communist propaganda by growers' associations and members of Congress, defended by Eleanor Roosevelt among others, and credited with pressing Congress toward migrant relief. Steinbeck's Nobel citation in 1962 rested heavily on it; it remains a fixture of American curricula and of arguments about what novels can do.
