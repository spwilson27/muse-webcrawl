In geometry, Boy's surface is an immersion of the real projective plane in three-dimensional Euclidean space. It was discovered in 1901 by the German mathematician Werner Boy, who had been commissioned by David Hilbert to prove — as was then expected — that the projective plane could not be immersed in three dimensions. Boy instead constructed such an immersion, with self-intersections but no pinch points.

Properties

The real projective plane is a non-orientable closed surface, so it cannot be embedded in ℝ³ without self-intersection; Boy's surface shows an immersion is nevertheless possible. The surface has a curve of double points and one triple point where three sheets cross. Bernard Morin's model, built from the ideas of Morin and Jean-Pierre Petit, has threefold rotational symmetry with only one triple point — the minimum possible — and can be used to visualise turning a sphere inside out (sphere eversion), since the projective plane relates to the sphere through its double cover.

Models

Physical models of Boy's surface were built at the Mathematisches Forschungsinstitut Oberwolfach, whose logo depicts one, and a large model stands outside that institute, donated by Mercedes-Benz in 1991. Robert Bryant gave a parametrisation of Boy's surface with desirable curvature properties, and explicit polynomial parametrisations have been constructed by François Apéry, based on work of Morin.
