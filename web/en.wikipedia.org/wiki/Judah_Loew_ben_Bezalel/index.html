Judah Loew ben Bezalel (c. 1520 – 17 September 1609), widely known as the Maharal of Prague (from the Hebrew acronym of "Moreinu ha-Rav Loew", "Our Teacher, Rabbi Loew"), was a major Talmudic scholar, Jewish mystic, mathematician and philosopher who served as chief rabbi of Prague in the late sixteenth century.

Life

Loew was born probably in Poznań, Poland (some traditions say Worms), into a distinguished rabbinic family. He served as rabbi in Nikolsburg (Mikulov), Moravia, for about two decades, then in Prague, where he founded a yeshiva and clashed with communal lay leadership over corruption. In 1592 he famously met Emperor Rudolf II in an audience whose subject remains unknown. He died in Prague in 1609 and is buried in the Old Jewish Cemetery; his tombstone is among the cemetery's best known.

Thought and writings

Loew wrote extensively — works such as Gur Aryeh (a commentary on Rashi's Torah commentary), Tif'ereth Yisrael on the giving of the Torah, Netzach Yisrael on exile and redemption, and Be'er ha-Golah defending rabbinic aggadah against critics. His thought emphasizes dichotomies between the natural and supernatural orders, and he strongly opposed aspects of the pilpul method of Talmud study, advocating clearer pedagogy. His discourses influenced later Jewish philosophy and, through Hasidism and the writings of Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook, modern religious thought.

The golem legend

In later folklore, the Maharal is credited with creating a golem — a living being formed from river clay — to defend Prague's Jews from attack. Although early accounts of a Prague golem exist, the famous association with Loew himself was popularized in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, notably in Yudl Rosenberg's 1909 collection; historians generally treat the connection as legend rather than fact. The golem story has made Loew a figure of world literature and popular culture.
