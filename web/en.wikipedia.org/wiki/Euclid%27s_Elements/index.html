The Elements (Ancient Greek: Στοιχεῖα) is a mathematical treatise consisting of 13 books attributed to the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, written in Alexandria around 300 BC. It is a collection of definitions, postulates, propositions (theorems and constructions) and mathematical proofs of the propositions, covering plane and solid geometry, elementary number theory and incommensurable lengths.

The Elements is the most famous mathematical text of classical antiquity and was the standard textbook for teaching mathematics — especially geometry — from its publication until the late 19th or early 20th century. It has been called the most successful and influential textbook ever written, and was among the first works printed after the invention of the printing press.

Contents

Books I–IV treat plane geometry, including the Pythagorean theorem; Books V–X develop ratios, proportions and irrational magnitudes; Books VII–IX cover number theory, including Euclid's proof of the infinitude of primes; and Books XI–XIII treat solid geometry, culminating in the construction of the five regular Platonic solids.

Influence

The logical, axiomatic structure of the Elements became the model for mathematical rigor for over two thousand years. Its fifth postulate, the parallel postulate, eventually spurred the development of non-Euclidean geometry in the 19th century.
