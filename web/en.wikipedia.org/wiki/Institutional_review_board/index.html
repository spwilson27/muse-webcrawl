Institutional review board
Known in other countries as a research ethics committee (REC).

An institutional review board (IRB), also known as an independent ethics committee or ethical review board, is a committee established to review and monitor research involving human subjects. In the United States, IRBs are mandated by federal regulations — principally the Common Rule (45 CFR 46) administered by the Department of Health and Human Services, and FDA regulations for research on regulated products — and must approve, require modifications to, or disapprove covered research before it begins.

Composition and criteria

An IRB must have at least five members with varying backgrounds, including at least one scientist, one non-scientist, and one member unaffiliated with the institution. To approve research, an IRB must find, among other criteria, that risks to subjects are minimized and reasonable in relation to anticipated benefits, subject selection is equitable, informed consent will be obtained and documented, and privacy and confidentiality are protected. Some categories of research are exempt, and low-risk studies may receive expedited review. The system grew out of ethical scandals and reform documents — the Nuremberg Code, the Declaration of Helsinki, and, after revelations about the Tuskegee syphilis study, the U.S. National Research Act of 1974 and the Belmont Report (1979), which articulated the principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice.

Scope and criticism

Commercial (central) IRBs review multi-site studies for industry sponsors, while university IRBs oversee academic research, including much social-science and oral-history work whose inclusion under biomedical rules has been contested. Criticisms include inconsistent decisions between boards, delays, mission creep into low-risk research, and conflicts of interest for for-profit boards; defenders emphasize the protection of vulnerable participants. Comparable bodies outside the U.S. are generally called research ethics committees.
