Chlorofluorocarbon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A vintage aerosol spray can, once pressurised with chlorofluorocarbons]
A vintage aerosol spray can, once pressurised with chlorofluorocarbons

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) are synthetic organic compounds containing carbon, chlorine and fluorine, once widely used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and foam-blowing agents under trade names such as Freon. Chemically inert in the lower atmosphere — the very property that made them seem safe — they survive to reach the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light releases chlorine that catalytically destroys Ozone. Their phase-out under the Montreal Protocol (1987) is environmental policy's outstanding success story.

History and chemistry

CFCs were developed in 1928 by Thomas Midgley Jr. and colleagues at General Motors/Frigidaire as non-toxic, non-flammable refrigerants, replacing ammonia and sulphur dioxide, and were adopted globally after the Second World War. In 1974 Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland predicted their ozone-destroying potential (Nobel Prize 1995); the Antarctic 'Ozone hole' was reported by the British Antarctic Survey in 1985.

The Montreal Protocol

Signed in 1987 and progressively tightened, the Protocol phased out CFC production in developed countries by 1996 and developing countries by 2010 — the first treaty to achieve universal ratification. Substitutes (HCFCs, then HFCs) reduced ozone harm, but HFCs proved powerful greenhouse gases and were targeted in turn by the Kigali Amendment (2016). The ozone layer is projected to recover to 1980 levels around mid-century.

Legacy

CFCs are also potent greenhouse gases (thousands of times CO₂ per molecule), so the phase-out avoided substantial warming as well as ultraviolet damage. A 2018 detection of rogue CFC-11 emissions, traced largely to Chinese foam production, showed the regime's monitoring works — emissions fell again after enforcement.
