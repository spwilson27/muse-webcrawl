Infinity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hilbert's hotel illustration — a grand hotel with infinitely many rooms, all full]

Infinity is something without any bound or larger than any natural number. In mathematics, infinity is treated in several distinct ways: as a limit concept in calculus, as the size (cardinality) of infinite sets in set theory, and as points at infinity in geometry.

Georg Cantor's set theory (1870s) showed that infinities come in different sizes: the integers are countably infinite (cardinality ℵ₀, aleph-null), while the real numbers are uncountable — strictly larger — by Cantor's diagonal argument. The continuum hypothesis, asking whether any cardinality lies between them, was shown independent of ZFC by Gödel (1940) and Paul Cohen (1963).

Paradoxes and ideas

Zeno's paradoxes probed infinity in antiquity. Galileo noted that the squares are as numerous as the natural numbers though a proper subset; Hilbert's 'Grand Hotel' — a full hotel with infinitely many rooms that can still accommodate new guests — popularised the counter-intuitive arithmetic of the infinite.

In calculus, infinity appears in limits and improper integrals rather than as a number; the extended real line and the Riemann sphere adjoin points at infinity for convenience.
