Seed funding

Seed funding, or seed capital, is the earliest stage of venture capital financing, in which investors provide a startup company with capital in exchange for equity or convertible instruments, typically to support product development, market research, and initial operations until the company can generate its own cash flow or raise a larger institutional round.

Overview

Seed rounds historically ranged from tens of thousands to a few million dollars, though sizes have grown over time. Sources include the founders themselves ("bootstrapping"), friends and family, angel investors, seed-stage venture capital funds, crowdfunding, and startup accelerators such as Y Combinator, which provide standardised small investments and mentorship in cohorts.

Instruments

Modern seed financing commonly uses convertible notes or simple agreements for future equity (SAFEs), which postpone setting a valuation until a later priced round, when the instruments convert into shares at a discount or subject to a valuation cap. Traditional priced seed rounds, in which preferred stock is sold at an agreed valuation, remain common for larger financings.

Position in the funding sequence

Seed funding follows pre-seed financing and precedes the Series A round, in a sequence that traditionally progresses through Series B, C, and beyond as a company scales. Because seed-stage companies often have little revenue or track record, most fail to raise subsequent rounds, and seed investors expect high failure rates offset by occasional outsized returns. The stage is sometimes divided into "pre-seed" for prototype-stage funding and institutional seed for companies with early traction.
