Harmonograph

A harmonograph is a mechanical apparatus that employs pendulums to create a geometric image. The drawings created are usually Lissajous curves, or related drawings of greater complexity. The devices, which began to appear in the mid-19th century and peaked in popularity in the 1890s, cannot be conclusively attributed to a single person, although Hugh Blackburn, a professor of mathematics at the University of Glasgow, is commonly credited as the official inventor.

Types

A simple, or single-pendulum, harmonograph uses one pendulum to move a pen and another, gimballed, pendulum to move the drawing surface, producing elliptical Lissajous figures whose appearance depends on the ratio of the pendulum frequencies and their relative phase. A twin-elliptic harmonograph uses rotary motion of two pendulums to produce still more intricate patterns. The famous harmonographs built by the Newton & Co. workshops and exhibited in Victorian London drew large crowds, and the devices reflected the era's fascination with the visual representation of harmonic motion and musical intervals.

Modern examples

Large harmonographs survive in science museums, including the "Iron Genie" at the Museum of the History of Science in Oxford and installations at the Exploratorium in San Francisco. Artists and engineers continue to build harmonographs, and computer simulations of their damped, decaying curves are a popular graphics demonstration.

The patterns are governed by damped sinusoidal motion in two dimensions; as friction gradually reduces the amplitude, the curves spiral inward, giving harmonograph drawings their characteristic delicacy.
