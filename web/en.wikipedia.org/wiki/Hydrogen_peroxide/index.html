Hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide is a chemical compound with the formula H2O2. In its pure form it is a very pale blue liquid that is slightly more viscous than water. It is used as an oxidizer, bleaching agent, and antiseptic, usually as a dilute solution (3–6% by weight) in water for consumer use and in more concentrated forms for industrial applications. Hydrogen peroxide is a reactive oxygen species and the simplest peroxide, a compound having an oxygen–oxygen single bond.

Structure and properties

The molecule adopts a skewed shape with a dihedral angle of about 111.5° in the gas phase, owing to repulsive forces between the lone pairs of the oxygen atoms. Hydrogen peroxide is unstable and slowly decomposes in the presence of light into water and oxygen; the reaction is accelerated by catalysts including manganese dioxide, catalase, and transition-metal ions. It is a weak acid, with a pKa of about 11.6.

Production

Nearly all commercial hydrogen peroxide is manufactured by the anthraquinone process, in which an alkylanthraquinone is hydrogenated and then oxidized by air, releasing hydrogen peroxide. Global production is several million tonnes per year. It was first prepared in 1818 by Louis Jacques Thénard by reacting barium peroxide with nitric acid.

Uses

The largest use of hydrogen peroxide is in bleaching pulp and paper. It is also used in detergents, chemical synthesis (for example propylene oxide), wastewater treatment, and as a disinfectant and antiseptic. High-test peroxide (above 70%) has been used as a rocket propellant and oxidizer. Dilute solutions are sold in pharmacies for wound care and as a mouth rinse, although medical guidance has moved away from routine use on wounds because it can damage tissue.

Safety

Concentrated hydrogen peroxide is corrosive, can cause severe skin and eye burns, and forms explosive mixtures with organic materials. It is classified as a strong oxidizer, and its ingestion can cause gas embolism from released oxygen.
