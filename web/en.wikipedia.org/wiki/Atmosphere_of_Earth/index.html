Atmosphere of Earth

[Image: Earth seen from space — the thin blue line of the atmosphere glowing along the planet's limb above white clouds]

The atmosphere of Earth is the layer of gases, known collectively as air, retained by Earth's gravity that surrounds the planet. It is composed (by volume, dry air) of about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and trace gases including carbon dioxide (about 0.04%), water vapour in variable amounts, and it protects life by absorbing ultraviolet radiation, warming the surface through the greenhouse effect, and reducing temperature extremes.

The atmosphere has no abrupt top: it thins with altitude through the troposphere (where weather occurs), stratosphere (containing the ozone layer), mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere, fading into space over hundreds of kilometres.

Evolution and climate

Earth's early atmosphere, produced by volcanic outgassing, contained little free oxygen until photosynthetic cyanobacteria oxygenated it in the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago — a catastrophe for much existing life and the precondition for animal life. Human activity is now changing the atmosphere again, raising carbon dioxide from about 280 parts per million before industrialisation to over 420 ppm today, driving climate change.

Weather, climate and the water cycle are atmospheric phenomena; atmospheric science (meteorology and climatology) studies them with observations from surface stations, balloons, aircraft, radar and satellites, and with numerical models run on supercomputers.
