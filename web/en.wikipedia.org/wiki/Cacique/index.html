Cacique

A cacique (Spanish: [kaˈsike]; Portuguese: [kɐˈsikɨ]; feminine form: cacica), sometimes spelled as cazique, was a tribal chief of the Taíno people, the Indigenous inhabitants of the Bahamas, the Greater Antilles, and the northern Lesser Antilles at the time of European contact. The term is a Spanish transliteration of the Taíno word kasike.

History and usage

Spanish chroniclers applied the word to leaders throughout the Americas, well beyond its Taíno origin: caciques led chiefdoms (cacicazgos), inherited office (often through the maternal line), led warfare and ritual, and were accorded distinctive regalia such as the duho seat and guanín ornaments. Famous caciques encountered by the Spanish include Agüeybaná of Puerto Rico, Caonabo and Anacaona of Hispaniola, and Hatuey, who fled Hispaniola for Cuba and is honoured there as a national hero.

Caciquismo

In later usage, cacique came to mean a local political strongman or boss, and caciquismo the corrupt clientelist machine politics associated with such bosses — a central concept in the history of Restoration Spain and of much of Latin America into the 20th century.

Other uses

The word also names a genus of oropendola-like icterid birds (the caciques, genus Cacicus) and appears in numerous place names and brand names in the Spanish-speaking world.
