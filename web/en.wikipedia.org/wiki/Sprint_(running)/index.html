Sprint (running)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sprinting is running over a short distance at the top-most speed of the body in a limited period of time. It is the purest test of speed in athletics, used in many sports, and the centrepiece of the track programme at the Olympic Games. Standard outdoor sprint races are the 100 metres, 200 metres, and 400 metres; indoors, the 60 metres is contested. Sprint events also include the sprint hurdles and the 4 × 100 m and 4 × 400 m relays.

The races

The 100 metres, run on the home straight of a standard track, decides the unofficial title of "fastest man" or "fastest woman" in the world; the men's world record is 9.58 seconds, set by Usain Bolt in 2009, and the women's is 10.49 seconds, set by Florence Griffith Joyner in 1988. The 200 metres starts on the bend, and the 400 metres — one full lap — is a prolonged sprint demanding speed endurance. All sprint races are run in lanes from a crouched start in starting blocks, with a false-start rule that disqualifies an athlete after a single false start (reaction time below 0.1 seconds is deemed an anticipation).

Physiology and technique

Sprinting is powered almost entirely by the anaerobic energy systems, drawing on phosphocreatine and fast glycolysis. A race is conventionally divided into block clearance and acceleration (roughly the first 30–60 m), maximum-velocity running, and a speed-endurance phase in which all runners decelerate slightly. Elite sprinters combine long strides with very high stride frequency, brief ground-contact times, and large forces applied through the forefoot. Fast-twitch (type II) muscle fibres predominate in successful sprinters, and genetic factors such as the ACTN3 genotype have been studied in relation to sprint performance.

History

The sprint was part of the ancient Olympic Games as the stadion, a race of about 192 metres that was the sole event of the first recorded Games in 776 BC. Modern sprinting developed in nineteenth-century England and the United States and has been on the Olympic programme since 1896 for men and 1928 for women. Timing progressed from hand-held stopwatches to fully automatic electronic timing, mandatory for records from 1977.
