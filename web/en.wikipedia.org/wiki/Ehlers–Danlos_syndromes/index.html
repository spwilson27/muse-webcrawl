Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of genetic connective-tissue disorders caused by defects in the synthesis of collagen. Symptoms may include loose joints, joint pain, stretchy velvety skin, and abnormal scar formation. These can be noticed at birth or in early childhood. Complications may include aortic dissection, joint dislocations, scoliosis, chronic pain, or early-onset osteoarthritis.

EDS occurs due to mutations in one or more genes; the specific gene affected determines the type of EDS. Some cases result from a new mutation occurring during early development, while others are inherited in an autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive manner. Typically, these disorders arise from a defect in the structure of, or the processing of, collagen or proteins that interact with collagen.

Classification

In 2017, the International EDS Consortium published a revised classification recognizing 13 subtypes. The most common include:

  • Hypermobile EDS (hEDS) – the most frequent type; dominated by joint hypermobility, chronic pain, and soft skin. Its genetic basis remains incompletely understood.
  • Classical EDS (cEDS) – marked skin hyperextensibility and atrophic scarring, usually caused by variants in COL5A1 or COL5A2.
  • Vascular EDS (vEDS) – caused by COL3A1 variants; associated with arterial and organ rupture and a substantially reduced life expectancy.
  • Kyphoscoliotic, arthrochalasia, dermatosparaxis, cardiac-valvular and other rarer subtypes.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include generalized joint hypermobility (often assessed with the Beighton score), skin hyperextensibility, tissue fragility, easy bruising, and musculoskeletal pain. Gastrointestinal, autonomic, and cardiovascular manifestations occur in various subtypes. Vascular EDS may present with thin translucent skin, arterial dissection, intestinal perforation, and uterine rupture.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is based on clinical findings and confirmed, for most subtypes, by genetic testing. There is no cure; management is supportive and preventive, including physiotherapy, pain management, cardiovascular surveillance, and avoidance of contact sports and heavy lifting in fragile subtypes. People with vascular EDS require particular vascular monitoring and blood-pressure control.

History

The syndromes are named after the Danish dermatologist Edvard Ehlers and the French dermatologist Henri-Alexandre Danlos, who described the condition at the turn of the 20th century. Descriptions of hypermobile individuals date back to Hippocrates in 400 BC.
