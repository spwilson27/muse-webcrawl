Pigouvian tax

A Pigouvian tax (also spelled Pigovian tax) is a tax on a market activity that generates a negative externality — a cost imposed on third parties not reflected in the price. The tax is set equal to the marginal external damage, so that the private cost faced by the actor matches the social cost, restoring an efficient allocation. It is named after the British economist Arthur Cecil Pigou, who developed the concept in The Economics of Welfare (1920).

A Pigouvian tax internalises an external cost

Rationale

Where an activity such as pollution imposes costs on others, the unregulated market produces too much of it, because producers and consumers ignore the external damage. A per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity makes decision-makers internalise the harm, reducing the activity to the social optimum. Unlike direct regulation, the tax lets each polluter choose how to respond — abate, innovate, or pay — and raises revenue that can be recycled, an idea behind the "double dividend" hypothesis.

Examples

  • Carbon taxes on fossil fuels, pricing greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • Congestion charges, such as those in London, Stockholm and Singapore.
  • Tobacco and alcohol excise duties, partly justified by health costs borne by others.
  • Sugar taxes on soft drinks, adopted in the United Kingdom (the Soft Drinks Industry Levy) and elsewhere.
  • Plastic bag charges and landfill taxes.

Criticism

Critics note the difficulty of measuring marginal external damage, which the tax must equal to be efficient. Ronald Coase argued that externalities are reciprocal and that well-defined property rights with low transaction costs can reach efficient outcomes without taxes. Pigouvian taxes can also be regressive, falling hardest on low-income households, and are often politically unpopular; revenues may be used for compensation to mitigate these effects.
