Kurkh Monoliths

The Kurkh Monoliths are two Assyrian stelae that contain a description of the reigns of Ashurnasirpal II (r. 883–859 BC) and his son Shalmaneser III (r. 859–824 BC). They were discovered in 1861 by John George Taylor, the British Consul-General at Diyarbakır, at the Kurdish village of Kurkh near the Tigris in southeastern Turkey (the ancient city of Tushhan), and are now in the British Museum. The taller monolith is about 2.4 m high.

Significance

The monolith of Shalmaneser III records his campaigns in the west, culminating in the Battle of Qarqar (853 BC) against a coalition of Levantine kings — the earliest datable reference to such a coalition, and one of the most important extra-biblical sources for the history of ancient Israel. The inscription describes the forces of the coalition, including "Ahab the Israelite" (Ahabbu Sir'ilāia), king of Israel, credited with 2,000 chariots and 10,000 soldiers — the largest chariot contingent in the alliance — as well as Hadadezer of Damascus and Irhuleni of Hamath. This is the earliest known reference to Israel by that name in an Assyrian text and a fixed chronological anchor: Ahab's presence at Qarqar in 853 BC is a cornerstone of biblical chronology.

The stele shows Shalmaneser standing before divine symbols, and the text, written in the Akkadian language in cuneiform script, covers the king's first six regnal years. The second monolith, of Ashurnasirpal II, bears a banquet scene and an inscription summarising his conquests in the region.
