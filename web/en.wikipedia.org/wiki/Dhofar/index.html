Dhofar (Arabic: محافظة ظفار, Muḥāfaẓat Ẓuffār) is the largest of the eleven governorates of the Sultanate of Oman, occupying the country's southwest, bordering Yemen to the west and Saudi Arabia to the north. Its capital is Salalah. Dhofar is famous for two things unlike anywhere else in Arabia: the frankincense trade of antiquity, for which the region was the world's principal source, and the khareef, the summer monsoon that turns its coastal mountains green with mist and drizzle.

Geography and climate

Dhofar comprises a narrow coastal plain backed by the Dhofar Mountains (Jabal Dhofar, Jabal Qara, and Jabal Samhan) and, beyond them, gravel plains rising to the Rub' al Khali desert. From June to September the southwest monsoon drenches the seaward slopes in fog (the khareef), creating cloud forest and seasonal waterfalls; the interior remains arid. The region is a recognized center of plant and animal endemism, home to Arabian leopards and the frankincense tree (Boswellia sacra).

History

Frankincense from Dhofar was traded to Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome; the ports of Sumhuram (Khor Rori) and Al-Baleed served the incense route, and the Land of Frankincense sites are now a UNESCO World Heritage listing. Wabar/Iram traditions attach legendary lost cities to the region's sands. Dhofar was incorporated into Oman in the 19th century.

The Dhofar Rebellion

From the mid-1960s to 1976 the region was the theater of the Dhofar Rebellion, in which Marxist guerrillas of the PFLOAG, backed by South Yemen, fought the forces of Sultan Said bin Taimur and then of his son Qaboos. Qaboos's combination of military pressure — with British officers and Iranian troops — and a hearts-and-minds development campaign ended the war in 1976, and the rebellion proved the catalyst for Oman's modernization.

Culture and language

Dhofaris speak Arabic as well as Modern South Arabian languages such as Shehri (Jibbali) and Mehri, survivals of the ancient South Arabian tongues. Social and tribal structures, dress, and cuisine differ noticeably from northern Oman.

Economy and tourism

Salalah's port is among Oman's busiest; agriculture (coconuts, bananas, papaya) flourishes on the coastal plain. The khareef season draws hundreds of thousands of Gulf tourists seeking green landscapes and cool weather in midsummer.
