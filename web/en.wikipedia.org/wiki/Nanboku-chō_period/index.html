The Nanboku-chō period (Japanese: 南北朝時代, "period of the Northern and Southern Courts"), spanning 1336 to 1392, was a period in Japanese history in which two rival imperial courts existed simultaneously: the Southern Court of Emperor Go-Daigo and his successors, based at Yoshino in the mountains south of Nara, and the Northern Court of emperors installed and supported by the Ashikaga shogunate in Kyoto. The schism arose from the collapse of Go-Daigo's Kenmu Restoration and Ashikaga Takauji's rebellion, and ended in 1392 when the Southern emperor Go-Kameyama returned the imperial regalia to Kyoto under a reunification settlement brokered by the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu. It forms the early part of the Muromachi period.

Origins

Emperor Go-Daigo overthrew the Kamakura shogunate in 1333 and attempted direct imperial rule in the Kenmu Restoration, but his redistribution of rewards alienated the warrior class. Ashikaga Takauji, sent to suppress a Hōjō revival, turned against the court, seized Kyoto in 1336, and installed Emperor Kōmyō of the senior (Jimyōin) imperial line. Go-Daigo escaped to Yoshino with the imperial regalia and proclaimed his court the legitimate one, beginning nearly sixty years of rival sovereigns.

The two courts

The Southern Court (emperors Go-Daigo, Go-Murakami, Chōkei, and Go-Kameyama) survived at Yoshino through the loyalty of commanders such as Kusunoki Masashige — killed at the Battle of Minatogawa in 1336 — his son Masatsura, and Kitabatake Chikafusa, whose chronicle Jinnō Shōtōki argued the Southern line's legitimacy. The Northern Court (emperors Kōmyō, Sukō, Go-Kōgon, Go-En'yū, and Go-Komatsu) reigned in Kyoto under Ashikaga protection while the shogunate governed. Fighting between the courts and their warrior allies flared across Japan for decades, weakening imperial authority generally and strengthening regional warlords — a precondition for the later Sengoku age.

Reunification

By the 1380s the Southern Court was militarily exhausted. In 1392, under the Meitoku settlement, Emperor Go-Kameyama surrendered the regalia to the Northern emperor Go-Komatsu, on terms (later disputed) that the throne would alternate between the two imperial lines. The alternation was never honoured, but the schism formally ended, and the Ashikaga shogunate stood unchallenged as Japan's military government.

Historiographical controversy

Which court was legitimate was politically charged for centuries. Tokugawa-era scholarship and Kitabatake's argument favoured the South; in 1911 the Meiji government formally decreed the Southern Court the legitimate line for the era, and official emperor numbering follows that ruling, counting the Northern sovereigns as pretenders. The question still surfaces in debates over imperial historiography and the dating of reigns.
