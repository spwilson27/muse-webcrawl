Masking tape

Masking tape, also known as painter's tape, is a type of pressure-sensitive tape made of a thin and easy-to-tear paper, and an easily released pressure-sensitive adhesive. It is available in a variety of widths and is used mainly in painting, to mask off areas that should not be painted. The adhesive is designed to leave no residue or damage the surface it is applied to.

History

Masking tape was invented in 1925 by 3M employee Richard Drew. Drew observed auto-body painters struggling with strong adhesives that pulled paint off two-tone car finishes; he developed a gentler tape using a crêpe paper backing. The first tape was only adhesive at the edges, prompting a frugal painter's alleged remark about taking it back to the "Scotch bosses" — the origin of the Scotch tape brand name. Blue painter's tape was later formulated for cleaner removal after days of sun exposure.

Types and uses

Tapes are rated by adhesion and clean-removal period, from delicate-surface tapes for wallpaper and fresh paint to high-tack tapes for rough surfaces. Modern variants include green and blue painter's tapes, washi-style craft tapes, and frog tape with edge-sealing gel. Beyond painting, masking tape is used for labelling, bundling, bookbinding, and theatre floor marking (where gaffer tape is usually preferred for durability).

Composition

The backing is typically saturated crêpe paper; the adhesive is usually an acrylic or rubber-based pressure-sensitive formulation engineered for low peel strength and minimal residue.
