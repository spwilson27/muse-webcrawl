Partitioning of the Ottoman Empire

The partitioning of the Ottoman Empire (30 October 1918 – 1 November 1922) was the political and territorial division of the Ottoman Empire following the First World War. The empire, which had sided with the Central Powers, was defeated by the Allied Powers, and its territories were divided among Britain, France, Greece, Italy, and emerging national states, a process that reshaped the modern Middle East and the Balkans.

Wartime agreements

During the war the Allies concluded several secret agreements on the empire's future, including the Constantinople Agreement (1915), promising Constantinople and the Straits to Russia; the Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916), dividing the Arab provinces into British and French spheres; the Balfour Declaration (1917), supporting a Jewish national home in Palestine; and the Agreement of Saint-Jean-de-Maurienne (1917) concerning Italian claims in Anatolia.

Armistice and occupation

The Armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October 1918, ended hostilities. Allied forces occupied Constantinople and administered various regions. The sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922.

Treaties

The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) imposed a harsh settlement, partitioning Anatolia and confirming mandates over Arab territories, but it was never ratified and was resisted by the Turkish National Movement under Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk). The ensuing Turkish War of Independence led to the Treaty of Lausanne (1923), which recognised the Republic of Turkey and superseded Sèvres.

Mandates

Under the League of Nations mandate system, Britain administered Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq, while France administered Syria and Lebanon. Arab lands later became independent states, while the disposition of Palestine and the Armenian and Kurdish questions left enduring disputes.

Legacy

The partition ended over six centuries of Ottoman rule, created the modern state system of the Middle East, and remains central to debates about the region's borders and conflicts.
