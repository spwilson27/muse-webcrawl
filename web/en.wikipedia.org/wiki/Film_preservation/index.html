Film preservation, or film restoration, describes the practices by which archivists, studios, and institutions rescue decaying film stock and protect the content of motion pictures for future generations. Because early film bases are chemically unstable and all formats eventually degrade or become obsolete, preservation combines cold, dry storage with duplication onto new stock and, increasingly, digital scanning and restoration.

The nitrate problem

From the 1890s to the early 1950s, 35 mm film was made on a cellulose nitrate base — chemically similar to guncotton. Nitrate film is highly flammable, can ignite spontaneously when degraded, and decomposes into powder or a sticky mass. Catastrophic vault fires, such as the 1937 Fox vault fire in Little Ferry, New Jersey, destroyed most of some studios' early output. An often-cited estimate holds that around 75% of American silent feature films are lost.

Acetate and colour decay

"Safety film" on acetate base avoids fire risk but suffers from vinegar syndrome: deacetylation releases acetic acid, shrinking and embrittling the film in an autocatalytic process. Chromogenic colour stocks also fade as their dyes break down, shifting images toward magenta. Cold, dry storage dramatically slows all of these processes.

Preservation practice

Archivists inspect, repair, and duplicate endangered elements, ideally striking new preservation negatives and prints on polyester stock. The FIAF (International Federation of Film Archives), founded in 1938, coordinates archival practice worldwide. In the United States, the National Film Preservation Act of 1988 created the National Film Registry and the National Film Preservation Board.

Digital preservation

High-resolution scanning allows frame-by-frame digital restoration — removing scratches, tears, and dirt, stabilising images, and recovering faded colour — as in the restorations of classics such as Lawrence of Arabia and Metropolis (whose most complete cut resurfaced in Buenos Aires in 2008). Digital files, however, raise their own preservation problems: format obsolescence, data migration, and the cost of storing very large files indefinitely.

Lost films

Preservation efforts coexist with the sombre catalogue of lost films — works of major directors, including early Hitchcock and Lon Chaney's London After Midnight (1927), that survive in no known copy. Occasional rediscoveries in foreign archives, private collections, and unlikely places (such as the Dawson Film Find, unearthed from a Canadian swimming pool site in 1978) keep hopes alive.
