Imperial German Army

The Imperial German Army (German: Deutsches Heer) was the unified ground and air force of the German Empire from 1871 to 1919. It was formed after the unification of Germany under Prussian leadership in 1871 and was disbanded in 1919 following Germany's defeat in World War I and the imposition of the Treaty of Versailles.

Structure

The Imperial German Army was not a single unitary force but a federation of the armies of the empire's constituent states. The Prussian Army formed its core, alongside the separate armies of Bavaria, Saxony and Württemberg, which retained their own ministries of war and, in the Bavarian case, a distinct command in peacetime. In wartime, however, all contingents came under the supreme command of the Kaiser as Oberster Kriegsherr. The army was organised in peacetime into army corps districts across the empire, through which conscripts were raised and trained.

The General Staff

The Great General Staff in Berlin, led by the Chief of the General Staff, was responsible for war planning, training doctrine and the study of operations. Figures such as Helmuth von Moltke the Elder and Alfred von Schlieffen shaped its plans, most famously the Schlieffen Plan for a two-front war against France and Russia.

World War I

On mobilization in August 1914 the army expanded from a peacetime strength of roughly 800,000 to several million men, and eventually about 13 million served during the war. German forces fought on the Western Front, where the Schlieffen Plan's failure led to four years of trench warfare, and on the Eastern Front, where they won major victories such as Tannenberg and helped force Russia out of the war in 1918. The army also pioneered developments including stormtrooper tactics, and operated an air service, the Luftstreitkräfte. Defeat in 1918, the collapse of the home front and the German revolution led to demobilisation and the army's formal disbandment; the Treaty of Versailles restricted its successor, the Reichswehr, to 100,000 men.
