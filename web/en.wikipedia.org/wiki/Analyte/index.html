Analyte

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In analytical chemistry the analyte is the substance whose identity or amount is determined.

An analyte, in analytical chemistry, is the substance or chemical constituent of interest in a sample — the component that an analytical procedure seeks to identify, detect, or quantify. Examples include glucose in a blood sample, lead in drinking water, or caffeine in coffee.

Terminology

The analyte is distinguished from the matrix (everything else in the sample, which may interfere with the measurement) and from the measurand, the particular quantity intended to be measured. In titrations the analyte is the solution of unknown concentration against which a titrant of known concentration reacts. In chromatography the analyte is the separated component whose retention time and detector response reveal its identity and amount, usually calibrated against known standards and often measured against an internal standard.

Measurement considerations

Methods must separate or selectively detect the analyte in the presence of the matrix; performance is described by sensitivity, selectivity, detection limits, and accuracy. Sample preparation — extraction, digestion, dilution, derivatization — aims to present the analyte to the instrument in a measurable form without loss or contamination.
