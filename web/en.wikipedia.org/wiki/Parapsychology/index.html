Parapsychology is the study of alleged psychic phenomena — extrasensory perception (telepathy, clairvoyance and precognition) and psychokinesis — and other paranormal claims, such as near-death experiences and apparent reincarnation. Parapsychologists group these purported abilities under the term psi. Although parapsychology has produced published research for over a century, the mainstream scientific community considers it a pseudoscience because its phenomena have not been demonstrated under well-controlled, replicable conditions.

History

The field grew out of the Society for Psychical Research (founded 1882), which investigated mediums, hauntings and apparitions. In the 1930s J. B. Rhine at Duke University shifted the field toward laboratory experimentation, using Zener cards — packs of 25 cards bearing five symbols — to test guessing beyond chance, and coining the term "ESP". Rhine's results made headlines but his methods were soon criticised for sensory leakage and optional stopping. The Parapsychological Association was founded in 1957 and controversially admitted as an affiliate of the American Association for the Advancement of Science in 1969. Later flagship paradigms included the ganzfeld telepathy experiments of the 1970s–80s and remote-viewing programmes funded by US intelligence agencies (closed in 1995 after review found no operational value). In 2011 Daryl Bem's report of precognition effects in a mainstream psychology journal provoked a methodological storm and helped ignite psychology's replication crisis.

Criticism

Critics attribute positive findings to publication bias, selective reporting, flawed randomisation, multiple testing and, in a few historical cases, fraud. Meta-analyses continue to be disputed: proponents claim small but consistent above-chance effects, while sceptics argue the evidence diminishes as controls tighten — a pattern they take to show psi does not exist. Parapsychology persists at a handful of university units, such as the Koestler Parapsychology Unit at the University of Edinburgh, largely supported by private bequests.
