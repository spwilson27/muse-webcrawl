Tyrian purple

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tyrian purple (also called royal purple or imperial dye) is a reddish-purple dye produced by the Phoenicians from the mucus secretions of predatory sea snails of the family Muricidae, principally Bolinus brandaris and Hexaplex trunculus. It takes its name from the city of Tyre, a major centre of production.

Production

The dye's active compound, 6,6′-dibromoindigo, is released from a gland in the snail and develops its colour on exposure to sunlight and air. Enormous quantities of snails were needed — thousands for a single garment — making the dye more valuable than gold and restricting it to royalty and high priests. Huge heaps of discarded shells still mark ancient dye-works around the Mediterranean.

History

Purple-dyed cloth became the defining luxury of the Roman and Byzantine worlds; sumptuary laws reserved it for emperors, giving rise to the phrase "born in the purple". Production collapsed after the fall of Constantinople in 1453. In 1856 the young chemist William Henry Perkin, attempting to synthesise a substitute, accidentally created mauveine — the first synthetic dye.
