The Libor scandal was a series of investigations and enforcement actions that revealed, from 2012 onward, that traders and submitters at major banks had manipulated the London Interbank Offered Rate (Libor), the benchmark interest rate used to price hundreds of trillions of dollars of loans, mortgages and derivatives worldwide.

Background

Libor was calculated daily from estimates submitted by a panel of banks of the rate at which they could borrow from one another. Because submissions were judgements rather than actual transaction prices, the system depended on honesty. Investigations found two main forms of abuse: traders asking submitters to nudge rates up or down to benefit derivatives positions, and banks submitting artificially low rates during the 2008 financial crisis to appear financially stronger than they were.

Exposure and penalties

In June 2012 Barclays agreed to pay roughly £290 million (about US$450 million) to British and American regulators, and its chief executive Bob Diamond resigned. UBS paid about US$1.5 billion in December 2012; the Royal Bank of Scotland paid about US$612 million in 2013; Deutsche Bank paid a record US$2.5 billion in 2015. Several traders were prosecuted; the most prominent, former UBS and Citigroup trader Tom Hayes, was convicted in London in 2015 of conspiracy to defraud, though his conviction was overturned by the UK Supreme Court in 2025 on the basis that the jury had been misdirected.

Aftermath

Administration of Libor was transferred from the British Bankers' Association to ICE Benchmark Administration in 2014, and regulators ultimately decided the rate could not continue. Publication of most Libor settings ended on 31 December 2021, with the remaining US dollar settings ceasing in June 2023; markets moved to alternative rates such as SONIA, SOFR and €STR.
