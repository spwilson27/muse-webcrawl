Pointe-à-Pitre

Pointe-à-Pitre (Guadeloupean Creole: Pwentapit) is a commune in the French overseas department and region of Guadeloupe in the Caribbean. Although it is not the administrative capital — that is Basse-Terre — Pointe-à-Pitre is the archipelago's largest city and its economic hub, and its urban area, which includes the neighbouring communes of Les Abymes and Le Gosier, is home to roughly a third of Guadeloupe's population. The city stands on the western shore of Grande-Terre at the junction with Basse-Terre island, on a sheltered bay where the Rivière Salée salt-water channel divides the two islands.

History

The site, a point of pitch-producing land ("pointe à pitre", possibly from a fisherman named Pitre or from pitch trees), was long considered unhealthy marshland, but its deep, safe anchorage on the Petit Cul-de-Sac Marin led the French, under Governor Gabriel de Clieu and later during the British occupation of 1759–1763, to develop it as Guadeloupe's principal port. The town grew rapidly on a grid plan in the later eighteenth century as sugar and coffee exports boomed. It was badly damaged by the great earthquake of 1843, by a fire in 1871, and by the devastating hurricane of 1928, and much of its centre was rebuilt, including the celebrated ironwork market halls and the Saint-Pierre-et-Saint-Paul cathedral.

Economy and transport

Port Guadeloupe, centred on the modern Jarry terminal in Baie-Mahault, handles the great majority of the archipelago's freight, including bananas and sugar exports and petroleum imports. Pointe-à-Pitre International Airport at Le Raizet links Guadeloupe with Paris, the Caribbean, and North America. The city is also the traditional finish of the Route du Rhum, the single-handed transatlantic yacht race held every four years from Saint-Malo, France, since 1978 — a major festival in the city.

Culture

The Memorial ACTe, a major museum and memorial centre dedicated to the history of slavery, opened in 2015 on the site of the former Darboussier sugar factory on the city's waterfront. Markets such as the spice market by the harbour, Creole townhouses, and the carnival season — culminating in the Sunday afternoon déboulés — anchor local cultural life.
