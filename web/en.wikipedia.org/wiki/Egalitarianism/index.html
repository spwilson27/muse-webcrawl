Egalitarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Egalitarianism is a school of thought within political philosophy that builds on the concept of social equality, prioritising it for all people. Egalitarian doctrines generally hold that human beings are equal in fundamental worth or moral status, and that unjustified inequalities — of wealth, power, opportunity or recognition — should be removed or justified. As a doctrine it underlies much of socialist, anarchist and liberal thought, though the schools differ on what equality requires.

Key debates concern the “currency” of equality: resources (Dworkin), welfare, opportunity, capabilities (Sen, Nussbaum) or social relations themselves (relational egalitarianism, Elizabeth Anderson). “Luck egalitarianism” holds that inequalities arising from brute luck, as opposed to choice, are unjust. John Rawls's difference principle permits inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged. Critics from the right argue egalitarianism conflicts with liberty and desert; critics within the left argue that distributive equality without democratic equality in workplaces and politics is incomplete.

History

Egalitarian ideas appear in ancient philosophy (the Stoics' universal reason, early Christian community of goods) and radical Reformation movements (the Levellers' Agreement of the People, 1647), before becoming central to modern politics through the American and French revolutions' declarations of equal rights — whose exclusions (women, slaves, the propertyless) egalitarian movements have since contested from within the tradition.
