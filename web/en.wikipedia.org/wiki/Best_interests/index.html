Best interests

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Best interests
Type: Legal standard in family and children's law
Source: UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, Art. 3
Also called: "Welfare principle" (in some jurisdictions)
Applies in: Custody, adoption, care proceedings, immigration decisions concerning children
[Image: A family court setting: decisions on custody, adoption, and care are made by reference to the best interests of the child.]

Best interests is a legal standard according to which all decisions and actions concerning a child are to be evaluated by their effect on that child, with the child's best interests being a primary — and in some legal systems the paramount — consideration. Article 3(1) of the Convention on the Rights of the Child provides that "[i]n all actions concerning children, whether undertaken by public or private social welfare institutions, courts of law, administrative authorities or legislative bodies, the best interests of the child shall be a primary consideration."

Assessment

Because the standard is open-textured, statutes and guidance enumerate relevant factors. The welfare checklist in section 1(3) of England and Wales's Children Act 1989 is a widely studied example, directing courts to consider the child's ascertainable wishes and feelings (in light of age and understanding); physical, emotional and educational needs; the likely effect of any change in circumstances; age, sex, and background; any harm suffered or risk of harm; the capability of parents or others to meet the child's needs; and the range of powers available to the court. The Committee on the Rights of the Child's General Comment No. 14 (2013) treats best interests as a threefold concept: a substantive right, an interpretive principle, and a rule of procedure requiring that decision-making processes evaluate the impact on the child.

Applications

The standard governs custody and contact disputes, adoption, child protection and care proceedings, medical decisions for children unable to consent, and — increasingly — administrative decisions such as the deportation of a parent or the determination of a child's asylum claim, where courts including the UK Supreme Court in ZH (Tanzania) (2011) have held that children's interests must be identified and weighed first, though they may be outweighed by other considerations.

Criticism

Critics have long described the standard as indeterminate — open to judicial intuition and cultural assumptions — and it has been argued that it can marginalise children's own views and parents' rights. Modern practice responds by coupling best-interests assessment with the child's right to be heard (CRC Art. 12) and by requiring reasoned, evidence-based evaluations.
