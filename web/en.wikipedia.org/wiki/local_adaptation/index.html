Local adaptation

In evolutionary biology, local adaptation is a special case of divergent natural selection in which populations of a species evolve traits that confer higher fitness in their local environment than populations originating elsewhere. It is demonstrated when local genotypes outperform foreign genotypes in reciprocal transplant or common garden experiments.

Evidence and mechanisms

Local adaptation arises when selection differs among habitats and gene flow is limited enough for populations to respond. Classic evidence comes from studies of heavy-metal tolerance in grasses near mines, freshwater–marine divergence in sticklebacks, and serpentine-soil plants. Meta-analyses find local adaptation in roughly 70% of tested plant systems.

Detection

The standard criteria compare local versus foreign populations (locals should win at home) and home versus away performance of each population. Common garden experiments control for environmental differences by rearing all populations in one setting, while reciprocal transplants test performance in each native site.

Importance

Local adaptation matters for conservation (choosing seed sources for restoration), agriculture and forestry (provenance choice), and forecasting responses to climate change, where populations may be adapted to historical rather than future conditions.
