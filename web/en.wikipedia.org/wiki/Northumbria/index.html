Northumbria (Old English: Norþanhymbra Rīċe, "the kingdom of the Northumbrians") was an early medieval Anglo-Saxon kingdom in what is now northern England and south-east Scotland, stretching from the Humber estuary to the Firth of Forth. Counted among the kingdoms of the traditional Heptarchy, it was for a time the most powerful and culturally brilliant kingdom in Britain.

Formation

Northumbria formed from the union of two earlier kingdoms: Bernicia in the north (centred on Bamburgh) and Deira in the south (centred on York). Æthelfrith of Bernicia conquered Deira around 604; the union was made lasting under Edwin of Deira (r. 616–633), who converted to Christianity after marrying a Kentish princess and was baptised by Paulinus in 627.

Golden age

Under Oswald (r. 634–642) and Oswiu (r. 642–670), Northumbria dominated Britain. Oswald invited the Irish monk Aidan from Iona to found the monastery of Lindisfarne, seeding the Irish-influenced Christianity that produced the Lindisfarne Gospels. The Synod of Whitby (664) aligned the Northumbrian church with Roman practice. The 7th–8th centuries saw a Northumbrian golden age of learning: the Venerable Bede wrote his Ecclesiastical History of the English People at Wearmouth–Jarrow (completed 731), Alcuin of York became the leading scholar of Charlemagne's court, and Northumbrian sceatta coinage and stone crosses (Ruthwell, Bewcastle) reached exceptional quality.

Decline and Viking age

From the mid-8th century the kingdom suffered dynastic instability, and in 793 Vikings sacked Lindisfarne — the conventional start of the Viking Age in England. In 867 the Great Heathen Army captured York, and southern Northumbria became the Norse Kingdom of Jórvík. The northern rump around Bamburgh survived under English earls. After decades of struggle among Norse kings, Scots, and the rising Kingdom of Wessex/England, the last Scandinavian king of York, Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled and killed in 954, and Northumbria became an earldom of England. The name survives in the modern county of Northumberland and the region's identity.
