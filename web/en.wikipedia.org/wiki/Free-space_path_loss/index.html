Free-space path loss

In telecommunication, the free-space path loss (FSPL) is the attenuation of radio energy between two antennas that results from the spreading of the wavefront in free space, assuming the antennas are isotropic radiators and there are no obstacles or reflections. It is a standard component of the link budget used in designing radio and satellite communications systems.

Formula

The FSPL in decibels is given by:

FSPL(dB) = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) + 20 log10(4π/c)

where d is the distance between antennas, f is the signal frequency, and c is the speed of light. Equivalently, the loss grows with the square of distance and the square of frequency, so doubling either one adds about 6 dB of loss. A common engineering form, for distance in kilometres and frequency in megahertz, is FSPL(dB) = 32.45 + 20 log10(dkm) + 20 log10(fMHz).

Interpretation

The "loss" does not dissipate energy; it reflects that a receiving antenna of fixed effective aperture captures a shrinking fraction of the expanding spherical wavefront. For real antennas, antenna gain is applied separately, and the FSPL is combined with atmospheric, rain, and other losses in the full link budget, for example in the Friis transmission equation.
