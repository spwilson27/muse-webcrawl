Euroscepticism in the United Kingdom

Euroscepticism in the United Kingdom has been a significant element in British politics since the country's accession to the European Communities in 1973. Opposition to closer European integration, expressed in varying degrees by figures and factions in both the Conservative and Labour parties as well as by dedicated single-issue movements, ultimately culminated in the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union in 2020.

Early history

Britain joined the European Economic Community in 1973 under Edward Heath. In the 1975 referendum, two-thirds of voters chose to remain, but scepticism persisted: Labour's 1983 election manifesto proposed withdrawal, while Margaret Thatcher's 1988 Bruges speech set out a Conservative vision opposing a European superstate. The Maastricht Treaty rebellions of the early 1990s and the founding of the Referendum Party and UK Independence Party (UKIP) kept the issue prominent.

Road to the referendum

The eurozone crisis, concerns over immigration under free-movement rules, and UKIP's first-place finish in the 2014 European Parliament election increased pressure on David Cameron, who promised an in–out referendum. In the referendum of 23 June 2016, 51.9 per cent voted to leave.

Brexit and after

After years of parliamentary deadlock, the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 under Boris Johnson's revised withdrawal agreement. Eurosceptic campaigning thereafter largely shifted toward opposing any re-alignment with EU rules, while debate over the economic and constitutional consequences of Brexit continues.
