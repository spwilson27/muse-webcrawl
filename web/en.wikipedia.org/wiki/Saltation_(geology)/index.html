Saltation (geology)

Saltation
FieldSediment transport, geomorphology
AgentsWind (aeolian), water (fluvial)
Related processesCreep, suspension, traction

Saltation (from Latin saltatio, "dance") is a specific type of particle transport by fluids such as wind or water. It occurs when loose material is removed from a bed and carried by the fluid, before being transported back to the surface. Particles travel in ballistic trajectories: the fluid lifts them, they are carried a short distance, then gravity pulls them down to strike the bed again, often dislodging further particles in a self-sustaining cascade.

Mechanism

In saltation, initial particle movement is by rolling and sliding, but if the velocity of the fluid is strong enough to lift particles at their point of contact with the bed, then turbulence will keep them aloft briefly. As the particles descend at shallow angles they impact the bed at speed, ejecting new grains in a process called splash. Saltating grains typically travel distances of roughly ten to twenty times their hop height and follow asymmetric paths, rising slowly and falling steeply — the reverse of a purely ballistic arc because of the drag of the moving fluid.

The threshold at which saltation begins for a given grain size was studied classically by Ralph Bagnold, who showed that the fluid threshold (to initiate motion from rest) is higher than the impact threshold (needed to sustain motion once impacts are occurring), because splashing grains do much of the work of ejection.

In wind and water

Aeolian saltation of sand grains, usually 70–500 micrometres in diameter, builds and migrates dunes and causes abrasion of rock surfaces near the ground. Because air is much less dense than water, aeolian hops are far higher and longer than fluvial ones. In rivers, saltation is a principal mode of bed load transport for sand and fine gravel, contributing to the formation of ripples and the downstream movement of sediment. Very fine particles, by contrast, pass into suspension, while the largest move by rolling or sliding as traction (creep).

Applications

Understanding saltation matters for predicting dune encroachment on farmland and infrastructure, soil erosion and dust-storm generation, riverbed scour around bridge piers, and the interpretation of sedimentary deposits on Earth and Mars, where the thin atmosphere produces exceptionally long saltation trajectories.
