The middle ear is the air-filled cavity of the ear located between the tympanic membrane (eardrum) of the outer ear and the oval window of the inner ear. It contains the three smallest bones in the human body — the malleus, incus, and stapes, collectively called the ossicles — which transmit vibrations from the eardrum to the fluid-filled cochlea and amplify them in the process. The middle ear is connected to the nasopharynx by the Eustachian tube, which equalizes air pressure on both sides of the eardrum.

Structure

Tympanic cavity

The tympanic cavity is an irregular air space within the temporal bone, lying between the external acoustic meatus laterally and the inner ear medially. Its medial wall contains the oval window, into which the footplate of the stapes fits, and the round window, covered by a secondary tympanic membrane that allows fluid in the cochlea to move.

Ossicles

The malleus ("hammer") is attached to the inner surface of the eardrum. It articulates with the incus ("anvil"), which in turn articulates with the stapes ("stirrup"), whose footplate sits in the oval window. Vibration of the eardrum moves the chain in sequence. Two small muscles modulate the chain: the tensor tympani, attached to the malleus, and the stapedius, the smallest skeletal muscle in the body, attached to the stapes. Their contraction in the acoustic reflex stiffens the chain and dampens very loud sounds.

Eustachian tube

The Eustachian (auditory, or pharyngotympanic) tube connects the tympanic cavity to the nasopharynx. It is normally closed but opens during swallowing and yawning, equalizing pressure between the middle ear and the atmosphere — essential for normal vibration of the eardrum. It also provides a route by which infections can spread from the throat to the ear.

Function

Sound waves collected by the outer ear vibrate the eardrum. Air is much less dense than the fluid of the inner ear, so without a coupling mechanism most sound energy would be reflected at the oval window. The ossicles solve this impedance-matching problem in two ways: the eardrum has roughly 17–21 times the area of the stapes footplate, concentrating force onto the smaller area, and the ossicles act as a lever with a mechanical advantage of about 1.3. Together these raise the pressure at the oval window by roughly 22 times, allowing efficient transfer of sound into the cochlea.

Clinical significance

Otitis media, inflammation or infection of the middle ear, is one of the most common childhood illnesses; fluid accumulation behind the eardrum can cause pain and temporary hearing loss. Chronic cases may require tympanostomy tubes. Otosclerosis, abnormal bone growth around the stapes, progressively fixes it and causes conductive hearing loss, treatable by stapedectomy. A cholesteatoma, an abnormal skin growth in the middle ear, can erode the ossicles and surrounding bone if untreated.
