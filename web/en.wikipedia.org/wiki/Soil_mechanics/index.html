Soil mechanics

Soil mechanics is a branch of soil physics and applied mechanics that describes the behaviour of soils under stress and strain. It is a core discipline of geotechnical engineering and underlies the design of foundations, retaining structures, embankments, tunnels, and slopes. The field was founded as a modern science by Karl von Terzaghi in the early 20th century.

The soil as a three-phase material

Soil is treated as a three-phase system of solid particles, water, and air. Key state variables include porosity, void ratio, water content, saturation, and density. Particle-size distribution divides soil into gravel, sand, silt, and clay fractions, while the Atterberg limits (liquid limit and plastic limit) characterise the consistency of fine-grained soils.

Effective stress and strength

Terzaghi's principle of effective stress states that the stress carried by the soil skeleton equals the total stress minus the pore-water pressure (σ′ = σ − u). Shear strength is commonly described by the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion, τ = c′ + σ′ tan φ′, combining cohesion and internal friction. Water flow through soil follows Darcy's law, characterised by the coefficient of permeability.

Consolidation and settlement

Under sustained load, water is squeezed from a clay layer over time — consolidation — causing settlement that may continue for years. Terzaghi's one-dimensional consolidation theory predicts its rate and magnitude and is routinely used in foundation engineering.

Applications

Soil mechanics provides methods for calculating bearing capacity, lateral earth pressures on walls (Rankine and Coulomb theories), slope stability, and the response of soils to dynamic loads such as earthquakes, including liquefaction. Modern practice supplements classical theory with laboratory testing (triaxial, direct shear, oedometer tests) and numerical modelling.
