Roman Gaul refers to Gaul under the rule of the Roman Republic and Roman Empire, from Rome's first annexations in the 2nd century BC until the collapse of Roman authority in the 5th century AD. Rome first established the province of Gallia Narbonensis in the south (121 BC); Julius Caesar conquered the remainder during the Gallic Wars (58–50 BC), defeating the Arvernian leader Vercingetorix at Alesia in 52 BC.

Administration

Augustus reorganized Gaul into provinces: Gallia Narbonensis in the Mediterranean south, and the "Three Gauls" — Aquitania, Lugdunensis and Belgica — governed from Lugdunum (Lyon), seat of the imperial cult of Rome and Augustus. A network of roads, cities and aqueducts Romanized the region, and Gaul became one of the empire's richest areas, exporting wine, grain, pottery and glass.

Late antiquity

Gaul suffered in the 3rd-century crisis; a breakaway Gallic Empire (260–274) briefly ruled it. Christianity spread through its cities, and from the 5th century Germanic peoples — Visigoths, Burgundians and Franks — carved kingdoms from the Roman provinces. Clovis's Frankish conquests after 486 ended the last Roman rump state, that of Syagrius at Soissons.
