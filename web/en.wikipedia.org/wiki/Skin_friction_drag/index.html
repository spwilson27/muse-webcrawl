Skin friction drag

Skin friction drag is a component of parasitic drag that arises from the friction of a fluid against the surface — the "skin" — of a solid body moving through it. It results from the shear stresses at the wall within the boundary layer and is distinct from pressure (form) drag, which arises from the pressure distribution around the body. For streamlined bodies, such as aircraft wings and ship hulls, skin friction is a major fraction of total drag.

Definition

The local skin friction coefficient cf is defined as the local wall shear stress τw divided by the dynamic pressure of the free stream:

cf = τw / (½ ρ U²)

Integrated over the wetted area of the body, skin friction drag is the streamwise component of all these local shear stresses. The total skin friction coefficient Cf depends primarily on the Reynolds number, on whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent, and on the roughness of the surface.

Flat plate behaviour

For a smooth flat plate, classical results include the Blasius solution for a laminar boundary layer, giving an average coefficient of about 1.328 / √ReL, and empirical power-law relations for turbulent flow such as 0.074 / ReL1/5. Because turbulent skin friction is much higher than laminar, techniques that maintain laminar flow — careful shaping of aerofoils and smooth surfaces — are important in aircraft design, while ships employ antifouling coatings and, in some cases, air lubrication or riblets to reduce frictional resistance.

In practice

For a cruising airliner, skin friction can account for roughly half of the total drag, which is why surface condition, paint and even insect contamination on wing leading edges affect fuel burn. For ships, frictional resistance can dominate the total at low speeds, and hull roughness growth through fouling has a significant economic and environmental cost.
