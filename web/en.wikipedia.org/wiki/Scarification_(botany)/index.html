Scarification (botany)

Scarification in botany involves weakening, opening, or otherwise altering the coat of a seed to encourage germination. Scarification is often done mechanically, thermally, or chemically on seeds that have a hard seed coat. Because many plant species have seeds that are impermeable to water and gases, germination cannot occur until the seed coat is altered; in nature this may happen through freezing and thawing, passage through an animal's digestive tract, fire, or microbial activity.

Methods

Mechanical scarification involves nicking the seed coat with a file or knife, or abrading seeds with sandpaper. Thermal scarification uses brief exposure to hot water or heat. Chemical scarification soaks seeds in concentrated sulfuric acid or other agents for a controlled time to erode the coat. Care must be taken not to damage the embryo inside.

Use

Scarification is commonly applied to hard-coated seeds such as those of legumes (including sweet pea, lupin, and honey locust) and other species with physical dormancy. It is distinguished from stratification, which simulates cold, moist winter conditions to break physiological dormancy. Some seeds require both treatments.
