Nan Madol is a ruined archaeological site and former capital city adjacent to the eastern shore of the island of Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia. It consists of nearly a hundred small artificial islets built of stacked basalt and coral stone in a shallow lagoon, linked by a network of canals — for which it is sometimes called the "Venice of the Pacific".

History

Nan Madol was the ceremonial and political seat of the Saudeleur Dynasty, which unified Pohnpei's population under a single ruler. Construction is believed to have begun around 1180–1200 AD, with the great stone walls and platforms built over the following centuries. The Saudeleur capital housed the ruling priest-kings, nobles and priests, while commoners lived on the main island.

According to Pohnpeian legend and oral history, the Saudeleur rulers became increasingly oppressive, and the dynasty was overthrown by the semi-mythical hero Isokelekel, traditionally dated to the early 17th century. The site was gradually abandoned, though it retained ritual significance.

Construction

The islets were constructed by stacking naturally formed prismatic basalt columns — quarried from distant parts of Pohnpei and transported by raft — in log-cabin fashion, filled with coral rubble. Some walls rise 7.6 metres (25 ft) above the water. The largest structure, the royal mortuary islet of Nandauwas, features massive enclosing walls. The total weight of stone used has been estimated in the hundreds of thousands of tons. How the massive basalt logs were transported and raised remains a subject of local legend, which attributes the work to magic.

Archaeology and heritage

The site was documented by European visitors in the 19th century and investigated by colonial administrators and archaeologists in the 20th. In 2016 Nan Madol was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, and simultaneously placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger due to threats from siltation and vegetation overgrowth of the canals.
