Casamance (French: Casamance; Portuguese: Casamança) is a region of Senegal in West Africa, lying south of The Gambia and north of Guinea-Bissau. It takes its name from the Casamance River, which flows westward through the region to the Atlantic Ocean. Cut off from northern Senegal by Gambian territory, Casamance has a distinct climate, culture and history.

Geography

Unlike the arid Sahel of northern Senegal, Casamance is lush and green, receiving considerably more rainfall. The landscape includes mangrove swamps along the coast and lower river, rice paddies, palm groves and dense forest in the south. The region is conventionally divided into Lower Casamance (around Ziguinchor), Middle Casamance (Sédhiou) and Upper Casamance (Kolda).

History

The Portuguese established trading posts in the area from the 16th century, and Ziguinchor was founded by them in 1645. The territory was ceded to France in 1888. After Senegalese independence in 1960, Casamance remained administratively part of Senegal, but grievances among the predominantly Diola population — culturally and religiously distinct from the Wolof-dominated north — fed a separatist movement, the Movement of Democratic Forces of Casamance (MFDC). The resulting Casamance conflict, a low-level insurgency beginning in 1982, has continued intermittently despite peace agreements.

Economy

Agriculture dominates: rice is the staple crop, alongside groundnuts, cotton, mangoes and cashews. Fishing and tourism, particularly around Cap Skirring, are also significant.
