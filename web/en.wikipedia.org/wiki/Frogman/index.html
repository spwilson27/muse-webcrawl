Frogman

A military frogman equipped with a rebreather to avoid bubbles.

A frogman is someone trained in scuba diving or swimming underwater for tactical, military or police operations, for underwater demolition, reconnaissance, mine clearance and ship inspection. The word originated in the 1940s from the frog-like appearance of a swimmer wearing fins and a tight diving suit; it remains in use for military and police divers of many nations, including British Royal Navy divers and the tradition from which the US Navy SEALs descend.

History

Although prototype military divers existed in the First World War — notably Italy's Gamma group — frogmen came into prominence in the Second World War. Italian Decima MAS frogmen, using human torpedoes (maiale), sank British warships at Alexandria in 1941. Britain's Royal Navy responded with the COPP beach-reconnaissance parties, chariot torpedo riders and clearance divers, while the United States formed Underwater Demolition Teams after casualties from uncharted obstacles at Tarawa in 1943; these UDTs cleared beaches at Normandy and in the Pacific and are the forerunners of today's combat diver units.

Equipment and roles

Frogmen typically use closed-circuit rebreathers that recycle exhaled gas, producing no tell-tale bubbles, along with fins, masks, compasses and underwater weapons and tools. Their tasks include sabotage of enemy vessels with limpet mines, reconnaissance of landing beaches, demolition of obstacles, and recovery or inspection work. The term is distinguished from the broader "combat diver" and from recreational scuba divers.
