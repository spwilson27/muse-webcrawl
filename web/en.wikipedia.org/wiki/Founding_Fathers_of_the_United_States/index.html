The Founding Fathers of the United States were the political leaders and statesmen who participated in the American Revolution by signing the Declaration of Independence, taking part in the Revolutionary War, or assisting in framing the Constitution of the United States in 1787. The term, popularized by Warren G. Harding in 1916, embraces a broad group: the 56 signers of the Declaration, the 55 Framers of the Constitution, and other leaders of the founding era.

The core figures

Historians commonly single out seven central figures: George Washington, commander of the Continental Army and first president; John Adams, advocate of independence and second president; Thomas Jefferson, principal author of the Declaration; James Madison, chief architect of the Constitution and the Bill of Rights (including the Tenth Amendment); Alexander Hamilton, first secretary of the treasury; Benjamin Franklin, diplomat and polymath; and John Jay, co-author of The Federalist and first chief justice.

The founding

Between 1776 and 1791 the founders declared independence, fought an eight-year war, governed under the Articles of Confederation, and then—concluding that the Articles were unworkable—drafted a federal constitution at the Philadelphia Convention of 1787, secured its ratification in a hard-fought contest that produced The Federalist Papers, and added a Bill of Rights to answer Anti-Federalist objections.

Ideas and legacy

The founders drew on Enlightenment thought—Locke, Montesquieu, and the English republican tradition—tempered by practical experience of colonial self-government. Their settlement combined popular sovereignty, federalism, separation of powers, and enumerated rights, but also entrenched compromises over slavery that the nation later resolved only by civil war. The founders' intentions remain central to American constitutional interpretation, notably in the jurisprudence of originalism, even as historians debate whose interests the founding served.
