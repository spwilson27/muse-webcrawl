Fuke-shū

Fuke-shū (Japanese: 普化宗, "Fuke school") was a Japanese sect of Zen Buddhism that emerged during the Edo period. Its adherents, known as komusō ("priests of nothingness"), were famous for wearing large woven basket hats (tengai) that covered their heads and for playing the shakuhachi bamboo flute as a form of meditation and alms-seeking.

History

The sect traced its lineage to the Chinese Chan master Puhua (Japanese: Fuke), a contemporary and friend of Linji (Rinzai). In Edo-period Japan the komusō were granted special privileges by the Tokugawa shogunate, including permission to travel freely throughout the country — privileges that were sometimes abused, as some komusō were in fact shogunal spies or rōnin concealing their identities under the baskets. Honkyoku, the solo shakuhachi pieces played by the monks, became an important repertoire of Japanese traditional music.

Suppression and legacy

The Meiji government abolished the Fuke sect in 1871, partly because of its entanglement with the fallen shogunate, though shakuhachi playing survived as a secular art and the komusō image remains iconic.
