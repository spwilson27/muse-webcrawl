Rib

In vertebrate anatomy, ribs are the long, curved bones that form the rib cage. In humans and most mammals they surround the chest (thorax), protecting vital organs such as the heart and lungs and assisting in breathing by expanding and contracting the chest cavity.

Human anatomy

Humans normally have 24 ribs, in 12 pairs. The first seven pairs are "true ribs", connected directly to the sternum by their own costal cartilages. Pairs 8–10 are "false ribs", whose cartilages join the cartilage above them, and pairs 11 and 12 are "floating ribs", with no anterior attachment. Each typical rib consists of a head that articulates with the thoracic vertebrae, a neck, a tubercle, and a curved shaft (body) ending in costal cartilage. Between adjacent ribs run the intercostal muscles, nerves and blood vessels.

Function

Beyond protection, the rib cage's elasticity lets the intercostal muscles and diaphragm change thoracic volume during respiration. The ribs also serve as muscle attachments and, via the red marrow within them, contribute to blood cell production.

Clinical significance

Rib fractures are common injuries, usually from trauma, and are painful but typically heal without surgery; multiple fractures can produce a flail chest. Extra cervical ribs, present in a small percentage of people, can compress nerves and vessels at the neck. Rib anatomy is also the source of the biblical account of Eve's creation and of the persistent myth that men have one fewer rib than women — in fact the counts are the same.

Other animals

Most vertebrates have ribs, though their number varies greatly: snakes may have hundreds of pairs. In fish, two sets of ribs can occur. Rib bones of livestock, such as spare ribs and short ribs, are widely eaten.
