Quartodecimanism

Quartodecimanism (from the Latin quarta decima, "fourteenth") was the practice, in early Christianity, of celebrating the Paschal observance on the 14th day of Nisan — the date of the Jewish Passover — whatever day of the week it fell on. The practice was characteristic of the churches of Roman Asia and gave rise to one of the earliest major controversies over the date of Easter.

The Quartodeciman practice was centred in the province of Asia.

The controversy

Quartodecimans ended their Lenten fast on 14 Nisan, commemorating the crucifixion, and celebrated the Pascha that evening, following the chronology of the Gospel of John. Most other churches, including Rome, fasted until the following Sunday, the day of the resurrection. Around 155 CE, Polycarp of Smyrna visited Anicetus of Rome; they disagreed but parted in peace. In the 190s, however, Pope Victor I attempted to excommunicate the Asian churches, drawing a sharp rebuke from Irenaeus of Lyons, who urged tolerance of differing customs.

Resolution

The First Council of Nicaea (325) decreed that Easter be celebrated uniformly on a Sunday, independent of the Jewish calendar. Quartodeciman practice gradually disappeared from mainstream Christianity, though it survived for centuries in some regions and communities. The dispute is also connected with the Quartodecimans' veneration of the apostle John and Philip as authorities for their custom.

Legacy

The controversy is an early example of tension between local tradition and Roman claims to authority, and it shaped the later computus — the system for calculating the date of Easter. Small numbers of modern groups, including some Sabbatarian and Messianic congregations, observe a 14 Nisan Passover commemoration.
