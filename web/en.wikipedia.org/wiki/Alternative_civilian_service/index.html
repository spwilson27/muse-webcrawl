Alternative civilian service

Alternative civilian service, sometimes called alternative service, is a form of national service performed for reasons of conscience, in place of compulsory military service. It usually involves service in hospitals, care homes, schools, social services, environmental projects or other work of public benefit, and is performed under civilian rather than military administration.

Alternative service is offered in a number of countries that maintain conscription, as the mechanism by which recognized conscientious objectors — people whose moral, ethical or religious beliefs forbid them from bearing arms or serving in the armed forces — can fulfil their legal obligation to the state without violating their conscience. In many systems the duration of alternative service is set equal to, or somewhat longer than, the military service it replaces, a provision sometimes criticized as punitive.

Rationale and legal basis

The right to conscientious objection is derived from freedom of thought, conscience and religion, recognized in Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. Bodies such as the United Nations Human Rights Committee have held that states should provide alternative service of a genuinely civilian character, outside military command, and not of a punitive duration.

By country

In Germany, the Zivildienst (civilian service) operated from 1961 until conscription was suspended in 2011; at its height it supplied large numbers of workers to hospitals and care institutions. Finland maintains a civilian service (siviilipalvelus) of 347 days, longer than the shortest military service. Austria, Switzerland, Greece, Russia and South Korea (since 2020) also provide statutory alternative service, with conditions varying widely.

Criticism

Critics argue that longer alternative service penalizes conscience, that application procedures sometimes act as tribunals of belief, and that in some countries the available placements are effectively part of the military system. Supporters respond that alternative service provides valuable labour to the social sector and a fair way to share the burdens of national obligation.
