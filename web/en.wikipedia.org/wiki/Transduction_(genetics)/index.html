Transduction (genetics)

Transduction
Discovered1952, by Norton Zinder and Joshua Lederberg
VectorBacteriophage
TypesGeneralised, specialised (restricted)
Related processesConjugation, transformation

Transduction is the process by which foreign DNA is introduced into a cell by a virus or viral vector. It is a horizontal gene transfer mechanism in bacteria in which genes are moved from one bacterium to another by a bacteriophage. An example is the viral transfer of DNA from one bacterium to another and hence an example of horizontal gene transfer. Transduction does not require physical contact between the cell donating the DNA and the cell receiving it (which occurs in conjugation), and it is DNase resistant (transformation is susceptible to DNase).

Discovery

Transduction was discovered in Salmonella typhimurium in 1952 by Norton Zinder and Joshua Lederberg at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, during experiments designed to test whether gene exchange in Salmonella required cell contact like conjugation in E. coli. A filter that blocked cell contact did not block transfer, and the agent responsible proved to be the phage P22.

Generalised and specialised transduction

In generalised transduction, which occurs during the lytic cycle, random fragments of host DNA are mistakenly packaged into phage heads; the resulting particles can transfer any bacterial gene, at low frequency, to a recipient cell. In specialised transduction, a lysogenic phage excises imprecisely from the chromosome and carries only bacterial genes adjacent to its integration site — the classic example is phage lambda transferring the gal genes of E. coli.

Applications

Phage transduction is a workhorse of bacterial genetics for strain construction and fine-structure mapping, and in modern usage "transduction" also denotes the delivery of genes into eukaryotic cells by viral vectors such as lentiviruses and AAV in research and gene therapy.
