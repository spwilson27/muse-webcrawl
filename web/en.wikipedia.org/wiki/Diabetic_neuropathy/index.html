Diabetic neuropathy

Diabetic neuropathy
SpecialtyEndocrinology, neurology
SymptomsNumbness, tingling, pain, weakness
CausePoorly controlled blood sugar in diabetes
Risk factorsLong duration of diabetes, hyperglycemia, hypertension

Peripheral neuropathy most often affects the feet and legs first.

Diabetic neuropathy is nerve damage that occurs as a complication of diabetes mellitus, resulting from chronically high blood sugar injuring nerves throughout the body. It is one of the most common complications of diabetes, affecting up to half of people with the condition over their lifetime.

Classification and symptoms

The most common form is distal symmetric polyneuropathy, which typically begins in the feet with numbness, tingling, burning pain, and loss of protective sensation, progressing in a "stocking-glove" distribution; loss of sensation contributes to foot ulcers and amputations. Other forms include autonomic neuropathy (affecting digestion, bladder, sexual function, and blood pressure regulation), proximal neuropathy (pain and weakness in hips and thighs), and focal neuropathies such as carpal tunnel syndrome and cranial nerve palsies.

Causes and mechanism

Chronic hyperglycemia damages nerves through metabolic pathways — including the polyol pathway, advanced glycation end-products, and oxidative stress — and through injury to the small blood vessels (vasa nervorum) supplying nerves. Duration of diabetes and poor glycemic control are the strongest risk factors.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by tests of sensation (such as the 10-gram monofilament), nerve conduction studies, and exclusion of other causes. There is no cure; tight blood glucose control can slow progression, particularly in type 1 diabetes. Neuropathic pain is treated with agents such as duloxetine, pregabalin, gabapentin, and tricyclic antidepressants. Foot care and screening are essential to prevent ulcers.
