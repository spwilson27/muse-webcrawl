Optical isomerism

Optical isomerism is a form of stereoisomerism in which isomers have the same molecular formula and connectivity but differ in the spatial arrangement of their atoms, making them non-superimposable mirror images of each other. Such isomers are called enantiomers, and they rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions — hence the name "optical" isomers.

Chirality

Optical isomerism arises from chirality. In organic chemistry the most common source is a tetrahedral carbon atom bonded to four different groups, called a stereocenter or chiral center. A molecule with such a center generally exists as a pair of enantiomers.

Properties

Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, solubility in achiral solvents) except for the direction in which they rotate polarized light and their interactions with other chiral substances. This difference matters greatly in biology: enzymes and receptors are themselves chiral, so the two enantiomers of a drug may have very different effects. The differing smells of the enantiomers of carvone — spearmint and caraway — are a classic illustration.

Racemic mixtures

An equal mixture of two enantiomers is a racemic mixture (or racemate), which is optically inactive because the rotations cancel. Separating a racemate into its enantiomers is called resolution and is a key step in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

Nomenclature

Enantiomers are commonly distinguished by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog R/S system, which assigns priorities to the groups around a stereocenter, or by the direction of rotation: (+) dextrorotatory or (−) levorotatory.
