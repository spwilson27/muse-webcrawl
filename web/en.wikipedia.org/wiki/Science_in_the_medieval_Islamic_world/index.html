Science in the medieval Islamic world

Science in the medieval Islamic world was the science developed and practised during the Islamic Golden Age under the Abbasid Caliphate and subsequent Muslim polities, roughly from the 8th to the 15th century. Scholars working mainly in Arabic (and later Persian) made major advances in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, optics, chemistry, geography and engineering, while preserving and transmitting the learning of ancient Greece, Persia and India to later Europe.

The translation movement

From the late 8th century, caliphs and patrons in Baghdad sponsored the systematic translation of Greek, Syriac, Persian and Sanskrit works into Arabic, centred on institutions such as the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Ḥikma). Translators like Hunayn ibn Ishaq rendered Galen, Hippocrates, Euclid, Ptolemy and Aristotle, laying the foundation for original research.

Mathematics

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi's treatise on al-jabr gave algebra its name, and his name (as "algorithm") the term for systematic procedures. Islamic mathematicians developed trigonometry as a discipline in its own right, refined the decimal positional system and Hindu–Arabic numerals, and advanced geometry, spherical astronomy and the theory of equations; Omar Khayyam classified cubic equations, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi separated trigonometry from astronomy and stated the law of sines.

Astronomy and optics

Astronomers built observatories at Baghdad, Damascus, Maragheh and Samarkand, improved the astrolabe, and developed mathematical models — such as the Tusi couple and Ibn al-Shatir's models — that later appear in Copernicus's work. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), in his Book of Optics, founded experimental optics, overturning the emission theory of vision and pioneering an empirical, experimental method.

Medicine and other sciences

Physicians such as al-Razi (Rhazes) and Ibn Sina (Avicenna), whose Canon of Medicine remained a European textbook into the 17th century, systematised clinical medicine; Ibn al-Nafis described the pulmonary circulation of the blood. Al-Biruni made contributions to geodesy, geography and comparative religion; Jabir ibn Hayyan is associated with early chemistry and laboratory techniques; and engineers such as the Banu Musa brothers and al-Jazari designed automata and water-raising machines.

Transmission and legacy

From the 12th century, Latin translations made in Spain and Sicily carried Arabic learning — and with it the recovered Greek tradition — into medieval European universities, profoundly shaping the later development of science. Historians debate the causes of the tradition's relative decline in later centuries, pointing to institutional, economic and political factors rather than a single cause.
