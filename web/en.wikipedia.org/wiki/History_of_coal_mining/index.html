History of coal mining

The history of coal mining goes back thousands of years, with early mines documented in ancient China and the Roman Empire. Coal became a dominant energy source during the Industrial Revolution of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, powering steam engines, iron smelting, railways and steamships. The twentieth century saw peak employment in deep mines across Britain, Germany, the United States and elsewhere, followed by mechanisation, competition from oil and gas, and — in many countries — steep decline. Coal remains a major global fuel, with the largest production today in China, India, Indonesia and Australia.

Early history

Coal outcrops were gathered and shallow bell pits dug from Roman times in Britain; the Romans used coal at forts along Hadrian's Wall. In China, coal was mined and used for smelting well before the first millennium BC, and Marco Polo reported its use as fuel. Medieval "sea coal" shipped from Newcastle to London gave the trade its early centre. Monastic houses and later lay landlords developed mines in Durham, Northumberland and South Wales.

Industrial Revolution

Abraham Darby's use of coke for iron smelting (1709) and the spread of Watt steam engines created vast new demand. Canals and then railways carried coal cheaply. Deep shaft mining spread; children and women worked underground until reforms such as the British Mines Act of 1842 banned their employment below ground. Mining disasters from firedamp explosions and flooding spurred safety legislation and the invention of the Davy and Geordie safety lamps (1815).

Twentieth century

British coal employment peaked in 1913 at over one million miners producing 287 million tonnes. Nationalisation in 1947 created the National Coal Board. Longwall mechanisation raised productivity while shrinking the workforce. In the United States, the United Mine Workers under John L. Lewis won major gains, while mountaintop removal and large opencast mines transformed Appalachian mining. The 1984–85 British miners' strike ended in defeat for the NUM and accelerated pit closures; the last British deep mine, Kellingley, closed in 2015.

Modern era

Global production exceeds 8 billion tonnes annually, dominated by surface mines. Concerns over carbon emissions, air pollution and climate change have driven coal phase-outs in many OECD countries, while consumption in Asia continues to grow.
