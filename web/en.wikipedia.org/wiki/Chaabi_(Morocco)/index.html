Chaabi (Morocco)

A chaabi orchestra performing at a Moroccan wedding.

Moroccan chaabi (Arabic: الشعبي, "of the people" or "popular") is a genre of popular music in Morocco, descended from Moroccan folk music. It is the country's most widely enjoyed popular style, heard at weddings, festivals and celebrations throughout the kingdom. It should not be confused with Algerian chaabi, a distinct tradition centred on Algiers.

Characteristics

Moroccan chaabi draws on rural and urban folk idioms, including the aita of the Atlantic plains, Gnawa rhythms, Melhoun poetry and Andalusi influences. Songs are typically sung in Moroccan Darija (with some in Middle Atlas Tamazight) and deal with love, celebration, social commentary and everyday life. The sound is driven by percussion — bendir, taarija, darbuka and krakeb — together with violin, oud, banjo, and in modern recordings keyboards and electric instruments. Performances build gradually toward fast, trance-like rhythmic climaxes intended to get audiences dancing.

Artists

Among the best-known performers are the all-female group-style orchestras and singers such as Najat Aatabou, famous for songs of social realism sung from a woman's perspective; Daoudi; Stati; Senhaji; and the pioneering violinist-singer Hajja El Hamdaouia, one of the founders of the modern chaabi sound in Casablanca. Groups such as Nass El Ghiwane, though usually classified separately, emerged from the same popular milieu and influenced the genre.

Cultural role

Chaabi is the soundtrack of Moroccan festivities: no wedding is complete without a chaabi orchestra, and the genre dominates cassettes, radio and streaming in popular quarters of Moroccan cities. Because its lyrics use everyday speech and sometimes broach social taboos, chaabi has occasionally drawn criticism from religious conservatives while remaining firmly mainstream.
