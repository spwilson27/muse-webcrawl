American avocet

The American avocet (Recurvirostra americana) is a large wader in the avocet and stilt family, Recurvirostridae, found in western and central North America. It is readily recognised by its long, thin, upcurved bill, bold black-and-white patterning on the back and wings, long bluish-grey legs, and — in breeding plumage — a cinnamon-orange head and neck that fade to white in winter.

Behaviour and feeding

American avocets forage in shallow wetlands, mudflats and brackish ponds, sweeping their partly open bills from side to side through the water in a scything motion to stir up and seize aquatic invertebrates, small crustaceans, insects and seeds. They often feed in flocks, wading in lines, and swim readily thanks to their partially webbed feet.

Breeding

The species breeds around shallow lakes and marshes of the interior West, from the Canadian prairies south through the Great Basin and Great Plains. The nest is a simple scrape on bare or sparsely vegetated ground near water, lined with small pebbles and vegetation; both sexes incubate a clutch of usually four olive, blotched eggs for about 22–29 days. The downy chicks are precocial and can feed themselves shortly after hatching. In winter the birds move to coasts — California, the Gulf Coast, Florida and Mexico — and to coastal lagoons and estuaries.

Conservation

Market hunting in the 19th and early 20th centuries reduced the population, but protection under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act allowed recovery. The species is now classed as Least Concern, although it remains vulnerable to the loss and degradation of the shallow wetland habitats on which it depends.
