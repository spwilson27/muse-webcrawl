Graphic notation

Graphic notation (or graphic score) is the representation of music through visual symbols outside the realm of traditional music notation. Graphic scores use drawings, shapes, colours, pictures, and text instructions to indicate features of a performance, often leaving pitch, rhythm, or timbre deliberately open to the performer's interpretation.

History

Precursors include the shaped scores of the medieval and Renaissance periods and the eye music of composers such as Baude Cordier. The modern practice emerged in the 1950s among experimental composers dissatisfied with the precision — and limitations — of conventional notation. John Cage used geometric diagrams and the placements of imperfections in paper; Earle Brown's December 1952, consisting only of horizontal and vertical black rectangles, became an emblem of the movement; Cornelius Cardew's Treatise (1963–1967), a 193-page graphic score with no instructions, is the most famous large-scale example.

Purposes

Graphic notation serves several aims: to specify sounds that conventional notation cannot capture, especially in electronic and improvised music; to free performers from fixed pitches and rhythms and encourage creative agency; and to make the score itself a visual artwork. It is closely associated with indeterminacy and experimental music, and remains in use in free improvisation, education, and interdisciplinary performance.
