Murder hole

Not to be confused with a meurtrière (arrowslit).

A murder hole or meurtrière is a hole in the ceiling of a gateway or passageway in a fortification through which the defenders could fire, throw or pour harmful substances or objects — such as arrows, rocks, boiling water, hot sand or quicklime — down on attackers. Boiling oil was rarely used because of its cost. Similar holes, called machicolations, were often located in the curtain walls of castles, fortified houses and city walls, where they served the same purpose of attacking enemies from above.

The term is sometimes used for an arrowslit itself, but a true murder hole is set in a horizontal surface above a passage, typically between the portcullis and the inner gate of a gatehouse, trapping attackers in an enclosed killing area overlooked from the guardroom above. Examples survive in many British and continental castles, including the gatehouses of Bodiam Castle and the Tower of London.

Roman fortifications used a comparable feature, sometimes called a murder hole by analogy, and the concept also appears in Japanese castles as ishi-otoshi ("stone drops").
