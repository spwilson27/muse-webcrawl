In number theory, an abundant number (or excessive number) is a positive integer for which the sum of its proper divisors is greater than the number itself. The integer 12 is the first abundant number: its proper divisors are 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6, which sum to 16. By contrast, a number whose proper divisors sum to exactly the number is a perfect number, and one whose divisors sum to less is a deficient number.

Examples

The first several abundant numbers are 12, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 42, 48, 54, 56, 60, 66, 70, 72, 78, 80, 84, 88, 90, 96, 100, 102, 104, 108, 112, 114, 120, … Every multiple of a perfect number or of an abundant number is itself abundant, so there are infinitely many abundant numbers, both even and odd. The smallest odd abundant number is 945.

Properties

Abundant numbers have natural density roughly between 0.2474 and 0.2480, as established by Behrend and refined by Deléglise. Every integer greater than 20,161 can be written as the sum of two abundant numbers. An abundant number that is not the sum of two abundant numbers is called primitive abundant-related in some terminology; a related class is the weird numbers — abundant numbers that are not semiperfect, the smallest being 70.

History

The classification of numbers as abundant, perfect, or deficient goes back to Nicomachus's Introductio Arithmetica (c. 100 AD), which described the first several abundant numbers and attached moralising interpretations to the categories.
