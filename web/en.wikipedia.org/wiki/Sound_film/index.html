Sound film

A sound film is a motion picture with synchronised sound, or sound technologically coupled to the image, as opposed to a silent film. The first known public exhibition of projected sound films took place in Paris in 1900, but the field remained technologically and commercially difficult for decades. The decisive breakthrough came in the mid-to-late 1920s, when competing sound-on-disc and sound-on-film systems matured and the Hollywood studios converted production and exhibition to sound within a few years.

Technology

Two families of technology competed. Sound-on-disc systems, such as Warner Bros.' Vitaphone, played audio from phonograph records synchronised with the projector. Sound-on-film systems, such as Movietone and RCA Photophone, printed an optical soundtrack along the edge of the film itself, which proved more reliable and easier to edit and eventually prevailed.

Transition

The success of The Jazz Singer (1927), with its synchronised dialogue sequences, triggered the rapid conversion of the American industry in 1928–29, followed by Europe and the rest of the world. Studios rewired stages, theatres installed sound equipment, and actors' voices became a decisive career factor. Early sound films were often static because of noisy cameras and fixed microphones, but blimped cameras and boom microphones restored camera movement by the early 1930s. The musical film emerged as a signature genre of the new medium.

Impact

Sound transformed film language, international markets and stardom: the universal intertitle gave way to dialogue, national-language industries grew behind dubbing and subtitles, and silent-film careers such as Buster Keaton's and those of many foreign-born stars faltered. Within a decade the "talkie" had entirely displaced silent cinema as the commercial norm.
