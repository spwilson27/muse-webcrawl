Montségur is a commune in the Ariège department in southwestern France, in the foothills of the Pyrenees. The village is dominated by the ruined fortress of Montségur, perched on a rocky peak (a pog) at an altitude of 1,207 metres. The site is famous as the last major stronghold of the Cathars, whose destruction there in 1244 marked the effective end of organised Catharism in Languedoc.

History

The Cathar stronghold

A fortress existed on the site from early medieval times, but the castle known today was largely built up in the early 13th century by Raymond de Péreille, lord of Montségur, at the request of the Cathar church. From about 1232, when the Cathar bishop Guilhabert de Castres took refuge there, Montségur became the seat and sanctuary of the Cathar faith during the Albigensian Crusade and the subsequent Inquisition. By the early 1240s several hundred people — Cathar perfecti (the ordained elite), believers, soldiers, and their families — lived in the castle and in a village of terraces below it.

The siege of 1243–44

In May 1242 a party from Montségur murdered a group of inquisitors at Avignonet, an act that provoked the royal seneschal Hugues des Arcis to besiege the castle with a large army in May 1243. The siege lasted about ten months. Basque mercenary mountaineers eventually scaled the cliffs by night and seized a key outwork on the eastern ridge, allowing catapults to bombard the defences at close range. The garrison surrendered on terms in March 1244.

The burning of the perfecti

Under the surrender terms, ordinary defenders and believers who renounced their faith were allowed to leave, but the Cathar perfecti who refused to abjure — around 200 to 220 men and women — were burned alive on 16 March 1244 in a great pyre at the foot of the pog, in a meadow since known as the Prat dels Cramats ("field of the burned"). A memorial stele stands there today.

Aftermath and legends

The castle was partially rebuilt as a royal garrison fortress on the frontier with Aragon and was occupied until the 17th century. In later centuries Montségur acquired a rich legendary aura: tales of a lost Cathar treasure smuggled out during the siege, associations with the Holy Grail, and esoteric interest in the 20th century — including attention from the Nazi researcher Otto Rahn — have made it an emblem of Occitan identity and of resistance to persecution.

The site today

The ruins of the fortress, a small museum in the village, and the memorial at the Prat dels Cramats draw many visitors each year; the climb to the summit takes roughly half an hour on foot from the village.
