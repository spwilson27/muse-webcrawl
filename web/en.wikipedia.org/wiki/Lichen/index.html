Lichen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lichen is a composite organism: a symbiosis between a fungus (the mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (a green alga or cyanobacterium, the photobiont) living among the fungus's filaments. The fungus provides structure and protection; the photobiont provides sugars by photosynthesis — the same trick plants perform with their chloroplasts, evolved independently. About 20,000 lichen species are known. Once classified as single organisms, lichens are now understood as miniature ecosystems; many also host yeasts and bacteria.

Forms and ecology

Lichens grow as crusts (crustose), leafy lobes (foliose) or shrubby/hair-like tufts (fruticose), in colours from grey-green to brilliant orange. They grow extremely slowly — sometimes a fraction of a millimetre a year, and some Arctic lichens are centuries or millennia old — and colonise surfaces nothing else can: bare rock, bark, desert soil, tundra and gravestones. As pioneer species they help form soil by weathering rock. Lichens are exceptionally sensitive to air pollution (especially sulfur dioxide), making them natural air-quality indicators; their disappearance and return track clean-air laws. Reindeer depend on "reindeer lichen" in winter, and many birds and insects use lichens for food and camouflage.
