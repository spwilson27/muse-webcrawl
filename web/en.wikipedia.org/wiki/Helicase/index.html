Helicase

Helicases are a class of enzymes thought to be vital during DNA replication. They separate double-stranded DNA into single strands, allowing each strand to be copied; they also act on RNA in processes including transcription, RNA processing and translation. Helicases move along one strand of the nucleic acid in either a 3′→5′ or 5′→3′ direction, powered by the hydrolysis of ATP, breaking the hydrogen bonds between annealed complementary bases.

At the replication fork, helicase unwinds the parental DNA duplex.

Function

During replication, the replicative helicase — the MCM complex in eukaryotes, DnaB in bacteria — forms a ring around one strand and translocates along it, unzipping the duplex ahead of the DNA polymerase. Other helicases resolve DNA secondary structures such as G-quadruplexes, dismantle protein–DNA complexes, and act as RNA helicases (the DEAD-box and DEAH-box families) that remodel RNA and ribonucleoprotein particles. Helicases are classified into superfamilies (SF1–SF6) by conserved sequence motifs.

Clinical significance

Mutations in human helicase genes cause several genetic disorders. Defects in the RecQ family helicases cause Werner syndrome (WRN), Bloom syndrome (BLM) and Rothmund–Thomson syndrome (RECQL4) — characterised by genomic instability, cancer predisposition and premature ageing. Mutations in the XPD and XPB helicases of the TFIIH complex cause xeroderma pigmentosum and related repair disorders, and the RNA helicase DDX3X is implicated in intellectual disability.

History

The first DNA helicase activity was identified in E. coli in 1976, and the first eukaryotic helicases were characterised in the 1980s. Helicases have since been found in all forms of cellular life and in many viruses, which often encode their own (for example, the large T antigen of SV40).
