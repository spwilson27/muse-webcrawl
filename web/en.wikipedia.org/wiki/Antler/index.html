Antler

Antlers are extensions of an animal's skull found in members of the deer family (Cervidae). They are bony structures that are shed and regrown each year, typically borne by males (in reindeer/caribou, by females as well). Antlers grow from permanent bony pedicles on the skull and, while growing, are covered in a vascular skin called velvet.

Growth and function

Antler growth is among the fastest of any mammalian tissue and is driven by photoperiod and testosterone. Once growth is complete the velvet is rubbed off, leaving hard bone for the rutting season, when males use antlers in contests for mates. After the rut, antlers are cast and the cycle repeats. Unlike horns — permanent keratin-sheathed structures of bovids — antlers are pure bone and are regrown annually.

Human uses

Antlers have been used since prehistory for tools, weapons and carving; shed antlers are still collected for knife handles, buttons, dog chews and decoration. Velvet antler is used in some traditional medicines, though clinical evidence for benefit is limited.
