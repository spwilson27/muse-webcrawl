Diamond dust

Diamond dust is a ground-level cloud composed of tiny ice crystals. This meteorological phenomenon is also referred to simply as ice crystals and is reported in the METAR code as IC. Diamond dust generally forms under clear or nearly clear skies, so it is sometimes referred to as clear-sky precipitation. It is most commonly observed in Antarctica and the Arctic, but can occur anywhere with temperatures well below freezing.

Characteristics

Diamond dust crystals are typically hexagonal plates and columns, small and well separated, which sparkle in sunlight — giving the phenomenon its name — and often produce optical effects such as halos, sun dogs (parhelia), light pillars and tangent arcs. Unlike fog, the crystals do not usually obscure visibility greatly, though they can drift close to the ground in a shimmering layer. Diamond dust often forms during temperature inversions, when relatively moist air aloft mixes with colder air near the surface.

Occurrence

On the Antarctic plateau, diamond dust falls on a large majority of days of the year and contributes a significant fraction of the continent's precipitation, accumulating slowly as firn. It also occurs in Arctic Canada, Russia and other cold continental interiors during winter.
