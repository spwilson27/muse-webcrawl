Respiratory physiology

Respiratory physiology is the branch of physiology concerned with how the respiratory system takes in oxygen and expels carbon dioxide, and how these gases are exchanged and transported in the body. Its central processes are ventilation (the movement of air into and out of the lungs), external respiration (gas exchange between alveolar air and pulmonary capillary blood), transport of gases in the blood, and internal respiration (exchange between the systemic capillaries and the tissues).

Ventilation and mechanics

Breathing is driven by the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles, which change the volume of the thoracic cavity and hence the pressure within the lungs, as described by Boyle's law. Lung volumes and capacities — tidal volume, vital capacity, residual volume and total lung capacity — are measured by spirometry. The compliance of the lungs and chest wall, airway resistance, and the surface tension of the alveolar lining (reduced by surfactant) determine the work of breathing.

Gas exchange and transport

Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across the alveolar–capillary membrane down partial-pressure gradients. Oxygen is carried in blood mainly bound to haemoglobin in red blood cells, the relationship described by the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve, which shifts with temperature, pH (the Bohr effect) and 2,3-BPG. Carbon dioxide is carried mainly as bicarbonate ions, and additionally dissolved and bound to haemoglobin as carbamino compounds.

Control of breathing

Ventilation is controlled by respiratory centres in the medulla and pons, modulated by central and peripheral chemoreceptors that respond chiefly to arterial CO2 and pH, and in certain circumstances to low oxygen.
