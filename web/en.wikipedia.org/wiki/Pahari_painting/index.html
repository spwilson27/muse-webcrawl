Pahari painting

Pahari painting
RegionHill states of the western Himalayas (present-day Himachal Pradesh, Jammu, Punjab hills)
Periodc. 17th – 19th centuries
MediumOpaque watercolour (gouache) with gold and silver on paper
Major centresBasohli, Guler, Kangra, Chamba, Nurpur, Mandi, Garhwal

Pahari painting (from Hindi pahār, "hill") is the tradition of miniature painting that flourished between the 17th and 19th centuries in the small Rajput kingdoms of the western Himalayan foothills of northern India. Together with the painting of the Rajasthani plains, it forms the Rajput painting tradition. Pahari art is celebrated for its lyrical treatment of landscape, its refined line, and its devotion to the legends of Krishna.

Origins and schools

The earliest Pahari school is that of Basohli, emerging in the later 17th century with bold, expressive compositions, saturated primary colours, stylised faces with receding foreheads and large lotus-like eyes, and decorative details such as beetle-wing cases used for emerald jewellery. During the 18th century the centre of innovation shifted to Guler, where artists such as Pandit Seu and his sons Manaku and Nainsukh brought a new naturalism and delicacy, partly in response to Mughal painting styles reaching the hills.

The most famous phase of Pahari painting is the Kangra school, which flowered under the patronage of Raja Sansar Chand (r. 1775–1823) in the Kangra valley. Kangra miniatures are noted for their cool green landscapes, graceful female figures, and poetic rendering of atmosphere and season.

Themes

The dominant subjects are drawn from Hindu devotional literature: the Bhagavata Purana, the Gita Govinda of Jayadeva, the Rasikapriya and Satsai of Bihari, and the epics Ramayana and Mahabharata. Famous series illustrate the love of Radha and Krishna across the twelve months of the year (Baramasa), the modes of music (Ragamala), and classified types of heroines (Nayika bheda). Portraits of hill rajas, court scenes, and hunting pictures are also common.

Decline

The Sikh conquest of the hill states in the early 19th century and the subsequent British annexation dissolved the court patronage that sustained Pahari workshops. Many painters migrated to Sikh and later colonial courts; by the later 19th century the tradition had faded, leaving major collections in museums in India, Europe, and North America.
