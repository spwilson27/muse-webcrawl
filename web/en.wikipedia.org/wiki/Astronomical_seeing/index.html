Astronomical seeing

Astronomical seeing is the blurring and twinkling of astronomical objects such as stars caused by turbulence in the Earth's atmosphere, which has varying densities and temperatures and therefore varying refractive indices. Seeing is the main factor limiting the angular resolution of ground-based telescopes: even a perfect large telescope under typical seeing resolves no better than a small one, at roughly one arcsecond at a good site.


A point source smeared by atmospheric turbulence into a "seeing disc".

Measurement

Seeing quality is usually quoted as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the point spread function of a star image, in arcseconds. Sub-arcsecond seeing is excellent; many professional observatories in Chile, Hawaii and the Canary Islands average around 0.6–1.0 arcseconds. The Fried parameter, r0, describes the size of the atmospheric cells over which the wavefront is roughly flat — typically 10–20 cm at visible wavelengths at good sites — and sets the effective aperture beyond which a telescope gains no resolution without correction. The Antoniadi scale (I–V) is a qualitative scale long used by amateur observers.

Causes

Turbulence arises from temperature differences between the ground and air, wind shear between layers, jet-stream winds, and local heating of the telescope dome and mirror. Thin high cloud and poor transparency are separate from seeing, though the two are often confused: the steadiest nights may be hazy, and brilliantly clear nights after a cold front often have turbulent air.

Mitigation

Observatories are sited on high, isolated peaks with smooth laminar airflow. Modern techniques circumvent seeing: adaptive optics uses a deformable mirror, corrected hundreds of times per second by reference to a guide star or laser guide star, to restore near-diffraction-limited images especially in the infrared; speckle imaging and lucky imaging reconstruct sharp images from many short exposures; and interferometry combines widely separated telescopes. Space telescopes such as Hubble avoid the problem entirely.
