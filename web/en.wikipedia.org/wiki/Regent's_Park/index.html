Regent's Park is one of the Royal Parks of London, covering 166 hectares (410 acres) in the northwest of the city, spanning the City of Westminster and the London Borough of Camden. Named after the Prince Regent (the future George IV), it was laid out from 1811 to designs by the architect John Nash as part of his grand scheme linking the park, through a processional route, to Carlton House in central London.

History

The land was originally a hunting chase of Henry VIII, known as Marylebone Park, and remained Crown farmland until Nash's scheme transformed it into a planned landscape of villas set in parkland, encircled by elegant terraces of stuccoed houses such as those designed by Nash and Decimus Burton. Unlike the original plan for dozens of villas, only a handful were built. The park was opened to the public progressively during the nineteenth century; Primrose Hill, adjoining to the north, was added in 1842.

Features

The park contains an Inner Circle of gardens, including Queen Mary's Gardens with their rose collection, an Outer Circle road, a boating lake, and extensive sports pitches that make it London's largest outdoor sports area. On its northern edge the Regent's Canal runs past London Zoo, opened in 1828 and among the world's oldest scientific zoos. Other landmarks include the Regent's Park Open Air Theatre, Winfield House (the residence of the United States ambassador), the London Central Mosque, and sites of the University of London and Regent's University London. The Frieze Art Fair is held in the park each autumn. A bomb exploded on the park's bandstand in 1982, planted by the Provisional IRA, killed seven bandsmen of the Royal Green Jackets.
