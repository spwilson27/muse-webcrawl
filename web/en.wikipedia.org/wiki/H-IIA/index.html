H-IIA

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The H-IIA (H-2A) is a Japanese expendable launch system operated by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) for the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). Derived from the earlier H-II rocket, the H-IIA was designed to improve reliability and reduce costs, and it became Japan's principal large launch vehicle for government and commercial satellites after its first flight on 29 August 2001.

Design

The H-IIA is a two-stage liquid-fuelled rocket. Its first stage uses a single LE-7A engine burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, augmented by solid rocket boosters (SRB-A) whose number varies by configuration; the second stage uses a single LE-5B engine. Variants are designated by a four-digit code (for example 202, 204 and 2022/2024 configurations) indicating the number of boosters and fairing type. The standard H-IIA 202 can place roughly 10–15 tonnes into low Earth orbit and about 4–6 tonnes into geostationary transfer orbit, depending on configuration.

History

After an initial series of successes, flight 6 in November 2003 failed, destroying two reconnaissance satellites; the vehicle returned to flight in February 2005 and thereafter built one of the highest success rates of any launcher. Launches take place from the Tanegashima Space Center. The H-IIA has carried Earth-observation satellites such as Daichi (ALOS), the lunar orbiter Kaguya (SELENE) was launched on an H-IIA, as were the Hayabusa2 asteroid mission and numerous information-gathering satellites. Operations were planned to transition to the newer H3 rocket in the mid-2020s.
