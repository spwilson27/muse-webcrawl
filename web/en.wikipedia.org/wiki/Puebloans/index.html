The Puebloans — in older literature "Pueblo Indians" — are the Indigenous peoples of the American Southwest whose cultures are defined by settled farming villages (pueblos) of adobe and masonry. The term covers both the archaeological cultures of the pre-Columbian Southwest, above all the Ancestral Puebloans, and their living descendants, the modern Pueblo peoples.

Archaeological sequence

Archaeologists divide the pre-contact Pueblo sequence into stages (the Pecos Classification), from Basket Maker II (c. 100 BCE–500 CE), when maize farming spread through the region, through the Pueblo periods (c. 750–1600). Pueblo I villages of above-ground masonry rooms appeared on the Colorado Plateau; Pueblo II saw the florescence of Chaco Canyon, where great houses such as Pueblo Bonito anchored a regional system of roads and exchange. In Pueblo III, populations aggregated into large towns and cliff dwellings such as those at Mesa Verde. Prolonged drought in the late 13th century coincided with migration to the Rio Grande valley and the Hopi and Zuni areas (Pueblo IV), the world the Spanish encountered in 1540.

Architecture

Pueblo building is characterised by multi-storey terraced room blocks of adobe, sandstone and timber vigas, traditionally entered by ladders through roof hatchways. Circular ceremonial rooms called kivas — great kivas at Chaco being especially large — continue in use in modern Pueblos. Taos Pueblo's Hlauuma and Hlaauma are often described as the oldest continuously inhabited buildings in the United States.

Subsistence

Puebloan farming adapted maize, beans and squash — the "three sisters" — to an arid environment through dry farming, terracing, check dams and, along the Rio Grande and its tributaries, canal irrigation. Turkey domestication and, later, trade for macaws and copper bells from Mesoamerica attest to long-distance connections.

Descendant communities

The modern Pueblo peoples maintain direct cultural and, in most cases, linguistic and ceremonial continuity with the archaeological cultures — a continuity recognised in tribal oral traditions and affirmed in collaborations between tribes and archaeologists over repatriation, including under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990).
