Mary Robinson (Maid of Buttermere)

Mary Robinson (1778 – 1837), known as the Maid of Buttermere, was an innkeeper's daughter from the Lake District in England whose beauty was celebrated in Romantic literature, and who became nationally famous in 1802 when she was deceived into marriage by the bigamist and forger John Hatfield.

Fame

Mary Robinson worked at her father's inn, the Fish Inn at Buttermere in Cumberland. Travellers' accounts and guidebooks praised her looks; the "radical tourist" Joseph Budworth's 1792 account first spread her fame, and she became a minor celebrity, mentioned with ambivalence by Wordsworth in The Prelude and courted by curious visitors.

The Hatfield marriage

In 1802 a man calling himself Colonel Alexander Augustus Hope, supposedly brother of Lord Hopetoun, courted and married her. He was exposed by Samuel Taylor Coleridge in the Morning Post as John Hatfield, an impostor with a wife living, who had passed forged letters of credit. Hatfield was tried at Carlisle in 1803 and hanged for forgery. The case was a national sensation, reported across Britain.

Later life

Robinson's child by Hatfield died young. In 1807 she married Richard Harrison, a prosperous local farmer, with whom she had children. She died in 1837 and is buried at St Kentigern's churchyard, Caldbeck. Her story was novelised in Melvyn Bragg's The Maid of Buttermere (1987), and the Fish Inn still trades on her legend.
