In mathematics, modular arithmetic is a system of arithmetic for integers in which numbers "wrap around" after reaching a certain value, called the modulus. The modern approach to modular arithmetic was developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss in his book Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

Congruence

Given integers a, b and a positive integer n, a and b are congruent modulo n if n divides their difference a − b. This is written:

a ≡ b (mod n)

For example, 38 ≡ 14 (mod 12), because 38 − 14 = 24 is a multiple of 12 — equivalently, both numbers leave the same remainder when divided by 12. Congruence is an equivalence relation, and the integers modulo n form the ring Z/nZ with n residue classes {0, 1, …, n−1}.

Operations

Congruence is preserved under addition, subtraction, and multiplication: if a ≡ b and c ≡ d (mod n), then a + c ≡ b + d and ac ≡ bd (mod n). Division is more delicate: a modular inverse of a exists modulo n exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1, in which case the residues coprime to n form the multiplicative group (Z/nZ)× of order φ(n), Euler's totient function. Important results built on this include Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, the Chinese remainder theorem, and Wilson's theorem.

Applications

  • Clock arithmetic: hours wrap modulo 12 (or 24); days of the week repeat modulo 7.
  • Cryptography: modular exponentiation underpins RSA, Diffie–Hellman key exchange, and elliptic-curve methods.
  • Checksums: ISBN, credit-card (Luhn), and IBAN codes use modular arithmetic to detect errors.
  • Computing: hash functions, pseudorandom generators, and cyclic scheduling all rely on remainders.
  • Music and calendars: pitch classes form Z/12Z; calendrical cycles use interacting moduli.
