Toynbee Hall is a charitable institution in Whitechapel, in the East End of London, founded in 1884. It was the first of the university settlement houses, a model of social reform in which university graduates lived ("settled") in deprived neighbourhoods, sharing their knowledge and resources with local residents while learning from the community. The hall was named in memory of the Oxford historian and social reformer Arnold Toynbee, who had died the previous year at the age of 30.

History

Toynbee Hall was founded by the Reverend Samuel Augustus Barnett, vicar of St Jude's, Whitechapel, and his wife Henrietta Barnett. Appalled by the poverty of the East End and influenced by Toynbee's ideas, they persuaded Oxford and Cambridge graduates to take up residence in the settlement and run classes, clubs and advice services for local people. The purpose-built hall, designed in a Tudor style, opened on Commercial Street in 1885.

Residents and visitors at Toynbee Hall included future prime minister Clement Attlee, who worked there and later managed it, and William Beveridge, who served as sub-warden in 1903–1905; both men's experiences at Toynbee Hall shaped the thinking behind the British welfare state. Charles Booth's poverty research was partly organised from the hall, and the Workers' Educational Association grew out of classes held there. The settlement movement it inspired spread to the United States, where Jane Addams modelled Hull House in Chicago partly on Toynbee Hall after visiting in 1888.

Activities

Historically Toynbee Hall offered adult education classes, free legal advice (an early form of legal aid), debating societies, youth clubs and country holidays for East End children. The Whitechapel Gallery owed its founding exhibitions in 1901 to the Barnetts' art shows at the hall.

Present day

Toynbee Hall continues to operate as a charity from its Commercial Street site, which was redeveloped and reopened in 2020. Its modern work focuses on financial inclusion and debt advice, research into poverty, services for older people, youth work and free legal advice, serving the communities of Tower Hamlets. Its research arm has produced influential studies on poverty, consumer credit and social policy.
