Pearlite

Pearlite is a two-phased, lamellar (layered) microstructure composed of alternating layers of ferrite (about 88 wt%) and cementite (about 12 wt%) that occurs in some steels and cast irons. It forms when austenite of eutectoid composition (0.76 wt% carbon) is cooled slowly below the eutectoid temperature of 727 °C, in a eutectoid reaction in which austenite transforms to ferrite plus cementite. The name derives from its mother-of-pearl appearance under the microscope, an effect of light diffracting from the fine lamellae.

Formation and structure

During slow cooling, carbon diffuses away from the growing ferrite lamellae into adjacent cementite lamellae. The spacing between lamellae depends on the cooling rate: faster cooling (greater undercooling) produces finer pearlite with more closely spaced layers, while very slow cooling gives coarse pearlite. Under sufficiently fast cooling the eutectoid reaction is suppressed and martensite or bainite may form instead.

Properties

Pearlite is stronger and harder than ferrite alone but more ductile than fully cementitic structures. Fine pearlite is significantly stronger than coarse pearlite because the lamellar interfaces impede dislocation motion. Steels with a fully pearlitic microstructure — eutectoid and near-eutectoid steels — are used for rails, high-strength wire (including piano wire and tyre cord) and cutting tools. In hypoeutectoid steels pearlite appears alongside proeutectoid ferrite; in hypereutectoid steels it appears with proeutectoid cementite.

Related microstructures

Bainite resembles pearlite in being a ferrite–cementite aggregate but forms at lower temperatures with a different, non-lamellar morphology and carbon distribution. Spheroidite, produced by prolonged heating of pearlite just below the eutectoid temperature, consists of spherical cementite particles in ferrite and is the softest steel microstructure.
