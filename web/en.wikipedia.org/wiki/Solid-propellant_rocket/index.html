Solid-propellant rocket


A solid-propellant rocket uses a solid grain of fuel and oxidiser as propellant.

A solid-propellant rocket (or solid rocket) is a rocket whose motor uses a solid propellant grain — a mixture of fuel and oxidiser cast into the motor casing. Solid rockets are the oldest form of rocket, dating to gunpowder rockets in medieval China, and remain widely used in missiles, model rockets, and as boosters for space launch vehicles.

Design

The propellant grain contains both fuel and oxidiser: modern composite propellants typically combine ammonium perchlorate oxidiser with a rubbery binder such as HTPB that doubles as fuel, plus powdered aluminium. The shape of the central bore in the grain controls the burning surface area and hence the thrust profile over the burn.

Advantages and disadvantages

Solid motors are simple, reliable, storable for long periods, and can deliver very high thrust — the Space Shuttle and Ariane boosters are famous examples. However, once ignited a solid motor generally cannot be throttled or shut down, and its specific impulse is lower than that of liquid-propellant engines.
