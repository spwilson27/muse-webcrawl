Blister

Blister
SpecialtyDermatology
SymptomsRaised pocket of skin filled with clear fluid
CausesFriction, burns, frostbite, infection, allergy, autoimmune disease

A blister is a small pocket of body fluid (lymph, serum, plasma, blood, or pus) within the upper layers of the skin, usually caused by forceful rubbing (friction), burning, freezing, chemical exposure, or infection. Most blisters are filled with a clear fluid, either serum or plasma. However, blisters can be filled with blood (blood blisters) or with pus if they become infected. The word "blister" entered English in the 13th century, from Old French blestre.

Formation

A friction blister forms when repeated shear forces separate the epidermis from the dermis; plasma leaks into the pocket, protecting the raw tissue beneath while new skin grows. Friction blisters are common on the feet of walkers and runners and on the hands from tools or rowing. Thermal blisters follow second-degree burns and severe sunburn; frostbite also blisters. Other causes include contact dermatitis (such as poison ivy), herpes and hand, foot and mouth disease infections, insect bites, and autoimmune blistering diseases such as bullous pemphigoid and epidermolysis bullosa.

Treatment

Most blisters heal on their own and are best left intact, since the unbroken skin is a natural barrier against infection. Covering with a padded bandage or moleskin relieves pressure. Large or painful blisters may be drained with a sterilized needle at the edge while leaving the skin roof in place, then dressed. Signs of infection — spreading redness, warmth, pus, or red streaks — warrant medical attention. Blood blisters arise when a pinch or crush injures dermal blood vessels and usually resolve without treatment.

Prevention

Properly fitted footwear, moisture-wicking or double-layer socks, lubricants, taping, and gloves prevent most friction blisters; hikers are advised to stop and treat "hot spots" — the warm, red precursors of blisters — before they develop.
