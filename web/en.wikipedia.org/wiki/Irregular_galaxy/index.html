Irregular galaxy

An irregular galaxy is a galaxy that does not have a distinct regular shape, unlike a spiral or an elliptical galaxy. Irregular galaxies often appear chaotic, with no nuclear bulge and no trace of spiral arm structure. Collectively they account for roughly a quarter of all known galaxies, though estimates vary with survey depth.

Classification

In the Hubble sequence, irregulars are labelled Irr. Edwin Hubble distinguished Irr I galaxies, which show some structure such as loose spiral features and many young stars and H II regions, from Irr II galaxies, which appear amorphous and disorganised. Gérard de Vaucouleurs later introduced the classes Magellanic irregulars (Im), exemplified by the Large Magellanic Cloud, and dwarf irregulars (dI). Many "irregular" appearances result from gravitational interactions or mergers with neighbouring galaxies, which distort an originally regular shape.

Properties

Irregular galaxies tend to be rich in gas and dust and to host active star formation, giving them a bluish cast from young, massive stars. Dwarf irregulars are among the most common galaxies in the universe and are considered useful analogues of the young galaxies that formed in the early universe, being low in heavy elements (low metallicity) and relatively unevolved. Famous examples include the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, the starburst galaxy M82 (the Cigar Galaxy), and NGC 1427A.
