Escargot

Escargot (French for "snail") is a dish consisting of cooked land snails, a delicacy of French cuisine typically served as an appetiser. The best-known preparation, escargots de Bourgogne, presents snails in their shells with garlic-and-parsley butter (beurre d'escargot), baked until bubbling and eaten with special tongs and a narrow fork.

Preparation

The species most commonly eaten include the Burgundy snail Helix pomatia and Cornu aspersum. Snails are purged by fasting or feeding on clean greens, removed from their shells, cleaned, and cooked — traditionally simmered in court-bouillon — before being returned to cleaned shells with seasoned butter. Because wild Helix pomatia is protected in parts of Europe, much commercial escargot is farmed (heliciculture) or imported.

History and consumption

Snails have been eaten since prehistory; roasted snail shells are found at archaeological sites around the Mediterranean, and the Romans fattened snails for the table. The modern Burgundian dish is credited to the early 19th century and the chef of the statesman Talleyrand. France remains the largest consumer, with tens of thousands of tonnes consumed annually, especially at Christmas and New Year. Nutritionally, snails are high in protein and low in fat.
