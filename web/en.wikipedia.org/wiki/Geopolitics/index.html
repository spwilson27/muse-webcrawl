Geopolitics is the study of the effects of geography — human and physical — on politics and international relations, and of how states' power is distributed over geographic space. The term was coined by the Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén around 1899, drawing on the work of Friedrich Ratzel.

Classical geopolitics

Early theories emphasized territory and position. In 1904 Halford Mackinder proposed the Heartland theory, arguing that control of the Eurasian interior ("the pivot area") was the key to world power; Nicholas Spykman later countered with the Rimland thesis, stressing the importance of Eurasia's coastal fringes. In Germany, Karl Haushofer's school developed Geopolitik, which became associated with Nazi expansionism and discredited the field in the postwar West.

Cold War and after

Geopolitical thinking shaped the Cold War strategies of containment, control of sea lanes and chokepoints, and competition over resources. After the Cold War the field broadened: contemporary geopolitics addresses energy and trade routes, Arctic and maritime claims, cyber and space domains, climate change, and the geographic dimensions of supply chains and technology rivalry.

Critical geopolitics

Since the 1980s, "critical geopolitics" scholars have examined how geopolitical knowledge itself is produced — how maps, media and political discourse construct images of places and threats — rather than treating geography as a neutral determinant of policy.
