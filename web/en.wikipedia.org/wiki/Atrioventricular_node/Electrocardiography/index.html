Electrocardiography is the process of producing an electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG), a recording of the heart's electrical activity through repeated cardiac cycles. Electrodes placed on the skin detect the tiny voltage changes caused by cardiac muscle depolarisation and repolarisation.

The tracing

A normal beat shows a P wave (atrial depolarisation), the QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation, initiated after conduction through the AV node and bundle of His), and a T wave (ventricular repolarisation). The intervals between them — PR, QT and others — carry diagnostic information, as do axis, amplitudes and morphology.

Uses

The ECG is a first-line test for chest pain and palpitations. It diagnoses arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation and heart block, identifies myocardial infarction and ischaemia (e.g. ST elevation), and reveals hypertrophy, electrolyte disturbances and drug effects. Extended monitoring uses Holter monitors, event recorders and implantable loop recorders.

History

Willem Einthoven's string galvanometer (1901–1903) made the ECG practical; he named the PQRST waves and received the 1924 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The 12-lead system was standardised in the mid-20th century, and automated interpretation and wearable single-lead devices are now widespread.
