Atrioventricular node

The atrioventricular node (AV node; also called the node of Tawara or Aschoff–Tawara node) is part of the electrical conduction system of the heart. It electrically connects the atria and ventricles, delaying the impulse arriving from the sinoatrial node by roughly 0.09–0.12 seconds before passing it to the bundle of His, so that the atria can complete their contraction and empty into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins.

Structure

The AV node lies at the apex of the triangle of Koch in the right atrium, bounded by the tendon of Todaro, the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve, and the ostium of the coronary sinus. It consists of specialised cardiomyocytes that are smaller than working atrial cells and arranged in a compact mesh. Its blood supply, the AV nodal artery, arises from the right coronary artery in about 90% of people (right coronary dominance) and from the left circumflex artery in the remainder.

Function

Conduction through the AV node is slow — about 0.05 m/s — largely because of the small size of its cells, their sparse gap junctions, and the decremental nature of conduction. This produces the AV delay seen on the electrocardiogram as the PR segment. The node also has an intrinsic firing rate of roughly 40–60 impulses per minute and can act as a backup pacemaker if the sinoatrial node fails. Its refractory properties limit how many atrial impulses reach the ventricles during rapid atrial rhythms such as atrial fibrillation.

Clinical significance

Disease of the AV node causes atrioventricular block, graded from first-degree (prolonged PR interval) through second-degree to third-degree (complete heart block), in which no atrial impulses reach the ventricles and a pacemaker may be required. The node is also a limb of the re-entrant circuit in AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT), the commonest regular supraventricular tachycardia, treated acutely with vagal manoeuvres or adenosine and definitively by catheter ablation of the slow pathway. Drugs that slow AV nodal conduction include beta blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and digoxin.

History

The node was described in 1906 by Sunao Tawara, working in Ludwig Aschoff's laboratory in Marburg, in his monograph on the conduction system of the mammalian heart; Keith and Flack's description of the sinoatrial node followed in 1907.
