Atrial fibrillation (AF or A-fib) is an abnormal heart rhythm characterised by rapid and irregular beating of the atrial chambers of the heart. It is the most common serious cardiac arrhythmia in the world, affecting tens of millions of people, and often starts as brief episodes (paroxysmal AF) that become longer and more constant over time.

Signs and mechanism

Episodes may cause palpitations, chest discomfort, breathlessness, light-headedness and fatigue, though many are asymptomatic. Mechanistically, disorganised electrical wavelets in the atria — often triggered by ectopic activity near the pulmonary veins — replace the coordinated signal from the sinoatrial node. The atrioventricular node passes impulses to the ventricles irregularly, producing the classic "irregularly irregular" pulse. Diagnosis is by electrocardiography, which shows absent P waves and irregular QRS intervals.

Complications

Because the atria do not contract effectively, blood can stagnate and clot, especially in the left atrial appendage; embolised clots greatly increase the risk of ischaemic stroke. Persistent rapid rates can also cause tachycardia-induced heart failure.

Management

Treatment addresses rate control (beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers), rhythm control (cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation), and stroke prevention with anticoagulants guided by risk scores such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc. Risk factors include hypertension, heart failure, valvular disease, obesity, alcohol and hyperthyroidism.
