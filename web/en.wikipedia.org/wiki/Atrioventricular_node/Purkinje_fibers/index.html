The Purkinje fibers are specialised conducting fibers located in the inner ventricular walls of the heart, just beneath the endocardium, in a space called the subendocardium. They are larger than ordinary cardiac muscle cells, contain few myofibrils and abundant glycogen, and conduct electrical impulses faster than any other cardiac tissue.

Function

The fibers receive impulses from the bundle branches (divisions of the bundle of His) and distribute them almost simultaneously over both ventricles. Their rapid conduction (2–4 m/s, several times faster than working myocardium) produces a coordinated contraction that begins at the apex and papillary muscles and sweeps upward, efficiently ejecting blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery.

Clinical aspects

Purkinje cells have intrinsic automaticity at a slow rate (15–40 beats per minute) and can act as a last-resort "ventricular escape" pacemaker if higher pacemakers and conduction fail — a rhythm visible on the ECG in complete heart block. They are also implicated in triggering certain ventricular arrhythmias, and Purkinje-targeted ablation is used for some cases of idiopathic ventricular fibrillation. The fibers were first described by the Czech physiologist Jan Evangelista Purkyně in 1839.
