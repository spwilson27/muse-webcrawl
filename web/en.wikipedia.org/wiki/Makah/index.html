The Makah (in Makah, qʷidiččaʔa·tx̌) are a Native American people of the Pacific Northwest Coast, living at the northwestern tip of the Olympic Peninsula in Washington State, around the modern community of Neah Bay. The Makah Indian Tribe is a federally recognised tribe whose reservation was established under the Treaty of Neah Bay in 1855. The Makah are the southernmost of the Wakashan-speaking peoples and are culturally and linguistically closest to the Nuu-chah-nulth of Vancouver Island. They are renowned as expert mariners and whalers.


Makah canoes at Neah Bay in the early twentieth century.

Culture and history

Traditional Makah society was organised in five permanent villages — Neah Bay, Baada, Waada (Waatch), Sooes (Tsoo-Yess), and Ozette — with chiefs, commoners, and slaves in a ranked society centred on the resources of the sea. The Makah hunted gray and humpback whales from large cedar canoes with crews of eight, using harpoons with sealskin floats, in a pursuit surrounded by ritual preparation; they also fished halibut, salmon, and cod, hunted seals and porpoises, and traded widely. The archaeological site at Ozette, buried by a mudslide around 1700 and excavated in the 1970s, preserved tens of thousands of wooden and fibre artefacts and is one of the most important wet-site excavations in North America.

Treaty and reservation

In the Treaty of Neah Bay (1855), the Makah ceded most of their land in exchange for a reservation at Neah Bay, medical and educational services, and expressly reserved rights to fish, hunt, and whale "at usual and accustomed grounds." Smallpox and other epidemics, federal assimilation policies including boarding schools, and the suppression of the Makah language — the last fully fluent first-language speaker died in 2002 — severely affected the community, though language revitalisation programmes continue.

Whaling controversy

The Makah ceased whaling in the 1920s as commercial whaling depleted gray whale stocks. After the gray whale was removed from the endangered species list in 1994, the tribe asserted its treaty right and in May 1999 a Makah crew successfully hunted a gray whale, an event that drew international protest from animal welfare groups and support from those defending treaty rights. Litigation over environmental review requirements subsequently stalled further hunts, and the tribe has since sought a waiver under the Marine Mammal Protection Act to resume limited ceremonial and subsistence whaling.
