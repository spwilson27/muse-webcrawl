The Austronesian peoples, sometimes referred to as Austronesian-speaking peoples, are a large group of peoples in Taiwan, Maritime Southeast Asia, parts of mainland Southeast Asia, Micronesia, coastal New Guinea, Island Melanesia, Polynesia, and Madagascar who speak the Austronesian languages. Theirs is one of the widest pre-modern language dispersals in the world, spanning half the globe from Madagascar in the west to Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the east.

Origins and expansion

Linguistic and archaeological evidence points to an origin in Taiwan, whose indigenous peoples retain the greatest diversity of Austronesian languages. Beginning roughly 5,000–6,000 years ago, seafaring groups expanded southward through the Philippines and into Island Southeast Asia, carrying domesticated plants and animals, pottery traditions such as Lapita in the Pacific, and sophisticated outrigger-canoe navigation technology.

In the Pacific, Austronesian navigators settled the islands of Remote Oceania, reaching Samoa and Tonga by about 900 BCE and ultimately Hawai'i, New Zealand (Aotearoa) and Rapa Nui by around 1200 CE. In the Indian Ocean, sailors from the Indonesian archipelago settled Madagascar, where Malagasy developed with substantial Bantu admixture.

Culture

Shared cultural traits historically include tattooing, stilt houses, wet-rice and taro cultivation, betel-nut chewing, ancestor veneration, and elaborate seafaring traditions. Political organisation ranged from the decentralised villages of highland Taiwan to the kingdoms of Java and the chiefdoms of Polynesia, including the near-empire of Tonga.
