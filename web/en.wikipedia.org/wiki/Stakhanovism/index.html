Stakhanovite movement

The Stakhanovite movement (Stakhanovism) was a Soviet campaign of the 1930s that celebrated workers who greatly exceeded production norms, using them as models to raise labour productivity across the economy. It was named after the coal miner Alexei Stakhanov, who on 31 August 1935 was reported to have mined 102 tons of coal in a single shift — fourteen times his quota — at the Central Irmino mine in the Donbass.

A Soviet poster celebrating Stakhanovite workers

Development

The Communist Party publicised Stakhanov's feat and launched a nationwide movement in November 1935, when Stalin praised the Stakhanovites at an All-Union conference, declaring that "life has become better, comrades; life has become more cheerful". Record-breaking workers received higher pay, better housing, consumer goods and public honours. Output norms were raised in 1936 on the basis of Stakhanovite achievements.

Reception and criticism

The movement was controversial. Stakhanov's own record had been achieved with the support of assistants whose work was not credited, and many "records" were staged for propaganda. Ordinary workers often resented Stakhanovites because their feats led to higher quotas without matching pay, and some were assaulted or sabotaged. Historians note that while the campaign may have improved technique and training in some industries, it also disrupted production by privileging record attempts over steady output. After Stalin's death the movement faded, though the word "Stakhanovite" survives as a general term for an exceptionally hard or productive worker.
