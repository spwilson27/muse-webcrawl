Digital Multiplex

Digital Multiplex, commonly known as DMX or DMX512, is a standard for digital communication networks that are commonly used to control lighting and effects. It was originally intended as a standardised method for controlling stage lighting dimmers, which, prior to DMX, had employed various incompatible proprietary protocols. It soon became the primary method for linking controllers (such as a lighting console) to dimmers and special effects devices such as fog machines and intelligent lights.

History

The standard, developed by the Engineering Commission of the United States Institute for Theatre Technology (USITT), began in 1986, with final revisions in 1990 leading to USITT DMX512/1990. In 1998 the Entertainment Services and Technology Association (ESTA) began a revision process to make the protocol an ANSI standard, resulting in ANSI E1.11-2004, and an updated version, ANSI E1.11-2008. DMX has also expanded to uses in non-theatrical interior and architectural lighting.

Network and protocol

A DMX network employs a multi-drop bus topology with nodes strung together in a daisy chain. A network consists of a single DMX controller — the master — and one or more slave devices. Data is transmitted serially at 250 kbit/s over a balanced pair using RS-485 signalling levels, conventionally on five-pin XLR connectors (though three-pin XLR is common in practice). A single data link carries up to 512 channels — one universe — each channel having a value from 0 to 255. Larger installations use multiple universes, increasingly distributed over Ethernet via protocols such as Art-Net and sACN (ANSI E1.31). The remote device management extension, RDM (ANSI E1.20), adds bidirectional communication so fixtures can report status and be configured.

Applications beyond theatre

Besides stage and event lighting, DMX controls architectural façade lighting, holiday light displays animated to music, amusement-park effects, and some fountain and museum installations.
