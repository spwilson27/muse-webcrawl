Inguinal hernia

An inguinal hernia is a protrusion of abdominal contents through the inguinal canal in the groin. It is the most common type of hernia, particularly in men, in whom lifetime risk is roughly one in four. Inguinal hernias are classified as indirect, passing through the deep inguinal ring and following the spermatic cord (often congenital, from a persistent processus vaginalis), or direct, pushing through a weakness in the posterior wall of the canal within Hesselbach's triangle.

Signs and symptoms

The typical presentation is a groin bulge that enlarges with standing, coughing or straining and may disappear when lying down, sometimes with aching discomfort. A hernia that can be pushed back is reducible; one that cannot is incarcerated, and if its blood supply is cut off it becomes strangulated — a surgical emergency causing severe pain, vomiting and, untreated, bowel necrosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical, by examination of the groin standing and straining; ultrasound or other imaging is used when the diagnosis is uncertain or to distinguish a femoral hernia (more common in women and prone to strangulation).

Treatment

Definitive treatment is surgical repair — classically the open Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair, or laparoscopic approaches (TEP and TAPP). Watchful waiting is an accepted option for minimally symptomatic men, though many eventually choose surgery. Hernia repair is among the most common operations performed worldwide.
