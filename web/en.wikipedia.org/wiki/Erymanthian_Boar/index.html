Erymanthian Boar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, the Erymanthian Boar (Greek: ὁ Ἐρυμάνθιος κάπρος; Latin: aper Erymanthius) was a mythical giant wild boar which terrorised the region around Mount Erymanthos in Arcadia. Capturing it alive was the fourth of the Labours of Heracles.

The fourth labour

King Eurystheus ordered Heracles to capture the boar alive and bring it back to Mycenae. Heracles visited the centaur Pholus, who shared a meal and wine with him. The wine's scent attracted other centaurs, and a fight broke out in which Heracles accidentally wounded his friend Chiron with a poisoned arrow — an injury that eventually led to Chiron's surrender of his immortality.

Heracles then tracked the boar to Mount Erymanthos. On the advice traditionally attributed to Chiron, he shouted at the boar to drive it out of its thicket and into deep snow, where it tired quickly. Heracles caught it in a net, hoisted it onto his shoulders, and carried it back to Mycenae.

When Heracles returned with the living boar, Eurystheus was so frightened that he hid inside a large bronze storage jar (a pithos) and ordered Heracles to dispose of the animal. In some versions, Eurystheus decreed that the results of future labours should be displayed outside the city gates. The boar's tusks were said to be preserved in the temple of Apollo at Cumae.

Mount Erymanthos

Mount Erymanthos (Greek: Ερύμανθος) is a mountain range in the southern part of Achaea and the north of Elis in the Peloponnese. The Erymanthos river rises on its slopes. The boar is one of several destructive beasts of the region subdued by Heracles in his labours in the Peloponnese, alongside the Nemean lion, the Lernaean Hydra, and the Stymphalian birds.

In art

The scene of Heracles presenting the boar to the cowering Eurystheus in his jar is a favourite of Greek vase painting and appears on numerous black-figure and red-figure vessels, as well as in later Roman and Renaissance art.
