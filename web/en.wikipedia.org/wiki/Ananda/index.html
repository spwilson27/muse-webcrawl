Ānanda was a first cousin of Gautama Buddha and one of his principal disciples. For the last twenty-five years of the Buddha's life he served as his personal attendant, and he is remembered in the Buddhist tradition for his extraordinary memory: at the First Buddhist Council after the Buddha's death, Ānanda recited the discourses (suttas) he had heard, and his recitation forms the basis of the Sutta Piṭaka.

Life

According to tradition, Ānanda was born on the same day as the Buddha and was the son of Amitodana, a brother of the Buddha's father Śuddhodana. He joined the order of monks in the second year of the Buddha's ministry and later agreed to serve as attendant on conditions including that he would never accept gifts intended for the Buddha and would be present whenever the Buddha gave teachings to laypeople.

Role in the tradition

Ānanda is credited with persuading the Buddha to allow women to ordain, leading to the founding of the order of nuns (bhikkhunī), with Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī as its first member. He attained arahantship shortly before the First Council, traditionally held at Rājagṛha, where his recitation began with the formula "Thus have I heard" (evaṃ me sutaṃ), which still opens the suttas.
