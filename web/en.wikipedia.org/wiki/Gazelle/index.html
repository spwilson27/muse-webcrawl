A gazelle is one of many antelope species in the genera Gazella, Eudorcas and Nanger. Gazelles are known as swift animals, capable of bursts of speed as high as 100 km/h (60 mph) and able to sustain speeds of around 50 km/h (30 mph). They are found mainly in the deserts, grasslands and savannas of Africa, with some species in southwestern and central Asia.

Description

Gazelles are small to medium-sized antelopes, typically 60–110 cm (24–43 in) at the shoulder and weighing roughly 12–75 kg depending on species. Most are tan or fawn above with white underparts and a dark stripe along the flank. Both sexes usually carry horns — the males' thicker and longer — which are ringed and lyre-shaped in many species.

Well-known species include Thomson's gazelle and Grant's gazelle of East Africa, the springbok (often grouped with gazelles), the dorcas gazelle, the goitered gazelle of Asia, and the critically endangered dama gazelle, the largest of the group.

Behaviour

Gazelles are herbivores, grazing grasses and browsing shrubs, and can go long periods without drinking, obtaining moisture from plants. They live in herds ranging from a handful of animals to hundreds. When alarmed, Thomson's gazelles perform "pronking" or "stotting" — bouncing stiff-legged into the air — which may signal fitness to predators such as cheetahs, lions and leopards.

Many gazelle species have declined through hunting and habitat loss; several, including the dama gazelle and the Arabian gazelle's relatives, are endangered, while the Saudi gazelle is extinct.

In culture

Gazelles feature prominently in Arabic and Persian poetry as symbols of grace and beauty, and the word "gazelle" itself derives (via French and Arabic ghazāl) from that tradition.
