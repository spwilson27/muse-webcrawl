Cytotoxic T cell

A cytotoxic T cell (also known as TC, cytotoxic T lymphocyte, CTL, T-killer cell, or killer T cell) is a T lymphocyte (a type of white blood cell) that kills cancer cells, cells infected with intracellular pathogens such as viruses, and other damaged or dysfunctional cells.

Function

Most cytotoxic T cells express T-cell receptors (TCRs) that recognize a specific antigen presented on class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, which are displayed by nearly all nucleated cells. Cytotoxic T cells also express the CD8 glycoprotein, which binds to a constant region of the MHC class I molecule; for this reason they are commonly called CD8+ T cells.

When the TCR of a cytotoxic T cell recognizes its cognate peptide–MHC class I complex on a target cell, the T cell kills the target by releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes, which induce apoptosis, or through the Fas–FasL pathway. A single cytotoxic T cell can kill multiple target cells in sequence.

Development and activation

Cytotoxic T cells develop in the thymus from hematopoietic stem cells, undergoing positive and negative selection. Mature naïve CD8+ T cells circulate through secondary lymphoid organs; upon encountering antigen presented by dendritic cells, together with co-stimulation and help from CD4+ T cells, they proliferate and differentiate into effector cells and long-lived memory cells. Memory cytotoxic T cells mount faster, stronger responses on re-encountering the same antigen and are a key goal of vaccination against intracellular pathogens.

Clinical significance

Cytotoxic T cells are central to control of viral infections and to anti-tumour immunity. Their activity is harnessed in cancer immunotherapy, including checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies such as CAR T cells, and their dysfunction or depletion contributes to chronic infection and disease progression.
