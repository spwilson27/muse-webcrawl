Optogenetics

Optogenetics is a biological technique to control the activity of neurons or other cell types with light. This is achieved by expression of light-sensitive ion channels, pumps or enzymes specifically in the target cells. On a neuronal level, it combines optical and genetic methods to control the activity of specific neurons in living tissue, even within freely moving animals, with millisecond precision.

History

The field rests on the discovery of microbial opsins such as bacteriorhodopsin (1971) and channelrhodopsin (2002–2003). In 2005, a group led by Karl Deisseroth, together with work by Georg Nagel, Ernst Bamberg and others, demonstrated that the algal channelrhodopsin-2 could be expressed in mammalian neurons to control their firing with light. The technique was named "Method of the Year" by Nature Methods in 2010.

Method

Genes encoding light-sensitive proteins — excitatory tools such as channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), inhibitory tools such as halorhodopsin (NpHR) and archaerhodopsin (Arch), and various red-shifted and step-function variants — are delivered to cells using viral vectors and expressed under cell-type-specific promoters or in transgenic lines. Light is delivered via implanted optical fibres, LEDs, or through the skull, enabling researchers to activate or silence defined circuits and observe the behavioural consequences.

Applications

Optogenetics has been used to map neural circuits underlying behaviour, reward, memory, sleep and psychiatric disease models, and to explore potential therapies, including early clinical work on restoring vision in retinal degenerative disease.
