The Shadow over Innsmouth is a horror novella by American author H. P. Lovecraft, written in November–December 1931 and first published in April 1936 as a small standalone book by the Visionary Publishing Company — the only one of Lovecraft's works published as a book in his lifetime in the United States. It was reprinted in anthologies after his death and is one of the best-known tales of the Cthulhu Mythos. Set in the decaying fictional Massachusetts seaport of Innsmouth, the novella concerns the narrator's discovery that the town's sinister, odd-looking inhabitants are the product of generations of interbreeding between humans and the Deep Ones, amphibious beings who worship the sea gods Dagon and Cthulhu.

Plot

The unnamed narrator, touring New England on an antiquarian and genealogical trip, takes a cheap bus from Newburyport to Arkham that detours through Innsmouth, a half-deserted fishing town shunned by its neighbours for the "Innsmouth look" of its people — bulging eyes, narrow heads and shuffling gaits. Exploring the rotting waterfront, he meets the alcoholic Zadok Allen, who, bribed with liquor, tells the town's history: in the 1840s sea captain Obed Marsh brought back from the South Seas the Esoteric Order of Dagon, whose rituals summoned the Deep Ones from Devil Reef offshore; in exchange for fish, gold and strange jewellery, the creatures demanded human sacrifices and mating with the townsfolk, whose offspring pass as human in youth but gradually transform and finally go down to the undersea city of Y'ha-nthlei to live forever.

Stranded overnight at the Gilman House hotel, the narrator survives a famous chase sequence as townsfolk attempt to force his door and he flees across rooftops and out of town along the abandoned railway. He alerts the federal authorities, who (in an epilogue) raid Innsmouth, dynamite Devil Reef and consign many inhabitants to camps. Reviewing his own family history — he is descended from a Marsh of Innsmouth — he recognises the changes beginning in himself; the novella ends with his ecstatic acceptance of his heritage and his plan to free a cousin from an asylum and descend with him to Y'ha-nthlei: "We shall swim out to that brooding reef in the sea and dive down through the black abysses to Cyclopean and many-columned Y'ha-nthlei, and in that lair of the Deep Ones we shall dwell amidst wonder and glory for ever."

Background and legacy

Lovecraft modelled Innsmouth partly on Newburyport, Massachusetts, which he had visited; the story draws on his reading of works such as Irvin S. Cobb's "Fishhead" and on his own anxieties about heredity and "degeneration," themes modern critics discuss in the context of his prejudices about race and miscegenation. The novella was rejected by Weird Tales during Lovecraft's lifetime and reached a wide audience only after his death. It has been adapted into film (including Dagon, 2001), radio drama, comics and numerous video games, and the Deep Ones and the "Innsmouth look" remain staples of Lovecraftian horror.
