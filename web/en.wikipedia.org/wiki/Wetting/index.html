Wetting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wetting is the ability of a liquid to maintain contact with a solid surface, resulting from intermolecular interactions when the two are brought together. The degree of wetting is determined by a balance between adhesive forces (liquid-solid attraction) and cohesive forces (liquid-liquid attraction), and is quantified by the contact angle a droplet makes with the surface.

Contact angle

On an ideal surface the equilibrium contact angle is given by Young's equation, relating the interfacial tensions of the solid-vapour, solid-liquid and liquid-vapour boundaries. A contact angle of less than 90° indicates favourable wetting — the liquid spreads; more than 90° indicates poor wetting — the liquid beads up. Surfaces are called hydrophilic if water wets them readily and hydrophobic if it does not; engineered "superhydrophobic" surfaces, inspired by the lotus effect, achieve angles above 150°.

Applications

Wetting behaviour matters in coating and painting, printing, soldering, oil recovery from rock, waterproofing textiles, and the design of self-cleaning surfaces. Surfactants (wetting agents) are commonly added to liquids to lower surface tension and improve spreading.
