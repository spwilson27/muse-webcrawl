Archimedean solid

An Archimedean solid is a highly symmetric, semi-regular convex polyhedron composed of two or more types of regular polygons meeting in identical vertices. There are thirteen Archimedean solids, plus two infinite families — the prisms and antiprisms — that satisfy a looser version of the definition. They are named after Archimedes, who is reported by Pappus of Alexandria to have enumerated them, though his original work is lost.

Definition and classification

Unlike the five Platonic solids, whose faces are all the same regular polygon, Archimedean solids combine two or more kinds of regular polygon, arranged identically around every vertex; they are therefore vertex-transitive but not face-transitive. Each solid can be described by a vertex configuration listing the polygons meeting at a vertex — for example, the cuboctahedron is 3.4.3.4, with triangles and squares alternating.

The thirteen solids are: the truncated tetrahedron, cuboctahedron, truncated cube, truncated octahedron, rhombicuboctahedron, truncated cuboctahedron, snub cube, icosidodecahedron, truncated dodecahedron, truncated icosahedron, rhombicosidodecahedron, truncated icosidodecahedron, and snub dodecahedron. Two of them, the snub cube and snub dodecahedron, are chiral and occur in mirror-image pairs. Most can be derived from the Platonic solids by truncation, expansion, or snubbing operations.

History

Knowledge of the solids was recovered in the Renaissance: artists and geometers such as Piero della Francesca, Luca Pacioli, Albrecht Dürer, and Wenzel Jamnitzer described and depicted several of them, and Johannes Kepler gave the first complete treatment and the names still used in his Harmonices Mundi (1619).

Applications

The truncated icosahedron is familiar as the pattern of the traditional football (soccer ball) and the shape of the buckminsterfullerene (C60) molecule. Archimedean solids also appear in architecture, crystallography, and the design of geodesic structures.
