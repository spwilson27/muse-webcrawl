Scapa Flow

Scapa Flow is a body of water in the Orkney Islands, Scotland, sheltered by the islands of Mainland, Graemsay, Burray, South Ronaldsay and Hoy. Its sheltered waters have played an important role in travel, trade and conflict throughout the centuries. Vikings anchored their longships in Scapa Flow more than a thousand years ago. It was the United Kingdom's chief naval base during the First and Second World Wars, but the facility was closed in 1956.

Since the 1970s the site has worked as an oil terminal supporting North Sea oil production, and it remains an important site for ship-to-ship transfers, diving and maritime recreation.

First World War and the scuttling of the German fleet

Scapa Flow was the main anchorage of the Royal Navy's Grand Fleet throughout the First World War, and the fleet sailed from it to the Battle of Jutland in 1916. Its defences were considered so inadequate early in the war that blockships were sunk in the eastern entrances and booms and minefields laid. After the Armistice, 74 ships of the German High Seas Fleet were interned at Scapa Flow pending a decision on their fate. On 21 June 1919, believing the peace talks had broken down and that the British intended to seize the fleet, Rear Admiral Ludwig von Reuter ordered the ships scuttled; 52 of the 74 vessels sank. Most were later salvaged for scrap in the 1920s and 1930s, but seven wrecks remain on the seabed and are now scheduled monuments and popular dive sites.

Second World War

At the start of the Second World War, Scapa Flow again became the base of the Home Fleet. On 14 October 1939, the German submarine U-47, commanded by Günther Prien, penetrated the defences through Kirk Sound and torpedoed the battleship HMS Royal Oak at anchor, killing 835 of her crew. In response, Winston Churchill ordered permanent barriers — the Churchill Barriers — built across the eastern entrances, largely by Italian prisoners of war; they now carry a road linking the islands. The Royal Oak wreck is a designated war grave, marked by a buoy, and diving on it is prohibited.

Post-war use

The naval base closed in 1956. From 1977, the Flotta oil terminal in Scapa Flow has handled oil from North Sea fields via pipeline. The flow is also used for ship-to-ship transfers of oil and liquefied gas. Scapa Flow is one of Britain's best-known wreck-diving locations, with the remaining German wrecks — the battleships König, Kronprinz Wilhelm and Markgraf and the light cruisers Karlsruhe, Brummel, Dresden and Cöln — attracting divers from around the world.
