Dioecy

Holly is dioecious: male and female flowers grow on separate plants, and only the females bear berries.

Dioecy is a characteristic of a species whose members have distinct male and female individuals — in plants, separate male (staminate) and female (pistillate) plants; in animals, the condition is more often called gonochorism. The word derives from Greek meaning "two households". A dioecious species contrasts with a monoecious species, whose individuals bear both male and female reproductive structures.

In plants

Roughly 6% of flowering plant species are dioecious. Familiar examples include holly, kiwifruit, asparagus, pistachio, willows, poplars and cannabis. Because only female plants produce fruit, growers of dioecious crops must plant male pollinators nearby — typically one male for every several females in kiwifruit orchards, for instance.

Evolution and ecology

Dioecy enforces outcrossing, preventing self-fertilisation and promoting genetic diversity, but it carries costs: a dioecious plant depends on a mate of the opposite sex and on pollinators or wind to bring them together. Dioecy has evolved independently in many plant lineages, most often from hermaphroditic ancestors through intermediate states such as gynodioecy. Various mechanisms of sex determination occur, including XY, ZW and environmental systems.
