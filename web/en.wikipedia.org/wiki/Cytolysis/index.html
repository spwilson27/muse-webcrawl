Cytolysis

Cytolysis, or osmotic lysis, occurs when a cell bursts due to an osmotic imbalance that has caused excess water to diffuse into the cell. Water continues to enter until the cell membrane can no longer contain the internal pressure, and the cell ruptures, releasing its contents.

Mechanism

Cytolysis is chiefly a risk for cells placed in a hypotonic environment, where the solute concentration outside is lower than inside. Animal cells, lacking walls, swell and may burst — as red blood cells do in pure water, a special case called haemolysis. Plant, fungal, and bacterial cells are protected by their cell walls: internal turgor pressure rises as water enters, but the wall resists expansion.

Defences

Single-celled freshwater organisms such as Paramecium use contractile vacuoles to expel excess water continuously. Cells can also regulate their internal solute content and membrane channels to resist osmotic swelling.

Cytolysis in immunity and medicine

The immune system deliberately causes cytolysis of target cells: cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells release perforin, which punches pores in the target membrane, and granzymes that trigger death; the complement system's membrane attack complex likewise lyses pathogens and infected cells. The term is also used in oncology and microbiology for the destruction of cells by viruses, drugs, or physical disruption in the laboratory.
