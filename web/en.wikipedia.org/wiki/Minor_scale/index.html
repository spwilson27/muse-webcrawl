Minor scale

In Western music theory, a minor scale is a diatonic scale whose third scale degree is a minor third above the tonic — the feature that gives minor-key music its characteristic quality as compared with the major scale. The term most often refers to the natural minor scale, which corresponds to the Aeolian mode and shares its key signature with its relative major (a major third above).

The three forms

  • Natural minor — pattern of whole and half steps: W–H–W–W–H–W–W. In A minor: A B C D E F G A. Degrees: 1, 2, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭6, ♭7.
  • Harmonic minor — the seventh degree is raised (♮7), creating a leading tone and an augmented second between ♭6 and ♮7. In A harmonic minor: A B C D E F G♯ A. This gives the dominant chord a major quality (E major / E7) for strong cadences.
  • Melodic minor — the sixth and seventh degrees are raised when ascending (A B C D E F♯ G♯ A) to smooth the melodic line, and in classical practice descend in natural form. In jazz, the raised form is used in both directions ("jazz melodic minor").

Harmony

Chords built on the natural minor scale are: i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. The minor tonic chord, the subdominant minor chord, and the relative major chord feature prominently in minor-key progressions, including the "Andalusian cadence" (i–VII–VI–V) of Spanish music.

Usage

Minor keys are used throughout the Western classical repertoire (e.g., Bach's Toccata and Fugue in D minor, Mozart's Requiem) and in folk, rock, metal and popular music, where the natural minor (Aeolian) scale underpins many riffs and solos. Debates persist about whether the perceived "sadness" of minor is intrinsic or culturally learned.
