Cooperative binding

Cooperative binding occurs in a molecule, usually a protein, that has more than one binding site for a ligand, when the binding of a ligand at one site increases (positive cooperativity) or decreases (negative cooperativity) the affinity of the remaining sites for the same ligand. The classic example is the binding of oxygen to haemoglobin: binding of the first oxygen molecule makes it easier for the second, third and fourth to bind.

Hill equation

Cooperativity is quantified by the Hill equation, which relates fractional saturation of binding sites to ligand concentration. The Hill coefficient nH is 1 for non-cooperative binding, greater than 1 for positive cooperativity and less than 1 for negative cooperativity. For haemoglobin nH is about 2.8–3.0, less than the actual number of sites (four) because cooperativity is not perfectly concerted. The Hill coefficient therefore gives a lower bound on the number of interacting sites.

Mechanisms

Two classical models explain cooperative binding. In the concerted (MWC) model of Monod, Wyman and Changeux, all subunits switch together between tense (T) and relaxed (R) conformations. In the sequential (KNF) model of Koshland, Némethy and Filmer, ligand binding induces conformational changes in individual subunits, which then influence their neighbours. In haemoglobin, oxygen binding pulls the iron atom into the plane of the haem ring, shifting the proximal histidine and rearranging subunit interfaces, destabilising the T state in favour of R.

Physiological significance

Cooperativity gives haemoglobin a sigmoid oxygen dissociation curve, allowing it to load oxygen efficiently in the lungs and release it steeply in respiring tissues where the partial pressure of oxygen is lower. It also underlies switch-like responses in many regulatory systems, making cooperative enzymes and receptors sensitive regulators of metabolism and gene expression.
