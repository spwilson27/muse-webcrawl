Mind–body dualism

FieldPhilosophy of mind
Key figuresRené Descartes, Gilbert Ryle, David Chalmers
Main claimMind and body are distinct kinds of substance or property

Mind–body dualism is the view in the philosophy of mind that mental phenomena are non-physical, or that the mind and body are distinct and separable. It is contrasted with physicalism and other forms of monism, which hold that the mind is ultimately physical. The position is most closely associated with René Descartes, who argued that the mind (res cogitans, a thinking thing) is a substance wholly distinct from the extended material body (res extensa).

History

Dualist ideas appear in ancient thought, notably in Plato's distinction between the soul and the body, and in various religious traditions that treat the soul as separable from and surviving the body. Descartes gave the view its classic modern formulation in the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), arguing that he could clearly conceive of mind existing without body, and therefore that the two are really distinct substances.

Descartes located the interaction between mind and body in the pineal gland, a proposal that satisfied few of his successors. The problem of how two wholly different substances could causally interact became the central objection to his system.

Varieties

Substance dualism holds that mind and body are different kinds of substance. Property dualism, by contrast, accepts that there is only one kind of substance, usually physical, but holds that mental properties are irreducible to physical properties. Epiphenomenalism is a form of property dualism in which mental states are caused by physical states but have no effects on the physical world.

Interactionist dualism, the view Descartes defended, maintains that mental and physical events causally influence each other in both directions.

Criticism

The interaction problem, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in correspondence with Descartes, asks how an immaterial mind could move a material body without violating physical principles such as the conservation of energy and momentum. Gilbert Ryle famously dismissed dualism in The Concept of Mind (1949) as the dogma of the 'ghost in the machine', and most contemporary philosophers of mind favour physicalist accounts, though dualism continues to be defended by philosophers such as David Chalmers in response to the hard problem of consciousness.
