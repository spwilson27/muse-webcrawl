The Symposium (Ancient Greek: Συμπόσιον, Sympósion) is a philosophical dialogue by Plato, written c. 385–370 BC. Framed as a narrated recollection of a drinking party held in 416 BC to celebrate the tragedian Agathon's victory at the Lenaea, it consists of a series of speeches in praise of Eros — love — followed by Socrates' account of the teachings of the priestess Diotima. It is among Plato's most celebrated and literary works and a founding text of Western philosophy of love.

The speeches

  • Phaedrus praises Eros as the oldest god and the spur to honour and self-sacrifice.
  • Pausanias distinguishes Common Aphrodite's love (of the body) from Heavenly Aphrodite's love (of the soul), defending the latter within Athenian custom.
  • Eryximachus, a physician, extends Eros to a cosmic principle of harmony in nature and medicine.
  • Aristophanes, the comic poet, tells the famous myth of the original spherical humans, split in two by Zeus, each forever seeking its lost other half.
  • Agathon delivers a flowery encomium of Eros as young, beautiful and the source of all virtues.
  • Socrates recounts Diotima's lesson: Eros is neither god nor mortal but a great daimōn, child of Resource and Poverty; love is desire for the good and for immortality, fulfilled through "giving birth in beauty" — ascending the ladder of love from one beautiful body, to all bodies, to souls, laws, knowledge, and finally to the Form of Beauty itself.
  • Alcibiades, arriving drunk, praises Socrates himself — comparing him to a Silenus figure, ugly outside and divine within — and confesses his own failed attempt to seduce him.

Themes and influence

The dialogue develops Plato's theory of Forms, the idea later called "Platonic love", and a portrait of Socrates as the embodiment of philosophical eros. It has influenced philosophy, literature and art from Plotinus and Ficino's Renaissance commentary to modern writers, and remains a touchstone for discussions of desire, beauty and same-sex love in classical Athens.
