Common raven

Common raven
Corvus corax
Family: Corvidae
Order: Passeriformes
Conservation status: Least Concern

The common raven (Corvus corax), also known as the northern raven, is a large all-black passerine bird in the crow family, Corvidae. It is the most widely distributed of all corvids, found across the Northern Hemisphere in a wide range of habitats. Among the largest passerines, it typically measures 54–67 cm (21–26 in) in length with a wingspan of 115–150 cm (45–59 in), and can weigh up to about 1.6 kg. Its plumage is iridescent black, the bill is large and heavy, and the tail is wedge-shaped in flight — a key distinction from crows.

Behaviour and intelligence

Ravens are renowned for their intelligence. They solve novel problems, use tools on occasion, engage in play (including sliding down snowbanks and aerial acrobatics), and can imitate sounds, including human speech. They cache food, remember cache locations, and have been observed leading predators such as wolves to carcasses they cannot open themselves, then scavenging the remains. Ravens mate for life and are largely solitary or live in pairs, although juveniles may gather in flocks.

Diet and habitat

An opportunistic omnivore, the common raven feeds on carrion, insects, small animals, eggs, grains, berries, and human refuse. It inhabits wilderness and rural areas — mountains, deserts, tundra, sea cliffs, and forests — but adapts readily to human settlement where persecution is low. The nest, a bulky structure of sticks lined with finer material, is built on cliffs, trees, or human structures.

In culture

Ravens figure prominently in mythology and folklore: as tricksters in Indigenous traditions of the Pacific Northwest, as the companions of Odin (Huginn and Muninn) in Norse myth, and as omens in many cultures. The ravens of the Tower of London are a famous tradition, and the bird inspired Edgar Allan Poe's poem "The Raven" (1845).
