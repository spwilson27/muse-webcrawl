Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain is a drawing instruction book by American art teacher Betty Edwards, first published in 1979. One of the best-selling art instruction books ever written, it argues that drawing skill depends primarily on learning to see — to perceive edges, spaces, relationships and light as they are — rather than on innate manual talent, and it presents a course of exercises designed to shift the reader from symbolic, verbal thinking to visual perception.

Premise

Edwards drew on the split-brain research of Roger Sperry and the popular left-brain/right-brain distinction: the verbal, analytic "L-mode" was said to interfere with drawing by substituting learned symbols (a schematic eye, a lollipop tree) for actual observation, while the visual, spatial "R-mode" perceives what is really there. Exercises such as copying an upside-down image, drawing negative spaces, and "pure contour" drawing without looking at the page are designed to quiet the symbolic mode and make the perceptual shift. Later editions softened the neuroscience framing, treating L- and R-mode more as metaphors for two ways of thinking.

Contents and method

The course builds five component skills of drawing: perception of edges, of negative spaces, of relationships and proportions (sighting), of light and shadow, and of the gestalt or whole. Students draw a self-portrait before and after the course as evidence of rapid improvement, and the book supplies a drawing "toolkit" including viewfinders and picture planes.

Reception

The book has been translated into many languages and sold millions of copies; its exercises are widely used in art classes. Neuroscientists have criticised its hemispheric framing as oversimplified — lateralisation is real but far less tidy than the pop version — while many teachers defend the practical pedagogy, noting the exercises improve observational drawing whatever one's theory of the brain.
