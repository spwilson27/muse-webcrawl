Eight-to-fourteen modulation

TypeRun-length limited line code
Used inCompact Disc (CD), MiniDisc
Developed byPhilips / Kees Schouhamer Immink
Mapping8 data bits → 14 channel bits (+3 merging bits)

Eight-to-fourteen modulation (EFM) is the encoding technique used by the Compact Disc and MiniDisc formats to convert 8-bit data bytes into 14-bit channel patterns suitable for recording as pits and lands on an optical disc.

How it works

EFM was developed by Kees Schouhamer Immink at Philips. Each 8-bit byte is mapped, via a lookup table, to a 14-bit code word chosen so that between consecutive "1" bits there are at least two and at most ten "0" bits — a (2,10) run-length-limited constraint. Three merging bits are inserted between code words to preserve the run-length limits and keep the running digital sum low.

Purpose

The constraints serve several purposes: they ensure the shortest pit is long enough for the laser pickup to resolve reliably, they limit the longest pit so the decoder's clock can stay synchronised, and they keep the signal's low-frequency content small so the servo systems that focus and track the beam are not disturbed. EFM expands data by a factor of 17/8, a cost accepted for reliable optical readout.

Successor

DVDs use a variant called EFMPlus, which encodes 8 bits into 16 channel bits without merging bits. EFM remains fundamental to CD audio and CD-ROM technology.
