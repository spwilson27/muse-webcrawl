Hyperphagia

Hyperphagia
DefinitionAbnormally increased appetite and food intake
SpecialtyEndocrinology, psychiatry, neurology
Associated conditionsPrader–Willi syndrome, hypothalamic injury, hyperthyroidism, diabetes

Appetite is regulated by hypothalamic circuits responding to leptin, ghrelin and other signals.

Hyperphagia, also known as polyphagia, is an abnormally strong, excessive sensation of hunger and increased appetite that drives abnormally large food intake. It differs from ordinary hunger in that it persists after eating and is not relieved by normal satiety. The word comes from the Greek hyper- (excessive) and phagein (to eat).

Causes

Hyperphagia arises when the brain's appetite-regulating circuits — centred on the hypothalamus and the hormones leptin, insulin and ghrelin — are disrupted. It is a hallmark of Prader–Willi syndrome, a genetic disorder in which insatiable hunger from early childhood leads to severe obesity unless food access is strictly controlled. Other causes include damage to the hypothalamus from tumours (such as craniopharyngioma), surgery or trauma; endocrine disorders including uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (where it forms one of the classic "3 Ps" with polydipsia and polyuria), hyperthyroidism and Cushing syndrome; and certain medicines, notably atypical antipsychotics, corticosteroids and some antidepressants. It can also occur in Kleine–Levin syndrome and in binge-eating disorder.

Mechanisms

Satiety signalling depends heavily on the melanocortin pathway: leptin from adipose tissue activates POMC neurons in the arcuate nucleus, whose product α-MSH suppresses appetite via MC4R receptors. Genetic defects anywhere in this pathway (leptin deficiency, leptin receptor or MC4R mutations) cause severe early-onset hyperphagia and obesity. The first targeted treatment, setmelanotide, an MC4R agonist, is approved for several of these rare genetic forms.

Management

Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible, together with environmental control of food access, structured meals, and behavioural support; in Prader–Willi syndrome, lifelong supervision of diet is usually necessary to prevent life-threatening obesity and gastric complications.
