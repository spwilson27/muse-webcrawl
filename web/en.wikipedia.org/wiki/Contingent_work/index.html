Contingent work

Contingent work
TypesTemporary agency work, freelancing, contract work, part-time work, gig work
FieldLabour economics, employment law

Contingent work, also called casual work, is an employment relationship with limited job security, payment on a piece-work basis, typically part-time (typically with variable hours) that is not considered part of a permanent job. Contingent workers are employees of a firm in a broad sense but are distinct from regular full-time staff: they include temporary agency workers, on-call workers, contract company workers, independent contractors, freelancers, and day labourers. The phrase has been criticized by labour advocates as a euphemism for precarious employment.

History

Casual labour has always existed, but the modern contingent workforce expanded from the 1970s onward as firms sought flexibility and lower labour costs, aided by staffing agencies such as Manpower. In the United States the Bureau of Labor Statistics has periodically measured contingent and alternative work arrangements (in 1995, 2005 and 2017), finding roughly 10 percent of workers in alternative arrangements. The 2010s saw the growth of platform-mediated "gig work" (ride-hailing, food delivery, task platforms), which reignited debate over classification.

Advantages and disadvantages

For employers, contingent work offers flexibility to scale labour with demand and reduced obligations for benefits. For workers, it may offer autonomy and flexible schedules, but typically involves lower and more volatile pay, fewer benefits (health insurance, pensions, paid leave), less legal protection against dismissal, and limited access to collective bargaining. Legal disputes over whether workers are employees or independent contractors — e.g. California's AB5 law and Proposition 22, and the UK Supreme Court's 2021 Uber ruling — have been central to policy debates.
