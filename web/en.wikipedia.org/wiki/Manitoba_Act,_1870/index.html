The Manitoba Act, 1870 (French: Loi de 1870 sur le Manitoba) is an act of the Parliament of Canada that created the province of Manitoba. It grew out of negotiations between the Canadian government and the provisional government of the Red River Colony led by Louis Riel, following the Red River Resistance of 1869–70, which erupted when Canada acquired Rupert's Land from the Hudson's Bay Company without consulting its largely Métis inhabitants.

Terms

The Act admitted Manitoba as Canada's fifth province, though as a small "postage stamp" province of about 36,000 square miles around the Red River Settlement. It guaranteed the rights of existing landholders, set aside 1.4 million acres for the children of Métis families, made the province officially bilingual in its legislature and courts (sections 22 and 23), and protected denominational (Catholic and Protestant) school rights. Unlike the original provinces, Manitoba did not receive control of its natural resources, which Ottawa retained until 1930.

Legacy and litigation

The Métis land provisions were implemented slowly and controversially; many Métis families lost their grants to speculators and moved west to Saskatchewan. Manitoba abolished official French-language status in 1890 and banned public funding of Catholic schools, triggering the Manitoba Schools Question. In Reference re Manitoba Language Rights (1985), the Supreme Court of Canada held that section 23's bilingualism requirement remained binding and that nearly a century of English-only statutes were invalid — though they were given temporary effect while translation proceeded. The Manitoba Act is listed in the schedule to the Constitution Act, 1982 as part of the Constitution of Canada.
