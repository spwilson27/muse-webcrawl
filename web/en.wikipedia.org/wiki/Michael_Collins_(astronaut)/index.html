Michael Collins (astronaut)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Michael Collins (astronaut)
Born31 October 1930, Rome, Italy
Died28 April 2021 (aged 90)
MissionsGemini 10, Apollo 11
Collins before Apollo 11.

Michael Collins (1930–2021) was an American astronaut who flew the command module Columbia alone in lunar orbit during Apollo 11 while Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed below — the role that made him, in the press phrase, 'the loneliest man in history', a label he cheerfully rejected. His memoir Carrying the Fire (1974) is widely considered the finest book written by an astronaut.

Collins had earlier flown Gemini 10 (1966), performing two spacewalks and the first rendezvous with two separate target vehicles. As Apollo 11's CMP he was responsible for 18 contingency plans to rescue or rendezvous with a stricken lunar module; his anxiety during Eagle's descent, and his methodical search for it by telescope, are among the mission's quiet dramas. After splashdown he declined further flights — including an offered Apollo 17 command rotation path — and became director of the National Air and Space Museum, overseeing its creation.

Voice

Collins's writing gave the astronaut corps its literary standard: self-deprecating, precise, and funny about fear without denying it. His reflection that he felt 'very much a part of what was taking place on the lunar surface' — not lonely but alert, in a ship he trusted — is the corrective to the myth, in his own words.
