Environmental, social, and governance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) is a set of considerations, standards, and screening criteria used by investors, companies, and regulators to evaluate an organization's behavior and long-term risk in three domains: how it affects the natural environment, how it manages relationships with employees, suppliers, customers, and communities, and how it is governed. ESG grew out of earlier traditions of socially responsible investing and corporate social responsibility.

The three pillars of ESG.

The three pillars

Environmental

The environmental pillar concerns a company's impact on the planet: greenhouse gas emissions and climate strategy, energy and water use, waste management, pollution, resource depletion, and effects on biodiversity. Investors use these measures to judge exposure to climate-related financial risk and to environmental regulation.

Social

The social pillar covers how a company manages relationships with people: labor standards and workplace safety, diversity and inclusion, human rights in the supply chain, data protection and privacy, product safety, and community relations.

Governance

Governance concerns how a company is directed and controlled: board structure and independence, executive compensation, shareholder rights, audit practices, anti-corruption controls, tax transparency, and business ethics.

History

The term "ESG" was popularized by the 2004 United Nations Global Compact report Who Cares Wins, which argued that embedding environmental, social, and governance factors into capital markets makes good business sense. In 2006 the UN-backed Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI) were launched, and signatories — asset owners and managers committing to incorporate ESG factors into investment decisions — grew to represent tens of trillions of dollars in assets. Commercial rating agencies such as MSCI, Sustainalytics, and S&P Global developed ESG scores for companies, while reporting frameworks including the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), the Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB), and the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) sought to standardize disclosure. In the 2020s the European Union introduced binding rules, notably the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, while the International Sustainability Standards Board issued global baseline standards.

Criticism and debate

ESG has attracted substantial criticism from several directions. Financial critics argue that ESG scores from different agencies correlate only weakly, that ESG funds do not reliably outperform conventional funds, and that some products amount to "greenwashing". Political critics, particularly in the United States, have attacked ESG as an improper intrusion of political goals into investing, and several US states have adopted rules restricting ESG considerations by public pension funds. Defenders respond that ESG is fundamentally about managing long-term financial risk, and that better disclosure benefits markets regardless of political framing.
