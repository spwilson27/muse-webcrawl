Juba dance

The Juba dance, or pattin' Juba, is an African-American dance that originated among enslaved people on plantations in the southern United States. It involves stomping, slapping, and patting the body — arms, legs, chest, and cheeks — to create complex rhythms, typically without instruments.

Origins

The dance derives from West and Central African traditions, including the Kongo giouba and the ring shout. Because drums were banned on many plantations after slave revolts such as the Stono Rebellion, enslaved people developed body percussion — "patting Juba" — as a substitute accompaniment for dance and song.

Characteristics

Dancers perform in a circle, often with a soloist in the center performing improvisational steps while the group keeps rhythm and responds to calls. Rhyming verses, such as "Juba this and Juba that," accompany the dance. The form contributed to later African-American styles including tap dance, the cakewalk, and hambone.

Minstrelsy and legacy

In the 1840s the dancer William Henry Lane, known as "Master Juba," popularized the style in minstrel shows and is often credited as a founder of tap dance. Charles Dickens described a Juba performance in his American Notes (1842). The dance remains an important ancestor of African-American percussive dance traditions.
