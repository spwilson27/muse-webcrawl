Contact lens
UsesCorrection of refractive errors; cosmetic; therapeutic
MaterialsSilicone hydrogel, hydrogel, rigid gas-permeable plastics
TypesSoft, rigid gas-permeable, scleral, hybrid, cosmetic
Wearing schedulesDaily disposable, fortnightly, monthly, extended wear

Contact lenses are thin lenses placed directly on the surface of the eyes. They are worn to correct vision, for cosmetic effect, or for therapeutic reasons. An estimated 150 million people worldwide use contact lenses, primarily for vision correction of refractive errors such as myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and presbyopia.

History

Leonardo da Vinci sketched the concept in 1508, and René Descartes proposed a corneal lens in 1636. The first wearable glass scleral lenses were produced in the late 1880s by Adolf Eugen Fick and others. Plastic polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) lenses followed in the 1930s–40s; Otto Wichterle developed soft hydrogel lenses in the 1960s, commercialised in 1971, and silicone hydrogel materials from the late 1990s greatly improved oxygen transmission to the cornea.

Types

Soft lenses, made of hydrogel or silicone hydrogel, are the most widely used and are supplied as daily disposables or reusable lenses replaced fortnightly or monthly. Rigid gas-permeable lenses provide crisper optics and durability. Scleral lenses vault over the whole cornea and are used for irregular corneas such as keratoconus. Toric designs correct astigmatism, multifocal designs address presbyopia, and coloured or costume lenses change the appearance of the eye.

Care and risks

Reusable lenses require cleaning and disinfection with multipurpose solutions and regular case replacement. Poor hygiene and overnight wear increase the risk of microbial keratitis, a potentially sight-threatening corneal infection, as well as giant papillary conjunctivitis and corneal hypoxia. Lenses are regulated as medical devices in many countries and require a valid fitting prescription.
