Nonfinite verb

A nonfinite verb, also called a verbal, is a verb form that does not show tense, aspect, person, or number in the way a finite verb does, and therefore cannot typically serve as the sole predicate of an independent clause. In English, the nonfinite forms are the infinitive, the gerund (the -ing form used nominally), and the participles.

English examples

In the sentence "I want to leave," the infinitive "to leave" is nonfinite; in "Swimming is healthy," the gerund "swimming" functions as a noun; and in "the broken window," the past participle "broken" functions as an adjective. Nonfinite clauses serve as complements, adverbial modifiers, and reduced relative clauses: "The man standing by the door is my brother."

In other languages

Many languages make richer use of nonfinite forms than English. Latin employs infinitives, participles, gerunds, and gerundives extensively. Agglutinative languages such as Turkish and Japanese use converbs — nonfinite forms that chain clauses together. In some languages the finite/nonfinite distinction is realized morphologically on every verb, and the distribution of subjects (for example, nominative versus genitive case with infinitives) differs between finite and nonfinite clauses.
