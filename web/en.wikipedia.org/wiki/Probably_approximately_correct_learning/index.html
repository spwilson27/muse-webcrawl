Probably approximately correct learning

In computational learning theory, probably approximately correct (PAC) learning is a mathematical framework for analysing machine learning algorithms. It was proposed by Leslie Valiant in a landmark 1984 paper and underpins much of the theoretical study of supervised learning.

Definition

A concept class C is said to be PAC learnable if there exists an algorithm that, for any target concept in C, any distribution over examples, and any parameters ε, δ > 0, outputs with probability at least 1 − δ a hypothesis whose error is at most ε, in time polynomial in 1/ε, 1/δ and the size of the examples:

Pr[ error(h) ≤ ε ] ≥ 1 − δ

The name reflects the two tolerances: the learned hypothesis is probably (with confidence 1 − δ) approximately correct (within error ε).

Sample complexity

For a finite hypothesis class, the number of training examples needed to guarantee PAC learning grows roughly as (log |H| + log(1/δ)) / ε. For infinite hypothesis classes, learnability is characterized by the VC dimension: a concept class is PAC learnable if and only if its VC dimension is finite (the fundamental theorem of statistical learning).

Extensions

The basic model assumes examples are drawn from a fixed distribution and labelled exactly by the target concept. Variants relax these assumptions: agnostic PAC learning allows arbitrary labelling functions and asks the learner to compete with the best hypothesis in the class; other extensions handle noisy labels, quantum examples, and online settings. Valiant received the Turing Award in part for introducing the framework.
