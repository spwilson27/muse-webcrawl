Town meeting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Town meeting is a form of direct democratic rule practised in towns of New England in the United States, in which the community's voters meet annually (or on special call) to elect officers, raise taxes and enact laws. Two forms exist: the traditional open town meeting, where all registered voters may speak and vote, and the representative town meeting (used by larger Massachusetts towns), where voters elect representatives to the meeting. The moderator presides; the warrant (agenda) is published in advance; voters act as the town's legislature.

Dating from colonial Massachusetts and Vermont, town meetings were idealised by Tocqueville and by later civic theory as schools of self-government. Attendance has declined with town growth, commuting and the shift of decisions to Australian-ballot referendums, and political science research finds meetings skew toward older, homeowning, well-informed participants — yet in hundreds of towns the meeting still sets tax rates and budgets directly, a surviving instance of assembly democracy (comparable to Switzerland's Landsgemeinde) in an age of representation.
