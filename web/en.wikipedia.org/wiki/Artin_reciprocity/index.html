Artin reciprocity is the central theorem of class field theory, proved by Emil Artin in 1927. It describes the abelian extensions of a number field in terms of the arithmetic of the field itself, by means of a homomorphism—now called the Artin map or Artin symbol—from the group of fractional ideals (coprime to a modulus) onto the Galois group of any finite abelian extension. In doing so it unifies and generalises the classical reciprocity laws of Gauss and others.

Background

The law of quadratic reciprocity, proved by Gauss, relates the solvability of the congruences x2 ≡ p (mod q) and x2 ≡ q (mod p) for odd primes. Nineteenth-century mathematicians sought higher analogues—cubic, biquadratic, and general power reciprocity laws—and David Hilbert recast the problem in terms of splitting of primes in field extensions. Teiji Takagi's existence theorem (1920) established that abelian extensions correspond to generalised ideal class groups, but an explicit description of the correspondence was missing.

The Artin map

For a finite abelian extension L/K of number fields and a prime ideal 𝔭 of K unramified in L, there is a unique element of the Galois group, the Frobenius element, describing how automorphisms act on roots of unity–like data modulo primes above 𝔭. Extending multiplicatively over ideals defines the Artin map. Artin's reciprocity law states that this map induces an isomorphism between a generalised ideal class group of K (the ray class group modulo the conductor of the extension) and the Galois group of L/K, and that the kernel is precisely the image of the norm group from L. Notably, the map depends only on the ideal class, even though its definition through Frobenius elements appears to depend on choices.

Consequences

Artin reciprocity implies the classical reciprocity laws: quadratic reciprocity emerges from the case of quadratic extensions of ℚ, and cyclotomic extensions yield the rational reciprocity laws. It also explains the splitting behaviour of primes in abelian extensions entirely in terms of congruences, fulfilling Hilbert's vision. Reinterpreted through ideles by Chevalley and others, it became the model for the Langlands program's reciprocity conjectures, which extend the picture to non-abelian extensions.
