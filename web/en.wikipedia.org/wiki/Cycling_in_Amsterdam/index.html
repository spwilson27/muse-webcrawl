Cycling in Amsterdam

Cycling in Amsterdam is a defining feature of transport and daily life in the Dutch capital. Amsterdam is frequently ranked among the world's most bicycle-friendly cities: roughly a third to a half of all trips in the city are made by bicycle, and there are more bicycles than residents — an estimated 800,000-plus bikes for a population of about 900,000.

History

Cycling was widespread in the Netherlands from the early 20th century, but car-oriented planning in the 1950s and 1960s saw road space reallocated to motor traffic and cycling decline. In the early 1970s, protest movements — notably Stop de Kindermoord ("Stop the Child Murder"), formed after a rise in traffic deaths of children — pushed policy back toward cycling. From that point the city built a continuous network of separated cycle paths, and cycling rebounded strongly.

Infrastructure

Amsterdam has hundreds of kilometres of cycle paths (fietspaden), usually distinguished by red asphalt, with their own traffic lights, bridges, and tunnels. Bicycle parking is a major planning challenge: the city has built large multi-storey and underwater parking garages at stations, including a facility under the IJ harbour at Centraal Station holding thousands of bikes. Abandoned and wrongly parked bicycles are regularly removed by the municipality.

Culture

The typical Amsterdam bicycle is a sturdy upright roadster (oma fiets, "granny bike"), ridden in ordinary clothes for trips of all kinds — commuting, school runs, shopping, and carrying children in cargo bikes (bakfietsen). The OV-fiets rental scheme and numerous private rental shops serve visitors and rail passengers. Bicycle theft is common, and heavy chains and second-hand markets are facts of local life.

Challenges

Congestion on cycle paths from e-bikes, fat bikes, mopeds, and tourists, and the growing speed differences among users, are current policy concerns. The city continues to invest in widening paths and separating fast from slow traffic.
