Oxpecker

Oxpeckers
Genus: Buphagus
Family: Buphagidae
Species: 2 (red-billed, yellow-billed)
Range: Sub-Saharan Africa

The oxpeckers are two species of passerine birds of the genus Buphagus, family Buphagidae, endemic to the savannas of sub-Saharan Africa: the red-billed oxpecker (Buphagus erythrorynchus) and the yellow-billed oxpecker (Buphagus africanus). They are famous for riding on large mammals — buffalo, rhinoceros, giraffe, antelope and domestic cattle — and feeding on the ticks, botfly larvae and other parasites they find there.

Feeding ecology

Oxpeckers eat tens of thousands of arthropods a day using laterally flattened bills to comb through hair and scissor ticks from skin. Their relationship with hosts was long portrayed as a textbook mutualism, but research shows a more mixed picture: oxpeckers also feed on blood and tissue from open wounds, keeping them open and possibly slowing healing, which makes the relationship partly parasitic. Hosts nonetheless benefit from tick removal and from the birds' alarm calls when predators approach — rhinos with oxpeckers detect humans sooner, and the Swahili name for the bird, askari wa kifaru, means "the rhino's guard".

Biology

Oxpeckers have stiff tails used as props, strong feet for clinging to hosts, and brown plumage with pale rumps. They nest in holes, often lined with hair plucked from hosts, and roost on their hosts at night. Declines in large mammal populations and the use of cattle dips have reduced oxpecker numbers in parts of their range.
