Helicobacter pylori, previously named Campylobacter pylori, is a gram-negative, microaerophilic, spiral (helical) bacterium usually found in the stomach. Its helical shape is thought to have evolved in order to penetrate the mucoid lining of the stomach and thereby establish infection. The bacterium was first identified in 1982 by the Australian doctors Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, who found that it was present in patients with chronic gastritis and gastric ulcers — conditions previously attributed mainly to stress and diet rather than infection.

More than half of the world's population has H. pylori in their upper gastrointestinal tract, with infection far more common in developing countries. Most infected people never develop symptoms, but the infection is the primary identified cause of gastritis, peptic ulcers, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, and it is a major risk factor for gastric cancer; the IARC classifies H. pylori as a Group 1 carcinogen.

Survival in the stomach

H. pylori survives the stomach's acid using the enzyme urease, which converts urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide, neutralising acid in its immediate surroundings — the basis of the urea breath test used in diagnosis. Its flagella let it swim through mucus, and adhesins such as BabA attach it to stomach epithelial cells. Virulence factors including the cagA gene product, injected into host cells by a type IV secretion system, and the vacuolating toxin VacA, disrupt cell signalling and increase cancer risk.

History

Marshall and Warren published their findings in 1983–84 to considerable scepticism. In 1984 Marshall famously drank a culture of the bacterium, developed gastritis, and cured himself with antibiotics — a self-experiment that helped shift medical opinion. The pair received the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discovery of the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease". In 1994, US health authorities formally recommended antibiotic treatment for ulcer patients with the infection, transforming ulcer care.

Diagnosis and treatment

Non-invasive tests include the urea breath test, stool antigen testing, and serology; invasive diagnosis uses biopsy during endoscopy with histology or rapid urease testing. Standard eradication therapy combines a proton-pump inhibitor with two antibiotics (commonly clarithromycin and amoxicillin or metronidazole) for 10–14 days, though rising clarithromycin resistance has increased use of bismuth quadruple therapy.
