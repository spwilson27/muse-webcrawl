Free climbing

Free climbing is a form of rock climbing in which the climber uses only their body — hands, feet and friction — to make progress on the rock, using ropes, harnesses and protection equipment solely as a safety backup in case of a fall. It is distinguished from aid climbing, in which equipment is pulled or stood on to ascend, and from free solo climbing, in which no rope or protection is used at all.

Terminology

The term is often confused by the public with free soloing; in fact almost all roped rock climbing is free climbing. If a climber falls or rests on the rope ("hangdogging"), a redpoint ascent requires completing the climb without weighting the equipment. Styles of ascent include onsight, flash and redpoint.

Equipment and protection

Free climbers place removable protection such as nuts and camming devices (traditional or "trad" climbing), clip pre-placed bolts (sport climbing), or climb without a rope above crash pads (bouldering). A belayer manages the rope to arrest any fall.

History

Free climbing ethics developed in the early 20th century in Saxony and the Dolomites, and were advanced after the Second World War in Yosemite Valley, where climbers such as Royal Robbins championed clean climbing. In the 1970s and 1980s climbers like John Bachar and Lynn Hill pushed standards; Hill's 1993 free ascent of The Nose on El Capitan was a landmark. The free ascent of the Dawn Wall by Tommy Caldwell and Kevin Jorgeson in 2015 brought the discipline worldwide attention.
