Engines of Creation

AuthorK. Eric Drexler
Published1986
PublisherAnchor Press/Doubleday
SubjectMolecular nanotechnology

Engines of Creation: The Coming Era of Nanotechnology is a 1986 book by K. Eric Drexler that popularized the concept of molecular nanotechnology. It was the first book on the subject and remains a landmark in the public discussion of nanotechnology, exploring the potential of machines that manipulate matter atom by atom.

Contents

Drexler argues that the ability to arrange atoms in nearly any structure consistent with physical law — using molecular assemblers that position reactive molecules with atomic precision — would amount to a general technology for manufacturing. He traces the "engines of construction" already present in biology, such as ribosomes building proteins, and proposes artificial molecular machines that could build materials and devices far beyond current capabilities.

The book explores anticipated applications, including cheap and efficient manufacturing, advanced medicine with cell-repair machines, abundant solar energy, and greatly extended human lifespan. It also addresses dangers: Drexler coined the "grey goo" scenario, in which self-replicating molecular machines run amok and overwhelm the biosphere, and he discussed arms races, the need for safeguards, and the institutional challenge of guiding a powerful technology.

Influence and reception

Engines of Creation founded the popular nanotechnology movement and helped inspire the founding of the Foresight Institute in 1986, the same year as publication. The book influenced science fiction writers and technologists and shaped public expectations of nanotechnology for decades. Drexler later developed the technical case in his doctoral work published as Nanosystems (1992). His mechanistic vision was debated within the scientific community, most publicly in an exchange with Nobel laureate Richard Smalley, who argued that chemical constraints ("fat fingers" and "sticky fingers" problems) would prevent assemblers of the kind Drexler described; mainstream "nanotechnology" funding meanwhile came to focus on materials science and nanoscale phenomena rather than molecular manufacturing.

Sequel

In 2007 Drexler, with co-author Chris Peterson, released Engines of Creation 2.0, an updated and annotated free electronic edition. Drexler's 2013 book Radical Abundance revisited and reframed the argument around atomically precise manufacturing.
