Leap week calendar

A leap week calendar is a calendar system in which each year has a whole number of weeks — normally 52, with an extra seventh-day week (a leap week) added in some years instead of the leap day used by the Gregorian calendar. Because every year begins on the same day of the week, such calendars can be perennial: the same calendar layout repeats exactly each year, with the leap week absorbed into a designated month or appended at year's end.

Properties

A common year has 364 days (52 × 7), one or two days shorter than the Gregorian year, so leap weeks must be inserted on average about every 5.6 years — 71 leap weeks in a 400-year cycle of 146,097 days matches the Gregorian mean year of 365.2425 days exactly. Leap week designs differ in their leap rule: some use a fixed arithmetic cycle, others tie the leap week to astronomical events such as the solstice.

Examples

Historical and proposed leap week calendars include the Pax Calendar (invented by James Colligan in 1930), the Symmetry454 calendar and the Hanke–Henry Permanent Calendar proposed by Richard Henry and Steve Hanke, both of which add a leap week ("Xtra" / "Newton") at the end of December in leap years. The ISO week date system is a leap week calendar in effect, though it is used as a week-numbering system alongside the Gregorian calendar rather than as a complete replacement. Critics note that a leap week shifts dates by up to a week relative to the seasons within the cycle and complicates birthdays and anniversaries that fall in the leap week.
