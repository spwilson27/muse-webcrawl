A katana (刀 or かたな) is a Japanese sword characterised by a curved, single-edged blade with a circular or squared guard (tsuba) and a long grip to accommodate two hands. It became the signature weapon of the samurai, worn edge-up (uchigatana style) thrust through the belt, typically paired with a shorter wakizashi as the daishō set.

History

The katana developed from earlier tachi swords during the Muromachi period, as fighting on foot and quick-drawing techniques favoured a shorter blade worn edge-up for rapid deployment. In the relatively peaceful Edo period the katana became as much a symbol of samurai status — only samurai were permitted to wear the daishō — as a battlefield weapon. The Meiji government's Haitōrei edict of 1876 banned the public wearing of swords.

Forging

Traditional katana are forged from tamahagane steel, folded repeatedly to remove impurities and distribute carbon, and differentially hardened by coating the blade with clay before quenching: the exposed edge hardens into martensite while the body stays softer and more shock-resistant. This produces the visible temper line, the hamon, along the edge. Finished blades are polished and fitted with mountings (koshirae).

Cultural significance

The katana is one of the most recognisable weapons in the world and a central symbol in Japanese martial arts such as iaidō and kendō. Antique blades by masters such as Masamune are designated national treasures in Japan, and new traditionally made swords (shinsakutō) remain tightly regulated.
