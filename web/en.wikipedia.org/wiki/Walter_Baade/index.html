Wilhelm Heinrich Walter Baade (March 24, 1893 – June 25, 1960) was a German astronomer who worked in the United States from 1931 to 1958. He is best known for defining two distinct stellar populations and for discovering that the universe was roughly twice as large, and twice as old, as previously calculated.

Stellar populations

Working at Mount Wilson Observatory during the blackouts of the Second World War — as a German national he was exempt from war work, and Los Angeles' wartime blackout gave unusually dark skies — Baade used the 100-inch Hooker telescope to resolve stars in the central region of the Andromeda Galaxy for the first time. In 1944 he announced the division of stars into Population I (young, metal-rich, in spiral arms) and Population II (old, metal-poor, in globular clusters and galactic bulges), a framework that transformed the study of stellar evolution and galactic structure.

Distance scale revision

At the 1952 meeting of the International Astronomical Union in Rome, Baade announced that Edwin Hubble's distance scale was wrong by a factor of about two, because Hubble had conflated two types of Cepheid variable stars with different period–luminosity relations. The correction doubled the size and age of the universe, easing the embarrassment that the Earth had appeared older than the cosmos itself.

Other work

With Fritz Zwicky, Baade coined the term "supernova" and proposed that such explosions leave behind neutron stars. He also discovered 10 asteroids, including 1566 Icarus. The asteroid 1501 Baade and a crater on the Moon are named for him.
