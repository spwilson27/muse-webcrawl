Cordial

The word cordial has several related meanings in food and drink. Originally, a cordial was a medicinal tonic believed to invigorate the heart (Latin cor, "heart"), typically a sweetened, spiced alcoholic preparation. From this origin come the two modern culinary senses: a sweetened fruit concentrate diluted with water, and a type of sweetened flavored liqueur.

Cordial as a soft drink

In the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and other Commonwealth countries, a cordial is a sweet, concentrated, usually fruit-flavoured syrup or squash that is diluted with water (or sometimes carbonated water or lemonade) before drinking. Common flavours include lime, lemon, blackcurrant, orange, and elderflower. Well-known brands include Robinsons, Ribena (blackcurrant), and Bottlegreen. Undiluted cordials are intensely sweet and are typically mixed at ratios of about one part cordial to four or five parts water. Historically, lime cordial — most famously Rose's lime juice cordial, patented by Lauchlan Rose in 1867 — was carried aboard ships because preserved lime juice helped prevent scurvy, and the product was among the first commercially successful fruit concentrates.

Cordial as a liqueur

In the United States, "cordial" is largely synonymous with liqueur: a sweetened, flavored distilled spirit. In European Union law, liqueurs must contain a minimum quantity of sugar (generally at least 100 grams per litre, with exceptions), and typical examples include amaretto, Grand Marnier, and herbal preparations such as Chartreuse and Bénédictine, the latter descended most directly from the medicinal cordials compounded by monks and apothecaries. Cordials in this sense are served in small glasses after meals as digestifs, and are used extensively in cocktails.

History

Medicinal cordials flourished in early modern Europe, where distillers in Italy, France, and the Netherlands produced sweetened spirits infused with herbs, spices, and fruits for the apothecary trade and for wealthy households. "Comfortable" drinks such as ratafia and usquebaugh were credited with restorative powers. As sugar became cheaper and temperance movements promoted non-alcoholic refreshment, the term in Britain migrated toward fruit syrups, while in America it stayed attached to sweetened spirits.
