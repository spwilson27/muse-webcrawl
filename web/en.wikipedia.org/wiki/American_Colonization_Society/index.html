American Colonization Society

The American Colonization Society (ACS), formally the American Society for Colonizing the Free People of Color in the United States, was founded in 1816 to encourage and support the emigration of freeborn Black people and formerly enslaved people to Africa. The Society helped establish the colony of Liberia; between 1822 and the American Civil War it transported some 15,000 people there. Liberia's capital, Monrovia, was named after President James Monroe, a supporter of the scheme.

Origins and motives

The ACS drew a broad but contradictory coalition: some supporters hoped to end slavery gradually and saw colonisation as a path to emancipation conditional on removal; others — including slave-owners — wished to remove free Black people, whom they regarded as a threat to the institution of slavery; still others were evangelical missionaries or believed Black people could never be integrated as equals into American society. Notable supporters included Henry Clay, Daniel Webster, and (early on) John Randolph, while Bushrod Washington served as its first president.

Opposition

Most free Black Americans opposed the ACS from the start, insisting that they were Americans entitled to full rights at home; Black leaders convened in Philadelphia in 1817 to protest the plan. Abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison later attacked the Society as a pro-slavery fraud that deported only a tiny fraction of the free Black population while strengthening slavery.

Liberia

In 1821–22 the Society's agents acquired land at Cape Mesurado by treaty with local rulers. Settlers — known as Americo-Liberians — built a society modelled on American institutions; Liberia declared independence in 1847, after which the ACS declined in importance, surviving into the 20th century as a charitable body before its formal dissolution in the 1960s.
