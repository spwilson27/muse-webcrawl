Kusunoki Masashige (楠木 正成, 1294 – 4 July 1336) was a Japanese samurai of the Kamakura period remembered as the ideal of samurai loyalty. A minor landholder in Kawachi Province, he answered Emperor Go-Daigo's call to arms against the Kamakura shogunate, fought celebrated guerrilla campaigns in defence of the imperial cause, and died at the Battle of Minatogawa rather than abandon the emperor's failing strategy.

Rise and the Genkō War

When Go-Daigo rose against the shogunate in 1331, Masashige fortified mountain strongholds at Akasaka and Chihaya in the Kongō mountains. His defence of Chihaya Castle in 1333, with elaborate traps, rolling logs and stones, and deceptive tactics, tied down a huge shogunal army and became legendary. The shogunate fell that year when Ashikaga Takauji and Nitta Yoshisada turned against it, and Go-Daigo's Kenmu Restoration began.

Battle of Minatogawa and death

When Takauji rebelled against the Restoration, Masashige urged the emperor to withdraw and fight a guerrilla war, but Go-Daigo insisted on battle. Obedient to his sovereign, Masashige marched knowing he would die, taking leave of his son Masatsura with instructions to continue the cause. At the Minato River on 4 July 1336 his small force was overwhelmed; wounded many times, he died by suicide with his brother Masasue, the two reportedly wishing to be reborn seven times to destroy the emperor's enemies (shichishō hōkoku — "seven lives for the nation").

Legacy

The Taiheiki chronicle made Masashige the paragon of loyalty. In the Meiji era he was enshrined at Minatogawa Shrine in Kobe and his statue placed outside the Imperial Palace in Tokyo; imperial Japan held him up as a model of selfless devotion, and his chrysanthemum crest became a symbol of loyalist sacrifice.
