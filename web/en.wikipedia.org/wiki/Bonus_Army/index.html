The Bonus Army was a group of over 17,000 United States World War I veterans and their families and affiliated groups who gathered in Washington, D.C., in mid-1932 to demand early redemption of their military service bonus certificates. The self-named "Bonus Expeditionary Force" (BEF), led by former sergeant Walter W. Waters, was a prominent example of the social unrest caused by the Great Depression.

Background

The World War Adjusted Compensation Act of 1924 awarded veterans bonus certificates redeemable in 1945. Impoverished by the Depression, veterans demanded immediate cash payment. In 1931 Congress overrode President Herbert Hoover's veto to allow loans against the certificates, but veterans pressed for full early payment.

The march and encampment

In May 1932 thousands of veterans converged on Washington, setting up camps — the largest across the Anacostia River, where some 15,000 people lived in shanties built from scrap material, with streets, sanitation rules, and a camp newspaper. On 15 June the House of Representatives passed the Patman Bonus Bill, but the Senate defeated it two days later. Most marchers left; several thousand remained.

Eviction

On 28 July 1932, after clashes in which police shot two veterans dead, President Hoover ordered the Army to clear the downtown camps. Army Chief of Staff General Douglas MacArthur, accompanied by Dwight D. Eisenhower, led infantry, cavalry, and tanks under George S. Patton. Going beyond Hoover's orders, MacArthur crossed the Anacostia bridge and burned the main camp; a baby and an 11-year-old boy were among those reported killed, and hundreds were injured. The spectacle of troops dispersing destitute veterans badly damaged Hoover's standing months before he lost the 1932 election to Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Aftermath

Smaller "second Bonus Marches" occurred in 1933 and 1934; Roosevelt offered Civilian Conservation Corps jobs instead of payment. In 1936 Congress, overriding Roosevelt's veto, finally authorized early payment of the bonuses.
