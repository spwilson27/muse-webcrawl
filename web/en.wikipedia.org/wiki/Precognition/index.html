Precognition

Precognition (from the Latin prae- "before", and cognitio "acquiring knowledge") is the purported psychic phenomenon of seeing, or otherwise becoming directly aware of, events in the future — a form of extrasensory perception in which information is allegedly obtained about events that have not yet occurred and could not be inferred from present knowledge. Precognition is widely regarded as pseudoscience: controlled experiments have failed to produce replicable evidence for it, and no accepted mechanism by which it could work is known; belief in it nevertheless remains widespread and the concept is a staple of folklore, religion, and fiction.

History

Belief in prophetic dreams and omens is ancient and cross-cultural, from Mesopotamian and biblical prophecy to the Delphic oracle. In the twentieth century, J. W. Dunne's An Experiment with Time (1927) popularised precognitive dreams, and the Society for Psychical Research and later J. B. Rhine's laboratory at Duke University attempted controlled tests, including Zener-card and machine-based experiments, reporting results that other researchers could not reliably reproduce.

Scientific investigation

Parapsychologists have used forced-choice guessing, dream studies, and "presentiment" experiments measuring physiological responses before randomly selected stimuli. A widely publicised 2011 paper by Daryl Bem claimed evidence for retroactive influences on cognition, but independent replications failed, and the episode became an influential case study in psychology's replication crisis, prompting reforms such as pre-registration. Skeptics attribute positive results to publication bias, optional stopping, selective reporting, and the retrospective matching of vague impressions to later events — the "Jeane Dixon effect" — together with ordinary cognitive biases such as confirmation bias and memory distortion.

Cultural presence

Precognition features prominently in science fiction and fantasy — for example in Frank Herbert's Dune, Philip K. Dick's The Minority Report (and its film adaptation Minority Report, centred on "precog" psychics), and superhero narratives — and polls consistently show a substantial minority of the public reports having experienced apparently precognitive dreams.
