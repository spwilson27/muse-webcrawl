Geta

Geta (Japanese: 下駄) are traditional Japanese footwear resembling flip-flops combined with clogs. They consist of a flat wooden base (the dai) raised on two transverse wooden blocks called "teeth" (ha), and are held on the foot by a fabric thong (hanao) that passes between the big toe and the second toe. Geta are worn primarily with traditional Japanese clothing such as kimono and yukata, and keep the hem of the garment off the ground, as well as raising the wearer above rain-soaked or muddy streets.

Construction

Traditional geta are commonly made from a single piece of wood — paulownia (kiri) is prized for its lightness and resistance to moisture. The two teeth are cut from or attached to the base. Styles vary: everyday geta have two teeth, while very tall three-toothed geta (ashida) were historically worn in wet weather, and the towering three-toothed geta of oiran courtesans were part of their elaborate costume.

Use and sound

Geta produce a distinctive clacking sound against hard ground. In folklore this sound is associated with the benevolent karura and with the one-eyed, one-legged umbrella spirit Karakasa-obake, which is sometimes depicted wearing a single geta. Walking in geta takes some practice, as the wearer must grip the thong with the toes and roll the foot over the front tooth.

Modern use

Today geta are worn mainly with summer yukata at festivals and fireworks displays, by practitioners of traditional arts, and by workers such as sushi chefs in some traditional settings. Lower, single-toothed or flat-soled modern variants are also sold as casual summer sandals.
