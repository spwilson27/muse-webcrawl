Khanqah

A khanqah (Persian: خانقاه, also transliterated khaneqa, khanegah or khanaqa) is a building designed specifically for gatherings of a Sufi brotherhood (tariqa) and is a place for spiritual retreat and character reformation. In the past, and to a lesser extent today, khanqahs served as hospices for saliks (Sufi travellers), Murids (initiates) and talibs (Islamic students). Khanqahs are very often found adjoined to dargahs (shrines of Sufi saints), mosques and madrasas (Islamic schools).

In the Arab world, especially in North Africa, the khanqah is known variously as a zāwiyah (Arabic: زاوية; also spelled zawiya, zaouia and zaouïa), and in Turkey and formerly Ottoman areas it is tekke (Ottoman Turkish: تكيه), āsitāne, or dergâh. In South Asia the words khanqah and dargah are used interchangeably.

Functions

Khanqahs typically include a large hall for prayer and for the collective ritual remembrance of God (dhikr), cells (hujra) for the resident Sufis and for retreat (khalwa), a kitchen and refectory, and accommodation for travellers and the poor. They were frequently endowed as charitable foundations (waqf) by rulers and wealthy patrons, and in medieval times played an important role in spreading Islam to new regions, in education, and in providing welfare and hospitality along trade routes.

The life of the khanqah was organised around the authority of the shaykh, who instructed the murids in the practices of the order, led the dhikr, and supervised their spiritual progress. Many khanqahs grew up around the tomb of a founding saint, becoming pilgrimage centres in their own right.

Architecture

Khanqah architecture varies widely by region and period, but commonly centres on a courtyard or domed central hall surrounded by small cells for residents. Notable historical examples include the Khanqah of Sultan Baybars II in Cairo and the great khanqahs of Konya, Herat, and Bukhara associated with the Naqshbandi and other orders.
