Consciousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Consciousness is the state or quality of awareness of one's own internal and external existence — the subjective experience of sensations, thoughts and feelings. Despite being the most familiar phenomenon in human life, it has proved exceptionally hard to define and explain. Philosophers distinguish access consciousness (information available for reasoning and control) from phenomenal consciousness (what it is like to have an experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain). David Chalmers called the problem of explaining subjective experience the "hard problem" of consciousness, in contrast to the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions.

Science of consciousness

Neuroscience seeks the neural correlates of consciousness: the brain processes that accompany conscious rather than unconscious perception. Consciousness depends on the brainstem's arousal systems and widespread cortical activity; it vanishes in dreamless sleep, coma and general anaesthesia, and alters in dreaming, meditation and psychedelic states. Leading scientific theories include the global workspace theory (conscious information is broadcast widely across the brain) and integrated information theory (consciousness corresponds to integrated information, Φ). Disorders of consciousness — coma, vegetative and minimally conscious states — pose deep clinical and ethical problems, as do questions about consciousness in animals and, increasingly, in artificial intelligence systems. The synaptic networks of the nervous system somehow give rise to experience; how remains one of science's great open questions.
