Mi'raj

Placeholder image: A Persian miniature of the Mi'raj ascension

The Mi'raj (Arabic: المعراج, "the Ascension") is the second part of the Night Journey of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, following the Isra, the miraculous journey from Mecca to Jerusalem. According to Islamic tradition, Muhammad was carried on the winged creature al-Buraq to the Al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, and from there ascended through the seven heavens, meeting earlier prophets, before reaching the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary (Sidrat al-Muntaha) and the presence of God.

The account

The journey is alluded to in the Quran at Surah Al-Isra (17:1), which speaks of the servant whom God "took by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque," and in Surah An-Najm (53). Details are elaborated in hadith literature: Muhammad met Adam, John the Baptist and Jesus, Joseph, Idris, Aaron, Moses, and Abraham in successive heavens; was shown paradise and hell; and received, after negotiation in which Moses advised him to ask for a reduction, the commandment of the five daily prayers (salah) reduced from an original fifty. The event is traditionally dated to around 621 CE, in the difficult period after the Year of Sorrow.

Observance

The Isra and Mi'raj are commemorated by many Muslims on the 27th night of Rajab (Laylat al-Mi'raj), with night prayers, storytelling, and charitable acts. The rock within Jerusalem's Dome of the Rock is traditionally identified as the point from which the ascension began. Scholars and commentators have long debated whether the journey was bodily or a spiritual vision; the mainstream Sunni view holds that it was a bodily journey undertaken awake, while some interpretations treat it as a visionary experience.

Cultural legacy

The Mi'raj generated a rich literary and artistic tradition, including the Persian and Turkish Mi'rajnameh ("Book of the Ascension") manuscripts illustrated with miniatures of the journey, and it has been proposed as one of several possible influences — through medieval Latin and French translations of the Kitab al-Mi'raj — debated by scholars in relation to Dante's Divine Comedy.
