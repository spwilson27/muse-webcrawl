Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F)

UsePET imaging of glucose metabolism

Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F), abbreviated FDG or 18F-FDG, is a radiopharmaceutical used in positron emission tomography (PET). It is an analogue of glucose in which the normal hydroxyl group at the 2′ position is replaced by the positron-emitting radionuclide fluorine-18. After injection, FDG is taken up by tissues with high glucose demand — such as the brain, heart, and most cancers — and becomes trapped after phosphorylation because it cannot proceed further through glycolysis.

Clinical use

FDG PET, usually combined with CT (PET/CT), is a mainstay of oncology imaging, used to stage cancers, detect recurrence and monitor response to therapy; avid uptake reflects the elevated glycolysis of tumour cells (the Warburg effect). It is also used in neurology (patterns of hypometabolism in dementia and epilepsy), cardiology (myocardial viability), and the localisation of infection and inflammation. Quantification is commonly expressed as the standardised uptake value (SUV).

Production

Because fluorine-18 has a half-life of about 110 minutes, FDG is produced in a cyclotron, typically by nucleophilic substitution on a mannose triflate precursor, and must be synthesised, quality-checked and delivered to the scanner within hours. FDG was first synthesised in 1976 at Brookhaven National Laboratory by Tatsuo Ido and colleagues, and first administered to human volunteers at the University of Pennsylvania the same year.
