Narcolepsy

Narcolepsy
Specialty: Sleep medicine, neurology
Symptoms: Excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hallucinations
Usual onset: Childhood to young adulthood
Causes: Loss of hypocretin (orexin) neurons; genetic and autoimmune factors
Treatment: Stimulants, sodium oxybate, lifestyle measures

Narcolepsy is a chronic neurological disorder that affects the brain's ability to control sleep–wake cycles. People with narcolepsy often experience overwhelming daytime drowsiness and sudden attacks of sleep, which can occur at inappropriate times and places. The condition typically begins in childhood or adolescence and persists throughout life.

Signs and symptoms

The classic tetrad of narcolepsy symptoms consists of excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotions such as laughter), hypnagogic hallucinations (vivid dream-like experiences at sleep onset), and sleep paralysis. Not all patients exhibit all four symptoms. Night-time sleep is often fragmented despite the excessive sleepiness during the day.

Causes

Narcolepsy type 1 is caused by a selective loss of neurons in the hypothalamus that produce the neurotransmitter hypocretin (also called orexin), which regulates wakefulness. The loss is believed to be autoimmune in origin. Most people with type 1 narcolepsy carry the HLA-DQB1*06:02 gene variant, although the variant is also common in the general population. Cases with cataplexy increased notably after the 2009 swine flu pandemic and in some recipients of the Pandemrix vaccine. Narcolepsy type 2 presents with similar daytime sleepiness but without cataplexy and with normal hypocretin levels.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical history, overnight polysomnography, and the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT), which measures how quickly a person falls asleep during daytime nap opportunities and whether they enter REM sleep abnormally early. Low hypocretin-1 levels in cerebrospinal fluid can confirm type 1 narcolepsy.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed. Scheduled naps and good sleep hygiene help with daytime sleepiness. Medications include wake-promoting agents such as modafinil and armodafinil, traditional stimulants, solriamfetol, and pitolisant. Sodium oxybate (and lower-sodium oxybate formulations) can treat both cataplexy and excessive daytime sleepiness; certain antidepressants are used off-label to suppress cataplexy.

History

The term "narcolepsy" was coined by the French physician Jean-Baptiste-Édouard Gélineau in 1880, from the Greek narke (numbness) and lepsis (attack). The role of hypocretin deficiency was established around 1999–2000 through studies of narcoleptic dogs and human patients.
