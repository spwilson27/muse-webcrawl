Thermal conduction

Thermal conduction
Governing law: Fourier's law of heat conduction
Key property: thermal conductivity k, units W/(m·K)
Examples of k: silver ≈ 429, copper ≈ 401, aluminium ≈ 237, glass ≈ 1, wood ≈ 0.15, still air ≈ 0.026 W/(m·K)
Related modes: convection, thermal radiation

Thermal conduction (or heat conduction) is the transfer of heat between objects in physical contact, or within a single object, driven by a temperature difference. At the microscopic level, conduction is the spontaneous transfer of kinetic and internal energy through collisions and vibrations of particles and, in metals, through the movement of free electrons — without any bulk motion of the material itself. It is one of the three principal modes of heat transfer, alongside convection and thermal radiation.

Fourier's law

The empirical law of conduction, formulated by Joseph Fourier in the early nineteenth century, states that the rate of heat transfer through a material is proportional to the area and to the negative temperature gradient:

q = −kA (dT/dx)

where q is the heat flow (watts), A the cross-sectional area, dT/dx the temperature gradient, and k the material's thermal conductivity. The minus sign expresses that heat flows from hot toward cold. Materials with high k, such as metals, are good conductors; materials with low k, such as foams, fibreglass, and aerogels, serve as thermal insulators.

Mechanisms

In non-metallic solids, heat is carried by lattice vibrations, described quantum-mechanically as phonons. In metals, free electrons dominate: the same electrons that carry electric current also carry energy, which is why good electrical conductors are usually good thermal conductors — a relationship quantified by the Wiedemann–Franz law. In liquids and gases, conduction occurs through molecular collisions and diffusion, and conductivities are generally much lower than in solids; in gases the conductivity rises with temperature, while in most liquids it falls.

Steady state and applications

For a plane wall in steady state, the heat flow is q = kA(Thot − Tcold)/L, with L the thickness — the basis of the R-value used in building insulation. Conduction analysis underlies the design of heat exchangers, heat sinks for electronics, cooking, cryogenics, furnaces, and thermal protection systems. Where conduction through joined parts matters, interface effects and thermal contact resistance must also be considered.
