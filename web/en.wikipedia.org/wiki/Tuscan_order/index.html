The Tuscan order (Latin: ordo tuscanicus or ordo tuscanus) is one of the two classical Roman orders of architecture, alongside the Composite order, and one of the five orders codified by Renaissance theorists. It is a simplified adaptation of the Greek Doric order, with an unfluted shaft, a plain capital, and a simple base, and it takes its name from the Etruscan (Tuscan) temples of early Italy.

Description

Vitruvius described the Tuscan temple arrangement in his De architectura. In Renaissance treatises such as those of Sebastiano Serlio and Vignola, the Tuscan order was regularised: the column is typically seven diameters high, the capital consists of a simple echinus and abacus without ornament, and the entablature is plain, with an unadorned frieze. Unlike the Greek Doric, the Tuscan column stands on a base.

Usage

Because of its sturdy, unornamented character, the Tuscan order was considered appropriate for utilitarian and military buildings, prisons, docks, and the ground floors of palaces. It was revived in Renaissance and Baroque Italy and later used extensively in Georgian and colonial architecture in Britain and America for porches, courthouses, and rustic buildings.
