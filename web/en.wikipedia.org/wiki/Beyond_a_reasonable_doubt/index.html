Beyond a reasonable doubt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Beyond a reasonable doubt is the legal standard of proof required to convict a person of a crime in most common-law jurisdictions: the prosecution must prove every element of the offence to a degree of certainty such that a reasonable person would not hesitate to act on it in matters of importance — so that no reasonable doubt of guilt remains. It is the operational expression of the presumption of innocence and of Blackstone's ratio (better that ten guilty persons escape than one innocent suffer).

Application and definition debates

The standard is constitutionally required in the United States (In re Winship, 1970); England and Wales instruct juries that they must be "sure" of guilt; other formulations vary ("intimate conviction" in French law, moral certainty historically). Courts have struggled for centuries to define "reasonable doubt" without quantifying it — instructions range from elaborate to deliberately minimal ("a doubt for which a reason could be given"), percentages are disclaimed, and appellate cases police formulations that dilute the standard (e.g. Cage v. Louisiana, 1990). The standard contrasts with civil cases' balance of probabilities and intermediate standards (clear and convincing evidence) and does not apply to facts used at sentencing in all respects. Its real-world operation is debated in wrongful-conviction research, where confident-but-wrong eyewitness evidence is the recurring culprit.
