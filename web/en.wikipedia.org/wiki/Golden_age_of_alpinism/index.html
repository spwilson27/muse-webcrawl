Golden age of alpinism

The golden age of alpinism was the decade in British and continental mountaineering between Alfred Wills's ascent of the Wetterhorn in 1854 and Edward Whymper's ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865, during which many of the major peaks of the Alps saw their first ascents.

The period is defined by the activity of the (mainly British) members of the Alpine Club, founded in London in 1857, working with local guides from Chamonix, Zermatt, Grindelwald and other Alpine valleys. In rapid succession the great summits fell: Monte Rosa's highest point, the Dufourspitze (1855), Mont Blanc's remaining ridges, the Eiger (1858), the Grossglockner routes, Mont Pelvoux (1861), the Dent Blanche (1862), and many others.

Principal figures

Leading amateurs included Alfred Wills, John Tyndall, Francis Younghusband's predecessors such as Leslie Stephen, T. S. Kennedy, and above all Edward Whymper. Their indispensable guides included Michel Croz, Christian Almer, Melchior Anderegg, Jean-Antoine Carrel and Peter Taugwalder.

End of the golden age

The era came to a dramatic close on 14 July 1865, when Whymper's party made the first ascent of the Matterhorn via the Hörnli ridge. On the descent, four of the seven climbers — Michel Croz, Douglas Hadow, Charles Hudson and Lord Francis Douglas — fell to their deaths. The disaster provoked public controversy in Britain, with questions asked in Parliament and editorials questioning the morality of the sport. The golden age was succeeded by the "silver age of alpinism", in which the remaining great ridges and faces were climbed, before attention turned to the Caucasus, the Rockies and the Himalayas.
