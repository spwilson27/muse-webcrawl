Overtone singing

Overtone singing
Also known as: Throat singing (khoomei)
Regions: Tuva, Mongolia, Tibet, Siberia, Sardinia, Inuit
UNESCO: Mongolian khoomei inscribed 2009

Overtone singing is a vocal technique in which a singer manipulates resonances to make selected harmonics (overtones) audible as distinct pitches above a sustained drone, seemingly singing two notes at once.

Traditions

Central Asian Tuvan and Mongolian khoomei, sygyt, and kargyraa styles are best known; related practices occur in Tibetan chant, Inuit katajjaq (usually duet games), Sardinian tenore singing, and Xhosa umngqokolo. Mouth shape, tongue position, and sometimes false vocal folds shape the harmonic spectrum.

Modern use

Western practitioners and composers adopted overtone singing from the 1960s (Stockhausen's Stimmung), and it features in film scores and experimental music.
