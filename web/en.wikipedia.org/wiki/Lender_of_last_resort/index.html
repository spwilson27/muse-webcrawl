Lender of last resort

[Image: The Bank of England in the City of London, the archetypal lender of last resort, photographed from Bank junction]

A lender of last resort is an institution — normally a country's central bank — that provides liquidity to financial institutions that are solvent but cannot obtain funding from the market during a crisis. The function exists to prevent bank runs and financial panics from destroying solvent institutions and spreading through the system.

Bagehot's doctrine

The classic statement of the doctrine is by Walter Bagehot in Lombard Street (1873): in a panic, the central bank should lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral. Lending freely stops the panic; the penalty rate discourages routine use; good collateral protects the central bank and distinguishes illiquid from insolvent borrowers.

Practice

Central banks implement the function through discount windows, emergency lending facilities and market-wide operations. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, the Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank extended emergency lending far beyond traditional counterparties. The role raises issues of moral hazard: if institutions expect rescue, they may take greater risks.

International level

There is no true global lender of last resort. The International Monetary Fund performs a partial analogue by lending to member countries in balance-of-payments crises, and networks of central bank swap lines performed a similar function for major currencies in 2008 and 2020.
