Metacom, also known to English colonists as King Philip

Metacom (c. 1638 – August 12, 1676), also known as Metacomet and by his adopted English name King Philip, was sachem (elected chief) of the Pokanoket and the Wampanoag Confederacy from 1662 to 1676. He was the second son of the sachem Massasoit. Metacom is best known as the leader of the Native American forces in King Philip's War (1675–1678), one of the bloodiest conflicts in colonial American history relative to population.

Early life

Metacom was born around 1638, the younger son of Massasoit, the great sachem who had maintained peaceful relations with the Plymouth colonists since 1621. In the 1650s, Massasoit had his sons given English names by the Plymouth court: Wamsutta became Alexander, and Metacom became Philip. When Massasoit died in 1661, Wamsutta succeeded him but died in 1662 under suspicious circumstances after being summoned to Plymouth, and Metacom became sachem.

Rising tensions

During Metacom's tenure, relations between the Wampanoag and English colonists steadily deteriorated. English expansion onto Native lands, the imposition of colonial law on Native people, forced conversions, cattle damaging Native crops, and the subordination of sachems to colonial authority all fueled resentment. Metacom was repeatedly summoned before colonial courts, forced to sign agreements surrendering land and weapons, and humiliated. He began building alliances with other Native nations, including the Narragansett, Nipmuc, and Pocumtuc, in preparation for a possible war.

King Philip's War

War broke out in June 1675 after the murder of John Sassamon, a Christian Native informant, and the subsequent execution of three Wampanoag men by Plymouth Colony. Metacom's warriors raided Swansea and other frontier towns, and the conflict spread across New England. Native forces attacked dozens of towns, destroying many outright, while colonial forces responded with campaigns against Native villages, food supplies, and non-combatants.

The winter campaign of 1675–76, including the colonial assault on the Narragansett fort in the Great Swamp Fight, and the progressive destruction of Native food stores, undermined the Native war effort. In the spring and summer of 1676, Metacom's coalition fragmented. On August 12, 1676, Metacom was tracked to a swamp near Mount Hope in Bristol, Rhode Island, and shot dead by John Alderman, a Native soldier serving with Captain Benjamin Church's company.

Death and legacy

After his death, Metacom's body was beheaded and quartered; his head was displayed on a pike at Plymouth for two decades, and his wife and young son were sold into slavery in the Caribbean, along with hundreds of other Native captives. King Philip's War devastated both sides: hundreds of colonists and thousands of Native people died, and the Native nations of southern New England were shattered as political powers. Metacom has since been remembered variously as a villain in colonial accounts and as a hero of Native resistance.
