Emishi

The Emishi (Japanese: 蝦夷), also called Ezo in later sources, were a group of people who lived in parts of Honshū, the main island of Japan, particularly the Tōhoku region in the northeast. They are first attested in historical records of the 5th century and are generally considered distinct from the Yamato Japanese who founded the imperial state.

History

The Yamato court gradually expanded northward through a combination of diplomacy, fortified colonies, and military campaigns. The most famous resistance leader was Aterui, who defeated imperial forces at the Battle of Subuse in 789 before being captured and executed in 802 after surrendering to Sakanoue no Tamuramaro, who received the title Sei-i Taishōgun ("barbarian-subduing generalissimo") for the campaign.

By the 11th and 12th centuries, most Emishi groups had been absorbed or assimilated into Japanese society, though scholars debate their relationship to the later Ainu people of Hokkaido. Some historians argue for substantial continuity between Emishi and Ainu populations; others see the Emishi as largely distinct, possibly related to Jōmon-period peoples with their own languages.

Culture

The Emishi were skilled horsemen and archers, famed for mounted warfare that initially confounded imperial armies. They lived by hunting, fishing, and some agriculture, and traded horses, gold, and marine products with the Yamato state.
