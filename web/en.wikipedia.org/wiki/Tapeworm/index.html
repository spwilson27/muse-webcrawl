Tapeworm

Tapeworms are parasitic flatworms of the class Cestoda. They live in the digestive tracts of vertebrates as adults, and often in the bodies of various animals as juveniles. Tapeworms are ribbon-shaped and segmented, anchoring themselves to the host's intestinal wall with a specialised head called a scolex, and absorbing nutrients directly through their body surface. They have no digestive system of their own.

Anatomy

The body, or strobila, consists of a chain of segments called proglottids, produced behind the scolex. Each mature proglottid contains both male and female reproductive organs; gravid segments full of eggs detach and pass out with the host's faeces. Some species reach remarkable lengths — the fish tapeworm Diphyllobothrium latum can exceed 10 metres in humans.

Life cycle

Most tapeworms require two hosts. Eggs released into the environment are eaten by an intermediate host (such as cattle, pigs or fish), in whose tissues larvae form cysts. When a definitive host eats raw or undercooked meat containing cysts, the larvae develop into adult worms in the intestine.

Infection in humans

Intestinal infections (taeniasis) are often mild or asymptomatic, though they can cause abdominal discomfort and weight loss. Far more serious is cysticercosis, caused when humans ingest Taenia solium eggs and larvae form cysts in tissues including the brain (neurocysticercosis), a major cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic regions. Treatment uses anthelmintic drugs such as praziquantel and niclosamide; prevention rests on cooking meat thoroughly, good sanitation and meat inspection.
