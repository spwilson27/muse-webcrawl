Civil forfeiture in the United States

Civil forfeiture in the United States, also called civil asset forfeiture or civil judicial forfeiture, is a legal process in which law enforcement officers take assets from people suspected of involvement with crime without necessarily charging the owners with wrongdoing.

Civil forfeiture
Legal basis: In rem proceedings against property
Related: Criminal forfeiture, equitable sharing
Key reform: Civil Asset Forfeiture Reform Act (2000)

History

Rooted in British maritime and customs law and early U.S. statutes, civil forfeiture expanded dramatically during the War on Drugs from the 1980s, allowing seizure of cash, vehicles and real estate alleged to be proceeds or instruments of crime.

Procedure and criticism

Because cases are brought against the property itself (producing case names such as United States v. $124,700), the burden of proof is lower than in criminal trials and often falls on owners to prove innocence. Critics, including the ACLU and Institute for Justice, call lucrative "policing for profit" through federal equitable sharing; defenders say it disrupts criminal enterprises. The Supreme Court addressed its limits in cases such as Leonard v. Texas (Thomas concurrence) and Timbs v. Indiana (2019, excessive fines).

Reform

Many states have raised burdens of proof or required convictions, and federal policy on equitable sharing has shifted between administrations.
