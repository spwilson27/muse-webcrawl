Amylopectin is a water-insoluble polysaccharide and a highly branched polymer of α-glucose units found in plants. It is one of the two components of starch, the other being amylose. Amylopectin typically makes up about 70–80% of starch by weight, although some "waxy" starches are almost entirely amylopectin.

Structure

Amylopectin consists of chains of glucose residues joined by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, with branch points formed by α(1→6) bonds occurring roughly every 24–30 glucose units. A single molecule may contain tens of thousands — in some cases over 100,000 — glucose units, making amylopectin one of the largest naturally occurring molecules. Its clustered branching gives starch granules a semi-crystalline structure.

Properties and digestion

Because branch points prevent the tight helical packing seen in amylose, amylopectin is more readily gelatinised and more rapidly attacked by digestive enzymes: pancreatic α-amylase cleaves the α(1→4) chains, while debranching enzymes handle the α(1→6) points. Foods rich in amylopectin therefore tend to be digested quickly and to have a higher glycaemic index than high-amylose foods.

Comparison with glycogen

Amylopectin is structurally similar to glycogen, the storage polysaccharide of animals and fungi, but glycogen is branched more frequently (about every 8–12 residues) and has shorter chains, suiting it to faster mobilisation.

Uses

Purified and modified amylopectin-rich starches are used in foods as thickeners and stabilisers, and in industry in adhesives, paper sizing and textiles. Inherited defects of glycogen branching — relevant in animals — have parallels in plant breeding for waxy maize and waxy rice varieties, prized for their sticky texture.
