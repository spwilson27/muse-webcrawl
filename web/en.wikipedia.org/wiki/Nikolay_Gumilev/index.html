Nikolay Stepanovich Gumilev (15 April 1886 – 26 August 1921) was a Russian poet, literary critic, translator and army officer. He was a co-founder of the Acmeist movement, together with his wife Anna Akhmatova and the poet Osip Mandelstam, and a hugely influential figure in early 20th-century Russian poetry. He was arrested and executed by the Cheka in 1921 in the fabricated Tagantsev conspiracy case.

Life

Gumilev was born in Kronstadt, the son of a naval physician, and studied at the Sorbonne in Paris, where his first collection, The Path of the Conquistadors (1905), appeared. He travelled extensively in Africa — Ethiopia, Somalia, Egypt — expeditions that fed his poetry with exotic imagery and later included ethnographic collecting for the Academy of Sciences. In 1911 he founded the Poets' Guild in St. Petersburg, the institutional home of Acmeism, which prized clarity, craft and "beautiful clarity" against Symbolist mysticism.

War and revolution

When the First World War began, Gumilev volunteered for a hussar regiment and was twice awarded the Cross of St. George for bravery. After the October Revolution he remained in Russia, taught poetry in Petrograd, translated Coleridge, Shakespeare and others, and openly refused to conceal his monarchist and religious views.

Arrest and death

In August 1921 Gumilev was arrested by the Cheka in the Tagantsev case, an alleged monarchist plot now generally regarded as fabricated. Despite appeals from Maxim Gorky and others, he was shot, along with some sixty others, on 26 August 1921; the exact place of execution and burial remains uncertain.

Legacy

Gumilev's work was banned in the Soviet Union for decades but circulated in samizdat; he was formally rehabilitated in 1992. His collections include Romantic Flowers (1908), The Tent (1921) and the posthumous Pillar of Fire. His son with Akhmatova, Lev Gumilev, became a controversial historian and ethnologist.
