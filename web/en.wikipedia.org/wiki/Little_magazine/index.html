Little magazine

A little magazine is a magazine that publishes experimental, avant-garde, or otherwise non-commercial writing and art, usually with a small circulation, low budget, and short lifespan. Little magazines are typically published outside the mainstream commercial press, often by the editors themselves, and they have played a central role in the development of modern literature.

History

Little magazines emerged in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as vehicles for new literary movements. Publications such as Poetry (founded 1912 in Chicago by Harriet Monroe), The Little Review, Blast, and The Dial published the early work of writers such as T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, James Joyce, and Virginia Woolf, frequently serializing works that commercial publishers considered too difficult or risky.

Characteristics

Little magazines are defined less by size than by purpose: they prioritize artistic or intellectual aims over profit, often champion a particular movement or group of writers, and provide a first venue for experimental work. Many are short-lived because of financial precariousness, but their influence on literary history is disproportionate to their circulation.

Legacy

Scholarly interest in little magazines has grown with digitization projects that archive complete runs. The term is also used more broadly for small literary journals, zines, and independent periodicals that continue the tradition.
