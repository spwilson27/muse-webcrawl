Overview

The nonfiction novel is a literary genre that, broadly speaking, depicts real historical figures and actual events woven together with fictitious conversations and using the storytelling techniques of a conventional novel. The genre is sometimes known as "creative nonfiction" or "faction", a portmanteau of fact and fiction.

The nonfiction novel emerged as a distinct form in the mid-twentieth century, as writers sought to apply the dramatic structure, scene-setting, and psychological depth of the novel to journalistic material.

History

Early precursors include works such as John Hersey's Hiroshima (1946), which used novelistic techniques to recount the experiences of survivors of the atomic bombing. The form gained prominence with Truman Capote's In Cold Blood (1966), a detailed account of the 1959 murders of the Clutter family in Kansas, which Capote described as a "nonfiction novel".

Norman Mailer further developed the form with The Armies of the Night (1968), which won the Pulitzer Prize, and The Executioner's Song (1979), an account of the life and execution of murderer Gary Gilmore.

Characteristics

Nonfiction novels are distinguished from ordinary journalism by their use of literary techniques: fully realised scenes, extended dialogue, shifting points of view, and attention to interior states of mind. At the same time, they claim fidelity to verifiable fact, a claim that has frequently been debated by critics and by the subjects of the works themselves.
