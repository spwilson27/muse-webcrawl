Sir Peter Frederick Strawson (23 November 1919 – 13 February 2006) was an English philosopher who spent most of his career at the University of Oxford, where he was a fellow of University College and, from 1968 to 1987, Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy. He was a leading figure in "ordinary language" philosophy and in the revival of metaphysics within analytic philosophy.

"On Referring" and Russell

Strawson first came to prominence with his 1950 paper "On Referring," a critique of Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions. Against Russell's analysis of "The present King of France is bald," Strawson argued that sentences are not in themselves true or false; rather, statements are made by uses of sentences, and where a referring expression fails of a referent, the question of truth or falsity simply does not arise — the statement presupposes, rather than asserts, the existence of its subject.

Individuals and descriptive metaphysics

His most celebrated book, Individuals: An Essay in Descriptive Metaphysics (1959), distinguished descriptive metaphysics — describing the actual structure of our thought about the world — from revisionary metaphysics, which seeks to replace it. Strawson argued that material bodies and persons are the basic particulars of our conceptual scheme: persons are primitive entities to which both physical characteristics and states of consciousness are ascribed.

Other work

In The Bounds of Sense (1966), Strawson offered an influential reconstruction of Kant's Critique of Pure Reason, separating what he took to be Kant's enduring arguments from transcendental idealism. His 1962 paper "Freedom and Resentment" reshaped debates about free will and moral responsibility by focusing on the "reactive attitudes" — resentment, gratitude, indignation — that structure interpersonal life, arguing that these attitudes are neither justified nor undermined by the truth or falsity of determinism. He was knighted in 1977.
