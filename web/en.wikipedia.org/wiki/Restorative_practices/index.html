Restorative practices

Restorative practices is an approach to preventing and responding to harm and conflict by building and repairing relationships within a community. Rooted in restorative justice — which brings together those harmed, those responsible and their communities to decide how to make things right — the broader field of restorative practices applies the same principles proactively, especially in schools, workplaces, social services and criminal justice settings.

Practices

The continuum of practices ranges from informal techniques — affective statements and questions, impromptu conversations — to formal processes including restorative conferences, mediation and circles (proactive community-building circles and responsive circles after an incident). In schools, restorative practices have been adopted as an alternative or complement to punitive discipline such as suspension and exclusion, with the aim of keeping students in class, teaching accountability, and reducing repeat incidents and disparities.

Foundations

The field draws on Indigenous traditions of communal decision-making, on the restorative justice movement that developed in Canada, New Zealand and Australia from the 1970s (including family group conferences in New Zealand youth justice), and on social science concepts such as the "social discipline window" — combining high expectations with high support — promoted by the International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP).

Evidence and debate

Studies of school programmes report reductions in suspensions and improvements in school climate, though results vary with implementation quality, and critics argue the approach can be applied inconsistently or as a relabelling of weak discipline. In criminal justice, restorative conferencing has shown high victim satisfaction and modest reductions in reoffending in randomised trials.
