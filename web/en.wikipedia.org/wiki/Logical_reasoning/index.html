Logical reasoning

Logical reasoning is a mental activity concerned with drawing conclusions from premises in a way that respects the rules of logic. It is the process of using a rational, systematic series of steps based on sound mathematical procedures and given statements to arrive at a conclusion. Logical reasoning is distinguished from ordinary thinking by its explicit attention to the form of arguments: whether a conclusion genuinely follows from its premises.

Forms of reasoning

Deductive reasoning

Deductive reasoning moves from general premises to a conclusion that must be true if the premises are true; the conclusion makes explicit what is already contained in the premises. The classic example is the syllogism: "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal." Deductive arguments are assessed as valid or invalid, and sound if valid with true premises.

Inductive reasoning

Inductive reasoning generalises from observations to probable conclusions — for example, inferring that the sun will rise tomorrow from its having risen every observed day. Inductive conclusions go beyond the premises and are supported to a degree rather than guaranteed, raising the philosophical problem of induction associated with David Hume.

Abductive reasoning

Abductive reasoning, or inference to the best explanation, selects the hypothesis that best explains the evidence: a doctor diagnosing from symptoms, or a detective reconstructing a crime. The term was developed by Charles Sanders Peirce.

Study and assessment

Logical reasoning is studied in philosophy, mathematics, psychology and artificial intelligence. Psychologists have shown that human reasoning often departs from logical norms (for example in the Wason selection task) and have proposed mental-models and dual-process theories to explain this. Logical reasoning tests — syllogisms, sequences, analogies and logic puzzles — are widely used in admissions tests such as the LSAT and in employment screening, and formal checking of reasoning is performed by proof assistants and automated theorem provers in computing.
