Glutamate receptor

Glutamate receptor
LigandL-glutamate
ClassesIonotropic (NMDA, AMPA, kainate); metabotropic (mGluR)
RoleExcitatory neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity

Glutamate receptors are synaptic and non-synaptic receptors located primarily on the membranes of neuronal and glial cells. Glutamate is the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate nervous system, and glutamate receptors mediate the great majority of fast excitatory synaptic transmission in the brain. They are central to synaptic plasticity — learning and memory — and, when overactivated, to excitotoxic injury in stroke and neurodegenerative disease.

Ionotropic receptors

Ionotropic glutamate receptors are ligand-gated ion channels, named after the agonists that activate them:

  • AMPA receptors mediate most fast excitatory transmission; their trafficking into synapses underlies long-term potentiation.
  • NMDA receptors are coincidence detectors: they require both glutamate binding and postsynaptic depolarization (to expel a blocking Mg2+ ion), and pass Ca2+ that triggers plasticity cascades.
  • Kainate receptors modulate transmission both pre- and postsynaptically.

Metabotropic receptors

The metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) are G protein-coupled receptors, grouped into Group I (mGluR1, 5), Group II (mGluR2, 3) and Group III (mGluR4, 6, 7, 8). They modulate synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability on slower timescales and are drug targets in schizophrenia, anxiety, Parkinson's disease and fragile X syndrome.

Clinical significance

Antagonists such as ketamine and memantine act at NMDA receptors; the AMPA receptor modulator perampanel is an antiepileptic. Autoimmune anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, made widely known in the 2010s, is caused by antibodies against the receptor's NR1 subunit.
