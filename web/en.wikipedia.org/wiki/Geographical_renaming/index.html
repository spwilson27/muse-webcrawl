Geographical renaming

Place names change with political and linguistic shifts

Geographical renaming is the changing of the name of a geographical feature or area. Place names (toponyms) are frequently changed for political, cultural, linguistic or administrative reasons, and the study of such changes sheds light on the history of a region.

Reasons for renaming

Common motivations include:

  • Political change — new regimes erase names associated with former rulers or ideologies, as when Saint Petersburg became Petrograd, then Leningrad, then Saint Petersburg again.
  • Decolonisation — countries replace colonial names with indigenous or national ones: Bombay became Mumbai, Salisbury became Harare, and Burma's official English name became Myanmar.
  • Honouring individuals — cities are named or renamed for leaders, such as Ho Chi Minh City (formerly Saigon) or Astana's brief renaming to Nur-Sultan.
  • Linguistic standardisation — governments adopt a single official spelling or endonym, as with the Turkification of place names in Turkey or Hellenisation in Greece.
  • Disambiguation and administration — duplicate names are altered to avoid confusion in postal and administrative systems.

Exonyms and endonyms

The name used by locals (endonym) often differs from the name used by outsiders (exonym): München/Munich, Warszawa/Warsaw, Beijing/Peking. International bodies such as the United Nations Group of Experts on Geographical Names work to standardise usage and promote local names.

Controversy

Renaming is frequently contested. Disputes include the Macedonia naming dispute between Greece and North Macedonia, resolved by the 2018 Prespa Agreement, and debates over restoring indigenous names to mountains and towns in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States.
