Symbolic anthropology

Symbolic anthropology (also called interpretive anthropology) is a major school of cultural anthropology that studies culture as a system of symbols and meanings, which the anthropologist's task is to interpret. It rose to prominence in the United States in the 1960s and 1970s, drawing on Max Weber's sociology and the hermeneutic tradition.

Major figures

Its most influential figure is Clifford Geertz, who defined culture as "webs of significance" that people themselves have spun, and advocated "thick description" — the meticulous interpretation of symbolic action, exemplified by his essay on the Balinese cockfight as a text in which the Balinese "read" their own status drama. In Britain and America, Victor Turner developed related approaches through his work on ritual symbols, liminality and social drama; David Schneider analysed kinship as a symbolic system, and Mary Douglas examined how symbols of purity and pollution organise societies.

Criticisms

Critics have charged symbolic anthropology with neglecting material conditions, power and history, and with treating cultures as coherent texts while overstating the anthropologist's ability to read them. Even so, its emphasis on meaning, ritual and interpretation permanently reshaped the discipline and influenced history and literary studies.
