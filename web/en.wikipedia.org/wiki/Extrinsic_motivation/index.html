Extrinsic motivation

Extrinsic motivation
FieldPsychology, organisational behaviour
DefinitionBehaviour driven by external rewards or pressures
ContrastIntrinsic motivation
Key theorySelf-determination theory

Extrinsic motivation refers to behaviour that is driven by external rewards such as money, grades, fame, praise, or by avoidance of punishment. This contrasts with intrinsic motivation, which refers to doing an activity because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable. In self-determination theory, extrinsic motivations range along a continuum from purely external regulation through introjected, identified and integrated regulation, the latter forms being progressively more autonomous as the person internalises the value of the activity.

Types

  • External regulation: acting purely to obtain a reward or avoid a punishment.
  • Introjected regulation: acting to avoid guilt or anxiety, or to protect self-esteem.
  • Identified regulation: acting because the person consciously values the goal.
  • Integrated regulation: the extrinsic goal is fully assimilated with the person's values and identity.

Overjustification effect

A substantial body of research shows that expected tangible rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for already interesting tasks — the overjustification effect. Experiments by Mark Lepper and colleagues, and meta-analyses by Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan, found that contingent rewards reduce subsequent free-choice engagement, while informational verbal praise may enhance intrinsic motivation. This has practical implications for education, workplace incentive design and parenting.

Applications

Extrinsic motivators are used in performance pay, sales commissions, loyalty programmes, grades and gamification. Their effectiveness depends on task type: for routine, uninteresting work, rewards tend to improve performance, whereas for creative and heuristic tasks they can be counterproductive.
