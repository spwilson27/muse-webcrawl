Third Anglo-Ashanti War

The Third Anglo-Ashanti War, also known as the "First Expedition to Kumasi", was fought between the British Empire and the Ashanti Empire (Asante) of the Gold Coast (modern Ghana) from December 1873 to February 1874. A British expeditionary force under Major-General Sir Garnet Wolseley marched inland, defeated the Ashanti army at Amoaful, occupied and burned the Ashanti capital Kumasi, and withdrew after imposing the Treaty of Fomena. The campaign is the best known of the five Anglo-Ashanti wars; its combination of steam logistics, telegraphy, breech-loading rifles and organised African carrier corps made it a model "small war" of the Victorian era.

Background

Ashanti power rested on control of trade routes between the forest interior and the coast. In 1872 the Dutch ceded their Gold Coast forts, including Elmina, to Britain; the Ashanti claimed Elmina as their tributary outlet to the sea, and in 1873 an Ashanti army invaded the British-protected coastal states, defeating allied Fante forces. London authorised a punitive expedition. Wolseley arrived at Cape Coast in October 1873 with a small cadre of officers, organised some 2,500 British troops (landed in December–January) with West India regiments, naval brigades and thousands of local carriers, and built a road, telegraph line and chain of stockaded depots through the forest to the Pra River.

Campaign

Crossing the Pra in January 1874, Wolseley's column fought the hard action of Amoaful on 31 January, where Ashanti musketeers in forest ambuscade inflicted heavy casualties — including on the 42nd Highlanders — before disciplined volley fire and the bayonet carried the position; the villages of Becquah and the Ashanti camp were taken in the following days. The Asantehene Kofi Karikari fled, and on 4 February the British entered an abandoned Kumasi, looted the royal palace of its gold regalia and burned the town. Threatened by the approaching rains and sickness, Wolseley withdrew to the coast. The Treaty of Fomena (signed in 1874) obliged the Ashanti to pay an indemnity of 50,000 ounces of gold, renounce claims to Elmina and the coastal states, and keep the trade roads open.

Consequences

The campaign made Wolseley's reputation and popularised the phrase "all Sir Garnet" (meaning everything in order). Britain formally annexed the coastal protectorate as the Gold Coast Crown Colony in July 1874. Ashanti, weakened and soon convulsed by civil war, faced further British expeditions in 1896 (when Asantehene Prempeh I was exiled) and 1900 (the War of the Golden Stool) before annexation in 1902.
