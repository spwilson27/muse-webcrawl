Petrolatum, commonly called petroleum jelly, is a semi-solid mixture of hydrocarbons, originally promoted as a topical ointment for its supposed healing properties. It is obtained from petroleum by dewaxing heavy lubricating oils; refined white petrolatum is widely used in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, while less refined grades are used industrially.

History

In 1859, workers on Pennsylvania oil rigs noticed a waxy residue ("rod wax") building up on drilling equipment, which they used on cuts and burns. The chemist Robert Chesebrough investigated the material, refined it, and in 1872 patented his process, marketing the product as Vaseline. It became a household staple worldwide, though many early medical claims for it were exaggerated.

Properties and uses

Petrolatum is hydrophobic, odourless when refined, and an effective occlusive: applied to skin it forms a barrier that reduces transepidermal water loss, and dermatologists recommend it for moisturising, protecting minor wounds and burns, preventing diaper rash, and managing eczema. It is generally regarded as non-comedogenic in its refined form and has very low allergenicity. Industrial uses include lubrication, rust prevention, and leather conditioning. Unrefined or poorly refined petrolatum may contain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; cosmetic and pharmaceutical grades are therefore highly purified.
