In linguistics, modality is the phenomenon whereby language is used to qualify a statement about the world — to express possibility, necessity, obligation, permission, ability, desire or degrees of commitment to truth. It is one of the major domains of grammatical meaning, alongside tense and aspect.

Types of modality

Epistemic modality concerns the speaker's judgement about the likelihood or truth of a proposition: "She must be home by now" (inference), "He might come" (possibility). Deontic modality concerns obligation and permission, typically imposed by rules or authority: "You must wear a seatbelt", "You may leave". Dynamic modality concerns ability and willingness inherent in the subject: "She can swim". Further types sometimes distinguished include evidentiality (marking the source of information), volition and circumstantial possibility, which depends on external facts rather than on rules or knowledge.

Expression across languages

English expresses modality mainly with the modal auxiliary verbs — can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would — supplemented by adverbs such as possibly and necessarily. Other languages use dedicated modal verbs, verbal affixes, clitics, particles or mood systems such as the subjunctive of Romance languages. In formal semantics, modality is standardly analysed with possible-worlds machinery borrowed from modal logic: necessity means truth in all accessible worlds, possibility truth in at least one.
