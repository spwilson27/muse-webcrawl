Mont Pelerin Society

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mont Pelerin Society (MPS) is an international organisation of economists, philosophers, historians and business leaders founded in 1947 by Friedrich Hayek to preserve and spread classical liberal ideas at a moment when collectivism seemed ascendant. Named for the Swiss resort above Lake Geneva where 39 scholars met from 1 to 10 April 1947 (among them Milton Friedman, George Stigler, Karl Popper and Ludwig von Mises, with journalists and industrialists in support), the Society committed itself to long-term intellectual work — no manifestos, no party — through closed conferences and personal networks.

Historians of neoliberalism treat the MPS as the movement's coordinating nucleus: its members founded the think-tank archipelago (the Institute of Economic Affairs, 1955; the Heritage Foundation and Atlas Network later) that carried free-market ideas into policy in the Thatcher–Reagan era and beyond, and eight of its members received Nobel Memorial Prizes in economics (the commonly cited count, including Hayek and Friedman). Membership is by invitation and has numbered in the several hundreds; internal feuds (over method, over Mises's and Hayek's divergent liberalisms) punctuate its history. Whether the Society was a scholarly forum or, in critics' accounts, the general staff of a “thought collective” remains the organising debate of its historiography.
