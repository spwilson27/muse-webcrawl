Vitis vinifera, the common grape vine, is a species of flowering plant in the family Vitaceae, native to the Mediterranean region, Central Europe and southwestern Asia. It is the principal species used in wine production worldwide, and is also cultivated for table grapes, raisins, juice and grape seed oil.

Description

V. vinifera is a liana that climbs by tendrils and can grow 12–18 metres tall when unchecked. Its leaves are lobed and toothed, its flowers small and greenish in panicles, and its fruit — berries in clusters — range from green to deep purple-black. Thousands of cultivars (varieties) exist, including Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Merlot, Pinot noir and Riesling.

History and cultivation

The species was domesticated from the wild subspecies sylvestris in the South Caucasus and Near East between roughly 6000 and 8000 years ago, spreading with Neolithic agriculture and later with Greek, Phoenician and Roman trade. Because V. vinifera is highly susceptible to the root louse phylloxera, which devastated European vineyards in the 19th century, most vines today are grafted onto resistant American rootstocks derived from species such as V. riparia and V. rupestris.

Uses

About 70–80% of the harvest goes to winemaking, the rest to table fruit, raisins and juice. Grapes are also a source of tartaric acid, grape seed extract and polyphenols such as resveratrol. The species accounts for the overwhelming majority of the world's vineyard area, commonly estimated at around 7 million hectares globally across all grape uses.
