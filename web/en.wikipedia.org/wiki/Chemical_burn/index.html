Chemical burn

A chemical burn caused by exposure to a corrosive substance.

A chemical burn is damage to the skin or other tissues caused by contact with a corrosive or irritant chemical, most commonly strong acids or bases (alkalis). Unlike thermal burns, chemical injury continues as long as the agent remains in contact with the tissue, and damage may deepen over hours; some agents, such as hydrofluoric acid, also cause systemic poisoning. Chemical burns occur in industrial accidents, laboratory work, household exposure to cleaning products, and in assaults with acid.

Mechanisms

Chemical burn
Causes: Corrosive acids, alkalis, oxidizers, vesicants
Mechanisms: Coagulative necrosis (acids); liquefactive necrosis (alkalis)
First aid: Remove agent; copious water irrigation
Specialty: Emergency medicine, burn care

Acids typically cause coagulative necrosis, producing an eschar that limits deeper penetration, whereas alkalis cause liquefactive necrosis, saponifying fats and dissolving proteins, so they penetrate more deeply and are usually more destructive. Oxidizing agents, reducing agents, desiccants and vesicants (blistering agents such as mustard gas) injure tissue by their own characteristic mechanisms. Severity depends on the agent's concentration, the quantity, and the duration of contact.

Signs, symptoms and treatment

Symptoms range from redness and irritation to severe pain, blistering, blackened or whitened dead skin, and — with eye exposure — loss of vision. Ingested corrosives can perforate the oesophagus and stomach. Immediate treatment consists of removing contaminated clothing and the agent from the skin, followed by prolonged irrigation with water; neutralization on the skin is generally avoided because it can generate heat. Specific antidotes exist for a few agents: calcium gluconate for hydrofluoric acid burns is the classic example. Deep burns are managed like thermal burns, sometimes with excision and skin grafting, and may leave strictures, contractures and scarring.

Prevention and acid attacks

Prevention relies on protective equipment, labelling, safe storage and regulation of corrosive sales. Deliberate acid attacks remain a serious problem in some countries and have prompted legislation controlling the sale of strong acids and prescribing severe penalties.
