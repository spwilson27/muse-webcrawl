Chang: A Drama of the Wilderness

Chang: A Drama of the Wilderness, also known simply as Chang (Thai for "elephant") and as Chang: The Man from the Jungle, is a 1927 American silent documentary film directed by Merian C. Cooper and Ernest B. Schoedsack, the team later responsible for King Kong (1933). Shot in the jungles of Siam (now Thailand), the film presents — in a dramatised documentary style — the struggle of a poor farmer and his family against wild animals as they attempt to clear and defend a homestead. Chang was one of the films nominated for the Academy Award for Unique and Artistic Production at the first Academy Awards ceremony in 1929.

Plot

Kru, a Lao farmer living at the edge of the jungle in northern Siam, builds a house and clears land for rice, but his crops and livestock are continually threatened by leopards, tigers, snakes and a herd of wild elephants. With the help of his family and neighbours, Kru traps a leopard, survives a tiger attack, and finally confronts the elephant herd that stampedes through the village — "Chang", the wild elephant, being the embodiment of the jungle's menace. The film ends with the capture of a baby elephant, which the family tames, suggesting a truce between man and wilderness.

Production

Following their earlier documentary Grass (1925), Cooper and Schoedsack spent some eighteen months in Siam filming with the cooperation of the Siamese royal family. The human story was staged with a real local family, but the animal sequences were photographed in the wild or in prepared clearings, at considerable danger: Schoedsack shot charging animals at close range, and a famous scene of a tiger leaping toward the camera was filmed through a protective pit arrangement. The climactic elephant stampede, in which a herd demolishes the village set, became the film's signature spectacle and reportedly cost the lives of no animals, though animal-welfare standards of the era were lax and some animals were killed for dramatic scenes.

Reception and legacy

The film was a commercial and critical success on release and demonstrated that Cooper and Schoedsack could combine documentary footage with narrative drama — a formula they carried to its extreme in King Kong. At the first Academy Awards, Chang was nominated in the one-time category of Unique and Artistic Production, losing to Sunrise: A Song of Two Humans. Restorations of the film have been issued on home video, and it is now regarded as a classic of early documentary and adventure cinema, albeit one viewed critically for its staged scenes and colonial-era attitudes.
