Charitable trust

A charitable trust is an irrevocable trust established for charitable purposes. Unlike a private trust, it benefits the public (or a section of it) rather than identifiable individuals, and it is generally exempt from the rule against perpetuities, so it may last indefinitely. In common-law jurisdictions charitable trusts are supervised by the courts and by public officials such as the state attorney general or, in England and Wales, the Charity Commission.

Charitable purposes

The classic statement of charitable purposes comes from the preamble to the English Statute of Charitable Uses 1601. In Commissioners for Special Purposes of Income Tax v Pemsel (1891), Lord Macnaghten grouped them into four heads: the relief of poverty, the advancement of education, the advancement of religion, and other purposes beneficial to the community. Modern statutes, such as the Charities Act 2011 in England and Wales, list a wider set of purposes and require that each be for the public benefit.

Cy-près

When a charitable purpose fails or becomes impracticable — for example, a gift to a charity that no longer exists — the court may apply the property cy-près ("as near as possible") to a similar charitable purpose, rather than letting the trust fail, provided the settlor showed a general charitable intent.

Tax treatment

Charitable trusts commonly enjoy exemption from income and other taxes, and donors may receive deductions or relief on gifts, subject to the rules of the jurisdiction. In the United States, charitable trusts must generally be organised and operated exclusively for purposes recognised under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code to obtain federal tax exemption. Split-interest vehicles such as charitable remainder trusts and charitable lead trusts combine charitable and private interests.
