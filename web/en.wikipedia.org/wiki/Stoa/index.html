A stoa (plural stoas, stoai, or stoae), in ancient Greek architecture, is a covered walkway or portico, commonly for public use. Early stoas were open at the entrance with columns, usually of the Doric order, lining the side of the building; they thus created a safe, enveloping, protective atmosphere.

This, an architectural form widespread in the ancient world, was also used by the ancient Greeks as the place where philosophy was taught; the philosophical school of Stoicism takes its name from the Stoa Poikile ("Painted Porch") in Athens, where its founder Zeno of Citium taught.

Structure

A typical stoa consisted of a long, narrow building with a roof supported by one or two rows of columns along its open front, and a solid wall at the back, sometimes divided into rooms. Stoas often surrounded public spaces such as the agora (marketplace) and sanctuaries, providing shelter from sun and rain for merchants, shoppers, and gatherings.

Famous stoas

The Stoa Poikile (c. 460 BC) in Athens was decorated with paintings of famous battles, including the Battle of Marathon. The Stoa of Attalos in the Agora of Athens, built by King Attalos II of Pergamon in the 2nd century BC, was reconstructed in the 1950s and houses the Museum of the Ancient Agora. The Royal Stoa served as the office of the king archon. In the Roman period, monumental stoas with two storeys became common in the cities of the eastern Mediterranean.
