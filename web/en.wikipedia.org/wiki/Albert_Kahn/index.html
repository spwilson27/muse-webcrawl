Albert Kahn (21 March 1869 – 8 December 1942) was a German-born American architect, often called "the architect of Detroit." He was a leading designer of industrial buildings in the early 20th century and, through his firm Albert Kahn Associates, designed hundreds of factories for the automobile industry, including the great Ford River Rouge Complex.

Early life and career

Kahn was born in Rhaunen, in the Rhine Province of Prussia, and emigrated with his family to Detroit in 1881. Largely self-educated, he apprenticed with the Detroit firm of Mason & Rice, won a scholarship to study architecture in Europe in 1891, and in 1895 founded his own practice with George Nettleton and Alexander Trowbridge. After his partners' departures he continued on his own, forming the firm that became Albert Kahn, Inc.

Industrial architecture

Working with his brother Julius Kahn, an engineer who patented a reinforced-concrete truss system, Kahn revolutionized factory design. Their concrete-frame buildings — beginning with the Packard Motor Car Company plant in 1903 — offered wide, fireproof, naturally lit interiors suited to the moving assembly line. Kahn's firm designed plants for Packard, Hudson, Ford, General Motors, Chrysler and many others, including the Ford Highland Park Plant and the vast River Rouge Complex in Dearborn.

Other work

Beyond factories, Kahn designed prominent commercial, civic and institutional buildings in Detroit, including the Fisher Building (1928), the General Motors Building, the Detroit Athletic Club, the Detroit News Building and numerous buildings for the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor. During the Second World War his office converted to defense work, designing plants and airfields, and between 1929 and 1932 the firm also designed hundreds of factories in the Soviet Union under contract with the Soviet government.

Death and legacy

Kahn died in Detroit on 8 December 1942. His firm, Albert Kahn Associates, continues in practice. His work is credited with transforming industrial construction and giving architectural form to mass production.
