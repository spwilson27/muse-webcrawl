Mosquito control

Mosquito control manages the population of mosquitoes to reduce their damage to human health, economies, and enjoyment. Mosquitoes transmit pathogens that cause diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, Zika virus, West Nile virus, chikungunya, yellow fever, and lymphatic filariasis, and control efforts are a major component of public health in much of the world.

Methods

Source reduction

Because mosquitoes lay eggs in standing water, eliminating or managing larval habitats — draining marshes, emptying containers, covering water storage, and maintaining drainage — is the oldest and often the most sustainable control method. In the early twentieth century, large drainage and oiling campaigns helped suppress malaria in parts of the United States, Italy, and the Panama Canal Zone.

Chemical control

Larvicides such as Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti), methoprene, and monomolecular films kill larvae in water. Adulticides, typically pyrethroids or organophosphates, are applied as space sprays or ultra-low-volume aerosols, often by truck or aircraft during outbreaks. Insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying with insecticides such as DDT were central to the global malaria eradication campaigns of the mid-twentieth century, though resistance has since spread widely.

Biological control

Natural enemies including larvivorous fish such as mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis), copepods, dragonfly nymphs, and bacterial agents are used to suppress larvae. Infection of mosquitoes with the bacterium Wolbachia, which reduces their ability to transmit dengue and other viruses, has been deployed in field releases in Australia, Indonesia, Brazil, and elsewhere with significant reductions in dengue incidence.

Genetic and sterile techniques

The sterile insect technique releases large numbers of radiation-sterilized males. Genetic approaches include releasing males engineered with a self-limiting gene (as developed by Oxitec), and research into gene drives designed to spread sterility or disease resistance through wild populations — promising, but raising ecological and ethical questions that remain under active debate.

Personal protection

Repellents such as DEET and picaridin, protective clothing, window screens, and bed nets reduce individual exposure and are often the first line of defence where organized control programmes are absent.
