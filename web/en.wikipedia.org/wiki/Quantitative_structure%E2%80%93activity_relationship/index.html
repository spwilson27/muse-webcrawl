Quantitative structure–activity relationship

Quantitative structure–activity relationship models (QSAR models) are regression or classification models used in the chemical and biological sciences and engineering. Like other regression models, QSAR regression models relate a set of "predictor" variables (X) to the potency of the response variable (Y), while classification QSAR models relate the predictor variables to a categorical value of the response variable.

In QSAR modeling of biological activity, the predictors are the structural or physicochemical properties of the molecules, and the response is a biological activity, such as the binding affinity of a drug to its target. QSAR models can be used in drug discovery to rank candidate molecules, predict toxicity, and understand structure–activity relationships.

History

The foundations of QSAR are often traced to the work of Hansch and Leo in the 1960s, who related the biological activity of compounds to their hydrophobic, electronic, and steric properties using multiple linear regression. The Free–Wilson analysis, published at about the same time, used indicator variables for substituents. Modern QSAR makes use of machine learning methods including artificial neural networks, random forests, and support vector machines, applied to molecular descriptors calculated by cheminformatics software.

Applications

QSAR is widely used in pharmaceutical research, in the prediction of ADMET properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity), and in regulatory toxicology, where models such as those used by the OECD QSAR Toolbox support the assessment of chemical safety. The validity of QSAR models has been codified in the OECD principles for validation, which require a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined domain of applicability, appropriate measures of goodness-of-fit, robustness and predictivity, and a mechanistic interpretation where possible.
