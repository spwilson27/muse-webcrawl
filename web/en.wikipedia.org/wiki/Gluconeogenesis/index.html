Gluconeogenesis

Gluconeogenesis (GNG) is a metabolic pathway by which certain organs, principally the liver and to a lesser extent the kidney cortex, synthesise glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors such as lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids. It is essential for maintaining blood glucose during fasting, because the brain, red blood cells, renal medulla and some other tissues depend heavily on glucose.

Pathway

Gluconeogenesis shares most steps with glycolysis run in reverse, but the three irreversible glycolytic steps are bypassed by distinct enzymes. Pyruvate is first carboxylated to oxaloacetate by pyruvate carboxylase in mitochondria, then converted to phosphoenolpyruvate by PEPCK. Later, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase bypasses phosphofructokinase, and glucose-6-phosphatase — found only in liver and kidney — releases free glucose into the blood.

Substrates and the Cori cycle

Lactate produced by exercising muscle and red blood cells returns to the liver in the Cori cycle. Glycerol from triglyceride breakdown enters at the level of dihydroxyacetone phosphate, and glucogenic amino acids such as alanine and glutamine feed in at pyruvate or cycle intermediates. In ruminants, propionate from rumen fermentation is the principal substrate.

Regulation

The pathway is stimulated by glucagon and cortisol during fasting and inhibited by insulin. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate reciprocally regulates glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, preventing a futile cycle. Gluconeogenesis is energetically expensive, consuming four ATP, two GTP and two NADH per glucose formed. In uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, excessive hepatic gluconeogenesis contributes to hyperglycaemia, and the drug metformin acts in part by suppressing it.
