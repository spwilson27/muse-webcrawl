The Authoritarian Personality

The Authoritarian Personality is a 1950 social psychology book by Theodor W. Adorno, Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and Nevitt Sanford, produced at the University of California, Berkeley, as part of the Studies in Prejudice series sponsored by the American Jewish Committee. One of the most influential works in postwar social science, it sought to explain the psychological roots of fascism and ethnic prejudice in the wake of the Second World War and the Holocaust.

The F-scale

The authors developed a questionnaire instrument, the F-scale (for "fascism"), designed to measure an underlying authoritarian character structure indirectly, without mentioning Jews or other target groups. High scorers, the authors argued, tended to share a syndrome of nine interrelated traits: conventionalism, authoritarian submission, authoritarian aggression, anti-intraception (rejection of introspection and imagination), superstition and stereotypy, preoccupation with power and toughness, destructiveness and cynicism, projectivity, and exaggerated concern with sexual conduct. The theory drew on Freudian psychoanalysis: harsh, punitive, status-anxious parenting was said to produce repressed hostility that is displaced onto out-groups.

Criticism and legacy

The book was heavily criticized on methodological grounds: the F-scale items were all worded in the same direction, making scores vulnerable to acquiescence bias; the samples were not representative; and later researchers such as Milton Rokeach argued that authoritarianism exists on the political left as well as the right. Subsequent research revised the concept into "right-wing authoritarianism" (Bob Altemeyer's RWA scale), focusing on submission, aggression and conventionalism. Despite its flaws, The Authoritarian Personality founded the modern study of prejudice and authoritarianism and remains a landmark in psychology and political science.
