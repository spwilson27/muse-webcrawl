Mainframe computer

TypeLarge-scale computer systems
Principal makerIBM (IBM Z family); others historically include Unisys, Fujitsu, Hitachi
StrengthsTransaction throughput, I/O capacity, reliability, security, virtualisation
Typical usersBanks, insurers, governments, airlines, large retailers

A mainframe computer, informally called a mainframe or "big iron", is a large computer system used primarily by big organisations for bulk data processing and very high-volume transaction processing, such as banking, insurance, census, and reservation systems.

Characteristics

Mainframes are distinguished by massive internal data throughput, huge numbers of simultaneous users and virtual machines, extensive redundancy enabling non-stop operation, and long backward compatibility. The dominant modern line is IBM Z, running operating systems such as z/OS (descended from MVS), z/VM, and Linux.

History

The term arose in the 1950s–60s around systems like IBM's System/360 (1964), which unified a compatible family of machines. Despite repeated predictions of the mainframe's demise since the 1990s, the class persists in finance and government because of its unmatched throughput, data integrity, and total cost profile at extreme scale.
