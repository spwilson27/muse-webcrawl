Bistability

ConceptBistability
FieldDynamical systems, physics, biology
Stable statesTwo
RelatedMultistability, hysteresis

In a dynamical system, bistability means the system has two stable equilibrium states. A bistable system can be understood in terms of a potential energy surface with two local minima separated by a local maximum (an unstable equilibrium point). A particle or state resting in one of the minima will not spontaneously move to the other; an external input of sufficient energy is required to push it over the barrier.

Mathematical description

A simple one-dimensional model is the differential equation dx/dt = r + x − x³, or the gradient dynamics of the double-well potential V(x) = x⁴/4 − x²/2. As a control parameter varies, bistability typically appears and disappears through a saddle-node bifurcation, and the region of bistability is often accompanied by hysteresis: the state of the system depends on its history, not just on the current parameter values.

Examples

  • Electronics: a flip-flop or Schmitt trigger has two stable output states, forming the basis of digital memory.
  • Mechanics: a light switch or a ball resting in either of two wells of a double-well potential.
  • Biology: bistable biochemical networks, such as the lac operon and cell-cycle switches, allow cells to commit to one of two fates; the lac repressor system shows hysteresis in response to inducer concentration.
  • Perception: bistable (multistable) perception occurs with ambiguous figures such as the Necker cube, which flip between two interpretations.
  • Optics: optical bistability in nonlinear resonators, used in all-optical switching.
