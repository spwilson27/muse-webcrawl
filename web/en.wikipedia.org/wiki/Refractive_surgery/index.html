Laser refractive surgery reshapes the cornea to correct the eye's focusing power.

Refractive surgery is elective surgery on the eye that changes its refractive state to correct vision disorders such as myopia (short-sightedness), hyperopia (long-sightedness), astigmatism, and presbyopia, reducing or eliminating dependence on glasses and contact lenses. Most procedures reshape the cornea — the eye's principal focusing surface — with an excimer laser; others implant an artificial lens or replace the natural lens.

History

Early surgical correction of refraction included José Barraquer's keratomileusis in the 1950s–60s, in which corneal tissue was removed, reshaped, and reattached, and the radial keratotomy popularised by Svyatoslav Fyodorov in the 1970s, which flattened the cornea with spoke-like incisions. The excimer laser's ability to ablate tissue precisely enabled photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) in the late 1980s and then LASIK in the 1990s, which became the world's dominant refractive procedure.

Procedures

  • LASIK — a thin corneal flap is created (by microkeratome or femtosecond laser), the underlying bed is reshaped with the excimer laser, and the flap is replaced.
  • PRK — the corneal epithelium is removed and the surface ablated directly; no flap, but slower, more uncomfortable recovery.
  • SMILE — a femtosecond laser cuts a lens-shaped piece of tissue (a lenticule) inside the cornea, removed through a small incision; used mainly for myopia.
  • Phakic intraocular lenses — an implantable lens is placed in front of the natural lens, useful for high prescriptions unsuitable for laser surgery.
  • Refractive lens exchange — the natural lens is replaced with an intraocular lens, as in cataract surgery, often chosen by older patients.

Risks and outcomes

Modern laser procedures achieve uncorrected vision of 20/20 or better in the large majority of suitable candidates, and patient satisfaction is generally high. Complications include dry eye, glare, halos and starbursts at night, under- or over-correction, flap complications (LASIK), haze (PRK), and rarely corneal ectasia — progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea. Careful screening for corneal thickness, topography, and stable prescription is essential; the procedures do not stop the eye's later ageing changes such as presbyopia or cataracts.
