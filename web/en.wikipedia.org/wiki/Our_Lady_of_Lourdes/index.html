Our Lady of Lourdes is a title of the Virgin Mary in honour of the Marian apparitions reported in 1858 by Bernadette Soubirous, a fourteen-year-old peasant girl, in the grotto of Massabielle near Lourdes in the French Pyrenees. Lourdes has since become one of the most visited Catholic pilgrimage sites in the world, famed for the spring of water believed to have healing properties.

The apparitions

Between 11 February and 16 July 1858, Bernadette reported eighteen visions of a lady she described as "a small young lady" (in her Occitan dialect, aquero), who eventually identified herself, in the local patois, as "Que soy era Immaculada Counceptiou" — "I am the Immaculate Conception" — four years after Pope Pius IX had defined that doctrine. During the apparitions the lady instructed Bernadette to pray for sinners, to have a chapel built, and to drink and wash in a spring that Bernadette uncovered by digging in the mud of the grotto. A diocesan inquiry authorised by the Bishop of Tarbes concluded in 1862 that the apparitions were authentic.

The sanctuary

The Sanctuary of Our Lady of Lourdes now comprises the grotto, the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary (completed 1899), the Basilica of St. Pius X (completed 1958, one of the world's largest churches), baths, and extensive hospitality for the sick. Some five to six million pilgrims visit each year, including many organised "malades" processions of the sick and disabled. The sanctuary records claimed cures, of which the Church's medical bureau has recognised a small number as medically inexplicable after rigorous investigation.

Cultural impact

Bernadette Soubirous was canonised in 1933, and her feast day is 16 April. The apparitions inspired works such as Franz Werfel's novel The Song of Bernadette (1941) and the subsequent film, as well as countless replicas of the Lourdes grotto worldwide. Lourdes water is bottled and carried home by pilgrims, and the site remains a major economic engine of the region.
