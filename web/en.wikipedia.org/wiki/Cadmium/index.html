Cadmium is a chemical element with the symbol Cd and atomic number 48. This soft, silvery-white metal is chemically similar to the two other stable metals in group 12, zinc and mercury. Like zinc, it demonstrates oxidation state +2 in most of its compounds; like mercury, it has a lower melting point (321 °C) than the transition metals.

History

Cadmium was discovered in contaminated zinc compounds in 1817 by Friedrich Stromeyer (and independently by Karl Samuel Leberecht Hermann) in Germany. Stromeyer named it after cadmia, the Latin name for calamine, a zinc mineral itself named for the Greek mythological figure Cadmus. It was produced industrially from the early 20th century as a by-product of zinc smelting.

Applications

Cadmium compounds have been used as pigments (the brilliant cadmium yellow, orange, and red paints), as stabilisers in PVC, and in nickel–cadmium rechargeable batteries. Cadmium telluride (CdTe) is used in thin-film solar cells; cadmium sulfide is a photoconductor; and cadmium's high neutron-absorption cross-section makes it useful in control rods of nuclear reactors. Most cadmium is still obtained as a by-product of zinc production.

Toxicity

Cadmium and its compounds are highly toxic and carcinogenic. Chronic exposure damages the kidneys, softens bones, and damages the lungs; the most infamous episode was itai-itai ("ouch-ouch") disease in Toyama Prefecture, Japan, where mining waste contaminated rice paddies. Because of its toxicity, cadmium use is heavily restricted, for example under the European Union's RoHS directive, and nickel–cadmium batteries have largely been displaced by lithium-ion types.

Occurrence

There are no significant cadmium ores; the only notable mineral is greenockite (CdS). Cadmium occurs at roughly 0.1 ppm in the Earth's crust and accumulates in the food chain, notably in rice, shellfish, and organ meats.
