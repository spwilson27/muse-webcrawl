Leó Szilárd

Leó Szilárd (Hungarian: Szilárd Leó; born Leó Spitz; February 11, 1898 – May 30, 1964) was a Hungarian-born American physicist and inventor. Trained in Berlin, he conceived the nuclear chain reaction in 1933, co-wrote the letter that launched the Manhattan Project, and with Enrico Fermi co-invented (and held the patent on) the nuclear reactor. After the Second World War Szilárd became an influential advocate of civilian control of atomic energy, founded the Council for a Livable World, and shifted his research to molecular biology. He was one of the "Martians" — the group of Hungarian émigré scientists who reshaped 20th-century physics.

Early life and Berlin years

Szilárd was born in Budapest into a middle-class Jewish family; the family name was changed from Spitz to Szilárd in 1900. His studies in electrical engineering were interrupted by service in the Austro-Hungarian army in the First World War. After the collapse of the Hungarian Soviet Republic he emigrated to Berlin in 1919, where he took his doctorate under Max von Laue with a celebrated dissertation on thermodynamics that gave the first clear analysis of the information content in Maxwell's demon (the "Szilárd engine"). He worked as assistant to Laue and became a Privatdozent, filing numerous inventive patents, including — with Albert Einstein — the Einstein–Szilárd refrigerator, an absorption refrigerator with no moving parts.

The chain reaction and the road to the bomb

After the Nazi takeover Szilárd fled to London in 1933. Reading H. G. Wells's The World Set Free and, that September, an argument by Ernest Rutherford dismissing atomic energy as "moonshine", Szilárd — by his own account while waiting at a traffic light in Southampton Row — conceived the idea of a neutron-driven nuclear chain reaction: a neutron absorbed by a nucleus that then emits two neutrons could multiply without limit. He filed a patent on the chain reaction in 1934 (assigning it to the British Admiralty for secrecy). In 1939, after the discovery of fission, Szilárd and Walter Zinn at Columbia confirmed neutron multiplication in uranium fission. Convinced that Nazi Germany might build a bomb, Szilárd drafted the famous Einstein–Szilárd letter of August 1939 to President Franklin D. Roosevelt, which Einstein signed and which set in motion the American atomic programme.

Manhattan Project and dissent

At the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory, Szilárd worked with Fermi on the first reactor, Chicago Pile-1 (December 1942), and co-held the basic reactor patent. Increasingly troubled by the military use of the bomb and by secrecy, he drafted the Franck Report arguments in petition form: the Szilárd petition of July 1945, signed by 70 Chicago scientists, urged that Japan be given a demonstration and warning before the bomb was used on a city. It was not delivered to President Truman before the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. After the war Szilárd campaigned for the McMahon Act's civilian control of atomic energy, co-founded the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists, and later founded the Council for a Livable World (1962) to lobby for arms control. His satirical story "My Trial as a War Criminal" and the collection The Voice of the Dolphins (1961) reflect his political thought.

Biology and later life

From 1946 Szilárd turned to biology at the University of Chicago, contributing ideas on bacterial growth (with Aaron Novick), enzyme feedback, ageing theory, and the founding of the institute that became the Salk Institute for Biological Studies, where he was a resident fellow at his death. Diagnosed with bladder cancer in 1960, he designed his own radiation treatment plan and survived; he died of a heart attack in La Jolla in 1964.
