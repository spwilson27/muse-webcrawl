Gross negligence manslaughter

Gross negligence manslaughter is a form of involuntary manslaughter in English law, and in other common-law jurisdictions, where the death results from a grossly negligent act or omission by the defendant. It applies where the defendant owed the deceased a duty of care, breached that duty in a way that caused or significantly contributed to the death, and the breach was so serious ("gross") that it amounts to a criminal act or omission.

Elements of the offence

The modern test derives from R v Bateman (1925), in which a doctor was convicted after a childbirth death, and R v Adomako (1994), in which an anaesthetist failed to notice that a patient's ventilator had become disconnected. The House of Lords in Adomako held that the prosecution must prove:

1. the existence of a duty of care owed by the defendant to the victim;
2. a breach of that duty;
3. that the breach caused the death; and
4. that the breach was grossly negligent — i.e. that the conduct was, in the jury's view, so bad in all the circumstances as to amount to a criminal act or omission.

Later case law, including R v Misra (2004), rejected challenges that the test was too circular or uncertain to comply with the European Convention on Human Rights, and R v Rose (2017) clarified that foreseeability of a serious and obvious risk of death must be assessed on the facts reasonably available to the defendant at the time of the breach.

Contexts

Prosecutions most commonly arise in medical practice, workplace deaths, and the operation of vehicles and vessels. Organisations may separately be prosecuted for corporate manslaughter under the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007. The maximum sentence for gross negligence manslaughter is life imprisonment, with sentencing guidelines providing starting points based on culpability.
