Icelandic folklore

Icelandic folklore comprises the folk tales, legends, and beliefs of Iceland, rooted in the island's Norse heritage and medieval sagas and Eddas, and shaped by its volcanic landscape, long winter darkness, and centuries of isolation. It remains a living presence in Icelandic culture, where belief in hidden beings has historically been widespread.

Elves and hidden people

The most famous figures are the huldufólk, or hidden people — beings resembling humans who live in rocks and hills in a parallel world. Closely related are the elves (álfar). Stories tell of people who encounter hidden folk, are warned not to disturb their dwellings, or are punished for doing so. Construction projects in Iceland are still sometimes said to be routed to avoid elf sites, and an official "elf school" in Reykjavík teaches the lore to visitors.

Trolls, ghosts, and monsters

Trolls (tröll) figure in many tales as powerful, often stupid giants turned to stone by sunlight — a motif invoked to explain Iceland's dramatic rock formations. Icelandic ghosts are typically corporeal and dangerous rather than spectral. The sea and lakes harbour monsters such as the Lagarfljót worm, a serpent said to inhabit Lake Lagarfljót. The thirteen Yule Lads (jólasveinar), mischievous troll-descended pranksters who visit children on the nights before Christmas, along with their mother, the child-eating ogress Grýla, and the giant Yule Cat, form a distinctive Christmas folklore.

Collections

The great 19th-century collection of Jón Árnason, inspired by the Brothers Grimm, preserved hundreds of Icelandic tales in print and remains the foundational source for the study of Icelandic folklore.
