Gavelkind

TypeSystem of land tenure and inheritance
RegionKent, England; also Ireland and Wales (analogous customs)
Abolished1925 (Administration of Estates Act)

Gavelkind (/ˈɡævəlkaɪnd/) was a system of land tenure associated principally with the county of Kent in England, under which land was inherited equally by all sons (partible inheritance) rather than passing intact to the eldest son under primogeniture. The word is thought to derive from Old English expressions connected with the payment of rent or tribute (gafol).

Characteristics

Under gavelkind, on the death of a tenant his lands were divided among all his sons (and, failing sons, among daughters), a custom sometimes summarised by the Kentish saying "the father to the bough, the son to the plough." Other distinctive features included the tenant's power to alienate land from the age of fifteen, the absence of escheat for felony — a felon's lands passed to his heir rather than to the Crown — and dower rights for widows amounting to half the land rather than a third.

History

The custom survived the Norman Conquest in Kent, which claimed the privilege of "Invicta" (unconquered). Over the centuries many landowners obtained private acts of Parliament to "disgavel" their estates. Gavelkind as a rule of descent was finally abolished by the property legislation of 1925, in particular the Administration of Estates Act 1925, which unified the rules of inheritance for real property. Analogous partible customs existed in Ireland (where gavelkind was suppressed under English rule in the early 17th century) and in parts of Wales.
