Hydrogen line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A radio telescope dish at night with a spectrum plot showing the sharp spike of the 21-cm line]
A radio telescope dish at night with a spectrum plot showing the sharp spike of the 21-cm line

The hydrogen line, or 21-centimetre line, is the electromagnetic radiation emitted when the electron in a neutral hydrogen atom flips its spin relative to the proton's (the hyperfine transition), at a frequency of 1420.4 MHz — a wavelength of 21.1 cm. Predicted by H. C. van de Hulst in 1944 and detected in 1951, it is radio astronomy's most important spectral line: because it penetrates dust, it maps the structure of the Milky Way and other galaxies and the distribution of neutral hydrogen across cosmic time.

Contents1. Physics
2. Astronomy

Physics

The transition between the two hyperfine ground states of hydrogen is extremely improbable for any one atom (a spontaneous lifetime of about 11 million years), but hydrogen's cosmic abundance makes the line bright in aggregate. Its frequency is so fundamental that it was chosen as the unit of frequency and length on the Pioneer plaque and in SETI messaging — the 'water hole' where civilisations might meet by radio.

Astronomy

21-cm surveys traced the Milky Way's spiral arms (1950s), measured galactic rotation curves — whose flatness beyond the visible disk became key evidence for Dark matter (Vera Rubin's optical work later confirmed) — and now map cosmic structure. Redshifted to metre wavelengths for the early universe, the line is the target of experiments (HERA, the Square Kilometre Array) seeking the signal of the cosmic dawn and reionisation, including the contested EDGES claim (2018) of an anomalously deep absorption trough.
