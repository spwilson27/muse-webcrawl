Complement system

The complement system, also known as the complement cascade, is a part of the immune system that enhances ("complements") the ability of antibodies and phagocytic cells to clear microbes and damaged cells from an organism, promote inflammation, and attack the pathogen's cell membrane. It is part of the innate immune system, which is not adaptable and does not change over the course of an individual's lifetime. The complement system consists of a number of small, inactive proteins made by the liver and present in the blood, which are activated by proteases in cascading sequence reactions.

Overview

Complement activation proceeds through three initiation pathways:

  • The classical pathway, triggered by antibody (IgM or IgG) bound to antigen, via the C1 complex.
  • The lectin pathway, triggered when mannose-binding lectin binds carbohydrate patterns on microbial surfaces.
  • The alternative pathway, continuously ticking over at a low level through spontaneous hydrolysis of C3, and amplified on surfaces lacking host regulatory proteins.

All three pathways converge on the formation of C3 convertase, which cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b. From this point the cascade produces three main effects:

  1. Opsonisation — coating of pathogens with C3b, which phagocytes recognise and engulf.
  2. Inflammation — the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a recruit and activate immune cells and increase vascular permeability.
  3. Membrane attack — assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b–C9), which forms pores in target cell membranes and can lyse bacteria and enveloped cells.

Regulation

Because the cascade is powerfully destructive, it is tightly regulated by proteins such as C1 inhibitor, factor H, factor I, CD46, CD55 (decay-accelerating factor), and CD59 (protectin), which protect host cells. Deficiencies in complement components predispose to recurrent infections — particularly with encapsulated bacteria and Neisseria — while regulatory defects cause diseases such as hereditary angioedema (C1 inhibitor deficiency), paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (loss of CD55/CD59), and atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome. Complement also contributes to immune-complex diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus.

History

The system was identified in the 1890s: Paul Ehrlich coined the term "complement", and Jules Bordet showed that fresh serum contained a heat-labile factor that complemented antibodies in destroying bacteria, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1919.
