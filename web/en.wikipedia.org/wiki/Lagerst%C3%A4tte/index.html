Lagerstätte

A Lagerstätte (plural Lagerstätten) is a sedimentary deposit that exhibits extraordinary fossils with exceptional preservation — sometimes including preserved soft tissues. The term is German, roughly meaning "storage place" or "mother lode", and was introduced into palaeontology by Adolf Seilacher in 1970. These formations may have resulted from carcass burial in an anoxic environment with minimal bacteria, delaying decomposition.

Types

Palaeontologists distinguish two kinds: Konzentrat-Lagerstätten ("concentration deposits"), with an abundance of disarticulated organic hard parts such as bones or shells, and Konservat-Lagerstätten ("conservation deposits"), where soft tissues are preserved as impressions or carbon films. The latter are rarer and disproportionately important for understanding the evolution of soft-bodied organisms.

Famous examples

Well-known Lagerstätten include the Cambrian Burgess Shale of British Columbia, the Jurassic Solnhofen Limestone of Germany (source of Archaeopteryx fossils), the Eocene Messel Pit near Frankfurt, the Cretaceous Santana Formation of Brazil, and the Ediacaran deposits of South Australia. Such sites provide windows into ancient ecosystems that the ordinary fossil record cannot supply.

Formation

Exceptional preservation generally requires rapid burial, low oxygen, and conditions that suppress scavenging and microbial decay — for example stagnant bottom waters, obrution deposits from sudden sediment flows, or hypersaline lagoons.
