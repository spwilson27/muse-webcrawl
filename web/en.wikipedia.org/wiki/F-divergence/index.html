f-divergence

In probability theory, an ƒ-divergence is a function Df(P ‖ Q) that measures the difference between two probability distributions P and Q. It is defined using a convex function f and generalises many notions of divergence, including the Kullback–Leibler divergence, total variation distance, Hellinger distance and the chi-squared divergence.

Definition

Let P and Q be probability distributions over a space Ω, with P absolutely continuous with respect to Q. For a convex function f with f(1) = 0, the ƒ-divergence is defined by an integral (or sum, in the discrete case) of f(dP/dQ) with respect to Q, where dP/dQ is the Radon–Nikodym derivative.

Properties

ƒ-divergences are non-negative and vanish exactly when P = Q. They satisfy the data-processing inequality: applying the same channel (stochastic transformation) to both distributions cannot increase the divergence. Most ƒ-divergences are not symmetric and do not satisfy the triangle inequality, so they are not metrics in general.

Examples

Choosing f(t) = t log t gives the Kullback–Leibler divergence; f(t) = |t − 1|/2 gives the total variation distance; and f(t) = (√t − 1)2 gives the squared Hellinger distance. ƒ-divergences play a central role in information theory, statistics, and variational inference, where they underlie methods such as variational autoencoders and f-GANs.
