Nebbiolo (Italian: [nebˈbjɔːlo]; Piedmontese: nebieul) is an Italian red wine grape variety predominantly associated with its native Piedmont region, where it makes the Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita (DOCG) wines of Barolo, Barbaresco, Gattinara, Ghemme and Roero. Nebbiolo is thought to derive its name from the Italian nebbia, meaning "fog", which characterises the Langhe hills in late October, when the variety — one of the last to be picked — is harvested.

Characteristics

Nebbiolo produces lightly coloured red wines which can be highly tannic in youth, with characteristic aromas of tar and roses. With age, the wines develop a brick-orange hue at the rim of the glass and reveal complex scents of violets, truffles, cherries, tobacco, dried herbs and liquorice. The grape buds early — exposing it to spring frosts — and ripens very late, and it is notoriously site-sensitive, performing best on calcareous marl soils on south- and southwest-facing slopes. Despite its prestige, it accounts for only a small share of Piedmont's production and has historically resisted transplantation: plantings outside Italy, in California, Australia, Mexico and elsewhere, have produced mixed results.

History

The variety is first mentioned in 1266 in the statutes of La Morra, and Andrea Bacci praised the wine of "Nebbiolo" in 1606. In the 19th century the modern dry style of Barolo was developed, traditionally credited to the Marchesa Giulia Falletti, with the help of the French oenologist Louis Oudart. DNA profiling has shown Nebbiolo to be related to several Piedmontese varieties and possibly to the Lombard grape Chiavennasca, as Nebbiolo is known in Valtellina, where it produces the wines of Valtellina Superiore and the dried-grape Sforzato.

Clones and synonyms

Important clonal forms include Lampia, Michet and Rosé, the latter now recognised as a distinct variety. Regionally the grape is known as Chiavennasca (Valtellina), Picotendro (Valle d'Aosta), Prünent (Val d'Ossola) and Spanna (northern Piedmont).
