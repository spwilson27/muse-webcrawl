Islamic geometric patterns are one of the major forms of Islamic ornament, which tends to avoid using figurative images, as it is forbidden to create a representation of an important Islamic figure (such as a prophet) according to many holy scriptures. The geometric designs in Islamic art are often built on combinations of repeated squares and circles, which may be overlapped and interlaced, as can arabesques (with which they are often combined), to form intricate and complex patterns, including a wide variety of tessellations.

These patterns adorn the walls of mosques and palaces as tilework, carved plaster and wood, pierced screens, and illuminated manuscripts. Western appreciation of the tradition grew through sites such as the Alhambra in Granada, whose tilings famously influenced the artist M. C. Escher after his visits in 1922 and 1936.

Construction methods

Historically, patterns were constructed with compass and straightedge on incised plaster or paper grids by master craftsmen whose workshop scrolls — most famously the Topkapi Scroll — preserved pattern repertoires. Medieval Persian craftsmen developed the girih system: a set of five tiles (a regular decagon, an elongated hexagon, a bowtie, a rhombus, and a regular pentagon) decorated with strap lines that generate complex decagonal patterns. A 2007 study by Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt showed that by the 15th century, girih tilings in monuments such as the Darb-e Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453) embodied quasi-crystalline geometry closely related to Penrose tilings, centuries before their Western mathematical description.

Symmetry

Islamic tilings exhibit all 17 wallpaper symmetry groups; the Alhambra is often (though contestably) said to contain examples of every group. Common motifs include the eight-pointed star (khatam), the "breath of the compassionate" pattern of overlapping squares, and 6-, 8-, 10-, 12-, and 16-fold rosettes.

Meaning

Scholars debate how far the patterns carried symbolic meaning. Common readings see the infinite repeat as an expression of tawhid (divine unity) and the indivisibility and infinity of God, while others emphasize the craft tradition's practical and aesthetic logic. In any case, geometry stands alongside calligraphy and the arabesque as one of the three canonical forms of Islamic decoration.
