Jewish partisans were fighters in irregular military groups participating in the Jewish resistance movement against Nazi Germany and its collaborators during World War II.

A number of Jewish partisan groups operated across Nazi-occupied Europe, some made up of a few escapees from ghettos and camps, and others, such as the Bielski partisans, numbering in the hundreds and including women and children. They were most numerous in Eastern Europe, but also existed in occupied France and Belgium, where they worked with the local resistance. Many individual Jewish fighters also took part in the other partisan movements in the Soviet Union, Poland, Yugoslavia, Greece, and Italy.

Operations

Jewish partisans engaged in guerrilla warfare and sabotage against the Nazi occupation, dynamited railroad lines, bridges, and tunnels used by German supply trains, collected intelligence, and rescued fellow Jews from ghettos and camps. In Lithuania alone, some 1,000 partisans took part in actions that killed approximately 3,000 German soldiers. Many groups operated from forest camps; the largest, led by Tuvia Bielski in the Naliboki forest of western Belarus, sheltered about 1,200 people by the end of the war.

Ghetto fighters

Before and alongside partisan warfare in the forests, Jewish underground organizations fought inside the ghettos, most famously in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943, led by organizations including the Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB) and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW). Uprisings also occurred in Białystok, Częstochowa, and elsewhere, and fighters escaped to join partisan units in the surrounding forests.

Notable partisans

  • Tuvia Bielski and his brothers, leaders of the Bielski partisans
  • Abba Kovner, poet and partisan leader in Vilna
  • Mordechai Anielewicz, commander of the ŻOB in Warsaw
  • Vitka Kempner, partisan and later member of the Nakam group
  • Parmineh (Fanya) ... — women couriers (kashariyot) linked ghettos and partisan units across occupied Poland

Challenges

Jewish partisans faced overwhelming obstacles: lack of weapons, training, and supplies; antisemitism within some local partisan movements; hostile local populations; and the constant threat of German reprisals. Many partisan units refused Jewish recruits, forcing Jews to form their own groups or conceal their identity.
