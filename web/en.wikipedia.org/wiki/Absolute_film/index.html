Absolute film

Absolute film is an experimental film movement of the 1920s, centred in Germany, in which filmmakers created abstract, non-narrative works composed of moving shapes, light and rhythm — an attempt to make cinema analogous to music, sometimes called "visual music." The term derives from the idea of film freed from representation, pursuing the medium's own "absolute" values.

Origins

The movement grew out of painting: Hans Richter and the Swedish artist Viking Eggeling, influenced by Cubism and abstraction, first designed scroll paintings that unrolled sequences of forms in time, then transferred the idea to film. Eggeling's Diagonal-Symphonie (completed 1924) and Richter's Rhythmus 21 (1921), consisting of rectangles that expand, contract and shift in black, white and grey, are founding works. Walter Ruttmann's painted Lichtspiel: Opus I–IV (1921–25) added colour and were sometimes screened with live musical accompaniment.

The 1925 matinee

A landmark screening, "Der absolute Film," was held in Berlin in May 1925, presenting works by Richter, Eggeling, Ruttmann, Fernand Léger and Dudley Murphy (Ballet mécanique), René Clair and others, and is regarded as the public debut of avant-garde film as an art form in Germany.

Legacy

Oskar Fischinger extended the absolute film into the sound era, synchronising abstract animation precisely to music in his Studies, work that later influenced Disney's Fantasia. The movement's ideas — direct animation, rhythmic montage of pure form, and synaesthetic correspondences between sound and image — fed into later experimental animation, motion graphics and music video.
