Empanada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Golden baked empanadas with crimped repulgue edges on a tray]

An empanada is a turnover of dough folded over a filling — baked or fried — found across Spain, Portugal, Latin America and the Philippines. The name comes from empanar, to wrap in bread. Galicia's large empanada, a double-crusted pie of tuna, peppers or pork, is the ancestor; in the Americas the form shrank to the hand-held half-moon with its crimped rope edge, the repulgue, whose pattern can signal the filling inside. Fillings map the region that makes them: Argentina alone argues province by province over beef empanadas — Tucumán's (with potato and spring onion), Salta's (spicier, with potato), with or without raisins, olives and egg.

History

Stuffed pastries are ancient on the Iberian peninsula — Arab-influenced cooking layered with older Roman traditions — and a Galician empanada is already depicted in 12th-century sculpture at Santiago de Compostela Cathedral. Spanish and Portuguese colonisation carried empanadas to every shore of their empires, where they absorbed local ingredients: corn dough in Venezuela (fried, crisp, stuffed with cheese or shredded beef), wheat in Argentina, rice flour nowhere but imagination everywhere in the Filipino empanada of Ilocos, orange-tinted with annatto and filled with longganisa and egg. The British Cornish pasty is a close European cousin that travelled with miners to the same continents.

World family

The empanada belongs to a planetary family of filled dough: samosas, calzones, gujiya, börek, pasties. What distinguishes it is less the shape than the social role — street food, fiesta food, lunchbox food, sold from carts and bakeries, eaten walking. Empanada festivals and eating contests run from Buenos Aires to Madrid, and frozen empanadas fill supermarket aisles in Miami and Madrid alike.
