Cord blood bank

A cord blood bank is a facility which stores umbilical cord blood for future use. Cord blood, which is rich in hematopoietic stem cells, is collected from the umbilical cord and placenta after childbirth and cryopreserved. Because the stem cells in cord blood can be used to treat a range of blood and immune system disorders — including leukemias, lymphomas, and certain inherited metabolic and immunodeficiency diseases — cord blood banking developed along both public and private models.

Public and private banking

Public cord blood banks accept donations without charge, test and type the units, list them on registries such as Be The Match (the U.S. National Marrow Donor Program), and release them to any suitably matched patient worldwide. The first public bank was established at the New York Blood Center in 1992 following the first successful cord blood transplant, performed in Paris in 1988 for a child with Fanconi anemia using a sibling's cord blood.

Private (family) banks charge parents an initial processing fee and annual storage fees to reserve a child's cord blood for possible use by the child or a family member. Medical organizations including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists have generally discouraged private banking as "biological insurance", because the probability that a child will need their own stored unit is low, and a child's own cells may carry the same genetic defect or pre-leukemic cells that cause disease; they encourage donation to public banks instead. Private banking is recommended mainly when a family already has a child with a condition treatable by transplant.

Collection and processing

After delivery, the cord is clamped and blood remaining in the cord and placenta is drained by gravity or drawn into a sterile collection bag containing anticoagulant. Units are tested for infectious diseases, HLA-typed, and processed — commonly by volume reduction to remove excess plasma and red cells — before controlled-rate freezing and storage in liquid nitrogen vapor at about −196 °C. Only units meeting minimum cell-dose thresholds are retained for public inventories, since engraftment success correlates with total nucleated cell count.

Regulation

In the United States, publicly banked cord blood is regulated by the Food and Drug Administration as a biologic, and units must be licensed or used under an investigational protocol; accreditation bodies such as AABB and FACT/NetCord set additional standards. Many countries operate national public banking programs coordinated through the World Marrow Donor Association.
