The Sydney Harbour Bridge is a steel through-arch bridge in Sydney, Australia, spanning Sydney Harbour (Port Jackson) from the Sydney central business district to the North Shore. Affectionately nicknamed "the Coathanger" for its arch-based design, it is one of Australia's most recognisable landmarks, together with the nearby Sydney Opera House, and was the world's widest long-span bridge until 2012. It carries road, rail, bicycle and pedestrian traffic.

History

Proposals to bridge the harbour dated from the early 19th century. The project was driven by engineer John Bradfield of the New South Wales Department of Public Works, and the design and construction contract was awarded to the English firm Dorman Long & Co. of Middlesbrough, with Sir Ralph Freeman as consulting engineer. Construction began in 1923, displacing hundreds of families in The Rocks and Millers Point whose homes were demolished. The two halves of the arch met on 19 August 1930. The bridge was officially opened on 19 March 1932 — famously, moments before Premier Jack Lang could cut the ribbon, Captain Francis de Groot of the paramilitary New Guard galloped forward and slashed it with his sword, protesting that a member of the Royal Family had not been invited to open it. The ribbon was retied and Lang performed the ceremony.

Structure

The bridge's steel arch spans 503 metres and its top stands 134 metres above sea level; it can rise or fall by up to 18 centimetres with temperature changes. The arch bears the entire load, transferred to massive abutment towers clad in concrete and granite, largely decorative in function. Some 52,800 tonnes of steel were used, much of it imported from Britain, joined by about six million rivets. The deck carries the Bradfield Highway, one of Sydney's shortest and busiest highways, two railway tracks serving the North Shore line, and dedicated footpaths and a cycleway.

Tourism and culture

Since 1998 tourists have been able to climb the arch with BridgeClimb Sydney, an attraction inspired by the bridge's celebrated maintenance painter (and later actor) Paul Hogan, who worked on the bridge before his fame. The bridge is the centrepiece of Sydney's New Year's Eve fireworks and was listed on the Australian National Heritage List in 2007.
