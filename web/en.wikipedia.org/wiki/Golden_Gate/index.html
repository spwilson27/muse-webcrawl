Golden Gate

The Golden Gate is a strait on the west coast of North America that connects San Francisco Bay to the Pacific Ocean. It is defined by the headlands of the San Francisco Peninsula and the Marin Peninsula, and is spanned by the Golden Gate Bridge.

Geography

The strait is roughly 5 km (3 mi) long and 1.6 km (1 mi) wide at its narrowest, with strong tidal currents carrying large volumes of water between the bay and the ocean. Deep water at the entrance made it an ideal natural harbour, though treacherous currents, fog, and rocky shores challenged mariners.

History

Native peoples, including the Ohlone and Coast Miwok, lived around the strait for thousands of years. European ships sailed past the entrance for centuries without noticing the bay behind it; the strait was first recorded by Europeans in 1769 when the Portolá expedition reached San Francisco Bay by land. In 1846, during the Mexican–American War era, John C. Frémont named the strait Chrysopylae ("Golden Gate"), by analogy with the Golden Horn of Constantinople, foreseeing its commercial importance.

Golden Gate Bridge

The Golden Gate Bridge, designed under chief engineer Joseph B. Strauss, opened to pedestrians in May 1937. Its 1,280 m (4,200 ft) main span was then the longest in the world, and its International Orange colour and Art Deco towers made it an enduring symbol of San Francisco and California.
