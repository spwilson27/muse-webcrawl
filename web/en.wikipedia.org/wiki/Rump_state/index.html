Rump state

A rump state is the remnant of a once much larger state, left with a reduced territory in the wake of secession, annexation, occupation, decolonisation, or a successful coup d'état or revolution. Not to be confused with a puppet state, a rump state retains the legal continuity of the original state, and its government is generally the continuation of the previous one.

Examples

Historical

  • The Byzantine Empire after the Fourth Crusade of 1204, reduced to a fraction of its former territory before recovering Constantinople in 1261.
  • The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth after the partitions, eventually eliminated in 1795.
  • The Republic of China, whose government retreated to Taiwan in 1949 after losing the Chinese Civil War, while retaining the claim (and initially the UN seat) of China as a whole.
  • The Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro) after the secession of Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina and North Macedonia in the early 1990s.
  • Sudan after the independence of South Sudan in 2011, losing about a quarter of its territory and most of its oil reserves.
  • The Austrian Empire's successor state of German Austria and then the First Austrian Republic after 1918, a German-speaking remnant of the Habsburg monarchy.
  • The Ottoman Empire in its final years, reduced to Anatolia and Istanbul before becoming the Republic of Turkey.

Terminology

The term "rump" alludes to the back or hindquarters of an animal remaining after the rest has been cut away; it gained currency in English after the Rump Parliament of 1648–1653, the remnant of the Long Parliament after Pride's Purge. Applied to states, it implies that the remnant government claims, or is regarded as, the legitimate continuation of the original polity even though it controls only part of its former territory.

Distinctions

A rump state differs from a government-in-exile (which controls no territory), from a breakaway state (which is the seceding part rather than the remnant), and from a successor state in the strict legal sense, since a rump state maintains the original state's legal personality, treaty obligations and international memberships. The term may be applied pejoratively or descriptively; some remnants, like the Republic of China on Taiwan, reject the implication of diminished legitimacy, while in other cases—such as post-2011 Sudan—it is largely neutral.
