Feudal Society (French: La Société féodale) is a landmark work of medieval history by the French historian Marc Bloch, published in two volumes in 1939 and 1940. It is one of the foundational texts of the Annales School of historiography and remains among the most influential studies of European feudalism.

Bloch sought to explain feudalism not merely as a legal or military arrangement but as a total social structure: a set of mental attitudes, kinship ties, economic practices, and political institutions that bound medieval Europeans together between roughly the 9th and the 13th centuries.

Overview

Written during the final years of Bloch's life — he was executed by the Gestapo in 1944 for his work in the French Resistance — Feudal Society drew on an enormous range of sources: charters, chronicles, legal codes, literature, and place-names. Bloch's comparative method ranged across France, England, Germany, and Italy, and even gestured toward Japan as a comparative case of feudal development.

Structure of the work

The first volume, The Growth of Ties of Dependence (1939), examines the material and mental environment of early medieval Europe: the invasions of Vikings, Muslims, and Magyars; the weakness of the state; the survival of kinship groups; and the emergence of the bond between lord and vassal, sealed by homage and the grant of a fief. The second volume, Social Classes and Political Organization (1940), analyses the nobility, the clergy, the peasantry, and the eventual re-emergence of royal and princely government.

Key arguments

Bloch defined a feudal society by a cluster of features: a subject peasantry; the widespread use of the service tenement (the fief) instead of a salary; the supremacy of a class of specialized warriors; ties of obedience and protection binding man to man; fragmentation of authority; and, amid all this, the survival of other forms of association, family and State. He stressed that feudal relations arose as a response to insecurity — "in the melancholy twilight of the Carolingian empire" men sought protectors close at hand.

Equally influential was Bloch's insistence on studying the mentalité of feudal people: their attitudes toward time, money, religion, and the supernatural, which differed profoundly from those of the modern world.

Reception and influence

The English translation by L. A. Manyon, with a foreword by M. M. Postan, appeared in 1961 and carried Bloch's ideas to a wide Anglophone audience. Together with The Historian's Craft (published posthumously), Feudal Society established Bloch as one of the most important historians of the twentieth century. Later scholars, including Susan Reynolds in Fiefs and Vassals (1994), have challenged the coherence of "feudalism" as a concept, but Bloch's broad social vision remains the starting point for the debate.
