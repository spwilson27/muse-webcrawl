Fertigation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fertigation is the injection of fertilizers, soil amendments, and other water-soluble products into an irrigation system, so that water and plant nutrients are delivered to crops together. The word is a portmanteau of fertilization and irrigation. Fertigation is used extensively in commercial agriculture and horticulture, especially with drip irrigation, and is also applied through sprinkler systems and in hydroponics. By spoon-feeding nutrients in small, frequent doses matched to crop growth stages, it can raise nutrient-use efficiency substantially relative to broadcasting dry fertilizer.

Methods and equipment

Nutrients enter the irrigation water through several kinds of injector:

  • Venturi injectors — a pressure difference across a constricted section of pipe sucks fertilizer solution into the flow; simple and cheap, but they impose a significant pressure loss.
  • Fertilizer tanks — a sealed tank of concentrated solution is placed across a pressure differential so that a portion of the flow passes through it; the dose declines as the tank dilutes.
  • Injector pumps — electrically or water-driven piston or diaphragm pumps meter concentrate precisely into the line; these dominate modern systems, often controlled by computers that adjust dose by water flow, EC (electrical conductivity), and pH sensors.

Backflow preventers are legally required in most jurisdictions so fertilizer cannot siphon back into wells or public water supplies.

Advantages and disadvantages

Fertigation places nutrients in the wetted root zone exactly when the crop needs them, reducing losses to leaching and volatilization, cutting labour and tractor passes, and allowing correction of deficiencies mid-season. Reported fertilizer savings are commonly 20–40% compared with conventional practice. Drawbacks include the cost of equipment, the need for soluble, compatible fertilizer grades (mixing calcium with phosphates or sulfates can precipitate and clog emitters), and the risk that mismanaged systems will still pollute groundwater with nitrate. Regular flushing of lines, filtration, and water-quality testing are essential maintenance.

Common materials

Typical fertigation inputs are urea and ammonium nitrate solutions, potassium nitrate, monoammonium and monopotassium phosphates, chelated micronutrients (iron, zinc, manganese), and acids such as nitric or phosphoric acid used to control pH and prevent emitter clogging. Liquid animal manures and treated wastewater are also sometimes applied this way. Fertigation grew rapidly in Israel and other water-scarce regions alongside drip irrigation and is now standard in greenhouse vegetable production, orchards, vineyards, and turf management worldwide.
