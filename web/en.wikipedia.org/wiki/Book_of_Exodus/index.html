The Book of Exodus (from Ancient Greek Ἔξοδος, "way out"; Hebrew: שְׁמוֹת, Shemot, "Names") is the second book of the Bible and of the Torah. It tells the foundational story of the Israelites: their enslavement in Egypt, their liberation under the leadership of Moses, the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai, and the construction of the Tabernacle. The Exodus narrative is central to Jewish identity and is retold annually at Passover.

Summary

The book opens with the Israelites multiplied in Egypt and oppressed by a new Pharaoh. The infant Moses is saved from Pharaoh's decree, flees to Midian, and is called by God at the burning bush. After the Ten Plagues, Pharaoh lets the Israelites go; the sea is parted for them and closes over the pursuing Egyptians. In the wilderness the people receive manna, reach Mount Sinai, and enter the covenant: Moses receives the Ten Commandments and the law codes, while the people sin with the golden calf. The book closes with detailed instructions for, and the completion of, the Tabernacle and priestly vestments, and the descent of God's glory upon the sanctuary.

Composition and historicity

Tradition ascribes the book to Moses, but modern scholarship, following source criticism, sees Exodus as a composite work drawing on multiple sources (conventionally J, E, and P), reaching its final form in the Persian period (roughly the 5th century BCE), though preserving older traditions and poetry such as the Song of the Sea (chapter 15). Mainstream archaeology finds no direct evidence of a mass migration of the biblical scale, and most scholars treat the Exodus as a national foundation myth—possibly preserving memories of smaller groups' experiences—while its theological and cultural influence is undisputed.

Themes

Key themes include liberation from oppression, covenant, law, divine presence, and the transformation of a tribal group into a nation under God. The Exodus story has profoundly influenced later liberation movements and political theology.
