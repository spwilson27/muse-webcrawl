The Otto cycle is an idealized thermodynamic cycle that describes the functioning of a typical spark ignition piston engine. It is the thermodynamic cycle most commonly found in automobile engines. The cycle is named after Nicolaus Otto, whose compressed-charge engine of 1876 made the four-stroke design practical (an earlier patent by Alphonse Beau de Rochas, 1862, described the principle).

The cycle

In the ideal cycle, the working fluid undergoes: (1) adiabatic (isentropic) compression as the piston moves from bottom dead centre, raising pressure and temperature; (2) heat addition at constant volume, modelling spark ignition and combustion; (3) adiabatic expansion — the power stroke; and (4) heat rejection at constant volume, modelling exhaust blowdown. The four-stroke engine realises the cycle with separate intake and exhaust strokes. Thermal efficiency depends only on the compression ratio r and the heat-capacity ratio γ:

η = 1 − 1/rγ−1

Higher compression raises ideal efficiency, but in practice knock (detonation) limits petrol engines to compression ratios of roughly 9:1–12:1. Real efficiencies reach about 30–40% for modern engines, below the ideal figure because of heat losses, friction, and non-instantaneous combustion; the Atkinson cycle variant used in many hybrids trades power density for higher effective expansion.
