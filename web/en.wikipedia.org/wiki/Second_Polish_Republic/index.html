The Second Polish Republic (Polish: Druga Rzeczpospolita) was the Polish state that existed from 1918, when Poland regained independence after 123 years of partitions, until the invasion of Poland in 1939. It was one of the most populous and ethnically diverse states of interwar Europe, with significant Ukrainian, Jewish, Belarusian, and German minorities making up roughly a third of its population.

Formation and borders

Poland re-emerged as the German, Austro-Hungarian, and Russian empires collapsed at the end of the First World War. Józef Piłsudski proclaimed the republic in November 1918. Its borders were settled by the Treaty of Versailles, the Treaty of Riga (1921), plebiscites in Upper Silesia, and a series of conflicts, above all the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921, in which Poland's victory at the Battle of Warsaw (the "Miracle on the Vistula") halted the Red Army's westward advance.

Politics

The democratic March Constitution of 1921 gave way to instability, with frequent cabinet changes. In May 1926 Piłsudski seized power in a coup and thereafter dominated Polish politics through the authoritarian-leaning Sanation movement; the April Constitution of 1935 strengthened the presidency. After Piłsudski's death in 1935, power passed to a colonels' regime led by figures such as Edward Rydz-Śmigły and Foreign Minister Józef Beck, who pursued a policy of balancing between Germany and the Soviet Union.

Society and economy

The republic built the new Baltic port of Gdynia, developed the Central Industrial Region in the 1930s, and sustained a vibrant cultural life — Warsaw and Lwów were centres of mathematics (Stefan Banach, the Lwów School), literature, film, and a large Yiddish and Hebrew culture. Yet the economy remained predominantly agrarian and was hit hard by the Great Depression, and relations with ethnic minorities and neighbours were often strained.

Destruction

On 1 September 1939 Nazi Germany invaded from the west, and on 17 September the Soviet Union invaded from the east, under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. Poland was partitioned and occupied; the Polish government went into exile in London, and the state's legal continuity was maintained by the government-in-exile until 1990.
