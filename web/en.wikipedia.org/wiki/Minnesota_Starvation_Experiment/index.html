The Minnesota Starvation Experiment, also known as the Minnesota Semi-Starvation Experiment, was a clinical study performed at the University of Minnesota between 19 November 1944 and 20 December 1945. Led by physiologist Ancel Keys, the study investigated the physiological and psychological effects of severe and prolonged dietary restriction and the effectiveness of dietary rehabilitation strategies, in order to guide Allied relief workers helping famine victims in Europe and Asia at the end of the Second World War.

Thirty-six conscientious objectors underwent six months of semi-starvation.

Design

From more than 400 conscientious-objector volunteers in the Civilian Public Service, 36 healthy young men were selected. After a three-month control period on about 3,200 kcal per day, the men underwent six months of semi-starvation at roughly 1,560 kcal per day, based on famine foods of potatoes, turnips, rutabagas, bread and macaroni, while walking about 22 miles (35 km) per week; the target was a loss of about 25 per cent of body weight. This was followed by a three-month restricted rehabilitation phase and an eight-week unrestricted rehabilitation period.

Findings

The men lost on average about 25 per cent of body weight and showed dramatic changes: heart volume shrank, resting metabolic rate fell by about 40 per cent, and strength, endurance and body temperature declined. Psychologically, the volunteers became obsessed with food — collecting recipes, hoarding utensils, dreaming of meals — and suffered depression, irritability, apathy and loss of libido; several withdrew and one mutilated himself. During refeeding, recovery of weight (often overshooting into fat) took months, and the psychological symptoms gradually resolved. The results were published in the two-volume The Biology of Human Starvation (1950) by Keys, Josef Brožek and colleagues.

Legacy

The experiment remains the most comprehensive study of human semi-starvation and is still cited in research on famine relief, eating disorders and refeeding syndrome. Its ethical acceptability — it caused serious suffering, albeit among informed volunteers — has been debated, and such a study could not be performed under modern research ethics standards.
