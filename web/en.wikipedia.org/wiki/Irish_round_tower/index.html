Irish round tower

Irish nameCloigtheach ("bell house")
Periodc. 9th–12th centuries
HeightTypically 18–40 m (60–130 ft)

Irish round towers (Irish: cloigtheach, "bell house") are early medieval stone towers found chiefly at monastic sites in Ireland, with a few examples in Scotland and one on the Isle of Man. Built mostly between the 9th and 12th centuries, they are among the most distinctive monuments of early Christian Ireland.

Form and function

The towers are tall, slender, slightly tapering cylinders of mortared stone, capped with a conical roof. The doorway is characteristically raised several metres above the ground and was reached by a ladder that could be drawn up. Inside, wooden floors divided the tower into storeys, lit by small windows, with a single window (or four, in some late examples) near the top.

The Irish name indicates their primary function as bell towers from which a handbell was rung to summon the monastic community. They also served as places of refuge and safekeeping for relics, books and treasures during raids, including Viking attacks, and as landmarks and symbols of a monastery's prestige.

Distribution

About 120 towers are known to have existed; some 65 survive in varying states of repair. Fine complete examples stand at Clonmacnoise, Monasterboice, Glendalough, Devenish Island and Kilmacduagh, whose tower leans noticeably from vertical. Related towers occur at Abernethy and Brechin in Scotland.
