Military colours

Military colours, guidons and standards are the distinctive flags carried by military units as symbols of identity and rallying points in battle. In the British and Commonwealth tradition, infantry regiments carry two colours — the King's (or Sovereign's) Colour and the Regimental Colour — while cavalry regiments carry standards or guidons. Colours bear the regiment's battle honours and devices.

History

Battle standards are ancient, from Roman aquilae to medieval banners. By the early modern period each company carried its own colour, which served as the rallying point in the smoke of battle and on which troops formed. As firearms made massed colour parties untenable, the number of colours per regiment fell, and in most armies colours ceased to be carried in action during the late nineteenth century; the last British colours carried in battle are generally held to be those at Laing's Nek in 1881.

Battle honours

Colours are embroidered with the names of campaigns and battles in which the regiment distinguished itself. Because retired colours are traditionally laid up in churches, cathedrals and public buildings, they remain visible records of a unit's history. New colours are consecrated in formal ceremonies, and the ceremonial of Trooping the Colour marks the sovereign's official birthday in the United Kingdom.

Variations

Different arms use different forms: cavalry standards are smaller square flags, guidons are swallow-tailed, and some corps carry guns rather than colours as their symbol — the Royal Artillery, for example, treats its guns as its colours. Comparable traditions exist in many armies worldwide, including the regimental flags of the United States and the colours of Commonwealth forces.
