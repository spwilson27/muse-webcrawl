Nimrud (ancient Kalhu, biblical Calah) is an ancient Assyrian city in present-day northern Iraq, capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire under Ashurnasirpal II in the 9th century BC, heavily damaged by ISIL in 2015–2016.

Nimrud

Nimrud is the Arabic name of the ancient Assyrian city of Kalhu (biblical Calah), situated on the Tigris about 30 km south-east of Mosul in modern Iraq. Founded in the 13th century BC and made imperial capital by Ashurnasirpal II (reigned 883–859 BC), it was one of the great cities of the Neo-Assyrian Empire.

History

Ashurnasirpal II rebuilt Kalhu on a grand scale, enclosing some 890 acres with walls and crowning the citadel with his Northwest Palace, decorated with carved gypsum reliefs and guarded by colossal human-headed winged bulls (lamassu); he inaugurated it with a banquet for nearly 70,000 guests, recorded on the Banquet Stele. The city remained a royal capital under Shalmaneser III and others until Sargon II moved the court to Dur-Sharrukin around 706 BC, after which it stayed a major provincial centre until the fall of Assyria in 612 BC.

Excavations and destruction

Austen Henry Layard excavated Nimrud in 1845–51, recovering the Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III and sculptures now in the British Museum; later expeditions, notably Max Mallowan's (1949–63, with his wife Agatha Christie assisting), found thousands of carved ivories, and Iraqi archaeologists in 1988–92 uncovered the royal tombs of Assyrian queens, rich in gold jewellery. In 2015 the Islamic State bulldozed and blew up large parts of the site, an act condemned worldwide as cultural vandalism; Iraqi forces retook Nimrud in November 2016, and stabilisation and restoration work has followed.
