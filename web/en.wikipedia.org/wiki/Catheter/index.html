Catheter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In medicine, a catheter is a thin tube made from medical-grade materials that can be inserted into the body to treat disease or perform a surgical procedure. Catheters serve a wide range of functions: draining fluids, administering fluids or gases, providing access for surgical instruments, measuring pressures, and sampling tissue.

History

Catheter-like instruments have been used since antiquity; ancient Egyptian, Greek and Roman physicians employed reed, metal and bronze tubes to drain the bladder. Benjamin Franklin invented a flexible silver catheter in 1752 for his brother, who suffered from bladder stones. Modern disposable plastic catheters date from the 1940s, when David S. Sheridan developed the modern disposable catheter industry.

Types

Urinary catheters, such as the Foley catheter (introduced by Frederic Foley in the 1930s), drain urine from the bladder via the urethra and are retained by an inflatable balloon. Intravenous catheters give access to veins or arteries; central venous catheters and peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) deliver medication, nutrition and chemotherapy. Cardiac catheters are threaded through blood vessels to the heart for angiography, angioplasty and stent placement, pioneering work for which Werner Forssmann shared the 1956 Nobel Prize. Other types include chest drainage tubes, neurovascular microcatheters and condom catheters.

Materials and complications

Catheters are made from silicone, latex, polyurethane, PVC and other polymers, sometimes with antimicrobial or heparin coatings. The main risks of indwelling catheters are infection — catheter-associated urinary tract infection is one of the most common hospital-acquired infections — blockage, encrustation and tissue injury. Sizes are commonly measured on the French gauge scale.
