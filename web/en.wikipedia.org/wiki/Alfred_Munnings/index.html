Sir Alfred James Munnings, KCVO, PRA (8 October 1878 – 17 July 1959) was an English artist, best known for his paintings of horses and rural scenes. Regarded as one of the finest British equestrian painters, he served as a war artist in the First World War and as president of the Royal Academy of Arts from 1944 to 1949.

Early life

Munnings was born at Mendham Mill in Suffolk and apprenticed to a Norwich firm of lithographers before studying at the Norwich School of Art. He lost the sight of one eye in an accident in 1898 but continued painting, becoming associated with the Newlyn School in Cornwall. His paintings of gypsy and horse-fair scenes brought early success.

War artist

In 1918 Munnings went to France attached to the Canadian Cavalry Brigade and later the Forestry Corps, producing dramatic canvases including the Charge of Flowerdew's Squadron at Moreuil Wood. Dozens of his wartime works were acquired by the Canadian War Memorials Fund.

Later life and views

Between the wars Munnings became wealthy painting racehorses, hunters, and portraits of riders at meets and racecourses. From Castle House, Dedham, he dominated the English sporting-art market. As Royal Academy president, and in his notorious farewell address of 1949, he attacked modern art — Picasso and Matisse among his targets — with a vehemence that made headlines. His three-volume autobiography, including An Artist's Life (1950), remains a vivid account of the rural and artistic world he inhabited.

The Munnings Art Museum at Castle House displays the largest collection of his work.
