Unsecured debt

Credit-card balances are the most familiar form of unsecured consumer debt.

Unsecured debt is debt that is not backed by collateral — no specific asset is pledged that a lender can seize on default. It stands in contrast to secured debt such as a mortgage (backed by real estate) or an auto loan (backed by the vehicle). Because the lender bears greater risk, unsecured debt usually carries higher interest rates and is extended on the basis of the borrower's creditworthiness and income.

Common forms

Examples include credit-card balances, personal loans and lines of credit, student loans, medical bills, utility bills, and most corporate bonds and debentures. Landlords' rent claims and tax obligations are also typically unsecured for the creditor in practical terms, though statutory priorities may apply.

Treatment in bankruptcy

In bankruptcy, unsecured creditors rank behind secured creditors and priority claims. In a liquidation (Chapter 7 in the United States), general unsecured creditors are paid only from whatever remains after secured and priority claims, and often receive little or nothing. In corporate insolvency, the absolute priority rule governs the order of payment, and unsecured bondholders may recover only a fraction of face value or receive equity in the reorganized company.

Credit assessment

Because no collateral cushions losses, unsecured lending depends on credit scores, income verification, and default statistics. Pricing reflects expected losses: unsecured consumer credit in many countries carries annual percentage rates far above secured lending rates, and lenders manage risk through credit limits and portfolio diversification.
