Hydrogenation

Hydrogenation
Type: Chemical reaction
Reactant: Hydrogen (H₂)
Common catalysts: Ni, Pd, Pt
Uses: Food oils, fuels, pharmaceuticals

Hydrogenation is a chemical reaction between molecular hydrogen (H₂) and another compound or element, usually in the presence of a catalyst such as nickel, palladium or platinum. The process is commonly employed to reduce or saturate organic compounds. Hydrogenation typically constitutes the addition of pairs of hydrogen atoms to a molecule, often an alkene. Catalysts are required for the reaction to be usable; non-catalytic hydrogenation takes place only at very high temperatures.

History

Paul Sabatier is considered the father of hydrogenation: in the late 1890s he found that nickel catalysed the addition of hydrogen to carbon compounds, work for which he shared the 1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Wilhelm Normann patented hydrogenation of liquid oils in Germany and Britain in 1902–1903, leading to industrial hardening of fats. Murray Raney developed the porous nickel catalyst known as Raney nickel in 1926.

Process and mechanism

In heterogeneous catalysis, hydrogen dissociates on the metal surface and the unsaturated substrate adsorbs; hydrogen atoms add stepwise across a double or triple bond. Alkynes can be stopped at the cis-alkene stage with a poisoned catalyst such as Lindlar's catalyst. Homogeneous catalysts, including Wilkinson's catalyst, operate dissolved in the same phase. See Catalysis and Alkene.

Applications

The food industry hydrogenates vegetable oils to make semi-solid fats such as shortening and margarine; partial hydrogenation creates trans fats, now widely restricted for health reasons. In petrochemistry, hydrogenation and hydrocracking convert heavy fractions and remove sulfur, nitrogen and metals. Pharmaceutical and fine-chemical synthesis uses hydrogenation extensively, including asymmetric hydrogenation recognised by the 2001 Nobel Prize.

Related reactions

Dehydrogenation is the reverse process. Transfer hydrogenation uses donors other than gaseous H₂. Hydrogenolysis cleaves bonds, for example carbon–oxygen bonds in deoxygenation.
