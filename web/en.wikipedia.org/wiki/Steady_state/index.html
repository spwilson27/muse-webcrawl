Steady state

In systems theory, a system or a process is in a steady state if the variables (called state variables) which define the behaviour of the system or the process are unchanging in time. In continuous time, this means that for those properties p of the system, the partial derivative with respect to time is zero: dp/dt = 0.

The concept of steady state has relevance in many fields, in particular thermodynamics, economics and engineering. A steady state is not the same as a state of equilibrium: in a steady state, flows of matter or energy may pass through the system continuously, so long as inputs and outputs balance. An example is a bathtub with the tap running and the drain open, where the water level remains constant.

In chemistry

In chemical kinetics, the steady-state approximation assumes that the concentration of a reactive intermediate remains approximately constant during a reaction, which simplifies the derivation of rate laws. It is central to the derivation of the Michaelis–Menten equation in enzyme kinetics.

In electronics

In circuit analysis, the steady state of a circuit is the condition reached after transients have died away — for example, the constant DC voltages and currents reached long after a switch is closed, or the sinusoidal steady state of a linear circuit driven by an AC source.

In economics

In growth theory, a steady state is a situation in which key variables such as capital per worker and output per worker grow at constant rates (or not at all). The Solow–Swan model predicts that economies converge to a steady-state path determined by savings, population growth and technological progress.
