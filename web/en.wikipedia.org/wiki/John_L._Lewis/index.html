John Llewellyn Lewis (February 12, 1880 – June 11, 1969) was an American leader of organized labor who served as president of the United Mine Workers of America (UMW) from 1920 to 1960. A major player in the history of coal mining and a driving force behind the founding of the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), he helped organise the mass-production workers of steel, automobiles and rubber in the 1930s.

Early life and rise

Lewis was born in Cleveland, Lucas County, Iowa, to Welsh immigrant coal-mining parents, and worked in the mines as a teenager. He rose through the UMW as an organiser and statistician, taking the presidency in 1920. During the 1920s he centralised the union and fought losing battles against declining coal employment, but he built a formidable political machine.

New Deal and the CIO

The New Deal transformed Lewis's fortunes: Section 7(a) of the National Industrial Recovery Act and then the Wagner Act (1935) triggered a surge in union membership. Frustrated by the American Federation of Labor's craft-union conservatism, Lewis founded the Committee (later Congress) of Industrial Organizations in 1935 and backed the great organizing drives and sit-down strikes of 1936–37, including the Flint sit-down strike against General Motors. A celebrated orator with a Shakespearean delivery, he broke with President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1940 and resigned the CIO presidency.

Wartime strikes and later years

Lewis led controversial coal strikes during the Second World War, defying the government and public opinion, but won miners major gains in wages, pensions and health funds. He remained UMW president until 1960, increasingly autocratic and isolated as coal declined. President Lyndon Johnson awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964. He died in Alexandria, Virginia, in 1969.
