Laron syndrome

Laron syndrome, also called Laron dwarfism or growth hormone insensitivity, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterised by an inability of the body to respond to growth hormone (GH). It is caused by mutations in the growth hormone receptor (GHR) gene, which prevent production of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), the principal mediator of GH's growth-promoting effects. It was described by the Israeli endocrinologist Zvi Laron and colleagues from 1966 onwards among children of consanguineous Jewish families.

Features

Affected individuals have severe proportionate short stature, a characteristic face with a protruding forehead and sunken nasal bridge, small hands and feet, delayed puberty, and a tendency to obesity. Serum GH levels are high but IGF-1 levels are extremely low. Adult height untreated is roughly 100–140 cm. Strikingly, long-term follow-up of the largest cohort — a population in Loja province, Ecuador, of mainly Converso descent — found essentially no cases of type 2 diabetes or cancer among people with Laron syndrome, despite high rates of obesity, suggesting that low IGF-1 signalling protects against both diseases and prompting research into ageing and cancer-prevention pathways.

Treatment

Growth hormone itself is ineffective; treatment, where available, uses recombinant IGF-1 (mecasermin). Because the condition is so rare — a few hundred known cases worldwide, about a third of them in Ecuador — the drug's cost and intermittent supply have been persistent problems.
