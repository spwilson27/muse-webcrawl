Sequential analysis

Continuation region and stopping boundaries of an SPRT.

Sequential analysis is a branch of statistics in which the sample size is not fixed in advance; instead, data are evaluated as they are collected, and sampling stops as soon as a pre-specified criterion is met. Unlike classical fixed-sample designs, sequential methods make the decision to terminate the experiment part of the inference procedure itself, using formal stopping rules. The field was founded during the Second World War by Abraham Wald, whose sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) was developed for quality-control inspection and kept classified until 1945.

The sequential probability ratio test

The SPRT tests a simple null hypothesis against a simple alternative by accumulating the likelihood ratio as observations arrive. Sampling continues while the ratio lies between two boundaries; if it crosses the upper boundary, the alternative is accepted, and if it crosses the lower, the null is accepted. The boundaries are chosen from the desired probabilities of Type I and Type II errors. Wald showed, with Frederick Mosteller and others, that the SPRT minimizes the expected sample size among all tests with the same error probabilities — an important economy when each observation is expensive or destructive, as in munitions testing.

Applications

Clinical trials

Sequential methods are widely used in clinical trials, where interim analyses allow a trial to stop early for clear efficacy, harm, or futility — ethically important when patients are being assigned to an inferior treatment, and economically important when trials are long and costly. Group sequential designs, in which data are reviewed at a small number of planned interim points, dominate modern practice. Statistical care is required: repeated significance testing inflates the overall Type I error rate, so methods such as the O'Brien–Fleming and Pocock boundaries, and the alpha-spending approach of Lan and DeMets, distribute the error budget across looks at the data. Independent data monitoring committees typically conduct the reviews.

Other fields

Sequential sampling plans are used in industrial acceptance sampling, in surveillance for disease outbreaks, in A/B testing of online services (where naive "peeking" at results is a well-known pitfall addressed by always-valid inference), and in econometrics and engineering wherever observations arrive over time. Related theory includes optimal stopping problems and multi-armed bandits, in which sequential decisions balance exploration against exploitation.

Statistical theory

Because the sample size is a random variable, fixed-sample reasoning can mislead: estimates after early stopping tend to be biased away from the null, and confidence intervals must be adjusted for the stopping rule. Wald's equations and martingale methods provide the mathematical backbone of the theory. Modern developments include anytime-valid confidence sequences and e-values, which permit continuous monitoring without error-rate inflation.
