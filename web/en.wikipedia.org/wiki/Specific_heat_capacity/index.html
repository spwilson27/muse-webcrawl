In thermodynamics, the specific heat capacity (symbol c) of a substance is the amount of heat that must be added to one unit of mass of the substance to cause an increase of one unit in temperature. More precisely, it is the heat capacity of a sample of the substance divided by the mass of the sample. The SI unit is the joule per kilogram per kelvin (J·kg−1·K−1).

The heat Q required is given by Q = mcΔT, where m is mass and ΔT is the temperature change. For gases, values differ depending on whether heating occurs at constant pressure (cp) or constant volume (cV), with cp larger because work is done in expansion.

Values

Water has an exceptionally high specific heat capacity, about 4,184 J·kg−1·K−1 at 25 °C, one of the highest of common substances — a property moderating climates near large bodies of water and making water an excellent coolant. By contrast, metals have low values: aluminium ≈ 897, iron ≈ 449, copper ≈ 385, and lead ≈ 129 J·kg−1·K−1. Hydrogen gas has the highest value per unit mass (about 14,300 J·kg−1·K−1 at constant pressure).

Measurement and theory

Specific heat capacity is measured by calorimetry. Historically, the calorie was defined through the heating of water. At low temperatures the specific heats of solids fall toward zero, as described by the Debye model, whereas the classical Dulong–Petit law predicts a molar heat capacity near 3R (≈ 25 J·mol−1·K−1) for solid elements at high temperature.

Applications

The concept matters in climate science (thermal inertia of oceans), engineering (coolants, heat storage, building materials), cooking, and meteorology — coastal regions' milder climates are a direct consequence of water's high specific heat capacity.
