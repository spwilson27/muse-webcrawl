Microcar

A microcar is a term used for the smallest category of automobile. Microcars are generally defined by very small dimensions and small engines — historically often under 700 cc, and in some regulatory classes under 500 cc — and many have three wheels. They were especially popular in post-war Europe, where fuel shortages, material scarcity, and tax advantages for small vehicles encouraged their production. Famous examples include the BMW Isetta, the Messerschmitt KR200, and the Peel P50, long recognized as the smallest production car ever made.

History

Microcars emerged in large numbers after the Second World War. Aircraft manufacturers such as Messerschmitt and Heinkel, barred from building planes, turned to tiny three-wheeled cars; motorcycle makers likewise adapted their engines and plants. The Isetta, designed in Italy by Iso and licensed to BMW, became the best-selling single-cylinder car in the world, with its distinctive front-opening door. In Britain, three-wheelers such as the Bond Minicar and Reliant models benefited from being taxed and licensed as motorcycles.

The category declined in the 1960s as prosperity grew and conventional small cars such as the Mini and Fiat 500 made microcars seem crude. In Japan, however, the kei car class — a regulated category of small vehicles enjoying tax and insurance advantages — kept the spirit of the microcar alive and remains a major market segment.

Modern microcars

Interest has revived with electric urban vehicles. In Europe, "quadricycles" such as the Citroën Ami and Renault Twizy can be driven in some countries from age 14–16 without a full car licence. Modern revivals of classic designs, such as the electric Microlino inspired by the Isetta, market microcars as sustainable city transport. Microcars are typically defined in regulation by weight, power, and top speed rather than by name, and safety standards for the class remain less stringent than for full cars.
