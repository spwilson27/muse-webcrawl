General Council of the Judiciary

[Image placeholder: CGPJ headquarters]

The General Council of the Judiciary (Spanish: Consejo General del Poder Judicial, CGPJ) is the constitutional governing body of the judiciary of Spain. Established by the Spanish Constitution of 1978, it is responsible for the appointment, promotion, inspection, and discipline of judges and magistrates, with the aim of safeguarding judicial independence from the executive and legislative branches.

Composition

The Council consists of the President of the Supreme Court, who presides over it, and twenty members appointed by the King for a five-year term: twelve chosen from among judges and magistrates, and eight from among lawyers and jurists of recognised competence. The members are proposed by the Congress of Deputies and the Senate, each chamber electing ten by a three-fifths majority — a requirement intended to force consensus between the major parties.

Controversies

The appointment system has been criticised for politicising the body, since the parliamentary supermajority requirement has repeatedly led to deadlock. Most notably, the Council's mandate expired in December 2018 and remained unrenewed for years because the governing and opposition parties could not agree on candidates, drawing criticism from European institutions including the European Commission and GRECO, which have recommended that judicial members be elected by judges themselves. An agreement to renew the Council was finally reached in 2024.
