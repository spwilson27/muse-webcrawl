Reparations for slavery in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


"Forty acres and a mule"

Reparations for slavery is the application of the concept of reparations to victims of slavery or their descendants in the United States. The concept has been proposed as restitution for the institution of chattel slavery, which existed in the United States from the colonial period until its abolition by the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865, and, in broader proposals, for subsequent racial discrimination including Jim Crow segregation and redlining.

History

The idea dates to the Civil War era: in January 1865, General William Tecumseh Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15, setting aside confiscated coastal land in South Carolina, Georgia and Florida for freed families in plots of up to 40 acres — the origin of the phrase "forty acres and a mule". President Andrew Johnson reversed the policy later that year, returning the land to former owners. Freedpeople's later petitions, and early 20th-century organizing by figures such as Callie House of the National Ex-Slave Mutual Relief, Bounty and Pension Association, sought federal pensions for the formerly enslaved.

Modern movement

From 1989 until his death in 2017, Congressman John Conyers repeatedly introduced H.R. 40, a bill to establish a commission to study reparations proposals; the numbering commemorates the "forty acres" promise. The debate gained wide attention with Ta-Nehisi Coates's 2014 essay "The Case for Reparations" in The Atlantic. Supporters argue reparations are owed for unpaid labor, stolen wealth, and enduring racial wealth gaps; opponents raise objections about identifying beneficiaries and payers after many generations, and about the practicality and fairness of such programs.

Local and institutional initiatives

In the absence of a federal program, some local and institutional measures have been adopted. In 2021 Evanston, Illinois became the first U.S. city to approve reparations payments to Black residents, funded by a tax on recreational cannabis and focused on housing. California's Reparations Task Force (2020–2023) produced a major report documenting discrimination and recommending remedies, without direct cash payments being enacted at the state level. Universities including Georgetown and Princeton Theological Seminary, and institutions such as the Jesuit order, have created funds in response to research into their historical ties to slavery.
