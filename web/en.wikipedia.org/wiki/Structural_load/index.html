Structural load

Principal loads acting on a building: gravity (dead and live), wind, and snow.

A structural load or structural action is a force, deformation, or acceleration applied to structural elements of a structure or its components. Loads cause stresses, deformations, and displacements; structural engineers must ensure that a structure and its materials can resist the loads expected over its design life, as prescribed by building codes such as ASCE 7 in the United States and Eurocode 1 in Europe.

Types of load

  • Dead load — the permanent weight of the structure itself and fixed attachments (walls, floors, roofing, fixed equipment). Relatively well known and constant.
  • Live load (imposed load) — movable or transient loads: occupants and furniture in buildings, vehicles on bridges, stored goods. Variable and code-specified by occupancy (e.g., 40 psf / ~1.9 kN/m² for residential floors).
  • Wind load — pressure and suction from wind, depending on speed, terrain, height, and shape; design uses gust factors and pressure coefficients.
  • Snow and rain loads — including drift accumulation and ponding.
  • Seismic (earthquake) load — inertial forces from ground motion, represented by design spectra or equivalent lateral forces.
  • Soil and fluid loads — earth pressure on retaining walls and basements, hydrostatic pressure, flood and wave loads.
  • Thermal and settlement actions — imposed deformations from temperature change and differential foundation settlement.
  • Accidental loads — impact, blast, fire, and other abnormal events.

Load paths and combinations

Loads travel through the structure along a load path — from slab to beams, columns, and foundations to the ground. Because different loads peak at different times, codes prescribe load combinations (e.g., 1.2D + 1.6L) in the load-and-resistance-factor design (LRFD) format, or combination factors (ψ) in the Eurocodes, calibrating safety against statistical variability of loads and resistances.

Point, line and area loads

Loads are idealized as concentrated (point) loads, line loads (per unit length), or distributed pressure (per unit area), and as uniform or triangular distributions, depending on how they are delivered to the member under analysis (tributary-area method).
