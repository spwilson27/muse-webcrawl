Belgravia is a district in Central London, within the City of Westminster, lying south-west of Buckingham Palace and between Knightsbridge, Victoria, Pimlico and Chelsea. It is famous for its grand white stucco terraces and garden squares, and is one of the wealthiest districts in the world, long associated with the British aristocracy and, in modern times, with embassies and international wealth.

History

Until the early 19th century the area was largely marshy open land — the "Five Fields" — crossed by the River Westbourne. In the 1820s Richard Grosvenor, 2nd Marquess of Westminster, commissioned the master builder Thomas Cubitt to develop the estate. Cubitt filled the low ground with earth excavated from St Katharine Docks and laid out Belgrave Square (begun 1825), Eaton Square, Chester Square and Wilton Crescent, with terraces designed largely by George Basevi and others in the Italianate classical style. The district was named after Belgrave in Leicestershire, one of the Grosvenor family's estate villages near their seat at Eaton Hall.

Character

Belgravia remains largely owned, through the Grosvenor Estate, by the Grosvenor family, now headed by the Duke of Westminster. Its squares host numerous foreign embassies, particularly around Belgrave Square. Housing consists mostly of large stucco terraced houses and mansion flats; mews streets behind them, once stabling, are now prized small houses. Notable residents over the years have included prime ministers Stanley Baldwin and Margaret Thatcher, and the composer Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart wrote his first symphony while lodging in Ebury Street in 1764, an event marked by a blue plaque.
