Andalusi music

A traditional Andalusi orchestra in North Africa.

Andalusi music (Arabic: الموسيقى الأندلسية), sometimes called Andalusian classical music, is a tradition of classical music originating in the Muslim courts of al-Andalus (medieval Iberia) between the 9th and 15th centuries. After the Christian reconquest and the expulsion of Muslim and Jewish communities from Spain, the tradition was preserved and developed in the Maghreb — Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya — where it remains a living art.

History

The tradition is associated with the court of Córdoba and with the legendary musician Ziryab (789–857), a Baghdad-born singer and oud player credited with founding a music school in Córdoba, adding a fifth string to the oud, and systematizing musical instruction. The repertoire was organized into suites called nawba (Arabic: نوبة), each built on a single melodic mode (tab') and unfolding through a sequence of rhythmic movements, alternating composed songs with improvisation.

Regional schools

In Morocco the tradition is known as al-Āla; in Algeria its schools include the san'a of Algiers, the gharnati of Tlemcen, and the malouf of Constantine; Tunisian and Libyan traditions are also called malouf or ma'luf. Ensembles typically feature the oud, rebab, violin (held vertically), qanun, tar and darbuka, with vocalists singing classical Arabic poetry, including muwashshah and zajal forms.

Legacy

Andalusi music influenced musical cultures around the Mediterranean and is celebrated at festivals such as the Fez Festival of World Sacred Music. Scholars debate the extent of its influence on medieval European music and the troubadour tradition.
