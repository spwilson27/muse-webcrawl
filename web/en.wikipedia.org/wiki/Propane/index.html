Propane

Propane is a three-carbon alkane with the molecular formula C3H8. It is a gas at standard temperature and pressure, but compressible to a transportable liquid. A by-product of natural gas processing and petroleum refining, it is commonly used as a fuel in domestic and industrial applications and is the primary component of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) in many markets.

Propane is normally a colourless, nearly odourless gas; an odorant such as ethanethiol is added so leaks can be detected by smell. It burns with a clean blue flame, producing carbon dioxide and water, and has an energy content of about 46 MJ/kg.

History

Propane was identified as a volatile component of gasoline by the American chemist Walter O. Snelling in 1910. Snelling and colleagues at the US Bureau of Mines developed methods to liquefy and stabilise the gas, and by 1911 he had produced the first commercial propane. Sales of LPG grew rapidly through the 1920s and 1930s for rural heating and cooking beyond the reach of natural-gas pipelines.

Production and storage

Propane is separated from raw natural gas (from which it may form several percent) and from gases produced in petroleum refining, particularly catalytic cracking. It is stored and transported as a liquid under modest pressure in steel vessels; when released through a regulator it vaporises for use. Unlike gasoline, liquid propane occupies about 1/270 of its gaseous volume, making storage efficient.

Uses

  • Heating, cooking and water heating in homes, especially off the gas grid
  • Barbecue grills, portable stoves and patio heaters
  • Forklifts, buses and other fleet vehicles using autogas
  • Agricultural uses such as crop drying and flame weeding
  • Refrigerant (designated R290) and aerosol propellant
  • Feedstock for steam cracking to produce propylene

Safety

Propane is flammable and, being denser than air, leaking gas can pool in low areas. It is an asphyxiant in confined spaces by displacing oxygen. Tanks are fitted with pressure-relief valves, and boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion (BLEVE) is a recognised hazard for vessels exposed to fire.
