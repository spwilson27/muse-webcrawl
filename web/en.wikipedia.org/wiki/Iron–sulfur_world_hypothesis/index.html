Iron–sulfur world hypothesis

The iron–sulfur world hypothesis is a theory of the origin of life proposed by the German chemist and patent attorney Günter Wächtershäuser, in a series of papers beginning in 1988. It proposes that life originated on the surfaces of iron sulfide minerals (such as pyrite) near hydrothermal vents, where a primitive surface metabolism — driven by the energy of pyrite formation — preceded the evolution of cells and genetic material.

Theory

Wächtershäuser suggested that the reaction FeS + H2S → FeS2 (pyrite) + H2 releases enough energy to drive carbon fixation, reducing carbon dioxide into organic molecules bound to the positively charged pyrite surface. On this "pioneer organism", two-dimensional surface chemistry gradually built up autocatalytic cycles resembling the reductive citric acid cycle; only later did lipids encapsulate the chemistry into cells, and later still did RNA and DNA take over information storage. The hypothesis thus inverts the "RNA world" order: metabolism first, genetics later.

Evidence and criticism

Supporters cite laboratory demonstrations that iron and nickel sulfides can catalyse steps of carbon fixation and peptide bond formation under vent-like conditions, and the fact that many ancient enzymes still contain iron–sulfur clusters at their active sites — a possible relic of mineral catalysis. Critics argue that the proposed surface reactions have not been shown to form a complete autocatalytic network, and question whether sufficient organic products can accumulate and detach from mineral surfaces. The related alkaline hydrothermal vent theory of Michael Russell and William Martin shares the vent setting and chemiosmotic emphasis.
