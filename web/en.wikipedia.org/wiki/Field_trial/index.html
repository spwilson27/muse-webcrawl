Field trial

The term field trial is used in several distinct senses. Most commonly it refers to a competition for working gundogs in which the dogs are judged on their ability to find, flush or point, and retrieve game under realistic hunting conditions. More generally, a field trial is any experiment or test of a product, crop variety, device or intervention carried out in real-world conditions ("in the field") rather than in a laboratory.

Gundog field trials

Field trials for gundogs originated in Britain in the 1860s, with the first trials held in 1865, as landowners sought to evaluate breeding stock by performance rather than appearance. Trials are run for retrievers, spaniels, and pointing breeds (HPRs — hunt, point, retrieve). Dogs work in pairs under judges, are required to be steady to shot, and are scored on game-finding, style, marking, handling and delivery. In the United Kingdom the Kennel Club licenses trials and awards field trial champion titles; in North America, organizations such as the AKC and CKC sanction retriever and pointing-dog trials, and horseback handling is common in American pointing dog stakes.

Field trials in research

In agriculture, field trials test crop varieties, fertilizers and pesticides in replicated plots under normal growing conditions. In engineering and public health, field trials evaluate devices or interventions in situ, complementing controlled trials by measuring effectiveness under the conditions of actual use. Such trials trade experimental control for realism and external validity.
