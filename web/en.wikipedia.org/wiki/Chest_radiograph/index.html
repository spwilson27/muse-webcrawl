Chest radiograph

A chest radiograph, commonly called a chest X-ray (CXR), is a projection radiograph of the chest used to diagnose conditions affecting the chest, its contents and nearby structures. It is the most frequently performed diagnostic X-ray examination worldwide, used to evaluate the lungs, heart, airways, bones and diaphragm.

Views

The standard examination consists of a posteroanterior (PA) view, taken with the patient upright and the X-ray beam passing from back to front, often supplemented by a lateral view. Anteroposterior (AP) views are used for patients unable to stand, such as in intensive care, but magnify the heart shadow. Other specialised views include lordotic, decubitus and expiratory films.

Systematic interpretation

Radiologists typically review the film in a structured sequence — often summarised by mnemonics such as ABCDE (Airway, Breathing, Circulation/Cardiac, Diaphragm, Everything else) — checking the trachea and bronchi, lung fields, heart size and contours (the cardiothoracic ratio should be under 0.5 on a PA film), the diaphragm and costophrenic angles, and finally bones, soft tissues and any tubes or devices.

Conditions diagnosed

Common findings include pneumonia, pulmonary oedema and heart failure, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, lung cancer and other masses, tuberculosis, and rib fractures. The chest radiograph is also used to verify placement of central venous catheters, endotracheal tubes and pacemakers.

Limitations

A normal chest radiograph does not exclude disease: small tumours, early infection and pulmonary embolism may not be visible, and CT is far more sensitive for many conditions.
