Deontic modality

Deontic modality (from Greek δέον, deon, "what is proper") is a linguistic modality that indicates how the world ought to be according to certain norms, expectations, or duties. It expresses obligation, permission, and prohibition, in contrast to epistemic modality, which concerns knowledge and belief about the world.

Expression

In English, deontic modality is typically expressed by modal auxiliaries such as must, shall, should, ought to, may (permission), and must not (prohibition), as well as by expressions like be required to, be allowed to, and it is forbidden to. Many languages mark deontic meanings with dedicated verb forms, particles, or affixes.

Semantics

In formal semantics, following the framework of Angelika Kratzer, deontic modality is analysed in terms of quantification over possible worlds that conform to a body of rules or norms (a deontic modal base and ordering source). Deontic necessity ("must") holds in all the best worlds according to the norms; deontic possibility ("may") holds in at least one. The same vocabulary often serves both deontic and epistemic readings, as in "You must be tired" (epistemic) versus "You must rest" (deontic).

Related fields

Deontic modality is central to deontic logic, the formal study of obligation and permission in philosophy and law, and to the drafting and interpretation of legal and regulative texts, where distinctions between mandatory, permissive, and prohibitory language are crucial.
