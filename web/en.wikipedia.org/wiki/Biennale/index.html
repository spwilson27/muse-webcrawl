A biennale (Italian for "biennial", i.e. every other year) is a large-scale international exhibition or festival, most commonly of contemporary art, that recurs every two years. The term derives from the Venice Biennale, founded in 1895, which remains the most prestigious event of its kind and the model for the format: a central curated exhibition alongside national pavilions representing participating countries.

History and proliferation

The Venice model was adapted around the world over the twentieth century, notably by the São Paulo Biennial (1951), the second-oldest, and later by exhibitions in Sydney, Havana, Istanbul, Lyon, Berlin, Shanghai, and Gwangju. Since the 1990s the number of biennales has grown dramatically — a phenomenon sometimes called "biennialisation" — as cities use recurring art events for cultural diplomacy, tourism, and urban branding. Well over a hundred biennial-type exhibitions are now held worldwide.

Structure and criticism

Most art biennales combine a thematic exhibition curated by an appointed artistic director with national or independent presentations. They serve as marketplaces of ideas and reputations, launching artists' careers and setting curatorial trends. Critics have questioned the format's sameness across locations, its environmental footprint from international travel, and its entanglement with real-estate development and state soft power; defenders point to biennales' role in bringing global art to local audiences and supporting experimental work outside commercial galleries.

Other uses

"Biennale" is also used for recurring events in other fields: the Venice Biennale organisation itself runs architecture, cinema (the Venice Film Festival), theatre, dance, and music sections, and many cities host design or architecture biennales. Compare the triennale (every three years), Milan's Triennale, and the quinquennial Documenta in Kassel.
