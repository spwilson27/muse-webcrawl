Thomas theorem

The Thomas theorem is a theory of sociology formulated in 1928 by William Isaac Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas in their book The Child in America:

If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.

In other words, the interpretation of a situation causes the action. This interpretation is not objective. Actions are affected by subjective perceptions of situations. Whether there even is an objectively "correct" interpretation is not important for the purposes of helping guide individuals' behaviour.

Explanation

The theorem emphasises that people do not respond to situations directly but to their definition of the situation — their beliefs about what is going on. Even a false belief can set real events in motion: for example, if depositors come to believe that a bank is about to fail, they may rush to withdraw their money, and the resulting run can cause the bank actually to fail. Because consequences follow from definitions, sociologists must study people's subjective interpretations, not only objective conditions, to explain social behaviour.

Influence

The Thomas theorem became a foundational idea of symbolic interactionism and of the sociology of knowledge. Robert K. Merton built directly on it in developing his concept of the self-fulfilling prophecy (1948), in which an initially false definition of a situation evokes behaviour that makes the false conception come true. The theorem is also closely related to labelling theory, to the sociological study of moral panics, and to placebos and other cases in medicine and economics where expectations alter outcomes.

Criticism and discussion

Commentators note that the theorem does not claim beliefs simply create reality: material conditions still constrain outcomes, and not all definitions are equally powerful — those held by institutions and people in authority usually matter most. The theorem is best read as a methodological principle directing attention to the causal role of meanings in social life.
