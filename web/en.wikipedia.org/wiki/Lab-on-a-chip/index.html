Lab-on-a-chip

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lab-on-a-chip (LOC) is a device that integrates one or several laboratory functions on a single integrated circuit (chip) only millimetres to a few square centimetres in size, in order to achieve automation and high-throughput screening. LOCs deal with extremely small fluid volumes — down to less than pico-litres — using microfluidics, and are a subset of microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) devices.

Technology

Chips are commonly fabricated in glass, silicon or polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) using photolithography and soft lithography. Channels tens of micrometres wide transport samples and reagents; mixing, separation (for example by capillary electrophoresis), chemical reactions and detection can all be performed on-chip. Pumping may be driven by pressure, electro-osmosis or centrifugal force, as in "lab-on-a-disc" formats.

Applications

  • Point-of-care diagnostics – rapid tests for infectious diseases, blood chemistry and pregnancy outside central laboratories.
  • Genomics – on-chip PCR and sample preparation for sequencing.
  • Drug discovery and organ-on-chip – microfluidic cell culture models that mimic tissue and organ function for screening.
  • Environmental and food testing – portable detection of pathogens and contaminants.

Advantages and limitations

Benefits include very low sample and reagent consumption, fast analysis, low cost per test and portability. Challenges include detection sensitivity at tiny volumes, fouling of channels, world-to-chip interfacing and scaling manufacturing. A related concept, "micro total analysis systems" (µTAS), was articulated in 1990 by Andreas Manz and colleagues.
