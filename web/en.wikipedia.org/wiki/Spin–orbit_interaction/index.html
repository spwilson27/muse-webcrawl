In physics, a spin–orbit interaction (also called spin–orbit effect or spin–orbit coupling) is a relativistic interaction between a particle's spin and its motion inside a potential. It causes shifts in the particle's energy levels and is responsible for the fine structure of atomic spectra.

Physical picture

A useful semi-classical picture considers an electron orbiting a nucleus: in the electron's rest frame, the positively charged nucleus circles the electron, producing a magnetic field proportional to the electron's orbital angular momentum. This field interacts with the electron's intrinsic magnetic moment, which arises from its spin. The resulting energy depends on whether the spin and orbital angular momenta are aligned or opposed, splitting levels that would otherwise be degenerate.

A naive calculation in this picture overestimates the effect by a factor of two. The discrepancy is resolved by the Thomas precession, a relativistic kinematic effect identified by Llewellyn Thomas in 1926; the full result also follows naturally from the Dirac equation.

HSO ∝ (1/r)(dV/dr) L · S

Because the coupling depends on the gradient of the potential and hence on the nuclear charge, it grows rapidly (roughly as Z4 for hydrogen-like atoms) and is much stronger in heavy elements.

In atoms

Spin–orbit coupling splits atomic terms into levels labelled by the total angular momentum quantum number J, producing the closely spaced doublets and multiplets seen in high-resolution spectra — for example the yellow sodium D lines. It is a key ingredient of the fine structure of the hydrogen spectrum, alongside the relativistic correction to the kinetic energy. In multi-electron atoms it underlies the LS-coupling (Russell–Saunders) scheme and, in heavy atoms where the effect is strong, the alternative jj-coupling scheme.

In solids

In semiconductors and at surfaces and interfaces, spin–orbit coupling gives rise to the Rashba effect (from structural inversion asymmetry) and the Dresselhaus effect (from bulk inversion asymmetry), which split spin bands in momentum space. These effects are central to spintronics, enabling electric-field control of electron spin, and to the spin Hall effect. Strong spin–orbit coupling is also an essential ingredient in topological insulators.
