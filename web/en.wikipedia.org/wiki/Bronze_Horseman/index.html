The Bronze Horseman (Russian: Медный всадник, Medny vsadnik) is an equestrian statue of Peter the Great in Senate Square in Saint Petersburg, Russia. It was designed by the French sculptor Étienne Maurice Falconet and cast in bronze between 1775 and 1782; it was commissioned by Catherine the Great. The statue's pedestal is the enormous Thunder Stone, the largest stone ever moved by humans, a granite monolith transported from Lakhta in 1768–1770. The statue gives its name to Alexander Pushkin's 1833 narrative poem The Bronze Horseman, one of the most famous works of Russian literature, and "Bronze Horseman" has become the statue's customary name.

The statue

Falconet depicted Peter not as a triumphant military commander but as a heroic lawgiver: the horse rears at the edge of a cliff, the rider's outstretched hand gesturing toward the Neva River, while a serpent — an allegory of treason and the obstacles Peter overcame — is crushed beneath the horse's hooves as its only support besides the tail. The inscription, in Latin and Russian, reads "To Peter the First, Catherine the Second, in the year 1782."

The Thunder Stone

The pedestal, known as the Thunder Stone (Grom-kamen), was found at Lakhta near the Gulf of Finland. Weighing an estimated 1,500 tonnes in its original form, it was hauled overland on a sledge running on bronze spheres and then by barge to Saint Petersburg in a celebrated feat of 18th-century engineering supervised by the Greek engineer Marinos Charvouris.

Pushkin's poem

Pushkin's poem The Bronze Horseman: A Petersburg Tale (written 1833, published posthumously in 1837) tells of the poor clerk Evgeny, driven mad by the loss of his beloved in the great flood of 1824, who curses the statue — and is then pursued through the streets by the Horseman come to life. The poem crystallised the statue's place in the mythology of Saint Petersburg and in the Russian debate over Peter's legacy.
