Phonological awareness

Phonological awareness is an individual's awareness of the sound structure of spoken language — the ability to recognise and manipulate units of oral language such as words, syllables, onsets and rimes, and individual phonemes. It is an umbrella term that includes phonemic awareness, the narrower ability to notice, think about, and work with the individual sounds (phonemes) in spoken words.

Levels of awareness

Phonological skills are usually described along a continuum from larger to smaller sound units: awareness of words in sentences; syllable segmentation and blending; onset–rime awareness (recognising, for example, that "cat" is made of /k/ + "at"); and phoneme-level skills, including phoneme blending, segmentation, deletion, and substitution. These skills generally develop in this order during the preschool and early school years.

Reading development

Phonological awareness is one of the strongest predictors of later reading achievement, because decoding in alphabetic writing systems requires mapping letters and letter patterns onto the sounds of spoken words. Research syntheses, including the report of the U.S. National Reading Panel (2000), found that explicit instruction in phonemic awareness improves early reading and spelling. Difficulties with phonological processing are a central feature of most accounts of developmental dyslexia.

Assessment and instruction

Common assessments ask children to blend sounds into words, segment words into sounds, delete a sound from a word ("say 'smile' without the /s/"), or identify words that rhyme or share initial sounds. Instruction typically uses oral games, rhymes, and songs in preschool, progressing to linking sounds with letters in phonics teaching.
