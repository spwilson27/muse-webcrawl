Speech act

In the philosophy of language and linguistics, a speech act is an utterance that has a performative function in language and communication. The theory was introduced by J. L. Austin in lectures published posthumously as How to Do Things with Words (1962) and systematized by his student John Searle. Examples include promising, ordering, apologizing, predicting, and declaring a couple married.

Austin's distinctions

Austin distinguished three aspects of an utterance:

  • Locutionary act — the act of saying something with a determinate sense and reference.
  • Illocutionary act — what is done in saying it: asserting, requesting, warning, promising.
  • Perlocutionary act — what is brought about by saying it: persuading, frightening, or enlightening the hearer.

Austin also introduced performative utterances such as "I pronounce you married," which do not describe an action but accomplish it, and catalogued the conditions ("felicity conditions") under which such acts succeed or misfire.

Searle's development

Searle proposed that speech acts are the basic units of linguistic communication and classified illocutionary acts into five types: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations. He also analyzed indirect speech acts, in which the primary act is performed by way of another — as when "Can you pass the salt?" functions as a request. Speech act theory became a foundation of pragmatics and has been applied in discourse analysis, law, and human–computer interaction.
