Captain Swing

ContextSwing Riots, southern England, 1830
NatureFictitious signature on threatening letters
TargetsThreshing machines, tithes, low wages

Captain Swing was the fictitious name signed to threatening letters sent to farmers, magistrates, and other targets during the Swing Riots, a widespread uprising by agricultural labourers in southern and eastern England beginning in the summer of 1830. The name functioned like that of "General Ludd" for the Luddites a generation earlier — a mythical leader whose signature lent collective menace to anonymous demands.

The Swing Riots

The riots began in Kent in August 1830 with the destruction of threshing machines, which labourers blamed for winter unemployment, and spread across southern England. Rioters demanded higher wages (typically two shillings a day), reduced tithes, and the destruction of threshing machines; rick-burning and machine-breaking were accompanied by letters signed "Swing" threatening worse if demands were not met. The word "swing" probably evoked the swinging flail of hand threshing, the gallows, or the whip of the parish overseer.

Repression and aftermath

The government of the Duke of Wellington, and then Earl Grey's ministry, responded with special commissions that tried nearly 2,000 prisoners. Nineteen men were hanged and about 500 transported to Australia. The disturbances, combined with the political crisis of the period, formed part of the background to the Reform Act 1832 and the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. The events were comprehensively documented by Eric Hobsbawm and George Rudé in their 1969 study Captain Swing.
