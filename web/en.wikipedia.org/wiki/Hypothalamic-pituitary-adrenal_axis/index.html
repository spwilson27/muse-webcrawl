Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis) is a major neuroendocrine system controlling reactions to stress and regulating many body processes, including digestion, the immune system, mood and emotions, and energy storage and expenditure. It is the common name for the set of interactions among three organs: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, and the adrenal glands.

Mechanism

In response to stress or circadian signals, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and vasopressin into the portal circulation. These stimulate the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which travels in the blood to the adrenal cortex and triggers release of glucocorticoids, chiefly cortisol. Cortisol mobilises energy, modulates immune function and then feeds back negatively on the pituitary and hypothalamus to shut the response down.

Rhythm and dysfunction

HPA activity follows a diurnal rhythm, with cortisol peaking shortly after waking. Chronic stress, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and other conditions are associated with dysregulated — either hyperactive or blunted — HPA responses. Endocrine disease directly involves the axis: Cushing's syndrome results from cortisol excess, while Addison's disease reflects adrenal insufficiency, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia stems from enzyme defects in cortisol synthesis.
