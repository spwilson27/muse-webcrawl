Arms control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arms control is a term for international restrictions upon the development, production, stockpiling, proliferation and usage of weapons, especially weapons of mass destruction, agreed between potential adversaries to reduce the risk, destructiveness and cost of war without necessarily reducing arsenals to zero (which is disarmament proper). It operates through treaties, verification regimes, export controls and confidence-building measures.

History

Early efforts (Hague Conventions 1899/1907, the failed League-era disarmament conference) gave way to Cold War bilateralism: the Hotline Agreement, Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963), Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), SALT and ABM agreements (1972), INF Treaty (1987, eliminating a class of missiles) and START reductions after 1991. Multilateral regimes followed for chemical weapons (CWC 1993, with the OPCW), biological weapons (BWC 1972), landmines (Ottawa 1997) and cluster munitions (2008); the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017) entered into force without the nuclear-weapon states.

Erosion and new domains

The 2010s–2020s saw the architecture erode: US withdrawal from the ABM (2002) and INF (2019) treaties, Russia's suspension of New START participation (2023), and stalemate over space weapons, cyber capabilities, autonomous weapons and hypersonics — while verification technology (satellites, on-site inspection, open-source intelligence) keeps evolving. Analysts debate whether arms control can be rebuilt for a multipolar nuclear order including China.
