Prisoner in the Vatican

The Vatican palaces, self-imposed confinement of the popes 1870–1929.

"Prisoner in the Vatican" (Italian: Prigioniero in Vaticano) is the phrase describing the self-imposed confinement of the popes within the walls of the Vatican between 1870 and 1929, during the unresolved Roman Question. After Italian troops captured Rome in 1870 and ended the Papal States, Pius IX and his successors refused to recognise the Kingdom of Italy's annexation of the city or to leave the Vatican precincts, regarding themselves as prisoners of the Italian state.

Background

Italian unification (the Risorgimento) progressively stripped the Papal States from the pope. After Rome was proclaimed capital of Italy, Italian troops breached Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, and a plebiscite ratified annexation. Pius IX, who had declared papal infallibility that same year at the First Vatican Council, denounced the seizure as robbery.

The standoff

Italy's Law of Guarantees (1871) unilaterally offered the pope sovereign honours, an annuity, and free use of the Vatican and Lateran palaces; Pius IX rejected it as a mere revocable statute, issued the Non expedit discouraging Catholic participation in Italian politics, and never again left the Vatican. The "captivity" was symbolic — the popes moved freely within the precinct and gardens — but it dramatised the papacy's refusal to accept the loss of temporal power, and it drew diplomatic support and Peter's Pence donations from Catholics worldwide.

Resolution

Negotiations with Mussolini's government culminated in the Lateran Treaty of 11 February 1929: Italy recognised the sovereignty of the Holy See over the newly created Vatican City State, and the Holy See recognised Rome as Italy's capital. Pius XI hailed the settlement, and the pope once again appeared in procession outside St Peter's.
