Theriac

Theriac is a medical concoction originally formulated in ancient Greece and used for nearly two millennia as an antidote to poison and as a universal remedy. Derived from the Greek thēriakē (pertaining to wild beasts, whose bites it was meant to cure), the preparation in its mature form combined dozens of ingredients — classically including viper's flesh, opium, wine, honey, and scores of herbs, spices, minerals and gums — compounded into an electuary that was aged, sometimes for years, before use. The most famous version, theriaca Andromachi or Venice treacle, was manufactured publicly under official supervision in Venice and other cities well into the eighteenth century.

History

Theriac descends from mithridatium, the universal antidote attributed to Mithridates VI, king of Pontus (r. 120–63 BC), who was said to have immunised himself against poisons by taking sub-lethal doses. After Pompey defeated Mithridates, the recipe came to Rome, where Andromachus the Elder, physician to Nero, revised it — adding viper flesh and increasing the opium — and described it in a poem in elegiac verse; his formula, refined by Galen, became canonical. Galen wrote a whole treatise on theriac and dosed the emperor Marcus Aurelius with it daily. Through the medieval period theriac was the sovereign remedy against plague, poisoning, snakebite and almost every disease, a staple of Islamic, Byzantine and European medicine alike.

Composition and manufacture

Recipes varied but the official Venice formula contained more than sixty ingredients, among them viper flesh, opium, cinnamon, saffron, myrrh, gentian, rhubarb, valerian, squill, bitumen and old wine, bound with honey. Because the ingredients were costly and fraud was common, cities such as Venice, Montpellier and Nuremberg staged the compounding publicly, with magistrates and physicians inspecting each component. The finished treacle was stored in decorated majolica jars (albarelli) and was reputed to improve with age — Galen recommended twelve years.

Decline

Scepticism grew in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: William Heberden's 1745 essay attacked theriac as a superstitious relic, and the preparation was progressively cut from official pharmacopoeias — removed from the London Pharmacopoeia in 1788, though it survived in French and German pharmacopoeias into the nineteenth century. Its one genuinely active principal, opium, explains some of its perceived efficacy as a painkiller and sedative. The word survives in "treacle", originally the same substance.
