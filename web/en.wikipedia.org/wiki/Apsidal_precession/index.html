Apsidal precession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In celestial mechanics, apsidal precession (also called apsidal motion or perihelion precession) is the gradual rotation of the line of apsides — the line connecting the periapsis and apoapsis (the nearest and farthest points) of an orbit — within the orbital plane. For a planet orbiting the Sun, apsidal precession means that the direction of perihelion slowly changes over time, so the orbit is not a fixed ellipse but traces a rosette pattern.

Causes

In a pure two-body system governed by Newton's inverse-square law of gravity, the orbit is a closed ellipse and no apsidal precession occurs (a consequence of Bertrand's theorem). Precession arises from any perturbation of the ideal inverse-square force:

  • Other gravitating bodies — the gravitational pulls of other planets perturb each orbit. In the Solar System, Jupiter dominates this effect.
  • Non-spherical central body — an oblate primary (quantified by the J2 quadrupole term) causes the orbits of satellites and rings to precess; this is important for artificial satellites and for Saturn's rings.
  • Tidal effects — tidal bulges raised on the bodies can advance or retard the line of apsides.
  • General relativity — Einstein's theory predicts an additional advance of perihelion for any orbit, largest where the gravitational field is strongest.

Mercury's perihelion

The most famous case is the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion. Mercury's perihelion advances by about 574 arcseconds per century, of which planetary perturbations explain about 531 arcseconds; the residual ~43 arcseconds per century was a known discrepancy in the nineteenth century, documented by Urbain Le Verrier in 1859. Attempts to explain it with an undiscovered planet ("Vulcan") failed. In 1915, Albert Einstein showed that general relativity predicts exactly this extra advance, providing one of the theory's first and most important confirmations — an early success of the theory alongside the bending of light and the gravitational redshift.

Other examples

The Earth's perihelion precesses with a period of roughly 112,000 years relative to the fixed stars; combined with the precession of the equinoxes, this produces the approximately 21,000-year cycle of climatic precession relevant to Milankovitch cycles and ice ages. Apsidal precession is dramatic in some exoplanet systems and in binary stars; the relativistic apsidal motion measured in binary pulsars, such as the Hulse–Taylor binary, provides further precision tests of general relativity. In galactic dynamics, apsidal precession rates constrain the distribution of dark matter.
