Life and training

James Gibbs (23 December 1682 – 5 August 1754) was a Scottish architect, the dominant designer of churches in Georgian Britain. A Catholic from Aberdeen, Gibbs travelled to Rome in 1703 to study for the priesthood, but instead trained in the studio of the Baroque architect Carlo Fontana — the only British architect of his generation formed in Italy. Returning in 1709, he found favour with Tory patrons and, after the Hanoverian succession limited his official prospects, built up the leading private practice of the day from his base in London.

Major works

Gibbs's masterpiece is St Martin-in-the-Fields, Trafalgar Square (1722–26), whose temple portico fronted by a tall steeple rising through the pediment became the template for countless Anglican and American colonial churches. At Oxford he built the Radcliffe Camera (1737–49), the circular domed library that is among the most famous buildings in England. Other works include St Mary le Strand (1714–17), the Senate House at Cambridge, Ditchley House, and the layout of St Bartholomew's Hospital. The term "Gibbs surround" — bold rusticated blocks around doorways and windows — preserves his name in architectural vocabulary.

A Book of Architecture

His A Book of Architecture (1728), the first published by a British architect working in Britain, spread his designs — 150 plates of churches, houses, and ornaments — throughout Britain and the colonies, shaping church architecture in America for a century.
