MOS Technology, Inc., also known as MOStek and later renamed CSG (Commodore Semiconductor Group), was an American semiconductor design and fabrication company based in Norristown, Pennsylvania. It is best known as the designer of the 6502 microprocessor and of the custom chips that powered Commodore International's home computers, including the VIC-20 and the best-selling Commodore 64.

History

MOS Technology was founded in 1969 to supply calculator chips and other metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) integrated circuits. Several of its engineers, including Chuck Peddle, came from Motorola's 6800 microprocessor team. Frustrated by the 6800's high price ($300), Peddle's team designed a dramatically cheaper processor: the 6501 and 6502, introduced in 1975 at $25 each — an announcement famously made at the Wescon trade show, where chips were sold out of a glass jar.

Motorola sued over the pin-compatible 6501, and MOS settled, but the 6502 went on to power the Apple I and Apple II, the Commodore PET and VIC-20, the Atari 8-bit family and 2600 (via its 6507 variant), the Nintendo Entertainment System (via Ricoh's licensed variant), and the BBC Micro. In 1976, facing financial difficulty, MOS was acquired by Commodore, which secured its chip supply; Peddle's KIM-1 single-board computer became a Commodore product.

Products

Beyond the 650x family — including the 6510 used in the Commodore 64 — MOS designed the 6522 VIA, the 6526 CIA, the VIC and VIC-II video chips, and the SID (6581/8580) sound chip, widely regarded as one of the most musical synthesizer chips of its era. After Commodore's bankruptcy in 1994, the group's remains were sold; the plant at Norristown, an EPA Superfund site, closed, and the group finally shut down in 2001.
