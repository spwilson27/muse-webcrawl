Pan (novel)

Pan is an 1894 novel by Norwegian author Knut Hamsun. Written in a lyrical, stream-of-consciousness style, it recounts a summer spent by the narrator, Lieutenant Thomas Glahn, in the forests of Nordland in northern Norway, and his hopeless love for Edvarda, the daughter of a local merchant. Pan is considered one of Hamsun's most important works and a landmark of early modernist and neo-romantic literature.

Plot

Lieutenant Glahn, a solitary former officer, lives in a hut in the forest near the small coastal trading post of Sirilund with his dog Aesop, hunting and revelling in nature. He falls in love with Edvarda Mack, the capricious daughter of the wealthy local trader, but their relationship falters through pride, misunderstandings, and the arrival of rivals, including a Finnish baron and a doctor. Glahn's jealousy and erratic behaviour drive Edvarda away; near the end of summer he accidentally shoots himself in the foot — or allows the shot to happen — and leaves Nordland. An epilogue, "Glahn's Death", narrated by a former hunting companion, tells of Glahn's later death in India in 1861, deliberately provoked at the hands of the narrator.

Themes and style

The novel takes its title from Pan, the Greek god of nature, and celebrates the irrational, sensual life of the natural world against bourgeois society. Hamsun's fluid, subjective narration and exploration of the "unconscious life of the soul" influenced modernist writers including Thomas Mann and Isaac Bashevis Singer; Singer called Hamsun the father of the modern school of literature. The book also includes the inserted tale of Iselin, a figure of Glahn's fantasies.
