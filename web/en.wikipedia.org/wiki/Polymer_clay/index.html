Polymer clay

Polymer clay is a type of hardenable modeling clay based on the polymer polyvinyl chloride (PVC). It typically contains no clay minerals, but like mineral clay it is worked by hand and then cured in an oven to harden — a process during which the plasticizer is absorbed and the PVC particles fuse. Liquid polymer clay formulations also exist.

History

Polymer clay was first developed in Germany in the late 1930s by doll maker Käthe Kruse as a substitute material; the formulation was not suitable for her production but was later commercialized by her daughter as FIMO (from Fina's Modelling Compound). In the United States, the Zenick family developed Sculpey, originally as a heat-transfer compound, which found its market as a modeling material. Other major brands include Premo, Cernit, and Kato Polyclay.

Composition and curing

Polymer clay consists of PVC resin suspended in a plasticizer, with pigments and fillers. It remains pliable indefinitely at room temperature and hardens when baked, typically at around 110–130 °C (230–265 °F) for 15–30 minutes per quarter-inch of thickness. Over-baking or overheating can release hydrogen chloride gas, so ventilation and temperature control are advised. Finished work can be sanded, drilled, painted, and glazed.

Techniques and uses

Artists use polymer clay for sculpture, jewelry, miniatures, beads, and vessels. Distinctive techniques include millefiori caning — building patterned logs of clay whose cross-section reveals an image when sliced — mokume gane layering, Skinner blends for color gradients, and image transfer. Because it is inexpensive and cures in a home oven, it is widely used by hobbyists, doll and miniature makers, and in prototyping.
