Robert Rosenthal (March 2, 1933 – January 5, 2024) was a German-born American psychologist who spent much of his career at Harvard University and the University of California, Riverside. He is best known for his research on experimenter expectancy effects and on behavioural confirmation in interpersonal settings, including the widely discussed "Pygmalion effect" in education.

Early life and education

Rosenthal was born in Giessen, Germany, and emigrated to the United States with his family in 1938, fleeing Nazi persecution. He grew up in New York and later moved to Los Angeles, earning his bachelor's degree and his PhD in psychology from the University of California, Los Angeles.

Expectancy research

In the early 1960s Rosenthal conducted experiments showing that researchers' expectations could unintentionally influence the results of experiments — including studies in which students running maze experiments obtained faster or slower performance from rats labelled as "bright" or "dull", although the animals had been assigned at random. He summarised this work in Experimenter Effects in Behavioral Research (1966).

Pygmalion in the classroom

With school principal Lenore Jacobson, Rosenthal reported a study in which elementary school teachers were told that certain randomly selected pupils were "about to bloom" intellectually; those pupils subsequently showed greater gains on IQ tests than their classmates. Published as Pygmalion in the Classroom (1968), the study became famous — and controversial, attracting criticism over its methods and statistics, and sparking decades of research into teacher expectations and self-fulfilling prophecies.

Meta-analysis and nonverbal communication

Rosenthal was a pioneer of meta-analysis, developing influential statistical methods for combining research results, including the "fail-safe N" for the file drawer problem, and served on the panel whose work on aspirin and heart-attack prevention is a classic example of the technique. He also studied nonverbal communication, developing the Profile of Nonverbal Sensitivity (PONS) test, and co-authored widely used texts on research methods and effect sizes.

Later career

After three decades at Harvard, Rosenthal moved in 1999 to the University of California, Riverside, where he was a Distinguished Professor of Psychology. He received numerous honours, including the Gold Medal Award for Life Achievement in the Science of Psychology from the American Psychological Foundation. He died in Riverside, California, in January 2024.
