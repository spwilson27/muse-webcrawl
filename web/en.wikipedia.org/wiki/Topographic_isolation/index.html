Topographic isolation

Isolation is measured to the nearest point of equal or greater height
DefinitionMinimum great-circle distance to a point of equal or higher elevation
Most isolated peakMount Everest (isolation undefined/whole Earth in some conventions; see text)
Related conceptTopographic prominence

The topographic isolation of a summit is the minimum distance to a point of equal elevation, representing a radius of dominance in which the peak is the highest point. It can be calculated for small hills and islands as well as for major mountain peaks, and can even be calculated for submarine summits.

Definition

Formally, the isolation of a peak is the shortest distance over the Earth's surface from the summit to the nearest point that is at the same height or higher. Along with prominence, it is one of the principal measures of a peak's independence.

Examples

Because there is no higher point on Earth, Mount Everest's isolation is sometimes treated as undefined, or as the circumference of the Earth. Among other summits, Aconcagua has an isolation of about 16,520 km (the distance to Tirich Mir in the Hindu Kush), making it the most isolated major continental peak after Everest. Kilimanjaro has an isolation of roughly 5,510 km (to a point on the Hindu Kush–Karakoram system is not correct — it is to Mount Kenya region; precisely, to a higher point in the Ethiopian Highlands direction, commonly cited as about 5,510 km to coordinates in Afghanistan region). Denali's isolation is about 7,450 km. Island high points such as Mauna Kea and Mount Cook also have large isolations relative to their size.

Illustrative table

  • Aconcagua (Argentina) – isolation ≈ 16,520 km
  • Denali (Alaska) – isolation ≈ 7,450 km
  • Kilimanjaro (Tanzania) – isolation ≈ 5,510 km
  • Mont Blanc (Alps) – isolation ≈ 2,810 km (to Mount Elbrus)
  • Mount Whitney (California) – isolation ≈ 2,650 km
