Joseph Proust

Joseph Louis Proust (26 September 1754 - 5 July 1826) was a French chemist. He is best known for discovering the law of definite proportions (also called Proust's law), which states that a chemical compound always contains its component elements in fixed ratio by mass.

Life

Proust was born in Angers, the son of an apothecary, and studied chemistry in Paris under Guillaume-Francois Rouelle. He spent much of his career in Spain, where the government invited him to teach chemistry and direct laboratories at Segovia, Salamanca and Madrid, equipping him with one of the finest laboratories in Europe. The Peninsular War destroyed his laboratory and library, and he returned to France in straitened circumstances, eventually retiring to Angers.

Law of definite proportions

Through careful analyses of compounds such as copper carbonate and the oxides of tin and iron, carried out largely between 1794 and 1804, Proust showed that a compound's composition is constant regardless of how it is prepared or where it is found. His conclusion was attacked by Claude Louis Berthollet, who believed composition could vary continuously; the ensuing debate was one of the famous controversies of early chemistry and ended in Proust's favor. Combined with Dalton's atomic theory, the law became a cornerstone of modern chemistry, though later work showed exceptions for non-stoichiometric ("berthollide") compounds.

Other work

Proust analyzed plant and food substances, distinguishing several kinds of sugars found in fruits, and investigated gunpowder substitutes and other practical problems for the Spanish and French governments. He was elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1816.
