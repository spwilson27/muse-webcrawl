Castra (Latin plural of castrum) were the fortified camps and fortresses built by the Roman army. A marching camp was constructed every evening on campaign, following a standardised plan, while permanent fortresses housed legions on the frontiers of the empire for centuries.

Layout

The castra followed a regular rectangular plan with rounded corners, surrounded by a ditch and rampart (later stone walls and towers). Two main streets crossed at the centre: the via praetoria and the via principalis, with four gates. At the centre stood the principia (headquarters), near the commander's house (praetorium), granaries (horrea), workshops, a hospital (valetudinarium) and barrack blocks arranged by unit. Permanent forts attracted civilian settlements (vici and canabae) outside the walls.

Legacy

Many European cities grew from Roman forts, and the element survives in English place names such as Chester, Lancaster, Doncaster and Winchester (from Latin castra via Old English ceaster). Authors such as Polybius, Hyginus and Vegetius describe camp construction in detail, and excavated examples include Vindolanda and Housesteads on Hadrian's Wall.
