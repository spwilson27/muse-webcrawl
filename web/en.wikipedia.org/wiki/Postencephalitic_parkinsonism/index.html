Postencephalitic parkinsonism

Postencephalitic parkinsonism is a disease believed to be caused by a viral illness that triggers degeneration of the nerve cells in the substantia nigra. It classically followed the epidemic of encephalitis lethargica (von Economo's encephalitis) that swept the world between about 1916 and the late 1920s, leaving many survivors with severe, delayed-onset parkinsonism months or years after the acute illness.

Clinical features

Patients developed the rigidity, bradykinesia, and tremor of Parkinson's disease, often at unusually young ages, together with distinctive features including oculogyric crises (forced upward deviation of the eyes), sleep disorders, and psychiatric symptoms. Some patients lapsed into states of profound akinesia lasting decades, remaining conscious but unable to move or speak.

Awakenings

The condition became widely known through neurologist Oliver Sacks's book Awakenings (1973), later adapted into a film, describing his treatment of institutionalized survivors at Beth Abraham Hospital in the Bronx with the then-new drug L-DOPA in 1969. Patients briefly "awoke" from their frozen states before developing severe side effects and relapsing.

Pathology and cause

Brains of affected patients show neurofibrillary tangles in the substantia nigra and other brainstem nuclei, resembling tau pathology rather than the Lewy bodies of idiopathic Parkinson's disease. Whether encephalitis lethargica was caused by the influenza virus of the 1918 pandemic, an enterovirus, or an autoimmune process remains unresolved; the disease largely vanished after the 1920s.
