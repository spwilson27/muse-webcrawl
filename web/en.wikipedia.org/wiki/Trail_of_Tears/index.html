The Trail of Tears was the forced displacement of approximately 60,000 people of the "Five Civilized Tribes" between 1830 and 1850, and the death of thousands of people during removal or from its aftermath. Members of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw, and Choctaw nations were forcibly removed by the United States government from their ancestral homelands in the Southeastern United States to areas west of the Mississippi River designated as Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma).

Background

The Indian Removal Act of 1830, signed by President Andrew Jackson, authorised the president to negotiate removal treaties with Native American tribes. The Choctaw were the first to be removed, in 1831; the Seminole resisted, leading to the Second Seminole War. The Cherokee removal of 1838–1839 followed the contested Treaty of New Echota and was enforced by the U.S. Army under General Winfield Scott, despite the Supreme Court's ruling in Worcester v. Georgia.

The journey

Some 16,000 Cherokee were rounded up into stockades and marched or transported west, many during the harsh winter of 1838–39. Thousands died of disease, exposure and starvation along routes of roughly 1,200 miles (1,900 km) on foot and by wagon, flatboat and steamboat. The Cherokee phrase for the removal, Nunna daul Isunyi, means "the trail where they cried".

Legacy

The Trail of Tears is commemorated by the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, established in 1987, which covers about 5,043 miles (8,116 km) across nine states. In 2009 and 2019, resolutions of the U.S. Congress formally expressed regret for the removal policies.
