The Kalevala (Finnish: [ˈkɑleʋɑlɑ]) is a 19th-century work of epic poetry compiled by Elias Lönnrot from Karelian and Finnish oral folklore and mythology, first published in 1835 and in its expanded, definitive form in 1849. It is regarded as the national epic of Finland and Karelia and is one of the most significant works of Finnish literature, a cornerstone of Finnish national identity formed while Finland was a Grand Duchy of the Russian Empire.

Compilation

Lönnrot, a district doctor in Kajaani, made eleven field trips between 1828 and 1844 into Finnish and Russian Karelia, recording thousands of verses of runo singing — trochaic tetrameter songs performed antifonally, often to the kantele — from singers such as Arhippa Perttunen and Ontrei Malinen. Believing the songs were fragments of a once-unified epic, he selected, ordered, and lightly rewrote them into a continuous narrative; the 1835 Old Kalevala had 32 poems, the 1849 edition 50 poems and 22,795 verses.

Contents

The epic tells of the creation of the world from a duck's egg, the adventures of the ancient sage and singer Väinämöinen, the smith Ilmarinen who forges the sky and the miraculous Sampo — a talisman of wealth coveted by Louhi, mistress of the northern land of Pohjola — and the reckless hero Lemminkäinen, killed and reassembled by his mother. A cycle tells of Kullervo, a slave boy whose life of vengeance ends in suicide, a story that later inspired Tolkien; the epic closes with Marjatta's virgin birth of a king who displaces Väinämöinen, an allegory of Christianity's arrival. The collecting method that produced the Kalevala — stitching oral song into epic — parallels the oral traditions studied by Milman Parry and influenced Longfellow's Hiawatha, whose metre it shares. It profoundly inspired Finnish art and music, above all Gallen-Kallela's paintings and Sibelius's tone poems.
