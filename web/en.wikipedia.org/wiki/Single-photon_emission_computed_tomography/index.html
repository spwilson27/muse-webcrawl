Single-photon emission computed tomography

Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT, or less commonly SPET) is a nuclear medicine tomographic imaging technique using gamma rays. It provides three-dimensional metabolic and functional information about organs, in contrast to the anatomical detail of X-ray CT. A SPECT scan integrates a gamma camera that rotates around the patient, acquiring projection images of a radiopharmaceutical's distribution, from which cross-sectional images are reconstructed.

Principle

The patient is given a radioactive tracer — most often technetium-99m bound to a compound that targets a particular tissue or process, such as sestamibi for myocardial perfusion or HMPAO for cerebral blood flow. As the tracer decays it emits single gamma photons; a collimator in front of the camera's scintillation crystal allows only photons arriving from known directions to be counted, so the position of each event can be recorded. Projections from many angles are reconstructed (filtered back-projection or iterative methods) into slices showing tracer concentration. Modern hybrid SPECT/CT scanners add a CT scan for attenuation correction and anatomical localisation.

Applications

Major uses include myocardial perfusion imaging to detect coronary artery disease, brain perfusion studies in dementia, stroke and epilepsy, bone scintigraphy for metastases and infection, parathyroid and thyroid imaging, and sentinel lymph node localisation. SPECT offers lower spatial resolution and sensitivity than PET but uses cheaper, longer-lived tracers that do not require an on-site cyclotron.

History

The technique grew from the Anger scintillation camera (1958) and tomographic reconstruction work in the 1960s–70s (notably by David Kuhl and Roy Edwards with the MARK series of scanners), becoming a routine clinical modality in the 1980s as rotating gamma cameras and computing improved.
