Catalina Sky Survey

Survey type: Astronomical survey
Organisation: University of Arizona
Location: Arizona, United States
Website: catalina.lpl.arizona.edu

The Catalina Sky Survey (CSS) is an astronomical survey to discover comets and asteroids. It is conducted at the Steward Observatory's Catalina Station, located near Tucson, Arizona, in the United States.

Besides identifying asteroids, it also searches for near-Earth objects, and a similar initiative is the Mount Lemmon Survey (MLS) which uses a different telescope. The campaign is principally funded by NASA.

Mission

The CSS program's primary goal is contributing to the inventory of near-Earth objects, in particular potential impactors on Earth which can be hazardous. The survey is credited with discovering many potentially hazardous asteroids and is considered one of the most productive near-Earth object search programs.

Techniques

The survey uses three telescopes: a 1.5 m (59 in) f/2 telescope on the peak of Mount Lemmon, a 68 cm (27 in) Schmidt telescope near Mount Bigelow (both in the Catalina Mountains of Arizona), and a 0.5 m (20 in) Uppsala Schmidt telescope at Siding Spring Observatory in Australia, which was used until 2013 as part of the Siding Spring Survey.

Discoveries

In 2005, CSS became the most prolific NEO survey, surpassing Lincoln Near-Earth Asteroid Research (LINEAR) in the total number of NEOs and potentially hazardous asteroids discovered each year. CSS discovered 310 NEOs in 2005, 396 in 2006, 466 in 2007, and in 2008 found 564 NEOs.

Notable discoveries include asteroid 2008 TC3, the first asteroid observed before impacting Earth, which struck Sudan in October 2008, and 2007 WD5, which in late December 2007 was shown to have a 1-in-75 chance of hitting Mars in January 2008 but was later confirmed to pass safely.
