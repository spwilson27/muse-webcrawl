Aspic

Aspic
TypeSavoury jelly
Main ingredientsMeat stock or consommé, gelatin; meat, eggs, vegetables or seafood
OriginFrance (medieval precursors across Europe)

Aspic is a savoury jelly made from meat stock or consommé that sets when cooled, thanks to the natural gelatin in the stock (supplemented today with added gelatin). Ingredients such as meat, poultry, eggs, vegetables or seafood are set into the jelly, often in a decorative mould, and the dish is served cold. A classic meatless version uses tomato juice and is known as tomato aspic.

History

Meat jellies appear in medieval European cookery, where slow-cooked broths from gelatin-rich cuts set naturally when cool, helping preserve cooked meats. Aspic became a showcase of French haute cuisine in the nineteenth century: chefs such as Marie-Antoine Carême used clarified aspic to encase elaborate preparations and to glaze cold meats, and the dish — painstaking and expensive — signalled luxury. In the twentieth century aspic spread widely in home cooking through commercial gelatin, reaching peak popularity in the 1950s in Britain and North America, before falling out of fashion; it persists in traditional forms across Europe, such as Polish galareta, Russian kholodets and Ukrainian kholodets.

Preparation

A well-flavoured stock is clarified, seasoned, and enriched with gelatin if needed. It is poured into a mould in layers with the solid ingredients, chilling between additions so the pieces remain suspended. The set aspic is unmoulded and served in slices, often with mayonnaise or a vinaigrette. Aspic also serves a practical role as a protective glaze for pâtés and cold buffet meats, delaying oxidation and drying.
