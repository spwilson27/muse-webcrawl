The aqueous humour is a transparent, colourless, watery fluid that fills the anterior and posterior chambers of the eye — the spaces between the cornea and the iris and between the iris and the lens. (The spelling "humor" is used in American English.) It is distinct from the vitreous humour, the gel that fills the much larger posterior segment behind the lens.

Production and circulation

Aqueous humour is continuously secreted by the ciliary processes of the ciliary body, at a rate of roughly 2–3 microlitres per minute, through a combination of active secretion, ultrafiltration, and diffusion from the blood plasma. From the posterior chamber it flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber, circulates by convection (warmed by the iris it rises, cooled by the cornea it sinks), and drains out of the eye at the iridocorneal angle. The main outflow route is the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm and thence to the episcleral veins; a smaller fraction leaves by the uveoscleral route through the ciliary muscle into the suprachoroidal space.

Functions and composition

The aqueous humour maintains the intraocular pressure (normally about 10–21 mmHg) that holds the eyeball's shape and keeps its optical surfaces at the correct distances. Because the cornea and lens are avascular, the aqueous also supplies them with nutrients — glucose, amino acids, and oxygen — and removes metabolic waste; it contains a much higher concentration of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) than plasma, thought to protect against ultraviolet-generated free radicals. Its composition resembles plasma but with far less protein. The fluid is replaced completely roughly every 100 minutes.

Clinical significance

A balance between production and outflow determines intraocular pressure. If drainage through the trabecular meshwork is obstructed, pressure rises and can damage the optic nerve, causing glaucoma — in open-angle glaucoma the meshwork's resistance increases gradually, while in angle-closure glaucoma the iris physically blocks the angle. Most glaucoma medications act on aqueous dynamics: beta-blockers such as timolol and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors reduce production, whereas prostaglandin analogues increase uveoscleral outflow and miotics such as pilocarpine increase trabecular outflow. Aqueous humour can leak from the eye after penetrating injury or surgery, and its abnormal contents (cells, flare, blood) are signs assessed during slit-lamp examination in conditions such as uveitis and hyphema.
