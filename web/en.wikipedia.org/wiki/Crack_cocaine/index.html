Crack cocaine

Crack cocaine, commonly known simply as crack, is a free-base form of cocaine that can be smoked. It is produced by processing powdered cocaine hydrochloride with an alkaline substance such as sodium bicarbonate and water, forming hard, off-white "rocks" that vaporise at a relatively low temperature and make a crackling sound when heated, which gives the drug its name.

Effects

Smoking crack delivers cocaine to the brain within seconds, producing an intense but short-lived euphoria lasting only about 5 to 15 minutes, followed by a sharp comedown that encourages repeated use. Like other forms of cocaine it is a powerful stimulant and is highly addictive. Health risks include cardiovascular events such as heart attack and stroke, respiratory damage, seizures and severe psychological dependence.

History and social impact

Crack emerged in the early 1980s in poor inner-city neighbourhoods of the United States, including New York, Los Angeles and Miami, where it was sold cheaply in small quantities. Its spread — often described as the "crack epidemic" — was associated with increases in crime and social disruption and provoked strong political responses. In the United States, federal sentencing law long imposed far harsher penalties for crack than for equivalent amounts of powdered cocaine (a 100-to-1 quantity ratio under the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986), a disparity widely criticised as racially biased and reduced to 18-to-1 by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010.

Legal status

Crack cocaine is classified as a Schedule II substance in the United States and as a Class A drug in the United Kingdom, and its manufacture, distribution and possession are criminal offences in most jurisdictions.
