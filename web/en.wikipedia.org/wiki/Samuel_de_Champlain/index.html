Samuel de Champlain

Samuel de Champlain (c. 13 August 1567 – 25 December 1635) was a French explorer, navigator, cartographer, draftsman, soldier, geographer, ethnologist, and diplomat. He made between 21 and 29 trips across the Atlantic Ocean, and founded Quebec City on 3 July 1608. Called the "Father of New France", he consolidated French colonies in the Americas and mapped extensive regions of what is now eastern Canada and the northeastern United States.

Early life and first voyages

Champlain was born in the port of Brouage (or possibly La Rochelle) into a family of mariners, and learned navigation, chart-making, and seamanship in the French navy and on voyages to Spain and the West Indies. In 1603 he joined François Gravé Du Pont's expedition to Canada, sailing up the St. Lawrence River and publishing an account, Des Sauvages, that year.

Exploration and settlement

Between 1604 and 1607 Champlain explored the Acadian and New England coasts, producing the first accurate maps of the region. In 1608, commissioned by Pierre Dugua, Sieur de Mons, he founded a settlement at Quebec, which became the capital of New France. In 1609 he accompanied his Huron and Algonquin allies against the Iroquois near the lake that now bears his name, Lake Champlain — an encounter that set French–Iroquois relations on a hostile course for a century.

Champlain travelled up the Ottawa River (1613) and in 1615 reached Lake Huron, wintering among the Huron and joining a raid into Iroquois territory in present-day New York. As the effective administrator of New France, he promoted settlement, negotiated alliances with Indigenous nations, and lobbied the French court for support of the colony through his published Voyages.

Later years and legacy

In 1629, during war between England and France, Champlain was forced to surrender Quebec to the Kirke brothers and was taken to England; the colony was restored to France by treaty in 1632, and Champlain returned in 1633. He died in Quebec on Christmas Day 1635, probably of a stroke. His explorations, maps, and writings laid the geographic and political foundations of French North America.
