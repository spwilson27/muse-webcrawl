Camera calibration, in computer vision and photogrammetry, is the process of determining the parameters of the camera model that produced an image or video. Calibration estimates both intrinsic parameters (internal geometry) and extrinsic parameters (the camera's position and orientation relative to a scene).

Parameters

Under the pinhole camera model, intrinsic parameters include the focal length in pixel units, the principal point (usually near the image centre), and often a skew coefficient. Real lenses deviate from the pinhole model, so calibration also estimates lens distortion coefficients — typically radial distortion and tangential distortion terms. Extrinsic parameters are a rotation and translation describing the camera's pose relative to a world coordinate frame.

Methods

The most common approach photographs a calibration target of known geometry — classically a planar chessboard pattern or grid of circles — from multiple viewpoints. Corners are detected and the parameters are fit by minimising reprojection error, usually with nonlinear optimisation (e.g. Levenberg–Marquardt). Zhengyou Zhang's 1999 flexible method, implemented in libraries such as OpenCV, made this procedure routine. In photogrammetry the same process has a longer history under the name self-calibration within bundle adjustment. Calibration-free and target-free methods instead exploit scene structure such as vanishing points, or use the camera's motion (self-calibration from moving cameras).

Applications

Accurate calibration is required for metric 3-D reconstruction, stereo vision, augmented reality, robot navigation, visual odometry and any task that converts pixel measurements into real-world distances or angles.
