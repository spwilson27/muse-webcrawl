Tate's thesis

Tate's thesis is the 1950 Princeton PhD dissertation of the American mathematician John Tate, entitled Fourier Analysis in Number Fields and Hecke's Zeta-Functions. Written under Emil Artin, it recast the analytic theory of Erich Hecke's L-functions in the modern language of harmonic analysis on the adeles and ideles of a number field. Although not formally published until it appeared in the proceedings volume Algebraic Number Theory (1967, eds. Cassels and Fröhlich), the thesis circulated in typescript and transformed algebraic number theory.

Content

Tate studied zeta integrals formed by integrating a multiplicative character of the idele class group against a Schwartz–Bruhat function on the adele ring, weighted by powers of the idele norm. Using Fourier analysis on the locally compact abelian group of adeles and the Poisson summation formula, he showed that these integrals converge for Re(s) > 1, have meromorphic continuation to the whole complex plane, and satisfy a clean functional equation s ↔ 1 − s. This proved at once the analytic continuation and functional equation of all Hecke L-functions, and made transparent that the functional equation is essentially Fourier duality (Pontryagin duality) on the adeles.

Significance

The thesis introduced the adelic viewpoint that became standard in number theory: global questions are treated simultaneously at all places of a field, with local factors handled uniformly by local harmonic analysis. Tate's local zeta integrals and local functional equations are the direct ancestors of the Langlands programme's treatment of automorphic L-functions; Robert Langlands has described learning the theory from the thesis as a formative influence. The notion of the local ε-factor and the systematic use of self-dual Haar measures originate here.
