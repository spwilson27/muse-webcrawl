Floating exchange rate

A floating exchange rate is a type of exchange rate regime in which a currency's value is allowed to fluctuate in response to foreign exchange market mechanisms — that is, supply and demand — rather than being pegged by the government or central bank to another currency or to gold. A completely floating regime, in which authorities never intervene, is called a pure or clean float; regimes in which the central bank occasionally intervenes to influence the rate without defending a specific target are called managed floats or "dirty floats."

History

Floating rates became the norm among major economies after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed rates in 1971–73. Most major currencies — the US dollar, euro, yen, and pound sterling — now float, while many smaller economies retain pegs or intermediate regimes.

Rationale

Under a float, the exchange rate acts as an automatic stabilizer: an external shock that reduces demand for a country's exports leads to depreciation, which makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, cushioning output. Floating also restores monetary policy independence: with free capital movement, a country with a fixed rate cannot set its own interest rates (the "impossible trinity" of international economics), whereas a floating country can.

Arguments for and against

Supporters argue that floating rates provide insulation from foreign inflation and shocks, eliminate the need for large foreign-exchange reserves, and prevent one-way speculative bets against unsustainable pegs. Critics argue that floats can be excessively volatile and prone to overshooting (as formalized by Rüdiger Dornbusch), that depreciation may fuel inflation through import prices, and that businesses face uncertainty that can discourage trade and investment. In economies with heavy foreign-currency debt, a sharp depreciation can cause balance-sheet crises — the "fear of floating" described by Guillermo Calvo and Carmen Reinhart, which leads many supposedly floating central banks to intervene heavily.
