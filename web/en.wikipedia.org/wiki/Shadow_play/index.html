Shadow play

Shadow play, also known as shadow puppetry, is an ancient form of storytelling and entertainment in which flat, articulated figures are held between a light source and a translucent screen, so that their shadows appear to the audience on the other side. It is considered one of the oldest forms of puppetry and a precursor of cinema.

History

Shadow theatre has traditions over a thousand years old in China, where legend attributes its invention to the Han dynasty court of Emperor Wu. It flourished in India, Southeast Asia, Turkey and Greece, and across the Islamic world. In 2011, Chinese shadow puppetry was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Regional traditions

In Indonesia and Malaysia, wayang kulit uses intricately carved leather puppets accompanied by a gamelan orchestra, performing episodes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata. Thailand has nang yai and nang talung; Cambodia has sbek thom. In Turkey and Greece, the comic trickster Karagöz (Karagiozis) anchors the tradition. Chinese pi ying puppets are made of translucent, painted donkey or ox hide. European shadow theatre developed in the 18th century, notably the ombres chinoises of France.

Technique

Puppets are cut from leather or other thin material, painted, and jointed with rods manipulated by the puppeteer, who often also narrates, voices all characters and directs accompanying musicians. Movement of the figure toward or away from the screen changes the size and sharpness of its shadow, an effect exploited for dramatic entrances and transformations.
