Bond duration

In finance, the duration of a financial asset such as a bond is the weighted average of the times until its fixed cash flows are received, the weights being the present values of those cash flows. Duration, usually measured in years, is also a measure of the sensitivity of the bond's price to changes in interest rates: the higher the duration, the more the price changes for a given change in yield.

Macaulay duration

Introduced by Frederick Macaulay in 1938, Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time to receipt of the cash flows:

D = Σ ( t × PV(CFt) ) / Price

For a bond paying annual coupons, it can be computed from yield, coupon rate, time to maturity and payment frequency. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity; for coupon bonds, duration is always less than maturity.

Modified duration

Modified duration adjusts Macaulay duration for the yield level: Dmod = D / (1 + y/m), where y is the yield and m the number of compounding periods per year. It gives the approximate percentage change in price for a one-percentage-point change in yield — for example, a modified duration of 7 means the price rises about 7% if yields fall by 1%. Because the price–yield relation is curved, the estimate improves when combined with convexity.

Uses

Portfolio managers use duration to match assets and liabilities (immunisation), to hedge interest-rate risk, and to express aggregate rate exposure via measures such as DV01, the value change for a one-basis-point move. Effective duration generalises the concept to bonds with embedded options, where cash flows themselves change with rates.
