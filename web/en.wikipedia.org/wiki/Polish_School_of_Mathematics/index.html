Polish School of Mathematics

The Polish School of Mathematics was the flourishing mathematics community that worked in Poland between the two World Wars, centred on Warsaw, Lwów and Kraków, and renowned for foundational work in set theory, topology, functional analysis and logic — fields now grouped under the umbrella of the Polish tradition of descriptive set theory.

Centres and figures

The Warsaw school, led by Wacław Sierpiński, Zygmunt Janiszewski and Stefan Mazurkiewicz, founded the journal Fundamenta Mathematicae in 1920, the first journal devoted to set theory and its applications. The Lwów school gathered around Stefan Banach and Hugo Steinhaus, who frequented the Scottish Café and recorded open problems in the famous Scottish Book; Banach's 1932 Théorie des opérations linéaires founded modern functional analysis. Kraków contributed mainly through logic and the work around Stanisław Zaremba. Alfred Tarski and the Warsaw logicians made Poland equally central to mathematical logic.

Destruction and legacy

The Second World War devastated the school: many of its members were murdered in the Holocaust or by the occupying powers, and others emigrated, carrying Polish mathematics to universities in the United States and elsewhere. Despite this, the school's journals, notation (such as "Polish notation") and concepts like Polish spaces remain embedded in modern mathematics.
