Saltpetre (also spelled saltpeter) is the common name for potassium nitrate (KNO3), a naturally occurring mineral and chemical compound that was for centuries one of the most strategically important substances in the world as the oxidising ingredient of gunpowder (black powder), in which it is mixed with charcoal and sulfur.

Sources and production

Saltpetre forms as crystalline efflorescences on soil and cave walls where nitrogen-rich organic matter decomposes, historically in India, China and parts of Europe. Because natural deposits were insufficient for wartime demand, it was produced artificially in "nitre beds" — heaps of manure, earth, ash and organic waste that were watered and left for nitrifying bacteria to convert ammonia into nitrates, then leached and crystallised. The related compound sodium nitrate from Chilean caliche deposits is known as "Chile saltpetre." In the modern era potassium nitrate is produced industrially by reacting potassium chloride with nitric acid or sodium nitrate.

Uses

Beyond gunpowder and other explosives and pyrotechnics, saltpetre has been used as a fertiliser (a source of both nitrogen and potassium), in food curing (it contributes to the colour and preservation of cured meats, food additive E252), in toothpaste for sensitive teeth, in tree-stump removers, and historically in medicine as a diuretic. A persistent folk claim that saltpetre was secretly added to soldiers' or prisoners' food to suppress libido has no scientific basis.

History

Chinese alchemists documented nitrate purification by the medieval period, and saltpetre was essential to the gunpowder weapons developed in China and transmitted westward. European states created saltpetre commissions and granted officials rights to dig in barns and stables, making it a heavily controlled commodity. During the American Civil War and the Napoleonic Wars, securing supplies of nitre was a major logistical concern; the search for deposits famously extended to caves such as Mammoth Cave in Kentucky.
