Hazaras

The Hazaras (Persian: هزاره‌ها, romanized: Hazāragān) are a Persian-speaking ethnic group native to the Hazarajat region of central Afghanistan, and one of the country's principal ethnic groups. Large Hazara communities also live in Pakistan — especially in Quetta — and in Iran, as well as a worldwide diaspora. Most Hazaras are Twelver Shia Muslims, distinguishing them from the largely Sunni majority in Afghanistan.

Origins

Hazara origins are traditionally traced to the Mongol armies of the thirteenth century and later Turkic and Mongol settlers, whose admixture with the local Iranian population produced the group's characteristic blend of features and culture. Their language, Hazaragi, is a dialect of Persian (Dari) with Turkic and Mongolian loanwords. The name is usually derived from the Persian hazār, "thousand", reflecting the Mongol military unit of a thousand soldiers.

History

The Hazaras maintained autonomy in their mountain homeland for centuries but were conquered in the 1890s by the Afghan emir Abdur Rahman Khan in campaigns accompanied by massacres, enslavement and land confiscation that killed or displaced a large share of the population. Through the twentieth century Hazaras were marginalised within Afghanistan, and many migrated to Iran and British India as labourers. During the Afghan civil wars of the 1990s they were again targeted, notably in the Taliban's 1998 massacre at Mazar-i-Sharif, and attacks on Hazara civilians and Shia mosques by militant groups have continued since, both in Afghanistan and in Quetta, Pakistan.

Culture

Hazara culture is known for its embroidery, music centred on the dambura lute, and traditions of migration and education; since 2001 Hazara communities in Afghanistan have placed strong emphasis on schooling, including for girls, and have produced prominent political, academic and sporting figures.
