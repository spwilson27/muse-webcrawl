Gelatin silver print

A gelatin silver print
TypePhotographic process
Introduced1870s–1880s; dominant c. 1900–2000
MediumSilver halide in gelatin on paper or film base

The gelatin silver process is the photographic process used with currently available black-and-white films and printing papers. A suspension of silver salts in gelatin is coated on a support such as glass, flexible plastic or film, baryta paper, or resin-coated paper. These light-sensitive materials are stable under normal keeping conditions and can be exposed and processed weeks or years after manufacture. The gelatin silver process was the dominant photographic process from the 1880s until the widespread adoption of digital photography in the 2000s.

History

The process grew out of Richard Leach Maddox's gelatin dry plate of 1871, which replaced the wet collodion process and allowed factory-made plates. Gelatin silver printing papers followed in the 1870s and 1880s, and gelatin roll film, popularized by George Eastman and Kodak from 1888, made photography accessible to amateurs. Variants of the same emulsion technology underpinned virtually all 20th-century photography, from cinema film to X-ray film.

Process

Exposure to light reduces some silver halide crystals to form an invisible latent image. In development, a chemical developer amplifies the latent image into visible metallic silver, forming the black parts of the image; a stop bath halts development, and a fixer (typically sodium thiosulfate, "hypo") dissolves the unexposed silver salts, after which the print is washed and dried. Papers are printed from negatives by projection (enlarging) or contact printing.

Archival properties

Properly processed, fixed, and washed gelatin silver prints on fibre-based baryta paper are among the most stable photographic media, with lifetimes of a century or more. Imperfect processing leaves residual chemicals that cause yellowing and fading, and the silver image can tarnish ("silver mirroring") in polluted or humid environments. Toning with gold or selenium improves permanence.
