Public Prosecution Service (Netherlands)

The Public Prosecution Service (Dutch: Openbaar Ministerie, OM) is the body responsible for criminal prosecutions in the Netherlands. It forms part of the Dutch judicial system but is not a court; it decides which criminal cases are brought before the courts, supervises the execution of court-imposed penalties, and, together with the police, directs criminal investigations.

Organisation

The OM is headed by the Board of Procurators General (College van procureurs-generaal), based in The Hague. Below it sit ten regional public prosecution offices aligned with the district courts, the National Public Prosecutor's Office for serious organised and economic crime and terrorism (Landelijk Parket), a functional office for environmental and financial offences, and the Central Processing Office for minor offences. Public prosecutors hold the rank of officier van justitie, with senior advocates-general representing the OM before the courts of appeal.

Discretion and the opportunity principle

Unlike systems with mandatory prosecution, Dutch law is governed by the opportuniteitsbeginsel (opportunity or expediency principle): a prosecutor may decline to prosecute, or settle a case out of court through a transactie or, since 2008, a prosecutorial penalty order (strafbeschikking), where the public interest does not require a trial. Victims dissatisfied with a decision not to prosecute may complain to the court of appeal under Article 12 of the Code of Criminal Procedure.

Political accountability

The Service falls under the political responsibility of the Minister of Justice and Security, who may issue instructions in individual cases, though such interventions are rare and must be documented. This sits within the Dutch inquisitorial tradition, in which the prosecutor is expected to act as a magistrate seeking the truth rather than as a purely adversarial party.
