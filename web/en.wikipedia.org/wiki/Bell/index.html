Bell

This article is about the percussion instrument. For other uses, see Bell (disambiguation).

A bell is a directly struck idiophone percussion instrument, usually a hollow metal vessel — most often of bronze — in the form of a cup or cone with a flared mouth, sounded by a swinging clapper inside or by an external hammer. Bells are among the oldest musical and signalling instruments, found in cultures worldwide; the largest bells exceed 200 tonnes, while handbells can weigh a few grams.

History

The oldest known bells are small bronze bells from Neolithic China, dating to roughly 2000 BC; the Chinese developed large ritual bells (such as the bianzhong chime bells of the Zhou dynasty) and, by the Han period, sophisticated bell casting. In Europe, bells spread with Celtic and Roman usage and became central to Christian worship from the early Middle Ages, summoning congregations and marking the hours. Medieval founders cast bells in pits beside the churches they were to serve, and the art developed regional profiles — the English bell profile, Eastern Orthodox shapes, and the great bourdon bells of cathedrals.

Acoustics

A bell's sound is characterised by a set of inharmonic partials — the hum tone (an octave below the strike note), prime, tierce (minor third), quint and nominal — which skilled founders tune by removing metal on a lathe. The perceived strike tone is a virtual pitch produced by the upper partials. Bell bronze, typically about 77% copper and 23% tin, gives a long-lasting resonance.

Uses

Bells serve as church bells (including change ringing in England and the carillon of at least 23 bells played from a keyboard), clock bells, school and fire bells, ship's bells, handbells, sleigh bells, and ritual bells in Hindu and Buddhist practice. Famous bells include the Liberty Bell in Philadelphia, Big Ben (the Great Bell of Westminster), the Tsar Bell in Moscow — at over 200 tonnes the largest bell in existence, though cracked and never rung — and the Great Bell of Dhammazedi, lost in a Burmese river in the seventeenth century.

Founding

Traditional casting uses loam moulds built up over a core with a false bell of clay defining the profile; modern founders use sand moulds and induction furnaces. Major foundries historically included Whitechapel Bell Foundry in London (closed 2017) and Paccard in France.
