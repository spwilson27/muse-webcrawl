William III (4 November 1650 – 8 March 1702), also widely known as William of Orange, was the sovereign Prince of Orange from birth, Stadtholder of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Guelders and Overijssel in the Dutch Republic from 1672, and King of England, Scotland and Ireland from 1689 until his death. He reigned jointly with his wife, Mary II, until her death in 1694.

William's invasion of England in November 1688, the Glorious Revolution, deposed his Catholic father-in-law and uncle James II and established parliamentary supremacy, sealed by the Bill of Rights 1689. In Europe he led the Grand Alliance against Louis XIV of France in the Nine Years' War. His victory over James at the Battle of the Boyne in Ireland (1690) is still commemorated by the Orange Order in Northern Ireland.

Early life and stadtholderate

William was born eight days after the death of his father, William II. His mother was Mary, eldest daughter of Charles I of England. The Dutch Republic at first dispensed with a stadtholder in Holland, but in the crisis of 1672 — the Rampjaar, when France invaded — William was appointed stadtholder and captain-general and organised the defence that flooded the land and halted the French advance.

Glorious Revolution

Invited by English opponents of James II, William landed at Torbay on 5 November 1688 with a Dutch army. James fled to France, and a Convention Parliament offered the crown jointly to William and Mary, who accepted the Declaration of Rights. In Scotland William's supporters defeated Jacobite forces at Killiecrankie and Dunkeld, though the subsequent Glencoe massacre of 1692 stained his reign.

Reign

The reign saw the financial revolution: the founding of the Bank of England (1694), the national debt, and the Triennial Act. The Act of Settlement 1701 excluded Catholics from the succession and settled the crown on the House of Hanover after Queen Anne.

War with France

William devoted his reign to containing Louis XIV. The Nine Years' War (1688–97) ended with the Treaty of Ryswick, in which Louis recognised William as king. On the eve of the War of the Spanish Succession William rebuilt the Grand Alliance but died before the conflict began.

Death and succession

In February 1702 William's horse stumbled on a molehill at Hampton Court; he broke his collarbone and died of pneumonia two weeks later. Jacobites toasted "the little gentleman in velvet" — the mole. He was succeeded by his sister-in-law, Anne.
