Arrowhead

An arrowhead or point is the sharpened tip of an arrow, or a symbolic depiction of it, designed to penetrate a target. Historically arrowheads were made of stone (flint, obsidian, chert), bone, antler, or metal, and attached to the arrow shaft by hafting, glue, or sinew. Archaeologists classify stone points by shape and use them as chronological and cultural markers — for example Clovis points in North America — while the study of their manufacture, lithic reduction by knapping, is a major part of experimental archaeology.

History

Stone projectile points date back tens of thousands of years; some of the earliest evidence for bow-and-arrow hunting comes from southern Africa (c. 60,000+ years ago) and from the Sri Lankan rainforest. In the Bronze and Iron Ages, cast and forged metal heads diversified into specialised forms: leaf-shaped and triangular heads for war and hunting, broadheads for large game, bodkin points for piercing mail, and blunt or forked heads for birds and small animals. Medieval English longbow arrows used a variety of head types recorded in archaeological assemblages such as those from the Tower of London.

Design

Key features of a point include the blade, edges, base (stemmed, notched, or tanged for hafting), and barb. Performance depends on sharpness, weight, and the cutting diameter versus penetration depth; modern hunting broadheads use steel blades, mechanical expanding designs, or traditional fixed blades.

Collecting

Arrowhead collecting is a popular hobby, but removal of artifacts from archaeological sites is restricted or illegal in many jurisdictions (in the United States, on federal land, under the Archaeological Resources Protection Act).
