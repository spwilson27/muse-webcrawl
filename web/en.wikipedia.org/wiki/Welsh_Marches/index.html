Welsh Marches


The Marches are a hilly border region dotted with medieval castles.

The Welsh Marches (Welsh: Y Mers) is an imprecisely defined area of land along the border between England and Wales. In the modern sense it usually refers to the English counties of Cheshire, Shropshire, Herefordshire, Worcestershire, and Gloucestershire where they adjoin Wales, and sometimes to the adjacent Welsh border counties as well. The landscape is largely rural and hilly, including parts of the Shropshire Hills and the Wye Valley.

History

The term derives from the medieval March of Wales (Latin: Marchia Walliae), a frontier territory established after the Norman Conquest. Marcher lords — Anglo-Norman barons granted lands along the frontier — held unusually wide powers, effectively ruling their lordships independently of the English crown while they conquered and administered territory in Wales. Dozens of castles, including those at Ludlow, Chepstow, and Pembroke, were built across the region. The Marcher lordships were abolished by the Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 under Henry VIII, which incorporated Wales into the English legal system.

Today

"Welsh Marches" survives as a geographical and cultural label used in tourism, food branding, and transport — notably the Welsh Marches line, the railway running from Newport through Hereford and Shrewsbury to Crewe.
