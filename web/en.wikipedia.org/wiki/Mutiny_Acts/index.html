Mutiny Acts

The Mutiny Acts were a series of annual Acts of the Parliament of England, later of Great Britain and the United Kingdom, that provided the legal basis for maintaining a standing army and for the discipline of the army in peacetime. Beginning with the first Mutiny Act of 1689, Parliament renewed the legislation annually for more than two centuries — a constitutional device that guaranteed regular parliamentary sessions and legislative control over the military.

Background

After the Glorious Revolution, the Bill of Rights 1689 declared that "the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless it be with consent of Parliament, is against law". The experience of James II's standing army, and the mutiny of troops at Ipswich and elsewhere in 1689, prompted Parliament to pass the first Mutiny Act, which — initially for a few months only, then annually — authorised courts martial to punish mutiny, sedition and desertion, offences for which common law provided no adequate penalty in peacetime.

Annual renewal and constitutional significance

Because the Act expired each year, the army's disciplinary code lapsed unless Parliament met and renewed it. This requirement is one reason Parliament has met every year since 1689. The Army Act 1881 consolidated the Mutiny Act provisions into a permanent code, but it too was kept in force by an annual Army (Annual) Act, preserving the principle of annual parliamentary consent. The annual act survived, as the Armed Forces Act cycle, into the modern era: since the Armed Forces Act 2006, continuation is required at least every five years, with annual continuation orders in between.

Scope and development

Successive Mutiny Acts regulated billeting, pay, enlistment, and the offences triable by court martial, and were extended to forces in Scotland, Ireland, the colonies and India. The naval equivalent was provided by the Articles of War and the Naval Discipline Acts. In the United States, the constitutional requirement that army appropriations span no more than two years echoes the same suspicion of standing armies that animated the Mutiny Acts.
