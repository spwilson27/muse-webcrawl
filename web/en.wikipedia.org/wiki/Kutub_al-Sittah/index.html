Kutub al-Sittah

Kutub al-Sittah (Arabic: ٱلْكُتُب ٱلسِّتَّة, romanized: al-Kutub as-Sitta, lit. "the Six Books") are six books containing collections of hadith (sayings or acts of the Islamic prophet Muhammad) compiled by six Sunni Muslim scholars in the ninth century CE, roughly 200 years after Muhammad's death. They are sometimes referred to as Al-Sihah al-Sitta, which translates as "The Authentic Six".

The books were first formally grouped together and defined as a canon in the 11th century by the scholar Ibn al-Qaisarani, who added Sunan ibn Majah to the list. Since then, they have enjoyed near-universal acceptance as part of the official canon of Sunni Islam.

Significance

Sunni Muslims view the six major hadith collections as their most important sources of reference for the Sunnah (the way of the Prophet) after the Quran. Of the six, Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim are considered the most reliable (sahih) of all the hadith collections. Not all Sunni Muslim jurisprudence schools regard the six books as equally authoritative; in particular, the position of Sunan ibn Majah as the sixth book has occasionally been disputed, with some scholars proposing the Muwatta of Imam Malik or the Sunan of al-Darimi in its place.
