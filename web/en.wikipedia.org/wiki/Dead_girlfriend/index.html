Dead girlfriend

The "dead girlfriend" trope takes its name from a storyline in The Amazing Spider-Man #121 (1973)

The dead girlfriend is a narrative trope in which a male protagonist's female love interest is killed (or otherwise harmed) in order to motivate the hero, advance his character development, or provide emotional stakes for his story, rather than for the woman's own narrative arc. The trope is closely associated with superhero comics but occurs across popular fiction. It overlaps with the fan-culture concept of "fridging", a term coined by comics writer Gail Simone in 1999, after the Green Lantern storyline in which Kyle Rayner's girlfriend Alexandra DeWitt was murdered and stuffed into a refrigerator.

Origin of the term

Simone created the website "Women in Refrigerators" listing female comic book characters who had been killed, maimed, depowered or assaulted, frequently as plot devices in the stories of male heroes. The phrase entered wider critical vocabulary as shorthand for writing in which female characters are treated as disposable sources of motivation for male protagonists. The specific phrase "dead girlfriend" is often traced to the death of Gwen Stacy, Spider-Man's girlfriend, in "The Night Gwen Stacy Died" (The Amazing Spider-Man #121, 1973), one of the most famous deaths in comics history, whose narrative function was largely to deepen Peter Parker's guilt and resolve.

Examples and analysis

Other frequently cited instances include the deaths or brutalisation of Barbara Gordon (shot and paralysed in Batman: The Killing Joke, 1988, principally to torment Commissioner Gordon and Batman), Karen Page in Daredevil comics and adaptations, and, in film, Rachel Dawes in The Dark Knight (2008), whose death drives both Batman's and Harvey Dent's arcs. Critics argue the trope reflects and reinforces an imbalance in whose interiority popular stories treat as important; defenders of individual stories sometimes respond that death and grief are legitimate dramatic material and that the trope label should not preclude well-written treatments. The debate has influenced contemporary writers to subvert the device, for example by centring the woman's perspective, reviving the character, or examining the hero's response more critically.

Related concepts

  • Fridging – the broader fan term popularised by Simone.
  • Disposable woman – a female character introduced primarily to be killed or discarded.
  • Women in Refrigerators – the 1999 website cataloguing the pattern in comics.
  • Damsel in distress – a related, older trope of female endangerment motivating a male rescuer.
