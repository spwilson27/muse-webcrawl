Bengali literature

Bengali literature spans more than a millennium, from early Buddhist mystical songs to the modern novel.

Bengali literature (Bengali: বাংলা সাহিত্য, Bangla Sahitya) is the body of written works in the Bengali language, spoken in the Bengal region of South Asia, today divided between Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal. It is one of the richest literary traditions of South Asia, encompassing poetry, drama, fiction and religious writing.

Early and medieval literature

The earliest surviving Bengali texts are the Charyapada, a collection of Buddhist mystical songs composed between roughly the 8th and 12th centuries and rediscovered in Nepal in 1907 by Haraprasad Shastri. Medieval Bengali literature flourished under both Hindu and Muslim patronage. Vaishnava poets inspired by Chaitanya Mahaprabhu produced the padavali lyric tradition, while the Mangal-Kavya narrative poems celebrated deities such as Manasa and Chandi. Muslim poets of the Arakan court, including Daulat Qazi and Alaol, composed romances in Bengali in the 17th century.

The Bengal Renaissance and the novel

The 19th century saw a flowering often called the Bengal Renaissance. Michael Madhusudan Dutt introduced blank verse and the sonnet into Bengali poetry and wrote the epic Meghnad Badh Kavya (1861). Bankim Chandra Chatterjee effectively created the Bengali novel with works such as Durgeshnandini (1865) and Anandamath (1882), the source of the hymn "Vande Mataram". Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's prose reforms shaped the modern written language.

Tagore and the twentieth century

Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913 for Gitanjali, transformed every genre he touched. His contemporaries and successors include Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay, whose novels portrayed rural Bengali society; Kazi Nazrul Islam, the fiery "rebel poet"; and the novelists Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay, author of Pather Panchali, Tarashankar Bandyopadhyay and Manik Bandyopadhyay.

Modern literature

After the Partition of Bengal in 1947, literature developed in parallel in West Bengal and in East Pakistan, later Bangladesh. Post-independence writers include Samaresh Majumdar, Sunil Gangopadhyay, Atin Bandyopadhyay and Mahasweta Devi in India, and Syed Waliullah, Akhteruzzaman Elias and Hasan Azizul Huq in Bangladesh. Bengali poetry remained vigorous through figures such as Jibanananda Das, whose Ruposhi Bangla is considered a high point of modern lyric verse.
