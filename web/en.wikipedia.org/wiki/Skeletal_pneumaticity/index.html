Skeletal pneumaticity

Skeletal pneumaticity is the presence of air spaces within bones. It is produced during development by the invasion of bones by diverticula of air sacs — in birds, diverticula of the lungs and air-sac system — producing pneumatic bones that are hollow and internally braced by struts rather than filled with marrow.

In birds

Most living birds have extensively pneumatized skeletons, which reduces body mass and is linked to their flow-through respiratory system. Pneumatic bones include the humerus, coracoid, sternum, and much of the vertebral column. The extent of pneumaticity is reduced in diving birds such as penguins and loons, whose denser bones aid buoyancy control.

In non-avian dinosaurs

Postcranial skeletal pneumaticity is a hallmark of saurischian dinosaurs. Sauropods had highly pneumatic vertebrae — in some species more than 60% air by volume — lightening their enormous necks. Theropods, including the ancestors of birds, also pneumatized their vertebrae and other bones, evidence that air sacs evolved long before flight. Pterosaurs independently developed extreme pneumaticity.

Functions

Proposed functions include mass reduction, structural stiffening of hollow tubes, and evidence for an air-sac respiratory system capable of supporting high metabolic rates. In fossils, pneumaticity is inferred from foramina and internal chambers (camerae and camellae) visible in broken or CT-scanned bone.
