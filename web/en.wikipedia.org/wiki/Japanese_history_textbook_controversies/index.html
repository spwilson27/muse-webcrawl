Japanese history textbook controversies

Textbook authorization has repeatedly become a diplomatic flashpoint
Key years1982, 2001, 2005
PartiesJapan; China; South Korea
Key figureSaburō Ienaga

Japanese history textbook controversies involve the content of school history textbooks approved by the Japanese Ministry of Education and their coverage of Imperial Japan's colonial rule and wartime conduct, especially the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War. Controversies have recurrently strained Japan's relations with China and South Korea, whose governments and publics accuse some Japanese textbooks of minimizing or whitewashing atrocities such as the Nanjing Massacre and the comfort women system.

The Ienaga lawsuits

Historian Saburō Ienaga waged a thirty-year legal battle (1965–1997) against the Ministry of Education's textbook screening system, suing over demands that he soften descriptions of Japanese wartime atrocities, Unit 731, and the Battle of Okinawa. Japan's Supreme Court ultimately ruled the screening system constitutional but found some of the ministry's specific objections illegal, in a landmark partial victory for textbook authors.

The 1982 controversy

In 1982, Japanese newspaper reports (later partly corrected) claimed the ministry had required changing "invasion" (shinryaku) of China to "advance" (shinshutsu). Protests from Beijing and Seoul prompted the Miyazawa statement, in which the government pledged to take neighbours' sensitivities into account — the origin of the "neighbouring country clause" in screening standards.

The 2001 New History Textbook

In 2001 the ministry approved the New History Textbook produced by the nationalist Japanese Society for History Textbook Reform (Tsukurukai). China and South Korea condemned it for omitting or downplaying the Nanjing Massacre, comfort women, and forced labour. In the event, adoption by school districts was minimal — well under one percent of schools — but the approval itself caused a serious diplomatic rupture; large anti-Japanese protests erupted in China in 2005 when another edition was approved.

Competing perspectives

Defenders of screening argue textbooks must be neutral and age-appropriate, and critics in Japan note that commercial Japanese textbooks in wide use include accounts of the atrocities that China and Korea demand. The dispute is intertwined with wider disagreements over apology, commemoration at Yasukuni Shrine, and territorial claims.
