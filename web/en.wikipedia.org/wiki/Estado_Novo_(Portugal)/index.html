Estado Novo (Portugal)

The Estado Novo ("New State") was the corporatist authoritarian regime installed in Portugal in 1933, lasting until it was overthrown by the Carnation Revolution on 25 April 1974. Dominated for most of its existence by António de Oliveira Salazar, prime minister from 1932 to 1968, it was one of Europe's longest-lived authoritarian regimes, characterised by nationalism, censorship, political police (PIDE), and defence of Portugal's colonial empire.

Origins

After the 1926 coup ended the unstable First Republic, Salazar, an economics professor, imposed fiscal orthodoxy as finance minister and then premier. The 1933 constitution formalised the Estado Novo: a single-party system under the National Union, corporative chambers instead of class politics, and the motto "Deus, Pátria, Família" (God, Homeland, Family).

The regime

Portugal stayed neutral in World War II, joined NATO in 1949, and experienced postwar economic growth and emigration. Repression by the PIDE, censorship, and rigged elections sustained the regime; opposition figures such as Humberto Delgado, who dared contest the 1958 presidential election, were persecuted. From 1961 the Portuguese Colonial War in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea consumed the budget and conscripted a generation.

Fall

Salazar was incapacitated in 1968 and died in 1970; Marcelo Caetano's cautious liberalisation failed. On 25 April 1974 the Armed Forces Movement overthrew the regime almost bloodlessly — the Carnation Revolution — leading to democracy and decolonisation.
