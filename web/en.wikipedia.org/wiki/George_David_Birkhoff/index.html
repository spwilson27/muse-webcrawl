George David Birkhoff (March 21, 1884 – November 12, 1944) was an American mathematician best known for what is now called the Birkhoff ergodic theorem, a fundamental result of ergodic theory with applications in statistical mechanics and dynamical systems. He was among the most influential American mathematicians of his generation and a long-time leader of the mathematics department at Harvard University.

Career

Birkhoff was educated at the Lewis Institute in Chicago and earned his PhD from the University of Chicago in 1907 under E. H. Moore, working on differential equations. After positions at the University of Wisconsin and Princeton, he joined Harvard in 1912, remaining there for the rest of his career and serving as dean of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences. In 1913 he proved Poincaré's last geometric theorem, a result about area-preserving maps of an annulus that the dying Poincaré had stated without proof, which brought Birkhoff instant international fame.

Work

  • Ergodic theorem (1931): for a measure-preserving transformation, time averages converge almost everywhere to space averages. John von Neumann proved a related mean ergodic theorem shortly after; the two results laid the foundations of ergodic theory.
  • Birkhoff's theorem in general relativity: any spherically symmetric solution of the vacuum Einstein field equations must be static and asymptotically flat, hence part of the Schwarzschild geometry.
  • Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem: the set of doubly stochastic matrices is the convex hull of the permutation matrices; equivalently, every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices.
  • Dynamical systems: his 1927 book of that name helped create the modern study of chaotic dynamics; he also proposed an "aesthetic measure" theory in Aesthetic Measure (1933).
  • Birkhoff's representation theorem for distributive lattices and the Birkhoff–Kellogg theorem in functional analysis.

Legacy

Birkhoff trained many students who became leaders of American mathematics, though his influence on hiring at Harvard has been criticised for favouring his own protégés and for documented biases against Jewish mathematicians. The Birkhoff Prize in applied mathematics, awarded jointly by the AMS and SIAM, is named after him, as is the Birkhoff interpolation scheme and the Birkhoff polytope.
