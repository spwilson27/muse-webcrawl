Mirza Taghi Khan Farahani (Persian: میرزا تقی‌خان فراهانی; 9 January 1807 – 10 January 1852), better known as Amir Kabir (امیرکبیر, "the great prince"), was the chief minister (prime minister) of Persia under Naser al-Din Shah Qajar from 1848 until 1851. He is widely regarded as the founder of modern Iran and one of the most capable statesmen in Iranian history, remembered for wide-ranging administrative, military, educational and anti-corruption reforms.

Early life

Amir Kabir was born in Hazaveh, a village near Arak, into a family of modest means: his father, Karbala'i Mohammad Ghorban, served as a cook in the household of the powerful Farahani family, whose head, Qa'em Maqam, was a leading minister. The young Taghi was educated within that household, where his intelligence attracted notice, and he rose through the scribal and financial administration of Azerbaijan under the crown prince.

Diplomatic career

Before becoming premier he undertook important missions abroad. He served on the Persian delegation to Russia after the Russo-Persian War and the murder of the Russian envoy Alexander Griboyedov, spent several years in the Ottoman Empire negotiating the settlement that led to the Treaty of Erzurum (1847) over the frontier with Iraq, and observed Ottoman Tanzimat reforms at first hand — experiences that shaped his reform programme.

Premiership and reforms

When Naser al-Din Shah ascended the throne in 1848, he appointed Amir Kabir as prime minister with the title Atabak-e Azam. In just over three years he:

  • Reorganised state finances, cut court stipends and pensions, and balanced the budget.
  • Founded the Dar ul-Funun in Tehran (opened 1851), Persia's first modern institution of higher learning, staffed largely by Austrian and other European instructors teaching medicine, engineering, mining and military science.
  • Reformed the army, establishing factories for uniforms, cannon and rifles, and a new conscription and pay system.
  • Founded one of Persia's first newspapers, Vaqaye'-e Ettefaqiyeh, and promoted industry, mining surveys and trade.
  • Curbed the influence of foreign legations and of court factions, and campaigned against bribery, torture and arbitrary confiscation.

Downfall and death

His reforms made powerful enemies, including the queen mother Mahd-e Olia and courtiers whose incomes he had slashed. In November 1851 the Shah dismissed him, and in January 1852 he was exiled to Kashan, where he was murdered on the Shah's orders — his veins opened while he was in the bath at the Fin Garden — on 10 January 1852.

Legacy

Amir Kabir is remembered in Iran as a national hero and martyred reformer. Amir Kabir University of Technology in Tehran and the Amir Kabir Dam bear his name, and his mausoleum lies in Karbala, Iraq. Historians often compare his truncated reform programme with the later modernisation of Iran.
