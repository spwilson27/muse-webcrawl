Public broadcasting

Public broadcasting (or public service broadcasting) involves radio, television and other electronic media outlets whose primary mission is public service. Such broadcasters are typically funded by licence fees, government grants, donations or a combination of these, rather than primarily by advertising, and are expected to serve the whole public with educational, cultural and informational programming independent of commercial and political pressure.

Public service broadcasters aim to inform, educate and entertain.

Models

The British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC), founded in 1922 and chartered in 1927, is the archetype: funded by a household licence fee and directed by its first director-general John Reith to "inform, educate and entertain". Other models include the United States' decentralised system of PBS television and NPR radio stations, funded by a mix of member donations, corporate underwriting and modest federal support through the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (established 1967), and Japan's NHK, funded by receiving fees.

Values and criticism

Supporters argue public broadcasters provide trusted news, children's and minority programming, and cultural content that markets under-supply, and act as a shared national forum in emergencies. Critics question compulsory fees in an era of abundant media, allege political bias, and argue that state funding can compromise independence. Events such as the Eurovision Song Contest are produced through cooperation between public broadcasters in the EBU.

History

Public broadcasting emerged in the 1920s as states organised the new medium of radio. After the Second World War it became the dominant model in most of Europe and the Commonwealth, before facing competition from commercial television, cable, satellite and streaming services from the late twentieth century onward.
