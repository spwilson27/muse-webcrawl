Africa was a province of the Roman Republic and later the Roman Empire, established after Rome's victory in the Third Punic War (149–146 BC) destroyed Carthage. Originally comprising the territory of Carthage in what is now northern Tunisia, it was later expanded into Africa Proconsularis, which included much of coastal North Africa and became one of the empire's richest provinces.

Establishment

After Carthage's destruction in 146 BC, the surrounding territory was organized as the province of Africa Vetus ("Old Africa"). The city of Utica served as the first capital. Under Augustus the province was enlarged with the former Numidian kingdom and reorganized as Africa Proconsularis, governed by a proconsul of consular rank, with Carthage — refounded as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar — as its capital.

Economy

Africa was famously the granary of Rome, supplying the bulk of the capital's grain by the imperial period; by some accounts it provided two-thirds of Rome's annual grain imports. It was also a major producer of olive oil — amphorae from the province are found across the Mediterranean — and a source of wild animals for the arena games. The fertility of its estates made the province's senatorial landowners among the wealthiest men in the empire.

Society and culture

The province was thoroughly Romanized in the cities while Punic and Berber culture persisted in the countryside. It produced emperors Septimius Severus and Macrinus, the jurist Ulpian, the writers Apuleius and Tertullian, and the Christian bishop Augustine of Hippo. Christianity spread early and vigorously here, producing the martyrs Perpetua and Felicity and the Donatist schism.

Later history

Diocletian's reforms subdivided the province into several smaller ones within the Diocese of Africa. In 439 AD the Vandals under Gaiseric captured Carthage, ending Roman rule; the Byzantine general Belisarius reconquered the region in 533–534, establishing the Exarchate of Africa, which endured until the Arab conquests of the 7th century.
