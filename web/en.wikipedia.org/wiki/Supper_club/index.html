Supper club

A supper club is a type of restaurant and social venue that became prominent in the United States during the 1930s and 1940s and remains a distinctive tradition in the Upper Midwest, especially Wisconsin. Traditionally, a supper club is a destination establishment — often in a rural or lakeside setting — where patrons spend the entire evening: cocktails at the bar, a leisurely multi-course dinner, and sometimes live entertainment, all in an unhurried, club-like atmosphere, despite membership not actually being required.

History

The first supper club is generally credited to Beverly Hills restaurateur Lawrence Frank in the 1920s. The format flourished during and after Prohibition, when roadhouses and former speakeasies reinvented themselves as respectable dining destinations. In the post-war decades, supper clubs became centres of small-town and resort social life, serving steaks, seafood, relish trays, and classic cocktails to generations of regulars.

Characteristics

Hallmarks of the traditional supper club include an old fashioned cocktail (in Wisconsin, made with brandy), a relish tray and bread basket served before the meal, prime rib and other hearty fare, fish fry on Fridays, salad bars, ice-cream drinks such as the grasshopper and pink squirrel for dessert, dim lighting, knotty-pine or dark wood decor, and family ownership spanning decades. Diners customarily order drinks at the bar and are seated when their table is ready, and the meal proceeds at a deliberately relaxed pace.

Modern usage

Since the 2000s the term "supper club" has also been adopted by underground dining movements — chef-hosted, often one-night-only meals in homes or pop-up spaces, ticketed in advance and publicised by word of mouth and social media — in the United Kingdom, United States, and elsewhere. These events borrow the name's sense of intimacy and occasion while differing greatly from the traditional American format.
