A broad-gauge railway is a railway with a track gauge (the distance between the rails) broader than the standard gauge of 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in). Broad gauges were adopted in several countries in the 19th century for reasons of stability, capacity, or national strategy, and several remain in intensive use today — notably the 1,676 mm Indian gauge, the world's most heavily used broad gauge, the 1,520 mm gauge of Russia and the former Soviet Union, and the 1,668 mm Iberian gauge of Spain and Portugal.

The Brunel gauge and the gauge war

Isambard Kingdom Brunel built the Great Western Railway in Britain to a gauge of 7 ft ¼ in (2,140 mm), arguing it allowed larger wheels, smoother running, and greater speed. The resulting "gauge war" with the standard-gauge companies ended with the Gauge Act of 1846, which mandated standard gauge for most new lines; the Great Western's broad gauge survived until 1892, when the last broad-gauge track was converted, ending decades of mixed-gauge track and break-of-gauge inconvenience.

Gauges in use

1,676 mm (5 ft 6 in)

Used in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Argentina, and Chile. In India it is the principal mainline gauge under Project Unigauge, carrying the majority of the country's rail traffic.

1,668 mm / 1,664 mm (Iberian gauge)

Spain and Portugal standardized on a gauge near 5 ft 5½ in. Spanish high-speed lines are built to standard gauge, with variable-gauge trains connecting the two networks.

1,600 mm (5 ft 3 in)

The gauge of Ireland and Northern Ireland, and of parts of Australia (Victoria and South Australia) and Brazil.

1,520 mm (Russian gauge)

Adopted in the Russian Empire and retained across the former Soviet Union, Finland (1,524 mm), and Mongolia — the second most extensive network by gauge in the world.

Technical considerations

Broad gauge permits a wider loading gauge, steadier ride at speed, and heavier axle loads, but costs more in land, structures, and materials, and creates costly breaks of gauge at borders with other gauges. Modern engineering holds that capacity and speed depend more on loading gauge and infrastructure quality than on track gauge itself, which is why new high-speed railways are overwhelmingly built to standard gauge even in broad-gauge countries.
