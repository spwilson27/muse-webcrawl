Exon

An exon is any part of a gene that is retained in the final mature RNA product after splicing removes the intervening introns. The term, coined by Walter Gilbert in 1978 from "expressed region," was proposed alongside his hypothesis that shuffling exons between genes accelerates evolution by recombining protein modules.

Structure and function

Exons may be protein-coding, or may form part of the untranslated regions (UTRs) of messenger RNA; some genes encode non-coding RNAs whose exons never produce protein. In humans, genes average roughly eight to ten exons, typically about 150 nucleotides long, separated by much longer introns. The exome — the union of all exons in the genome — makes up only about 1–2% of the human genome but contains the great majority of known disease-causing variants, which is why exome sequencing is a mainstay of medical genetics.

Alternative splicing

Through alternative splicing, exons can be included or skipped (exon skipping), extended, or swapped between transcripts, so one gene can yield many different proteins; an extreme case is the fruit-fly gene Dscam, whose tens of thousands of isoforms arise from mutually exclusive exon arrays. Splice-site mutations that corrupt exon recognition are a significant class of genetic disease.
