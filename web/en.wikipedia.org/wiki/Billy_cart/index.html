A billy cart is a traditional home-made gravity-powered cart, typically built by children in Australia and New Zealand. In North America similar vehicles are called soapbox racers or soapbox carts. The billy cart is characteristically crude: a wooden plank or box mounted on four wheels — often salvaged from prams or old bicycles — steered by a rope attached to a pivoting front axle, and powered solely by rolling downhill.

A classic homemade billy cart.

Construction

The classic design consists of a horizontal timber frame with a fixed rear axle and a front axle attached by a single central bolt so that it can pivot. Ropes fastened to each end of the front axle let the rider steer with their hands, while the feet rest on the axle itself and act as crude brakes by dragging on the ground. Wheels were historically whatever could be scavenged — pram wheels, ball-bearing wheels from roller skates, or bicycle wheels in larger "trolley" designs. Brakes, seats, and bodywork vary widely with the builder's materials and ambition.

History and culture

Billy carts became a fixture of Australian childhood in the early-to-mid twentieth century, when commercially made toys were scarce or expensive and suburban streets and hills offered ready courses. The name is sometimes said to derive from carts once drawn by billy goats, though the connection is uncertain. Organized downhill races — billy cart derbies — are still held by schools, councils, and community festivals across Australia, with classes ranging from traditional home-built carts to engineered racers, and safety requirements such as helmets and working brakes now standard.
