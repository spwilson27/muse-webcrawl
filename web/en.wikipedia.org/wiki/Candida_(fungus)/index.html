Candida (fungus)

KingdomFungi
PhylumAscomycota
ClassSaccharomycetes
GenusCandida
Notable speciesC. albicans, C. auris, C. glabrata

Candida is a genus of yeasts and one of the most common causes of fungal infections worldwide. Many species are harmless commensals living on the skin and mucous membranes of healthy people, but they can become opportunistic pathogens, particularly in immunocompromised hosts.

Biology

Candida species are diploid fungi that grow as unicellular yeasts and, in many species, can switch to filamentous forms (pseudohyphae and hyphae), a transition associated with tissue invasion. The genus contains over 150 species, though only a minority infect humans.

Clinical significance

Candida albicans is the most frequently isolated species and causes oral and vaginal candidiasis ("thrush") as well as invasive bloodstream infections. Other important species include C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis and the multidrug-resistant C. auris, identified in 2009, which has caused hospital outbreaks on several continents. Infections are treated with antifungal drugs such as azoles, echinocandins and amphotericin B.

Taxonomy

Modern molecular phylogenetics has shown the genus to be polyphyletic, and many clinically important species have been reassigned to new genera, though the name Candida remains in wide clinical use.
