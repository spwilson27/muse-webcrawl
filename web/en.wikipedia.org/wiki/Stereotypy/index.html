Stereotypy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stereotypy is a repetitive or ritualistic movement, posture, or utterance. Stereotypies may be simple movements such as body rocking, or complex, such as self-caressing, crossing and uncrossing the legs, and marching in place. They are found in people with intellectual disabilities, autism spectrum disorders, tardive dyskinesia, and stereotypic movement disorder, as well as in neurotypical individuals (particularly children). In animal behaviour, stereotypies — such as pacing or bar-biting in captive animals — are often interpreted as indicators of poor welfare or frustration.

Terminology and distinction

Stereotypies are typically repetitive, seemingly driven, and apparently purposeless motor behaviours that are not goal-directed. They differ from tics, which are usually briefer, preceded by an urge, and partially suppressible; from compulsions, which are performed to relieve anxiety about an obsession; and from mannerisms, which are distinctive but functional parts of ordinary movement. In common usage among autistic people and their advocates, the related term "stimming" (self-stimulatory behaviour) is often used, sometimes with positive connotations of self-regulation.

In humans

Primary stereotypies occur in otherwise typically developing children — for example thumb sucking, nail biting, hair twirling, and body rocking — and usually resolve with age. Secondary stereotypies are associated with conditions such as autism, intellectual disability, sensory deprivation (notably blindness), Rett syndrome, and Lesch–Nyhan syndrome. Stereotypies can also be induced or worsened by stimulant drugs and by chronic antipsychotic use, and they occur in catatonia and some frontotemporal dementias. When stereotypies cause injury, distress, or functional impairment, they may meet criteria for stereotypic movement disorder.

In animals

In ethology, stereotypic behaviour in captive animals — repetitive, invariant behaviour with no obvious goal or function — is a major focus of welfare science. Examples include pacing in large carnivores, crib-biting and weaving in stabled horses, feather-plucking in parrots, and route-tracing in zoo animals. Such behaviours are associated with barren environments, early weaning, or thwarted motivations, and environmental enrichment is a common mitigation.

Mechanisms and treatment

Stereotypies are thought to involve dysfunction of basal-ganglia circuits and dopaminergic signalling. Management focuses on treating underlying conditions, behavioural interventions, and environmental modification; medication is reserved for severe or harmful cases.
