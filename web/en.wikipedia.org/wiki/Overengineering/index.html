Overengineering

Overengineering (or over-engineering) is the act of designing a product or providing a solution to a problem in an excessively complicated manner, where a simpler solution can be demonstrated to exist or would have been more effective. It is generally undesirable because of the added cost, complexity, difficulty of use and maintenance burden it introduces relative to its benefits. The concept is related to the KISS principle ("keep it simple, stupid") and to the software development adage YAGNI ("You aren't gonna need it").

Causes

Overengineering can arise from requirements uncertainty, professional incentives that reward technical sophistication, perfectionism, anticipated future needs that never materialize ("gold plating"), or a failure to communicate with end users. In software, it often appears as excessive abstraction layers, premature optimization and architectures built for hypothetical scale.

Costs

Excess complexity increases development time, bug surface, training needs and failure modes, and can make a product fragile. In safety-critical systems, unnecessary mechanisms can themselves become hazards, though in other contexts what appears to be overengineering is deliberate redundancy for reliability.

Examples

Classic examples given in engineering folklore include pens designed to write in space where a pencil sufficed (a partly mythical anecdote), and consumer devices packed with rarely used features ("feature creep"). The Volkswagen "defeat device" era also produced critiques of complexity in emissions systems designed around testing regimes rather than real-world performance.
