Hawaiian sovereignty movement

The Hawaiian sovereignty movement (Hawaiian: ka ea Hawaiʻi) is a grassroots political and cultural campaign seeking to re-establish an autonomous or independent nation or kingdom of Hawaiʻi, on the grounds that the 1893 overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom and the subsequent annexation of Hawaiʻi by the United States in 1898 occurred in violation of international law and the rights of Native Hawaiians.

Proponents range from groups advocating full independence, to those seeking federal recognition and self-governance for Native Hawaiians analogous to that afforded to Native American tribes, to monarchists who favour restoration of the Hawaiian monarchy.

History

On 17 January 1893, Queen Liliʻuokalani was deposed in a coup led by the Committee of Safety, largely composed of American and European businessmen, with the support of the United States Minister to Hawaiʻi, John L. Stevens, and the landing of troops from USS Boston. A Provisional Government was established, followed by the Republic of Hawaii in 1894. Despite the Blount Report's conclusion that the overthrow was illegal, the islands were annexed by the United States through the Newlands Resolution in 1898 during the Spanish–American War.

The modern sovereignty movement emerged in the 1970s alongside the Hawaiian Renaissance, a revival of Native Hawaiian language, hula, navigation and traditional culture. Protests over the bombing of Kahoʻolawe island by the U.S. Navy, occupations of land, and campaigns over the use of ceded lands helped galvanise activism.

Apology Resolution

In 1993, on the centennial of the overthrow, the United States Congress passed the Apology Resolution (Public Law 103-150), signed by President Bill Clinton, which acknowledged the role of U.S. agents in the overthrow and stated that Native Hawaiians had never relinquished their claims to sovereignty. Sovereignty advocates frequently cite the resolution as legal and moral support for their claims.

Positions and organisations

Groups within the movement have included the Nation of Hawaiʻi, Ka Lāhui Hawaiʻi, and various royalist organisations claiming descent from the Hawaiian monarchy. Some activists have staged occupations of ʻIolani Palace grounds and declared the formation of independent governments. Others pursued the legislative route, notably through the proposed Akaka Bill in the U.S. Congress, which would have provided a process for federal recognition of a Native Hawaiian governing entity; the bill was repeatedly introduced in the 2000s but never enacted.

Legal issues

The status of Hawaiian lands, the Office of Hawaiian Affairs (OHA), and Hawaiian Home Lands has been litigated repeatedly. The 2000 U.S. Supreme Court decision in Rice v. Cayetano struck down the Hawaiians-only voting restriction for OHA trustees. Debates continue over whether statehood in 1959, approved by referendum, settled the question of sovereignty.
