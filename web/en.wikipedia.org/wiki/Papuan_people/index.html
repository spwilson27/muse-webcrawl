Papuan people

The Papuan people are the indigenous peoples of New Guinea and neighbouring islands in Melanesia, in the southwestern Pacific Ocean. "Papuan" is a broad term encompassing hundreds of distinct ethnic groups — including the Dani, Asmat, Korowai, Mee, Amungme, and many others — who speak a great diversity of languages and are distinguished from the Austronesian-speaking peoples of the region and from Aboriginal Australians, though all descend in part from some of the earliest modern human migrations out of Africa.

Diversity and history

New Guinea was settled by modern humans roughly 50,000 years ago, when it was joined to Australia as part of the Sahul landmass. The island's rugged terrain fostered extraordinary diversity: Papua New Guinea is the most linguistically diverse country on Earth, with over 800 living languages, many of them belonging to the proposed Trans–New Guinea family and other Papuan groupings unrelated to Austronesian. Highland Papuan societies independently developed agriculture — including cultivation of taro, bananas, and sugarcane and pig husbandry — around 9,000 years ago at sites such as Kuk Swamp, one of the earliest known agricultural landscapes.

Culture and modern life

Papuan cultures traditionally centre on village life, elaborate exchange systems (such as the Highlands' moka and tee ceremonies), bride-wealth, and in some areas ancestral and spirit beliefs, today combined in varying degrees with Christianity. The island is divided politically between Papua New Guinea (independent since 1975) in the east and several Indonesian provinces in the west, where Papuan identity is also bound up with debates over autonomy and self-determination. Distinctive art forms include Asmat carving, Sepik River sculpture, bilum woven bags, and the sing-sing festivals of traditional dress and dance.
