Saturation mutagenesis

Saturation mutagenesis is a method in protein engineering and molecular genetics in which every position of a gene or protein is systematically substituted — ideally with every other amino acid — to create a comprehensive library of variants whose functions are then measured. Site-saturation mutagenesis applies the approach to a single codon, while large-scale implementations, often called deep mutational scanning, mutate an entire gene or domain in one pooled experiment.

Method

Libraries are built using degenerate primers or synthetic oligonucleotides; a common scheme uses NNK codons (where N is any base and K is G or T), encoding all 20 amino acids in 32 codons with reduced stop-codon frequency. Variants are introduced into cells, subjected to a selection or screen — for example binding, fluorescence sorting or growth — and their frequencies before and after selection are counted by high-throughput DNA sequencing, yielding an enrichment score for every mutation.

Applications

Saturation mutagenesis maps structure–function relationships, identifies active sites and drug-resistance mutations, guides directed evolution of enzymes, and increasingly provides experimental data to interpret human genetic variants of uncertain significance and to train machine-learning models of protein function. Classic early saturation studies targeted enzymes such as T4 lysozyme and beta-lactamase; modern deep mutational scans have covered viral proteins such as the SARS-CoV-2 spike receptor-binding domain and human proteins including BRCA1.
