Erikson's stages of psychosocial development

Erikson's stages of psychosocial development, proposed by psychoanalyst Erik Erikson (elaborated with Joan Erikson), describe eight stages through which a healthily developing human passes from infancy to late adulthood. During each stage, the person experiences a psychosocial crisis which could have a positive or negative outcome for personality development; successful resolution yields a corresponding virtue or strength of the ego, while failure can impair later development.

The eight stages

StageCrisisApproximate ageVirtue
1Trust vs. mistrustInfancy (0–1½ years)Hope
2Autonomy vs. shame and doubtEarly childhood (1½–3)Will
3Initiative vs. guiltPlay age (3–5)Purpose
4Industry vs. inferioritySchool age (5–12)Competence
5Identity vs. role confusionAdolescence (12–18)Fidelity
6Intimacy vs. isolationYoung adulthood (18–40)Love
7Generativity vs. stagnationMiddle adulthood (40–65)Care
8Ego integrity vs. despairLate adulthood (65+)Wisdom

The model, set out in Childhood and Society (1950) and later works, extends Freud's psychosexual stages across the whole lifespan and embeds development in social and cultural context — in contrast to Freud, Erikson stressed the ego and the role of society. Joan Erikson later proposed a ninth stage addressing the crises of very old age. The theory has been influential in education and developmental psychology, though critics note its cultural specificity and the loose empirical grounding of fixed stage sequences.
