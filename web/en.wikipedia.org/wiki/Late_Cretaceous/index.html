Late Cretaceous

Late Cretaceous
Epoch: 100.5 – 66 million years ago
Preceded by: Early Cretaceous
Followed by: Paleocene (Paleogene)
Stages: Cenomanian, Turonian, Coniacian, Santonian, Campanian, Maastrichtian

The Late Cretaceous (100.5–66 Ma) is the younger of two epochs into which the Cretaceous Period is divided in the geologic time scale. Rocks from this epoch form the Upper Cretaceous Series. It was preceded by the Early Cretaceous and succeeded by the Paleocene of the Paleogene.

Geology and climate

During the Late Cretaceous, sea levels were among the highest of the Phanerozoic — up to 100–200 metres above present — flooding continental interiors with shallow epicontinental seas such as the Western Interior Seaway of North America. The Atlantic Ocean widened as the Americas separated from Africa and Europe. The climate was warm, with no permanent polar ice caps, though it cooled gradually toward the end of the epoch. Chalk deposits, which give the Cretaceous its name (Latin creta, chalk), were laid down extensively in Europe.

Life

Flowering plants (angiosperms) became dominant on land, and modern groups of insects, birds and mammals diversified. In the seas, mosasaurs, plesiosaurs and ammonites flourished. On land, the Late Cretaceous saw the apex of dinosaur diversity, including tyrannosaurids such as Tyrannosaurus rex, ceratopsids such as Triceratops, hadrosaurs, ankylosaurs and the giant titanosaurs of Gondwana.

The end-Cretaceous extinction

The epoch ended 66 million years ago with the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) extinction event, in which about 75% of species — including all non-avian dinosaurs — became extinct. The event is attributed chiefly to the impact of a large asteroid at Chicxulub, Mexico, possibly compounded by the Deccan Traps flood-basalt eruptions in India.
