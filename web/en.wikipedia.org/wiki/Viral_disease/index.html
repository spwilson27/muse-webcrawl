Viral disease

A viral disease (or viral infection) occurs when an organism's body is invaded by pathogenic viruses and infectious virus particles attach to and enter susceptible cells. Viruses cause a wide range of diseases, from the common cold to AIDS and COVID-19.

Virus particles as seen under an electron microscope.

Characteristics

Viral diseases are generally not responsive to antibiotics, which target bacteria, though some have effective antiviral drugs (such as antiretrovirals for HIV and direct-acting antivirals that cure hepatitis C) and many are preventable by vaccination. Infections may be acute (influenza), chronic (hepatitis B and C), or latent, with viruses persisting and reactivating later—see viral latency. Transmission routes include respiratory droplets, bodily fluids, insect vectors, and contaminated food and water.

Examples

  • Respiratory: common cold (rhinoviruses, coronaviruses), influenza, COVID-19, measles
  • Gastrointestinal: norovirus, rotavirus
  • Liver: hepatitis A, B, C, D, E
  • Skin/exanthems: chickenpox and shingles, herpes simplex, warts (HPV), smallpox (eradicated 1980)
  • Neurological: rabies, polio, viral meningitis and encephalitis
  • Systemic/immunological: HIV/AIDS, dengue, Ebola, Zika
  • Oncogenic: certain HPV types (cervical cancer), hepatitis B/C (liver cancer), EBV

Defense and treatment

The immune system—interferons, antibodies, and cytotoxic T cells—clears most viral infections, sometimes slowly. Vaccines have eradicated smallpox and brought polio near eradication, and control measles, rubella, yellow fever, and others. Antiviral drugs are available for a minority of viruses and often work best early in infection; supportive care remains the mainstay for most viral illnesses.
