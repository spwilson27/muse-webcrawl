A Theory of Justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Theory of Justice is a 1971 work of political philosophy by the American philosopher John Rawls (revised edition 1999), widely regarded as the most important work in Anglophone political philosophy of the 20th century. It revived normative political theory — then dominated by utilitarianism and scepticism about systematic moral argument — by constructing a contractarian theory of "justice as fairness".

Theory

Rawls asks what principles of justice free and equal persons would choose in an "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance" — not knowing their class, talents, sex, race or conception of the good. He argues they would choose two principles: first, equal basic liberties for all; second, social and economic inequalities arranged (a) under conditions of fair equality of opportunity and (b) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the "difference principle"). The priority of liberty over distributive demands, and of fair opportunity over the difference principle, orders the theory. The book ranges over institutions, stability and the congruence of justice with individual good; Rawls restated the view as a freestanding political conception in Political Liberalism (1993).

Reception

Responses defined a generation of theory: Nozick's libertarian Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), communitarian critiques (Sandel, Walzer, MacIntyre), feminist critiques (Okin), and luck-egalitarian and global-justice extensions. Its two principles frame policy debates on welfare, taxation and opportunity to this day.
