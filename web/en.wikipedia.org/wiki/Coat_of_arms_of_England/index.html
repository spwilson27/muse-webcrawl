Coat of arms of England

Arms of England: Gules, three lions passant guardant in pale Or.

The coat of arms of England is the coat of arms historically associated with the Kingdom of England. Its blazon is: Gules, three lions passant guardant in pale Or armed and langued Azure — three golden lions with blue tongues and claws, shown walking with their faces turned toward the viewer, arranged vertically on a red shield. In heraldry the animals are blazoned as "lions", although in the French language of early heraldry they were termed leopards, which is why they are sometimes called "the leopards of England".

History

The arms originated in the late twelfth century. Richard I (the Lionheart) used two lions on his first Great Seal and adopted the three-lion design on his second Great Seal, issued in 1198; the three lions have been the arms of England ever since. The design probably combined the single lion associated with earlier Norman and Angevin rulers, including Henry II, with an additional lion.

From the fourteenth century English monarchs quartered these arms with those of France, in support of their claim to the French throne, and later with the arms of Scotland and Ireland. After the Acts of Union, the arms of England became one component of the royal coat of arms of the United Kingdom, appearing in the first and fourth quarters of the version used in England. The three lions remain a potent national symbol, used by English sports bodies — most famously on the shirts of the England national football team and the England cricket team.
