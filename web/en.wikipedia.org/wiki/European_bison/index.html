The European bison (Bison bonasus), or wisent, is the heaviest wild land animal in Europe and one of two extant bison species alongside the American bison. Once ranging across the continent, it was hunted to extinction in the wild by 1927 and survives today because of captive breeding and reintroduction—one of conservation's emblematic rescues.

Description

Bulls stand up to about 1.9 m at the shoulder and can weigh over 900 kg. Both sexes bear short, upward-curving horns; the coat is dark brown and moults into a lighter summer pelage. Compared with the American bison, the wisent is taller and less massively forequartered, browsing more in woodland.

Extinction in the wild and recovery

The last wild lowland bison was killed in Białowieża Forest in 1919 and the last Caucasian bison in 1927. Zoos preserved 54 animals; a breeding programme coordinated from Poland rebuilt the species from just twelve founders, and releases began in Białowieża in 1952. By the 2020s free-ranging herds numbered in the thousands across Poland, Belarus, Russia, Romania, Germany and elsewhere, and the IUCN downlisted the species from Vulnerable to Near Threatened in 2020. The population's narrow genetic base and disease risks remain concerns.

Ecology

European bison are mixed feeders, grazing grasses and browsing bark, shoots and acorns, and shape forest structure by debarking trees and creating clearings. Herds are led by older cows; bulls are largely solitary outside the rut.
