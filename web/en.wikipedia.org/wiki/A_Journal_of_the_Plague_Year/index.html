A Journal of the Plague Year

A Journal of the Plague Year: Being Observations or Memorials, Of the most Remarkable Occurrences, As well Publick as Private, Which happened in London During the last Great Visitation In 1665 is a book by Daniel Defoe, first published in March 1722. It is an account of one man's experiences of the year 1665, in which the Great Plague struck the city of London. Presented as an eyewitness journal by a narrator identified only as "H.F.", it is in fact a novel — Defoe was about five years old during the plague — constructed from research, official records such as the Bills of Mortality, and oral tradition.

Content

H.F., a saddler who remains in London when wealthier residents flee, records the progress of the plague through the weekly Bills of Mortality, describes public policy — the shutting up of infected houses marked with red crosses, pest-houses, mass burial pits, and the flight of the Court — and recounts anecdotes of terror, devotion, quackery and unexpected survival. The work is notable for its systematic, almost journalistic detail and its mingling of statistics with individual stories.

Publication context and legacy

The book appeared in 1722 amid public anxiety that plague, then raging in Marseille, might again reach England, and it has often been read as a disguised call for preparedness. For a long time catalogued as genuine history, the Journal is now regarded as a pioneering work of the English novel and of creative non-fiction, frequently compared with Samuel Pepys's authentic diary of the same events.
