Expressionist dance

Expressionist dance emphasised emotional intensity and freedom from balletic form

Expressionist dance (German: Ausdruckstanz) was a movement in dance that developed in Central Europe, chiefly Germany, in the early twentieth century as part of the wider Expressionist movement in the arts. Rejecting the codified technique and narrative conventions of classical ballet, its practitioners sought an individual, emotionally charged form of movement that expressed inner experience directly. It is regarded as one of the principal roots of modern dance and of dance theatre.

Origins

The movement emerged around 1900 in reaction against the formalism of ballet and alongside reforms in theatre, painting and music. Influences included the free dance of Isadora Duncan, the eurhythmics of Émile Jaques-Dalcroze, and the movement theories of Rudolf von Laban, whose schools and writings provided much of the movement's theoretical foundation. Rather than virtuoso display, Expressionist dance prized natural, ecstatic and even ugly movement when these served the inner truth of the work; improvisation and the solo form were central.

Principal figures

Mary Wigman, a pupil of Laban, became the movement's most influential exponent, developing a style of intense, often sombre solos such as the Witch Dance (1914), performed masked and accompanied by sparse percussion. Kurt Jooss fused Expressionist principles with ballet technique and created the anti-war work The Green Table (1932), while Harald Kreutzberg and Gret Palucca were among its best-known soloists. In the United States the dancer and choreographer Hanya Holm, a Wigman alumna, carried the approach into American modern dance.

Decline and legacy

The movement flourished in Weimar Germany, but the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 forced many artists into exile and pushed the form toward state-approved folk and spectacle dance. After the Second World War, Ausdruckstanz receded in West Germany as American modern dance and neoclassical ballet gained ground. Its legacy persisted through Laban's notation and movement analysis, through Jooss's company work in England, and above all in the Tanztheater (dance theatre) developed from the 1970s by Pina Bausch, whose work at Wuppertal openly reclaimed the Expressionist inheritance.
