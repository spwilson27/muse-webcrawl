Hormone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hormone is a signalling molecule produced by glands of the endocrine system (or by scattered endocrine cells) and carried by the bloodstream to distant target organs, where it regulates physiology and behaviour. Hormones coordinate growth, metabolism, reproduction, stress responses and sleep, typically acting slowly and at very low concentrations.

Major human hormones include insulin and glucagon (blood sugar), adrenaline (stress response), thyroxine (metabolic rate), cortisol, the sex hormones oestrogen and testosterone, melatonin (sleep–wake cycles) and growth hormone. Plants have their own hormones — auxins, gibberellins, ethylene — governing growth and ripening.

Mechanism

Hormones act by binding receptors: peptide hormones bind cell-surface receptors and trigger internal signalling cascades, while steroid and thyroid hormones enter cells and alter gene expression directly. Feedback loops, usually negative, keep hormone levels in range; failures cause diseases such as diabetes (insulin) and hyper- or hypothyroidism.

History

The word hormone (from Greek hormao, "I excite") was introduced after the 1902 discovery of secretin by Bayliss and Starling, the first recognised hormone. Insulin's isolation in 1921–22 by Banting, Best, Macleod and Collip turned type 1 diabetes from a fatal wasting disease into a manageable one.
