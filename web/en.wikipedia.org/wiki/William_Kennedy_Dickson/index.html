William Kennedy Laurie Dickson (3 August 1860 – 28 September 1935) was a Scottish inventor and early film pioneer who, while working for Thomas Edison, developed the Kinetoscope and the Kinetograph — the first practical motion-picture camera — and shot many of the earliest films ever made. Historians of cinema regard him as one of the principal inventors of the medium, though Edison received most of the contemporary credit and the patents.

Early life

Dickson was born in Brittany, France, to Scottish parents; his father was the artist and astronomer James Waite Dickson. Raised mostly in England, he became fascinated by photography as a young man and in 1879, at nineteen, wrote to Thomas Edison seeking work. Rebuffed, he emigrated to the United States in 1881 and in 1883 was finally hired at Edison's laboratory in Menlo Park, New Jersey, initially working on ore-milling and metallurgy.

Work at Edison's laboratory

At Edison's new West Orange laboratory, Dickson — initially head of the photography department — became the lead experimenter on Edison's plan for "an instrument which does for the Eye what the phonograph does for the Ear". Working with assistants including William Heise and drawing on the work of pioneers such as Eadweard Muybridge and Étienne-Jules Marey, Dickson settled on perforated 35 mm celluloid film — the format that became the industry standard — and by 1891–1893 had completed the Kinetograph camera and the coin-operated Kinetoscope peephole viewer.

In the "Black Maria", the world's first purpose-built film studio, Dickson directed and photographed hundreds of short films, including Fred Ott's Sneeze (1894), and what is often cited as the first surviving motion picture with synchronised sound experiment, the so-called Dickson Experimental Sound Film.

Later career

Dickson left Edison in 1895 amid disputes over credit and pay, and helped found the American Mutoscope and Biograph Company, a major Edison competitor, for which he developed the Mutoscope and a camera that avoided Edison's patents. He filmed widely in Britain and South Africa during the Boer War, and later worked with the British War Office. His 1903 history of the Biograph company is an important early document of film history.

Legacy

Long overshadowed by Edison, Dickson has been reassessed by film historians as the key practical inventor of cinema technology in the United States. He died in Twickenham, England, in 1935.
