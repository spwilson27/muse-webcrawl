Wake Island is a coral atoll in the western Pacific Ocean, in the northeastern area of the Micronesia subregion of Oceania. It consists of three low islets — Wake, Wilkes, and Peale — enclosing a shallow central lagoon, with a total land area of about 7.4 square kilometres. It lies roughly two-thirds of the way from Hawaii to Guam and is administered by the United States as an unorganized, unincorporated territory.

History

The atoll was sighted by Spanish explorers in the sixteenth century and later visited by the British merchant captain Samuel Wake, for whom it is named. The United States formally took possession in 1899. In the 1930s, Pan American Airways built a seaplane base on Peale Island as a stopover on its trans-Pacific Clipper route.

Battle of Wake Island

Hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor, Japanese forces attacked Wake on 8 December 1941. The small US Marine Corps garrison, supported by civilian contractors, repelled the first amphibious assault on 11 December — one of the few early American successes of the war — sinking two Japanese destroyers. A second, heavier assault overwhelmed the defenders on 23 December 1941. The atoll remained under Japanese occupation until the end of the war; the surviving defenders endured captivity, and 98 American civilian prisoners were executed on the island in October 1943.

Postwar use

After the war Wake became an important refuelling stop for trans-Pacific flights and a site for missile testing and defence operations. President Harry S. Truman met General Douglas MacArthur on Wake in October 1950 during the Korean War. Today the island hosts Wake Island Airfield, operated by the United States Air Force, with a small resident population of military personnel and contractors. The atoll is also part of the Pacific Remote Islands Marine National Monument, and its reefs and lagoon support seabirds, sea turtles, and diverse marine life.

Geography and climate

Wake is a low atoll, rising only a few metres above sea level, with a tropical climate moderated by trade winds and occasionally affected by typhoons. There is no permanent fresh water source; supplies are collected from rainfall and desalination.
