LIBOR

LIBOR (London Interbank Offered Rate) was an interest-rate benchmark representing the average rate at which major global banks estimated they could borrow unsecured funds from one another in the London interbank market. For decades it was the world's most important benchmark interest rate, used to price an estimated hundreds of trillions of dollars of loans, mortgages, derivatives and other financial contracts. Following a rate-manipulation scandal and the thinning of the underlying interbank market, LIBOR was discontinued: most settings ceased at the end of 2021 and the remaining US dollar settings after 30 June 2023.

Calculation

Each business day, a panel of major banks submitted estimates of the rate at which they could borrow in reasonable market size for each currency and maturity. The administrator discarded the highest and lowest quartiles of submissions and averaged the remainder to produce the published rate. Because the submissions were estimates rather than records of actual transactions, the system depended on the honesty of submitters.

Scandal

Investigations beginning around 2012 revealed that traders at several banks had manipulated submissions to benefit derivatives positions or to make their institutions appear healthier during the financial crisis. Barclays was the first bank fined, in June 2012, and penalties across the industry eventually totalled billions of dollars, with criminal convictions of individual traders following. The scandal destroyed confidence in judgment-based benchmarks.

Replacement

Regulators concluded that LIBOR could not be sustained because banks made too few actual unsecured interbank loans to anchor it. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority announced in 2017 that it would no longer compel panel submissions after 2021. Markets transitioned to nearly risk-free overnight rates derived from real transactions — principally SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) for US dollars and SONIA for sterling — with "synthetic" LIBOR settings published temporarily to help legacy contracts wind down.
