Flywheel energy storage

Flywheel energy storage (FES) stores energy kinetically in a rotating mass (rotor or flywheel) and releases it by slowing the rotor. A motor/generator on the same shaft accelerates the flywheel to store energy and decelerates it to generate electricity. Modern FES systems spin composite rotors at 8,000 to more than 60,000 revolutions per minute inside vacuum enclosures on magnetic bearings, minimising friction and windage losses.

Principle

The stored energy is proportional to the rotor's moment of inertia and the square of its angular velocity:

E = ½ I ω²

Because energy scales with the square of speed, high-speed designs favour strong, light materials — carbon-fibre composites with very high tensile strength — over massive steel rotors. Rotor bursts are the principal safety hazard and are contained by the vacuum housing.

Characteristics and applications

Flywheels offer high power density, very fast response (milliseconds), deep discharge without degradation, and long cycle life (hundreds of thousands of cycles), but relatively low energy density and self-discharge rates (often 3–20% per hour from residual losses) make them suited to short-duration duties: frequency regulation on electricity grids (for example Beacon Power's 20 MW plants in New York and Pennsylvania), uninterruptible power supplies for data centres and hospitals, regenerative braking systems on trains and in Formula 1-derived KERS units, spacecraft attitude control and energy storage, and load-levelling for pulsed power and renewable integration. The first large commercial grid flywheel plants entered service in the 2010s.

History

Flywheels smoothed the power of steam engines and drove early gyro buses in the 1950s; the modern high-speed composite flywheel was developed from the 1970s onward with advances in materials, power electronics and active magnetic bearings.
