Antifibrinolytic

Antifibrinolytics are a class of drugs that promote blood clotting by inhibiting fibrinolysis — the enzymatic breakdown of fibrin clots. They are used to prevent or treat excessive bleeding in surgery, trauma, hemophilia, and heavy menstrual bleeding.

Agents and mechanism

The most widely used antifibrinolytics are the synthetic lysine analogues tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid (epsilon-aminocaproic acid). These competitively block the lysine-binding sites on plasminogen, preventing its conversion to plasmin and its attachment to fibrin, so existing clots are stabilized rather than dissolved. Aprotinin, a naturally occurring serine protease inhibitor, was formerly used in cardiac surgery but was withdrawn in many markets over safety concerns.

Uses and evidence

Large randomized trials demonstrated that early tranexamic acid reduces deaths from bleeding in trauma (CRASH-2) and postpartum hemorrhage (WOMAN trial) when given within three hours of onset. Antifibrinolytics are also standard in cardiac and orthopedic surgery, dental procedures in patients with bleeding disorders, and hereditary angioedema prophylaxis in some settings. Principal risks include thromboembolic events and, at high doses, seizures.
