In mathematics, in the field of complex analysis, the maximum modulus principle (or maximum modulus theorem) states that if f is a holomorphic function, then the modulus |f| cannot exhibit a strict local maximum that is properly within the domain of f.

Statement

Formally, if f is holomorphic on a connected open set D in the complex plane and takes a value of maximum modulus at an interior point z0 ∈ D — i.e.,

|f(z0)| ≥ |f(z)| for all z in a neighbourhood of z0

then f is constant on D. Equivalently, a non-constant holomorphic function on a bounded domain that extends continuously to the closure attains its maximum modulus on the boundary of the domain.

Proof idea

One proof uses the mean value property: the value f(z0) equals the average of f over any small circle around z0. If |f| has a local maximum at z0, the average can equal the central value only if |f| is constant on the circle and the values have aligned arguments, forcing f to be locally constant; connectedness then makes f constant throughout D. The result can also be derived from the open mapping theorem, since the image of an open set under a non-constant holomorphic map is open and hence cannot contain a boundary point of its range.

Consequences

Applying the principle to 1/f yields the minimum modulus principle: a non-constant holomorphic function without zeros in a domain attains its minimum modulus on the boundary. The principle underlies the Schwarz lemma, the Phragmén–Lindelöf principle for unbounded domains, and Hadamard's three-lines and three-circles theorems, and it is used to prove the fundamental theorem of algebra via Liouville-type arguments.
