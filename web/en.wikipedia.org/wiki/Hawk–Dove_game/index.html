Hawk–Dove game

The Hawk–Dove game (also known as the game of chicken) is a classic model in evolutionary game theory of conflict and aggression over a shareable resource. Formulated by John Maynard Smith and George R. Price in 1973, it examines two strategies: the Hawk, which escalates and fights until injured or the opponent retreats, and the Dove, which displays but retreats immediately if the opponent escalates.

Escalation versus retreat: the two strategies of the Hawk–Dove game.

The model

Let the contested resource be worth V and the cost of injury in an escalated fight be C. Hawk versus Hawk yields (V−C)/2 on average; Hawk versus Dove gives V to the Hawk and 0 to the Dove; Dove versus Dove gives V/2 to each. Neither pure strategy is generally stable: if C > V, the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) is a mixed one in which individuals play Hawk with probability V/C. The model explains why real animal contests are usually ritualised and rarely end in serious injury, and variants add strategies such as "Bourgeois" (play Hawk when owner, Dove when intruder), which can also be stable.

Applications

Beyond animal behaviour, the Hawk–Dove framework is applied to political brinkmanship (where it parallels the game of chicken in nuclear deterrence), microbial competition, and the evolution of cooperation and property conventions.
