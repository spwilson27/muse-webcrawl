Corium (nuclear reactor)

Corium, also called fuel-containing material (FCM) or lava-like fuel-containing material (LFCM), is a lava-like material that can form in the core of a nuclear reactor during a meltdown accident. It consists of molten nuclear fuel (uranium dioxide), fission products, control rods, structural materials from the affected parts of the reactor such as steel and concrete, and, where the melt interacts with air, their oxidation products. The name is a portmanteau of "core" and the suffix -ium used for elements.

Formation

In a severe loss-of-coolant accident, decay heat raises fuel temperatures above 2,000 °C. The zirconium alloy cladding reacts exothermically with steam (generating hydrogen), fuel rods collapse, and a molten pool of fuel and liquefied structures forms. This pool can relocate downward, melt through the reactor pressure vessel (as happened at Fukushima Daiichi units 1–3 in 2011), and spread onto the containment basemat, where molten core–concrete interaction erodes the concrete and incorporates it into the melt, generating non-condensable gases. Temperatures of a fresh corium pool are commonly estimated at 2,400–2,800 °C, decaying over days as short-lived fission products decay.

Known occurrences

Corium formed in the Three Mile Island Unit 2 accident (1979), where about twenty tonnes of molten material relocated to the lower head of the vessel — which, unexpectedly, held — and at Chernobyl Unit 4 (1986), where LFCM flowed through steam pipes and corridors and solidified into formations including the famous Elephant's Foot, a once-lethally radioactive glassy mass of corium, sand (dropped to smother the fire), and concrete. At Fukushima, corium from three melted cores breached the pressure vessels and is the object of long-term robotic investigation and decommissioning plans.

Composition and behaviour

Solidified corium is typically a ceramic-glass mixture rich in uranium and zirconium oxides, calcium and silicon oxides from concrete, and trapped fission products and actinides, making it both highly radioactive and a long-term source of heat and of leachable contamination. Modern reactor designs — such as the EPR and AP1000 — incorporate a core catcher, a sacrificial-concrete crucible or spreading compartment designed to receive, dilute, cool, and stabilize corium ex-vessel, thereby preventing basemat melt-through and containment failure.
