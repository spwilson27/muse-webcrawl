Germ (grain)

The germ of a cereal grain is the reproductive part that germinates to grow into a plant; it is the embryo of the seed. Along with bran, germ is often a by-product of the milling that produces refined grain products. Cereal grains and their components, such as wheat germ oil, rice bran oil, and maize bran, may be used as a source from which vegetable oil is extracted, or used directly as a food ingredient. The germ is retained as an integral part of whole-grain foods.

Non-whole-grain methods of milling are intended to isolate the endosperm, which is ground into flour, with removal of both the husk (bran) and the germ. Removal of bran is aimed at producing a flour with a white rather than a brown colour, and eliminating fibre, which reduces nutrition. The germ is rich in polyunsaturated fats (which have a tendency to oxidize and become rancid on storage) and so germ removal improves the storage qualities of flour.

Composition

Wheat germ is the nutrient-rich embryo of the wheat kernel. It contains concentrated amounts of vitamin E, folate, phosphorus, thiamin, zinc and magnesium, as well as essential fatty acids and fatty alcohols such as octacosanol. Because of its high oil content, wheat germ can quickly become rancid if not stored properly, and it is therefore often sold toasted or refrigerated.

Uses

Wheat germ is added to smoothies, cereals, yoghurt, casseroles and baked goods to increase their nutritional value. It is also sold as a dietary supplement in flake or oil form. In molecular biology, wheat germ extract is used as a cell-free system for in vitro protein synthesis, particularly for eukaryotic proteins that are difficult to express in bacterial systems.

Defatted germ

Defatted wheat germ, from which the oil has been extracted, is used as a protein-rich ingredient in food products and as a feed component. Defatting also greatly extends shelf life by removing the lipids most prone to oxidation.
