Letting agent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A letting agent is a facilitator through which an agreement is made between a landlord and tenant for the rental of a residential property. The term is most common in the United Kingdom, where letting agents act for landlords in finding tenants, drawing up tenancy agreements, collecting rent, and, where instructed, managing properties on an ongoing basis. Comparable professionals in other countries operate under titles such as rental agent, leasing agent, or property manager.

Services

Letting services are usually offered in tiers: a tenant-finding (let-only) service, in which the agent markets the property, conducts viewings, performs referencing and credits checks, and prepares the tenancy agreement; rent collection, adding the ongoing collection and accounting of rent; and full management, in which the agent additionally handles repairs, inspections, deposit protection, and legal notices. Agents are usually paid by the landlord as a percentage of rent or a fixed fee.

Regulation in the United Kingdom

Historically the lettings industry in England was lightly regulated, and anyone could set up as a letting agent. A series of reforms introduced deposit protection schemes, mandatory redress (ombudsman) membership, and client money protection. The Tenant Fees Act 2019 banned most fees charged to tenants in England, limiting permitted payments to rent, refundable deposits, and a small number of specified charges. Agents in Scotland and Wales operate under their respective devolved regulatory regimes, which include registers and codes of practice. Professional bodies such as Propertymark (formerly ARLA) and the UK Association of Letting Agents set voluntary standards, and government has periodically proposed a single regulator and mandatory qualifications for agents.

Criticism

Letting agents have been criticized for charging opaque or duplicative fees, for poor handling of repairs, and for conflicts of interest in acting simultaneously for landlord and tenant. Consumer organizations have long campaigned for tighter regulation, an effort that contributed to the fee bans and redress requirements introduced in the 2010s.
