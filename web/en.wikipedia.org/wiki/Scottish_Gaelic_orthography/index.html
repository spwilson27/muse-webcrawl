Scottish Gaelic orthography

Scottish Gaelic orthography is the writing system used for the Scottish Gaelic language (Gàidhlig). Gaelic has been written in Latin script, adapted from Irish conventions, since the medieval period; the modern system is largely regular and phonemically informed, though spelling reflects historical sound changes such as lenition and the distinction between "broad" and "slender" consonants.

Alphabet

Traditional Gaelic uses 18 letters: a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, l, m, n, o, p, r, s, t, u. The letters j, k, q, v, w, x, y, z appear only in loanwords and scientific terms. Letters were traditionally named after trees (the so-called tree alphabet: ailm, beith, coll, etc.). Vowels may carry a grave accent (à, è, ì, ò, ù) marking length or quality; the acute accent, once used in older texts, was dropped in modern orthography following the Gaelic Orthographic Conventions (GOC), first issued in 1981 and revised in 2005 and 2009.

Broad and slender consonants

A central principle is caol ri caol, leathann ri leathann ("slender with slender, broad with broad"): consonants are flanked by vowels of matching quality — slender (palatalised) consonants stand next to front vowels e/i, broad consonants next to back vowels a/o/u. Extra vowels are therefore written purely to signal consonant quality, as in balach (boy) versus balaich (boys). Consonant digraphs with h (bh, ch, dh, fh, gh, mh, ph, sh, th) represent lenited consonants.

Conventions and reform

The Gaelic Orthographic Conventions standardised spellings for education and publishing, simplifying some older forms (e.g., preferring dùthchas over variants) and regularising the use of hyphens and apostrophes. Written Gaelic also uses the definite article forms an, a', na, nam/nan and a system of initial mutations — lenition and eclipsis-like effects — that are marked in spelling.
