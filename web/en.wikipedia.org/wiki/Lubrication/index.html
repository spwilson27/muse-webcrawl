Lubrication

Lubrication
FieldTribology, mechanical engineering
PurposeReduction of friction and wear between surfaces in relative motion
Common lubricantsMineral oils, synthetic oils, greases, graphite, MoS2

Lubrication is the process or technique of using a lubricant to reduce friction and wear between two surfaces in close proximity and moving relative to each other. The lubricant—a film of oil, grease or another substance—is introduced between the surfaces so that they are separated wholly or partly, which reduces heat generation, energy loss and material damage. The study of lubrication, friction and wear is called tribology.

Regimes of lubrication

The relationship between friction and lubricant film thickness is often described by the Stribeck curve, which distinguishes several regimes:

  • Boundary lubrication — the surfaces are separated only by adsorbed molecular films; surface chemistry dominates.
  • Mixed lubrication — the load is shared between asperity contact and the fluid film.
  • Hydrodynamic (full-film) lubrication — motion drags lubricant into a converging gap, generating pressure that fully separates the surfaces, as in journal bearings.
  • Elastohydrodynamic lubrication — occurs in highly loaded non-conformal contacts such as gears and rolling bearings, where elastic deformation and pressure-induced viscosity increase sustain a thin film.

Lubricants

Common lubricants include mineral and synthetic oils, greases (oil thickened with soaps), solid lubricants such as graphite and molybdenum disulfide, and gases in air bearings. Additives—anti-wear agents, detergents, viscosity-index improvers, antioxidants and extreme-pressure compounds—tailor oils to applications from internal combustion engines to industrial gearboxes.

Applications

Lubrication is essential wherever machinery moves: engines, turbines, compressors, chains, cables and bearings. Inadequate lubrication is a leading cause of mechanical failure, while proper lubrication intervals and oil analysis are central to maintenance engineering. Biological systems also employ lubrication, for example synovial fluid in animal joints.
