Univalent foundations

Univalent foundations are an approach to the foundations of mathematics in which mathematical structures are built within a dependent type theory based on Martin-Löf type theory, augmented by the univalence axiom proposed by Vladimir Voevodsky. The univalence axiom states that isomorphic (more precisely, equivalent) mathematical objects can be identified — that the identity type between two types is equivalent to the type of equivalences between them: (A = B) ≃ (A ≃ B).

In univalent foundations, equivalent types are identical

History

The ideas grew out of the discovery, by several researchers in the 1990s and 2000s, that Martin-Löf type theory admits a model in simplicial sets in which identity types behave like path spaces in homotopy theory. Voevodsky developed the univalence axiom and began formalizing mathematics in the proof assistant Coq around 2006–2010. In 2012–2013 a special year at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton brought together logicians, topologists, and computer scientists, producing the collaboratively written textbook Homotopy Type Theory: Univalent Foundations of Mathematics (the "HoTT book").

Key ideas

  • Univalence axiom — equivalent types are equal, making isomorphism-invariance automatic rather than something to be proved for each construction.
  • Homotopy type theory (HoTT) — types are interpreted as spaces (homotopy types), terms as points, and identities as paths, giving a synthetic language for homotopy theory.
  • Higher inductive types — types defined simultaneously by their points and paths, allowing synthetic constructions such as circles, spheres, and quotients.
  • Function extensionality and propositional resizing — principles that follow from or accompany univalence in standard formulations.
  • Structure identity principle — for structured objects, identity coincides with structure-preserving isomorphism, a consequence of univalence.

Formalization

Univalent mathematics has been formalized in Coq (notably the UniMath library initiated by Voevodsky), Agda (including cubical Agda, where univalence is a theorem with computational content rather than an axiom), and Lean. The subject remains an active research area in mathematical logic and theoretical computer science.
