Organelle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An organelle is a specialised subunit within a cell that has a specific function, usually enclosed by its own membrane. The word means 'little organ', by analogy with the organs of the body. Organelles are a hallmark of eukaryotic cells, whose compartmentalisation lets incompatible chemistries proceed side by side.

Main organelles

The Nucleus houses the genome; mitochondria generate most ATP; the Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus process proteins and lipids; lysosomes digest waste; peroxisomes handle oxidation; and plant cells add chloroplasts for photosynthesis and a large central vacuole for storage and turgor. The Cytoskeleton and ribosomes are sometimes classed as organelles though not membrane-bound.

Origins

Mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from free-living bacteria captured by endosymbiosis, retaining their own small genomes. Other organelles likely evolved from invaginations of the cell membrane. Defects in organelles cause many inherited diseases, such as mitochondrial disorders and lysosomal storage diseases.
