Iraqi civil war (2006–2008)

The Iraqi civil war of 2006–2008 (sometimes called the first Iraqi civil war) was a period of intense sectarian conflict within the wider Iraq War, fought principally between Sunni insurgent groups and al-Qaeda in Iraq on one side and Shia militias and Shia-dominated security forces on the other, with coalition forces intervening throughout. It is generally dated from the bombing of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra on 22 February 2006 to the decline of violence in 2008.

Course of the conflict

The Samarra bombing — attributed to al-Qaeda in Iraq — destroyed the golden dome of one of Shia Islam's holiest shrines and triggered waves of reprisal killings, mosque attacks, and death-squad activity in Baghdad and the mixed provinces. Baghdad's neighbourhoods were violently cleansed along sectarian lines, and monthly civilian deaths reached into the thousands at the peak in late 2006. Key actors included the Sunni insurgency and al-Qaeda in Mesopotamia under Abu Musab al-Zarqawi (killed in June 2006), and Shia militias such as Muqtada al-Sadr's Mahdi Army and the Badr Organization.

Decline of violence

Violence fell sharply from late 2007 due to several converging factors: the US troop "surge" and new counterinsurgency strategy under General David Petraeus, the Sunni "Awakening" movement in which tribal fighters turned against al-Qaeda, a ceasefire by the Mahdi Army, and the near-completion of sectarian separation in Baghdad. In 2008, Iraqi government offensives in Basra (Operation Charge of the Knights), Sadr City, and Mosul further weakened militia control.

Human cost

The civil war displaced millions: by 2007 the UN estimated some two million Iraqis had fled abroad, mainly to Syria and Jordan, and another two million were internally displaced. Estimates of deaths during this period vary widely, but the Iraq Body Count project recorded tens of thousands of civilian deaths in 2006–2007 alone.
