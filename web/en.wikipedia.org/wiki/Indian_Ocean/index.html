Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean is the third-largest of the world's five oceanic divisions, covering about 70.6 million km², roughly 20% of the water on Earth's surface. It is bounded by Asia to the north, Africa to the west, Australia to the east and the Southern Ocean to the south. Unlike the Atlantic and Pacific, it is enclosed by land in the north, which gives it a distinctive monsoon climate: seasonal winds reverse direction, driving currents, rainfall and, historically, the sailing trade routes between East Africa, Arabia, India and Southeast Asia.

Geography

Important marginal seas and gulfs include the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the Andaman Sea. Major islands include Madagascar, Sri Lanka, Socotra, and the archipelagos of Indonesia, the Maldives and Seychelles. The deepest point is in the Java (Sunda) Trench. Key chokepoints for world trade — the Strait of Malacca, the Strait of Hormuz and the Suez Canal route via the Red Sea — all connect to the Indian Ocean, making it vital to global energy shipments.

Climate and ecology

The Indian Ocean is the warmest of the world oceans, and its monsoon system dominates the climate of South Asia, bringing the summer rains on which over a billion people's agriculture depends. Its ecosystems range from the coral reefs of the Maldives and Chagos to the kelp forests of South Africa. In December 2004, a magnitude ~9.1 earthquake off Sumatra triggered an Indian Ocean tsunami that killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries, one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history — a tragedy coordinated internationally in its aftermath much as the World Health Organization and relief agencies coordinate disaster response.
