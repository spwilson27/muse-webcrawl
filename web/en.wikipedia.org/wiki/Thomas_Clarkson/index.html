Thomas Clarkson (28 March 1760 – 26 September 1846) was an English abolitionist and a leading campaigner against the slave trade in the British Empire. He helped found the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade and, through tireless research, evidence-gathering and organising, supplied the ammunition for William Wilberforce's parliamentary campaign that abolished the British slave trade in 1807 and, eventually, slavery itself in 1833.

Conversion to the cause

Clarkson studied at St John's College, Cambridge, where in 1785 he won the university Latin essay prize on the question "Is it lawful to enslave the unconsenting?" Researching the essay — reading accounts of the trade's horrors — converted him to abolition; on the road to London he resolved, in a moment he later described as a spiritual summons, to devote his life to ending it. His expanded essay was published in English in 1786 and brought him into contact with Quaker abolitionists.

Campaigning

In 1787 Clarkson was a founding member of the twelve-man committee of the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade. Over the next years he rode some 35,000 miles around Britain's slave ports — Liverpool, Bristol, Lancaster — interviewing sailors, inspecting slave ships, and collecting artefacts of the trade (shackles, thumbscrews, branding irons) and African produce to display as evidence. He organised petitions and the famous diagram of the slave ship Brookes, and survived attempts on his life. After the trade's abolition in 1807 he campaigned for international abolition — lobbying at the Congress of Vienna (1814–15) and Paris — and for emancipation, travelling to France and, in 1840, chairing the opening of the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in his eightieth year. He died at Playford Hall, Suffolk, in 1846.

Legacy

Clarkson is commemorated by monuments at Wisbech and elsewhere, and his two-volume History of the Rise, Progress and Accomplishment of the Abolition of the African Slave Trade (1808) remains a primary source for the campaign.
