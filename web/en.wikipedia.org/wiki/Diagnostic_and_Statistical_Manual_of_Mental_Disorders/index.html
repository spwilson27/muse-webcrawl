The DSM is the standard classification of mental disorders used by clinicians in the United States.

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) is a publication by the American Psychiatric Association (APA) for the classification of mental disorders using a common language and standard criteria. It is the primary diagnostic reference used by mental health professionals in the United States, and is widely used internationally for clinical practice, research, insurance, and policy.

History

The first edition, DSM-I (1952), reflected psychodynamic thinking and listed 106 disorders. DSM-II (1968) was similar in approach. DSM-III (1980), under Robert Spitzer, revolutionised the manual with explicit diagnostic criteria, a multiaxial system, and a nominally atheoretical stance toward etiology; DSM-III-R followed in 1987 and DSM-IV in 1994 (text revision 2000). DSM-5 appeared in 2013, dropping the multiaxial system and reorganising disorders by presumed relatedness, and the text revision DSM-5-TR was published in 2022.

Notable changes

Successive editions have added, merged, and removed diagnoses, sometimes controversially: homosexuality was removed as a disorder in 1973–74; post-traumatic stress disorder was added in DSM-III; Asperger's disorder, added in DSM-IV, was folded into autism spectrum disorder in DSM-5; and prolonged grief disorder was added in DSM-5-TR.

Criticism

The DSM has been criticised for medicalising normal human experience, for the reliability and validity of some categories, for cultural bias, and for the influence of the pharmaceutical industry on its panels. Alternatives and complements include the ICD-11 of the World Health Organization and dimensional frameworks such as RDoC and HiTOP.
