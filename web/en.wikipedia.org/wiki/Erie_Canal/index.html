The Erie Canal is a historic canal in upstate New York that originally ran 363 miles (584 km) from the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. Completed in 1825, it was the first navigable waterway connecting the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes, and it dramatically reduced the cost of shipping goods between the eastern seaboard and the interior of North America.

Construction

Championed by New York governor DeWitt Clinton and derided by critics as "Clinton's Big Ditch", construction began on July 4, 1817, at Rome, New York. Built largely by hand with the labour of local workers and immigrants, the canal crossed rivers on aqueducts and climbed the Niagara Escarpment through a flight of locks at Lockport. The canal opened on October 26, 1825, when Clinton travelled by packet boat from Buffalo to New York City and ceremonially poured water from Lake Erie into the Atlantic.

Impact

The canal cut freight costs from about $100 per ton to under $10 per ton and travel time from weeks to days. It helped make New York City the pre-eminent port of the United States, fuelled the growth of canal towns such as Rochester, Syracuse and Buffalo, opened the Midwest to settlement and commerce, and inspired a nationwide canal-building boom. The canal was enlarged several times; the modern New York State Barge Canal, completed in 1918, follows a partly different route using canalised rivers.

Today much of the original canal corridor is preserved as the Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor, used chiefly for recreation and heritage tourism.
