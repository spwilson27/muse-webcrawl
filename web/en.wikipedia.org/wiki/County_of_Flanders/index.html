County of Flanders

The County of Flanders (Dutch: Graafschap Vlaanderen; French: Comté de Flandre) was one of the most powerful political entities of medieval Europe, located on the North Sea coast in what is now Belgium, France and the Netherlands. Ruled by the Counts of Flanders, it flourished from the 9th century until it was absorbed into the Burgundian and later Habsburg Netherlands.

History

The county was founded around 862 when Baldwin I became the first Count of Flanders, having married Judith, daughter of the Carolingian king Charles the Bald. In the High Middle Ages, Flemish cities such as Bruges, Ghent and Ypres grew rich on the cloth trade, weaving English wool into fine textiles, making Flanders one of the most urbanised regions of Europe.

The counts frequently clashed with the kings of France, their nominal overlords for the western part of the county ("Crown Flanders"), while the eastern part ("Imperial Flanders") belonged to the Holy Roman Empire. At the Battle of the Golden Spurs in 1302, Flemish urban militias famously defeated a French royal army near Kortrijk. In 1384 the county passed by marriage to the dukes of Burgundy, and later to the Habsburgs.

Legacy

The historical county corresponds roughly to the modern Belgian provinces of West Flanders and East Flanders, plus French Flanders (around Lille and Dunkirk) and part of Dutch Zeelandic Flanders. The name survives in the modern Flemish Region of Belgium.
