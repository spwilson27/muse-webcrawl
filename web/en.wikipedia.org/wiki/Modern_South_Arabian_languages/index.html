The Modern South Arabian languages (MSAL) are a group of Semitic languages spoken in the southern Arabian Peninsula, principally in the Dhofar region of Oman and the al-Mahra Governorate of eastern Yemen, and on the Yemeni island of Socotra. They descend from a branch of Semitic related to, but distinct from, Arabic — Arabic arrived in the region later — and are related to the Old South Arabian languages of ancient inscriptions (Sabaic, Himyaritic and others), though they are not direct descendants of the attested epigraphic languages. All of the Modern South Arabian languages are endangered, having no official status and traditionally no written form, and they are under heavy pressure from Arabic.

The languages

Mehri

Mehri is the largest of the group, spoken by the Mahra people in eastern Yemen and western Oman, with speaker estimates ranging from roughly 100,000 to over 200,000 including diaspora communities. It has two main dialect clusters, in Yemen and Oman.

Soqotri

Soqotri is spoken on Socotra, Abd al Kuri and Samhah islands by perhaps 50,000–70,000 people. It is notable for its archaic features and rich oral poetry tradition, and it is the most divergent of the group.

Jibbali (Shehri)

Jibbali, locally called Shehri or Ehkili, is spoken in the mountains of Dhofar, Oman, by an estimated 25,000–50,000 people. "Jibbali" refers to the mountains (jebel) where its speakers live.

Harsusi, Bathari and Hobyot

Harsusi is spoken by a few thousand people (or fewer) in the Jiddat al-Harasis of central Oman. Bathari, spoken on the coast of Dhofar, is moribund, with only a small number of fluent elderly speakers. Hobyot, spoken near the Oman–Yemen border, is also severely endangered.

Linguistic features

The Modern South Arabian languages preserve several archaic Semitic features lost in Arabic, including a series of glottalised (ejective) consonants and lateral fricatives reflecting Proto-Semitic sounds. They share features such as a definite article in some languages (Mehri ə-), complex verbal morphology with distinct imperfective patterns, and extensive systems of consonantal root derivation. Their vocabulary preserves many words for the region's distinctive ecology — monsoon (khareef), frankincense and pastoral life.

Documentation and endangerment

Because the languages were unwritten, knowledge of them long depended on oral tradition. Serious documentation began with the Austrian South Arabian expeditions of the late nineteenth century and Bertram Thomas's work in the 1930s, and it continues with modern projects — notably a major documentation project on Mehri, Jibbali, Harsusi and Bathari led by Janet Watson at the University of Leeds — which have produced grammars, dictionaries and digital archives, as well as a practical Latin-based orthography for Mehri. UNESCO classifies all six languages as endangered, and intergenerational transmission is weakening as Arabic dominates education and media.
