Crop art

Crop art is a folk-art form in which pictures and designs are created by gluing seeds, grains and other plant parts of naturally varying colours onto a backing. The craft is especially associated with the American Midwest, where seed art has been a competitive category at agricultural fairs for well over a century — most famously at the Minnesota State Fair, which has exhibited crop art since the early twentieth century.

Technique

The artist sketches a design, then applies seeds one by one with adhesive, exploiting the natural colour range of materials — white and yellow corn, red sorghum, flax, wild rice, sunflower seeds, and dozens of others. Rules at the Minnesota State Fair require that entries be made entirely from plant materials grown in the state, with no paint or artificial colouring. Works range from landscapes and portraits to satirical political pieces, which have become a popular contemporary genre at the fair.

Notable practitioners

Lillian Colton (1912–2007) of Owatonna, Minnesota, was the best-known crop artist; her meticulously detailed seed portraits of celebrities and historical figures won many fair prizes and are now held by museums, and she is credited with elevating the form. The fair's crop art exhibition draws some of its largest crowds each year, and the tradition has inspired related forms such as the "butter sculptures" of neighbouring fairs.

Related forms

Crop art should not be confused with crop circles (patterns flattened in standing crops) or with tanbo āto, the Japanese art of growing rice of different colours into giant field pictures; similar seed mosaics occur in folk traditions elsewhere, and seed pictures are an established category at horticultural shows in Britain and elsewhere.
