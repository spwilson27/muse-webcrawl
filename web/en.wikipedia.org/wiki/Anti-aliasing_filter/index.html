Anti-aliasing filter

An anti-aliasing filter (AAF) is a filter used before a signal sampler to restrict the bandwidth of a signal to approximately satisfy the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem over the band of interest. Since the theorem states that unambiguous reconstruction requires the signal to be band-limited below half the sampling frequency, the anti-aliasing filter attenuates components above the Nyquist frequency that would otherwise fold back into the sampled band as spurious aliases.

Analog filters

In audio and instrumentation systems the AAF is usually an analog low-pass filter — often a Butterworth, Chebyshev or elliptic design, or a Bessel filter where phase linearity matters — placed in front of the analog-to-digital converter. An ideal brick-wall response is physically unrealisable, so designers leave a transition band, for example sampling 44.1 kHz audio with a filter that passes 20 kHz and strongly attenuates by 22.05 kHz. Oversampling converters relax these requirements, allowing a gentle analog filter followed by a sharp digital decimation filter.

Optical anti-aliasing filters

Digital cameras face the spatial analogue of aliasing, visible as moiré patterns. Many camera sensors are fitted with an optical low-pass filter — a stack of birefringent plates that spreads each point of light over several photosites — to limit spatial detail beyond the sensor's Nyquist limit. Some high-resolution cameras omit the filter for maximum sharpness, accepting a greater risk of moiré.

Related concepts

Anti-aliasing filters should not be confused with the reconstruction (anti-imaging) filter used after a digital-to-analog converter, nor with graphical anti-aliasing techniques used to smooth rendered lines and edges, though all share the goal of suppressing aliasing artefacts.
