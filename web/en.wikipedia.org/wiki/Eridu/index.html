Eridu

Eridu (modern Tell Abu Shahrain, Iraq) is regarded as the earliest city in southern Mesopotamia and, in Sumerian tradition, the first city in the world.

Eridu (Sumerian: 𒉌𒆠, NUN.KI; modern Tell Abu Shahrain in Dhi Qar Governorate, Iraq) was an ancient Sumerian city in southern Mesopotamia, some 24 km south-west of Ur. Sumerian tradition held it to be the oldest city in the world: the Sumerian King List opens with the words "When kingship descended from heaven, kingship was in Eridu." Archaeology confirms a very early settlement, founded around 5400 BCE in the Ubaid period, making Eridu among the earliest urban sites in the region.

The temple sequence

Excavations by Iraqi archaeologists between 1946 and 1949 uncovered a remarkable sequence of superimposed temples built on the same spot over millennia, from a small Ubaid-period shrine of one room — among the earliest known temples in Mesopotamia — through ever larger tripartite buildings, crowned in the Third Dynasty of Ur by the ziggurat of Amar-Sin. The continuity of sacred architecture at Eridu is a key source for the development of Mesopotamian temple design.

Religious significance

Eridu was the cult centre of Enki (Akkadian Ea), god of fresh water, wisdom and creation, whose temple was the E-Abzu, "house of the deep waters". Set on what was then the shore of the Persian Gulf amid marshes and lagoons, the city embodied the Sumerian conception of the abzu, the subterranean freshwater ocean. In myth, Eridu's first king was Alulim, who was said to have reigned 28,800 years before the great flood.

Decline

Although revered throughout Mesopotamian history and rebuilt by kings of Ur and Babylon, Eridu's political importance faded early; settlement shifted as the coastline receded and the Euphrates changed course. The city was finally abandoned in the sixth century BCE. The site today is a windswept mound with the stump of its ziggurat, surrounded by desert.
