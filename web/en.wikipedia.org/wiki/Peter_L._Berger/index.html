Peter L. Berger

Born: March 17, 1929, Vienna, Austria

Died: June 27, 2017, Brookline, Massachusetts, United States

Known for: Sociology of knowledge, sociology of religion, secularization debates

Peter Ludwig Berger (March 17, 1929 – June 27, 2017) was an Austrian-born American sociologist and Protestant theologian, known for his work on the sociology of knowledge and the sociology of religion, and for his contributions to sociological theory and the study of modernization.

Life and career

Born in Vienna, Berger emigrated to the United States after World War II and was educated at Wagner College and the New School for Social Research, where he earned his Ph.D. in 1954. He taught at several institutions, including the New School and Rutgers, before joining Boston University in 1981, where he founded and directed the Institute for the Study of Economic Culture (later the Institute on Culture, Religion and World Affairs, CURA).

Major works

With Thomas Luckmann, Berger wrote The Social Construction of Reality (1966), one of the most influential works in the sociology of knowledge, arguing that social reality is created and maintained through habitualization, institutionalization, and legitimation; it was ranked among the most important sociological books of the twentieth century by the International Sociological Association. His The Sacred Canopy (1967) developed a theory of religion as a world-maintaining "sacred canopy" protecting society from anomie, and advanced a version of secularization theory — a position he later substantially revised, arguing in works such as The Desecularization of the World (1999, as editor) that the world remained intensely religious, with pluralism rather than decline of faith being modernity's hallmark.

Other books include Invitation to Sociology (1963), a widely read humanistic introduction to the discipline; The Noise of Solemn Assemblies (1961); A Rumor of Angels (1969); Pyramids of Sacrifice (1974) and The Capitalist Revolution (1986) on development and modernization; and his memoir Adventures of an Accidental Sociologist (2011).
