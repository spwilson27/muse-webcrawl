Pas d'armes

The pas d'armes (French for "passage of arms") was a type of chivalric hastilude (martial game) that evolved during the late Middle Ages and remained popular through the 16th century. In a pas d'armes, a knight or group of knights (tenans, "holders") would stake out a site — a bridge, a road, a city gate, or a fountain — and declare that no knight could pass without fighting them; challengers were the venans ("comers").

Character

Unlike the unruly mêlée tournament, the pas d'armes was highly theatrical and romanced: events were staged around elaborate fictional frameworks drawn from Arthurian and classical literature, with defenders adopting personae, heralds reciting allegorical prologues, and ladies awarding prizes. Famous examples include the Pas de la Belle Pèlerine (1449), the Pas du Perron Fée at Bruges, and the extravagant events at the court of René of Anjou, whose treatise on the form of a tournament set out detailed rules.

Legacy

The pas d'armes represented the late flower of tournament culture at the Burgundian and French courts, blending sport, theatre and pageantry, before the decline of armoured combat and the fatal accident of Henry II of France in 1559 brought the great age of the hastilude to an end.
