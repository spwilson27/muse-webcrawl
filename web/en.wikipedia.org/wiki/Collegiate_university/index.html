Collegiate university

A college quadrangle at Oxford, the archetypal collegiate university.

A collegiate university is a university in which functions are divided between a central administration and a number of constituent colleges. The colleges are residential communities to which students and academics belong, and they typically provide accommodation, dining, pastoral care, libraries and much small-group teaching, while the central university confers degrees, runs examinations and maintains laboratories and major libraries.

Oxford and Cambridge

The archetypes are the Universities of Oxford and Cambridge, whose self-governing colleges (39 at Oxford, 31 at Cambridge) own their own property, elect their fellows and admit undergraduates, while the university organises lectures, examinations and degrees. College tutorials and supervisions remain central to their teaching.

Other examples

Durham University follows a similar model, as do Lancaster, York and Kent in part. Abroad, the University of Toronto, several universities in India, and residential college systems at Yale, Harvard (houses), Rice and some Australian universities adapt parts of the model, though usually with colleges less autonomous than those of Oxbridge.

Advantages and criticisms

Supporters cite community, pastoral support and interdisciplinary mixing within small colleges. Criticisms focus on duplicated administration, uneven college wealth and the complexity of admissions shared between college and university.
