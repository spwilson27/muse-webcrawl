Surströmming

Surströmming (pronounced [ˈsʉ̂ːˌʂʈrœmːɪŋ]; Swedish for 'sour herring') is a lightly salted, fermented Baltic Sea herring traditional to Swedish cuisine since at least the 16th century. It is known for its strong, pungent smell — among the most powerful of any food in the world — which results from the fermentation process taking place inside the sealed can.

History

Surströmming originates from the coastal regions of northern Sweden, particularly the High Coast of Norrland. One popular legend holds that Swedish sailors sold rotten herring to Finnish merchants, who returned the following year asking for more because it had tasted so good. More plausibly, the dish arose because the light salting used less salt than a full cure, allowing fermentation to begin and preserving the fish when salt was expensive. The herring is caught in spring, just before spawning, and fermented in barrels for one to two months before being canned in early July.

Production and chemistry

The fermentation relies on Haloanaerobium bacteria, which produce carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, propionic acid, butyric acid, and acetic acid, giving the fish its characteristic odour of rotten eggs, rancid butter, and vinegar. Because fermentation continues in the can, the tins often bulge; opening one releases pressurised, foul-smelling brine, so cans are traditionally opened outdoors and often underwater to contain the smell.

Culinary use

Surströmming is usually eaten in late August during the traditional surströmmingspremiär. It is commonly served in a klämma: a sandwich of thin flatbread (tunnbröd) with butter, boiled potatoes, diced onion, sour cream, and chives. It is accompanied by beer, snaps, or milk. Swedish food authorities have advised moderation because of dioxin and PCB levels in Baltic herring.
