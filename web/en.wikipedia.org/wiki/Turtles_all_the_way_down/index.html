Turtles all the way down

"Turtles all the way down" is an expression of the problem of infinite regress. The saying alludes to the mythological idea of a World Turtle that supports a flat Earth on its back. It suggests that this turtle rests on the back of an even larger turtle, which itself is part of a succession of increasingly large turtles that continues indefinitely.

The saying has origins in folklore and has been used in various contexts, from philosophy to cosmology, to illustrate the absurdity of arguments that rely on an infinite chain of justifications. A popular version of the story involves a lecturer (sometimes identified as the philosopher William James, sometimes the astronomer or the psychologist) giving a public talk on cosmology. Afterwards, an elderly lady in the audience reportedly told him that his theory of the Earth orbiting the Sun was wrong: the world is really a flat plate supported on the back of a giant tortoise. When asked what the tortoise stands on, she replied that it stands on the back of another tortoise — "it's turtles all the way down."

History

The phrase in its modern form dates to the mid-19th and early 20th centuries. In 1838, Edgar Allan Poe used a related image when critiquing a theory that space is filled with successive layers of "turtles." William James recounted the anecdote of the old woman and the turtles in his writings on pragmatism around 1906–1907, and the story was later retold, with variations, by figures including Bertrand Russell and the physicist Stephen Hawking, who opened his 1988 book A Brief History of Time with a version of the tale.

The underlying mythology is much older. Hindu cosmology features a World Turtle, sometimes named Akupāra or Kurma, supporting elephants that in turn bear the world. Similar ideas appear in Chinese and Native American traditions, where the world rests on the back of a great turtle or tortoise.

In philosophy

The expression is a staple of discussions of the regress argument: any proposition that requires justification must be justified by another proposition, and so on. Philosophers distinguish three broad responses — foundationalism (the chain stops at self-evident foundations), coherentism (justification comes from mutual support within a system), and infinitism (the chain genuinely continues without end). The turtle story is usually told to mock naive infinitism, although some philosophers have defended infinitism as a serious position.

In popular culture

The phrase has been borrowed widely: it is the title of a 2017 novel by John Green about a teenage detective with obsessive–compulsive disorder; it appears in songs by artists such as Sturgill Simpson; and it serves as a shorthand in science writing for any self-undermining or endlessly recursive explanation. Terry Pratchett's Discworld novels literalise the myth, with the world carried through space on the backs of four elephants standing on the giant turtle Great A'Tuin.
