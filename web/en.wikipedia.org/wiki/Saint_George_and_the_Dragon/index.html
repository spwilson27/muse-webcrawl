Saint George and the Dragon is a legend of Saint George, the soldier-saint martyred in 303, in which George tames and slays a dragon that demands human sacrifice, rescuing a princess chosen as its victim. The story became one of the most famous in Christian hagiography and a central image of medieval chivalry, reaching its best-known form in Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (c. 1260).

The legend

In the Golden Legend, a dragon made its nest by the spring supplying the city of Silene in Libya, and the citizens had to offer it two sheep daily, then children chosen by lot. When the lot fell on the king's daughter, George arrived, made the sign of the cross, and wounded the dragon with his lance (Ascalon). He had the princess bind the subdued beast with her girdle and lead it into the city, where he promised to kill it if the king and people accepted baptism — 15,000 were baptized, and George slew the dragon and refused the king's treasure, giving it to the poor.

Origins and interpretation

The dragon episode appears centuries after George's martyrdom, earliest in Georgian and Greek texts of the 11th–12th centuries, grafted onto the saint's cult and resonating with older dragon-slayer myths — Perseus rescuing Andromeda (whose scene was also set in Lydda's region, George's burial place) and the Archangel Michael fighting the dragon of Revelation. Crusaders popularized George as the patron of soldiers, and the red-cross banner of St George became the emblem of England, Genoa, Georgia and many other lands.

In art

Notable depictions include Paolo Uccello's Saint George and the Dragon (c. 1470) in the National Gallery, London, Raphael's panels in the Louvre and Washington, Vittore Carpaccio's cycle in Venice, and Bernat Martorell's altarpiece. The motif persists in the flags of Moscow and Georgia and in the British sovereign coinage designed by Benedetto Pistrucci (1817).
