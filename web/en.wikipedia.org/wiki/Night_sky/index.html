The night sky is the appearance of the sky at night — the celestial objects and phenomena visible from a location on Earth after sunset, including stars, planets, the Moon, constellations and the band of the Milky Way. Its darkness is explained by the expansion and finite age of the universe, a puzzle once posed as Olbers' paradox. The visibility of the night sky depends on atmospheric conditions, the phase of the Moon and, increasingly, artificial light pollution.

What can be seen

Under dark, moonless conditions the unaided eye can see roughly 2,500–5,000 stars at a given moment, plus five planets (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn), the Andromeda Galaxy, meteor showers, the glow of airglow and zodiacal light, and artificial satellites and the International Space Station in orbit. Twilight is divided into civil, nautical and astronomical stages as the Sun sinks to 6°, 12° and 18° below the horizon; only after astronomical twilight is the sky fully dark.

Light pollution

Artificial skyglow now hides the Milky Way from a large fraction of humanity. The Bortle scale classifies sky darkness from pristine (class 1) to inner-city (class 9). Dark-sky reserves and parks, certified by organisations such as the International Dark-Sky Association, protect remaining dark sites for astronomy, ecology and cultural heritage.

Cultural significance

The night sky shaped calendars, navigation, mythology and religion in every human culture; constellations and asterisms record those traditions. It remains central to amateur astronomy and astrophotography.
