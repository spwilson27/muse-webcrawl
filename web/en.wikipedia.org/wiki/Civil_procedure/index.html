Civil procedure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil procedure is the body of law that sets out the rules and standards courts follow when adjudicating civil lawsuits (as opposed to procedures in criminal law matters). It governs how a case is commenced, service of process, the kinds of claims and defences available (pleading), discovery (compelled exchange of information), trial, judgment, remedies, and appeal — the machinery meant to make substantive rights real, and whose frictions and costs often decide cases before substance is reached.

Design questions

Systems organise the process differently: notice pleading versus fact pleading (the US Supreme Court's Twombly/*Iqbal* plausibility turn, 2007–09, is the live controversy); broad party-driven discovery (a distinctive American feature enabling fishing and settlement leverage alike, versus judge-managed evidence-gathering in civil-law systems); the jury's civil role (shrinking in the US, absent elsewhere); and cost rules (the American rule — each side bears its own costs — versus loser-pays). Codifications like the US Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (1938) — designed by Charles Clark to merge law and equity into one form of action decided on the merits — are themselves periodically re-reformed as docket pressure, mass torts, arbitration clauses and litigation finance reshape what "civil justice" can deliver.
