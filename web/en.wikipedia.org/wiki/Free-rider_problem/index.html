Free-rider problem

Free riding
FieldEconomics, political science
Related conceptsPublic good, tragedy of the commons, collective action problem

In economics and political science, the free-rider problem is a type of market failure that occurs when those who benefit from resources, public goods or services of a communal nature do not pay for them or under-pay, even though they would continue to receive the benefit. Free riders may overuse common property resources, or the good may be under-provided, because potential contributors have an incentive to let others bear the cost while still enjoying the benefit.

The problem

A public good is non-excludable — people cannot realistically be prevented from using it — and non-rivalrous — one person's use does not reduce its availability to others. National defence, clean air, street lighting and public broadcasting are standard examples. Because an individual's contribution is usually a negligible fraction of the total cost, a rational actor may reason that their own payment makes little difference to whether the good is provided, and so choose not to contribute.

If enough people free ride, the good is under-funded or not provided at all, even when its total benefit to society far exceeds its cost. Mancur Olson analysed the logic of such collective action in The Logic of Collective Action (1965), showing that large groups are especially prone to free riding in the absence of selective incentives.

Examples

  • Public broadcasting: viewers or listeners who never donate during pledge drives still receive the programming funded by others.
  • Trade unions: in workplaces where a union negotiates for all employees, non-members receive the benefits of collective bargaining without paying dues.
  • Climate change mitigation: countries benefit from global emissions reductions whether or not they bear reduction costs themselves.
  • Wikipedia and open-source software: the great majority of users never contribute edits, content or donations.

Proposed solutions

Governments typically address the free-rider problem through compulsory taxation, converting voluntary contributions into mandatory ones. Other approaches include turning the good into a club good (making it excludable, e.g. toll roads or paywalls), assurance contracts and dominant assurance contracts, lotteries, social norms and reputational sanctions, and government subsidies. Experimental economics shows that communication, peer punishment and repeated interaction can substantially increase contributions in public-goods games.
