Hungarian irredentism or Greater Hungary (Hungarian: Nagy-Magyarország) is a set of political ideas concerned with the restoration of Hungary's historical borders, particularly those that existed before the Treaty of Trianon in 1920. Under Trianon, following the First World War, the Kingdom of Hungary lost roughly 71 percent of its territory and about 64 percent of its population, including some 3.3 million ethnic Hungarians who became minorities in neighbouring Romania, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia.

Interwar period

Revision of Trianon became the central goal of Hungarian foreign policy under Regent Miklós Horthy. The slogan "Nem, nem, soha!" ("No, no, never!") expressed refusal to accept the settlement, and irredentist themes pervaded education and public life. Seeking support for revision, Hungary aligned itself with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy.

Vienna Awards and Second World War

The First Vienna Award (1938) returned southern Slovakia and part of Carpathian Ruthenia to Hungary; the Second Vienna Award (1940) awarded Northern Transylvania from Romania. Hungary also occupied parts of Yugoslavia in 1941 and annexed further territory in Transcarpathia. These gains were reversed by the Paris Peace Treaties of 1947, which restored the Trianon borders (with a small additional loss to Czechoslovakia).

Contemporary situation

Ethnic Hungarian minorities remain significant in southern Slovakia, Romania's Székely Land and Serbia's Vojvodina. Successive Hungarian governments since 1989 have pursued minority-rights policies rather than border change, although symbolic gestures — such as the 2010 introduction of simplified naturalisation for ethnic Hungarians abroad and "National Unity Day" commemorating Trianon on 4 June — keep the issue politically salient. Maps of Greater Hungary appear in nationalist contexts and occasionally provoke diplomatic friction with neighbours.
