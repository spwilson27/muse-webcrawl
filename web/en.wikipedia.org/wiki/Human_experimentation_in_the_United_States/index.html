Human experimentation in the United States

Numerous human experiments in the United States have been conducted without the knowledge or consent of their subjects, many later judged unethical or illegal. Exposés of these programmes contributed to reforms culminating in federal regulations for the protection of human research subjects.

Notable cases

The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972) observed untreated syphilis in African American men who were denied effective treatment even after penicillin became available; its disclosure in 1972 prompted a presidential apology and new federal rules. During the Cold War, the CIA's MKUltra programme administered LSD and other substances to unwitting subjects, while government radiation experiments exposed patients, prisoners and service personnel to ionising radiation. Other contested studies include the Willowbrook hepatitis experiments on institutionalised children and the Holmesburg prison dermatology tests.

Reform

The National Research Act of 1974 created the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects, whose Belmont Report set the ethical foundations of the Common Rule governing federally funded research.
