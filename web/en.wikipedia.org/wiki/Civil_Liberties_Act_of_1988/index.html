Civil Liberties Act of 1988

The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 (Public Law 100-383) is a United States federal law that granted reparations to Japanese Americans who had been interned by the United States government during World War II. Signed by President Ronald Reagan on August 10, 1988, the act formally acknowledged that the internment was the product of "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership," and it appropriated funds for redress payments of $20,000 to each surviving internee, along with a public education fund.

Background

Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin D. Roosevelt issued Executive Order 9066 in February 1942, under which some 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry living on the West Coast — the majority of them American citizens — were forcibly removed from their homes and confined in internment camps for the duration of the war. After the war, former internees and their descendants organized a redress movement, led in part by the Japanese American Citizens League, seeking an official apology and monetary compensation.

In 1980 Congress established the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, whose 1983 report, Personal Justice Denied, concluded that the internment had not been justified by military necessity. The commission recommended a formal apology and payments of $20,000 to each surviving internee.

Legislative history

The bill was championed in the House of Representatives by Norman Mineta and Robert Matsui, both of whom had been interned as children, and in the Senate by Spark Matsunaga and Daniel Inouye. It passed the House in 1987 — on the 200th anniversary of the signing of the United States Constitution — and the Senate in 1988, before being signed by President Reagan, who remarked at the signing ceremony that "blood that has soaked into the sand of a beach is all of one color," quoting a Japanese American soldier killed in action.

Provisions and implementation

The act authorized $20,000 in tax-free payments to each eligible surviving internee and established the Civil Liberties Public Education Fund to finance educational programs about the internment. Because appropriations lagged behind authorization, payments did not begin until 1990, under President George H. W. Bush, when the oldest survivors received checks accompanied by a signed letter of apology. In total, more than 82,000 individuals received redress payments before the program concluded.

The act is regarded as a landmark in American civil rights history and a model cited in later debates over reparations for other historical injustices.
