Mast (botany)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In botany and ecology, mast is the fruit of forest trees and shrubs, such as acorns from oaks, beechnuts, chestnuts and other nuts, as well as the fruit of soft-fruited species, that accumulates on the ground and serves as food for wildlife and livestock. Historically the term also referred to the right to pasture swine in woodland to feed on fallen nuts, a practice known as pannage.

Mast seeding

Many tree species, including oaks and beeches, exhibit mast seeding (or masting): the highly variable, synchronised production of very large seed crops in some years — mast years — interspersed with years of little or no seed production. During a mast year, trees across a whole region may produce bumper crops simultaneously.

Ecological role

Masting is thought to be an evolved predator-satiation strategy: in lean years, populations of seed-eating animals are held low, while in mast years the sheer abundance of seed overwhelms consumers, allowing a large fraction of seeds to survive and germinate. Mast crops drive fluctuations in populations of rodents, deer, bears, wild boar and birds, and in turn affect the predators that feed on them; heavy acorn years, for example, can be followed by increases in mice, ticks and the incidence of tick-borne disease. Mast is also an important autumn food resource managed in forestry and wildlife management, and in some regions fallen acorns are still used to fatten pigs, notably in the production of jamón ibérico in Spain.
