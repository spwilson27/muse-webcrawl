Line (poetry)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldPoetics
Related termsStanza; verse; enjambment; caesura

A line is a unit of language into which a poem or play is divided. It is the basic compositional unit of most verse: a sequence of words printed as a single row, ending where the poet places a line break rather than where the page margin falls. Groups of lines form stanzas, and the line's length and rhythm are principal elements of metre.

Characteristics

The line is not the same as a sentence or clause: a single sentence may run over several lines, and a single line may contain several sentences. Where a line break coincides with a strong syntactic pause, the line is end-stopped; where the sense runs on into the next line, the effect is enjambment. A pause within a line is a caesura.

Traditional metres define lines by counted syllables and stresses — the five-stress iambic pentameter of Shakespeare and Milton, the fourteen-syllable line, the French alexandrine, and the short lines of ballad metre. In syllabic and quantitative traditions the count is of syllables or of long and short quantities respectively. Free verse abandons fixed metre but retains the authored line break as a tool of emphasis, pacing and visual form.

In performance and on the page

Because the line is simultaneously a visual unit and a unit of breath and rhythm, readers and performers must decide how far to honour line endings when a poem is read aloud. Modernist poets such as William Carlos Williams made the line itself — its breaks and steps down the page — a central expressive device.
