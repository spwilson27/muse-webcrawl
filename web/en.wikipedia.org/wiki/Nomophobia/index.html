Nomophobia


Labelled placeholder: mobile phone anxiety

Nomophobia is the fear of being without or unable to use one's mobile phone. The term, a portmanteau of "no mobile phone" and "phobia," was coined during a 2008 study by the UK Post Office, which commissioned YouGov to evaluate anxieties suffered by mobile phone users. The study found that nearly 53 percent of mobile phone users in Britain tend to be anxious when they "lose their mobile phone, run out of battery or credit, or have no network coverage."

Although nomophobia does not appear in the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), it has been proposed as a "specific phobia," based on definitions given in the DSM-IV. Symptoms include anxiety, respiratory alterations, trembling, perspiration, agitation, disorientation and tachycardia when separated from one's phone.

Overview

One's cell phone is a convenient tool for communication, data storage, and constant internet access; as the reliance on smartphones increases, so does the prevalence of nomophobia, especially among younger users. A study conducted in 2019 reported that almost 60% of people experience a moderate-to-high level of nomophobia. The fear ranges across four dimensions: not being able to communicate, losing connectedness, not being able to access information, and giving up convenience.

Signs and symptoms

Signs of nomophobia include keeping the phone on at all times (including during sleep), frequently checking notifications, charging the battery multiple times a day, experiencing panic when the phone is misplaced, and feeling phantom vibrations. Physically, sufferers may report sweating, trembling, increased heart rate and shortness of breath.

Epidemiology

Prevalence estimates vary widely by study and instrument, but college students and adolescents consistently show the highest rates. Studies in various countries have reported that large majorities of smartphone users exhibit at least mild nomophobia.

Treatment

Cognitive behavioral therapy and graduated exposure (periods without the phone) are commonly suggested. Digital-detox practices — scheduled phone-free time, notification management, and app-usage limits — may reduce symptoms.
