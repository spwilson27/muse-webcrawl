The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) is a 1998 United States copyright law that implements two 1996 treaties of the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO). It criminalises the circumvention of technological protection measures controlling access to copyrighted works, and provides "safe harbour" limitations on liability for online service providers that respond to notices of infringing material.

Anti-circumvention (Section 1201)

Title I makes it unlawful to circumvent a technological measure that effectively controls access to a copyrighted work, and to traffic in tools primarily designed for circumvention — regardless of whether any actual copyright infringement occurs. The provision has been applied to DVD encryption (the DeCSS cases), printer cartridges, garage-door openers and phone unlocking, and has been criticised for chilling security research and repair. Every three years, the Librarian of Congress grants temporary exemptions through a rulemaking process for classes of works such as jailbreaking phones, accessibility uses and repair of devices.

Safe harbours and takedown (Section 512)

Title II (the Online Copyright Infringement Liability Limitation Act) shields service providers from monetary liability if they, among other conditions, expeditiously remove or disable access to material upon receiving a compliant "takedown notice" from a copyright holder, and adopt a policy against repeat infringers. Users may file counter-notifications to have material restored. The notice-and-takedown system underlies the moderation of platforms such as YouTube, and has been both credited with enabling user-generated-content services and criticised for over-removal and abusive notices; knowingly false notices are actionable under §512(f).

Other provisions and legacy

Other titles address webcasting royalties, the liability of vessel hull designs, and miscellaneous copyright matters. The DMCA remains one of the most consequential internet laws: the European Union adopted similar rules in the 2001 Copyright Directive, and debates over its reform — over right to repair, interoperability and takedown abuse — continue.
