Joseph Cornell (24 December 1903 – 29 December 1972) was an American visual artist and filmmaker, one of the pioneers of assemblage. He is best known for his "shadow boxes": glass-fronted wooden boxes containing arrangements of found objects — old photographs, maps of the constellations, corks, clay pipes, cordial glasses, parrots and star charts — that evoke memory, astronomy, childhood and vanished European elegance.

Life

Cornell was born in Nyack, New York, and after his father's early death became the mainstay of his family, caring for his mother and his brother Robert, who had cerebral palsy. Self-taught as an artist, he lived almost his entire adult life in a modest house on Utopia Parkway in Flushing, Queens, working as a textile designer and illustrator while haunting New York's second-hand bookshops and junk stores for materials. Shy and reclusive, he rarely travelled, yet his work dreams of distant places and times.

Work

Inspired by Max Ernst's collages and the Surrealists, Cornell began making boxes in the 1930s and was included in the Museum of Modern Art's landmark exhibition Fantastic Art, Dada, Surrealism (1936). Recurring series include the Soap Bubble Sets, the Medici Slot Machine boxes, the Pharmacy boxes with ranks of bottles, and the Habitat boxes for birds. He often dedicated works to ballerinas, actresses and historical figures he admired from afar. Cornell also made experimental collage films, notably Rose Hobart (1936), assembled from a B-movie and now considered a landmark of found-footage cinema.

Legacy

Cornell's boxes influenced generations of artists, from Robert Rauschenberg to contemporary installation art, and major retrospectives were held at the Museum of Modern Art (1980) and the Smithsonian American Art Museum (2006–07). His meticulously kept source files and diaries are themselves studied as artworks of memory and obsession.
