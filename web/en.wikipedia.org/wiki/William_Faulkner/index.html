William Faulkner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: William Faulkner at his home Rowan Oak, Oxford, Mississippi, in the 1950s: the pipe-smoking Nobel laureate in late middle age]

William Cuthbert Faulkner (25 September 1897 – 6 July 1962) was an American writer and Nobel Prize laureate from Oxford, Mississippi. In a shelf of novels and stories set in the fictional Yoknapatawpha County — 'my own little postage stamp of native soil' — he chronicled the American South's haunted history of slavery, defeat and decline in prose of extraordinary density, compression and lyrical power.

His masterpieces include The Sound and the Fury (1929), As I Lay Dying (1930), Light in August (1932), Absalom, Absalom! (1936) and the story 'A Rose for Emily' (1930). Faulkner received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1949, his acceptance speech — on man's duty to endure and prevail — becoming one of the century's famous addresses.

Yoknapatawpha

Born William Falkner (he added the 'u' later), Faulkner was a Royal Air Force cadet in the First World War and a sometime postmaster (resigned, he said, refusing to be at the beck and call of moneyed people) before Sherwood Anderson encouraged his fiction in New Orleans. The Sound and the Fury's four fractured viewpoints on the Compson family announced his mature manner: time fractured, sentences unspooling across pages, the past never past.

Later career

Under-appreciated in America for years — Malcolm Cowley's Portable Faulkner (1946) revived him — Faulkner wrote Hollywood screenplays (including work on The Big Sleep), won two Pulitzer Prizes for the later Yoknapatawpha novels, and served as a writer-in-residence and cultural emissary. He died in 1962 after a fall from a horse.
