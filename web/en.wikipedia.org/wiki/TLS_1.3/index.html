ProtocolTransport Layer Security, version 1.3
SpecificationRFC 8446 (August 2018)
PredecessorTLS 1.2 (RFC 5246)

TLS 1.3 is the thirteenth version of the Transport Layer Security protocol, published as RFC 8446 in August 2018 after a four-year development process in the IETF TLS working group. It secures the great majority of modern web traffic via HTTPS and represents the most extensive redesign of the protocol since its SSL origins.

Changes from TLS 1.2

TLS 1.3 removed a long list of legacy features implicated in earlier attacks: static RSA and static Diffie–Hellman key exchange, RC4, CBC-mode cipher suites, MD5 and SHA-1 signatures, and compression. Static key exchange was eliminated entirely, so every connection uses ephemeral (elliptic-curve) Diffie–Hellman and gains forward secrecy — compromise of a server's long-term key cannot decrypt recorded traffic. Only five AEAD cipher suites remain, all based on AES-GCM, AES-CCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.

Handshake and performance

The handshake was shortened from two round trips to one: the client predicts the server's key-exchange parameters and sends its share in the first message, and the server's certificate and Finished message follow encrypted under keys derived from the exchange. Session resumption can use pre-shared keys, optionally with 0-RTT early data, in which the client sends application data with its first message — a major latency win, at the price of replay-attack risk that must be managed by applications.

Deployment

All major browsers and TLS libraries (OpenSSL 1.1.1+, BoringSSL, NSS, SChannel) support TLS 1.3, and large content-delivery networks enabled it within months of standardization; by the early 2020s it accounted for the majority of HTTPS connections. Middlebox intolerance during rollout forced the protocol to adopt a "compatibility mode" that mimics a TLS 1.2 session-resumption handshake on the wire.
