Sound localization

Sound localization is a listener's ability to identify the location or origin of a detected sound in direction and distance. The auditory system uses multiple cues to determine where a sound comes from, in both the horizontal (azimuth) and vertical (elevation) planes.

Binaural cues

The two principal cues for horizontal localization are the interaural time difference (ITD) — the small delay between a sound reaching the nearer and farther ear, most effective at low frequencies — and the interaural level difference (ILD), caused by the head shadowing high-frequency sounds. This division of labour is described by the duplex theory.

Spectral cues and ambiguities

Vertical localization and front–back discrimination depend on spectral shaping by the outer ear (pinna), described by head-related transfer functions (HRTFs). Sounds lying on the "cone of confusion" produce identical binaural cues, so head movements and pinna cues help resolve them. Animals such as barn owls have specialised neural maps for sound location, and the same principles are exploited in microphone-array and robot-hearing systems.
