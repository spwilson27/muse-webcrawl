Flying ace

A flying ace, fighter ace, or air ace is a military aviator credited with shooting down five or more enemy aircraft during aerial combat. The term first came into use during the First World War, when French newspapers described Adolphe Pégoud as "l'as" (the ace) after he became the first pilot to down five German aircraft.

First World War

The concept of the ace emerged alongside fighter aviation in 1915–1916. Germany's system of honours popularized aces such as Oswald Boelcke and Max Immelmann, and later Manfred von Richthofen, the "Red Baron", who remains the highest-scoring ace of the war with 80 victories. Allied aces included René Fonck of France (75 victories), Billy Bishop of Canada (72), and Edward "Mick" Mannock and James McCudden of Britain. Aces were celebrated as national heroes and featured heavily in wartime propaganda.

Second World War

The far larger air battles of the Second World War produced much higher scores, particularly on the Eastern Front. Erich Hartmann of Germany is the highest-scoring fighter pilot in history with 352 victories. Finnish, Japanese, Soviet, British, and American forces all produced notable aces, including Richard Bong, the top American ace with 40 victories. Standards for confirming victories varied widely between air forces, so scores are not strictly comparable.

Later conflicts

Aces became rarer after 1945 as air combat grew less frequent and missiles replaced guns. Small numbers of pilots reached ace status in the Korean War, the Vietnam War, and the Arab–Israeli wars, where Israeli pilots recorded the highest jet-age totals.
