Sleepy Lagoon murder

The Sleepy Lagoon murder was the name that Los Angeles newspapers used to describe the death of José Gallardo Díaz, whose body was found on 2 August 1942 near a swimming hole — a reservoir locals called the Sleepy Lagoon — in southeast Los Angeles. The ensuing mass trial of Mexican-American youths became a landmark civil-rights case and a precursor to the Zoot Suit Riots of 1943.

The trial

Police rounded up more than 600 young Mexican Americans; 22 members of the so-called "38th Street gang" were indicted for the murder and tried jointly in People v. Zammora. The proceedings were marred by racial bias: defendants were denied haircuts and clean clothes, were kept separated from counsel, and the prosecution relied on prejudicial testimony about Mexican "bloodlust". In January 1943, twelve defendants were convicted of murder and five of assault.

Appeal and aftermath

A defence committee led by activist Alice McGrath, with support from figures including Carey McWilliams and Orson Welles, appealed. In October 1944 the Second District Court of Appeal unanimously reversed the convictions, citing insufficient evidence and the trial judge's bias and misconduct. Díaz's death itself was never conclusively explained.

Legacy

The case exposed systemic discrimination against Mexican Americans in Los Angeles and fed tensions that erupted in the Zoot Suit Riots. Luis Valdez's 1979 play Zoot Suit and its 1981 film adaptation retell the story of lead defendant Henry "Hank" Leyvas and the trial.
