Samuel Dashiell Hammett (May 27, 1894 – January 10, 1961) was an American writer of hardboiled detective novels and short stories. He was also a screenwriter and political activist. Among the enduring characters he created are Sam Spade (The Maltese Falcon), Nick and Nora Charles (The Thin Man), and the Continental Op (Red Harvest and The Dain Curse).

Life and career

Hammett left school at 13 and worked for the Pinkerton National Detective Agency from 1915 to 1922, experience that gave his fiction its authenticity. Suffering from tuberculosis contracted during First World War service, he turned to writing for pulp magazines, chiefly Black Mask, in the 1920s. Between 1929 and 1934 he published five novels that redefined crime fiction with their terse, detached prose style, in which characters are revealed by action and dialogue rather than introspection. Raymond Chandler, his great successor, wrote that "Hammett gave murder back to the kind of people that commit it for reasons, not just to provide a corpse."

Later years

After The Thin Man (1934) Hammett published no further novels, working intermittently in Hollywood and devoting himself to left-wing political activism with his partner, the playwright Lillian Hellman. In 1951 he was jailed for contempt of court after refusing to answer questions about a bail fund for convicted Communists, and he was blacklisted during the McCarthy era. He died of lung cancer in 1961 and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery. His influence on successors like James M. Cain and on the development of film noir is widely acknowledged.
