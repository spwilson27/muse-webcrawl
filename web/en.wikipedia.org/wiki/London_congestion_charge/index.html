London congestion charge

Introduced: 17 February 2003

Area: Central London

Operator: Transport for London

Hours: Weekdays and weekends, daytime/evening (varying by period)

The London congestion charge is a fee charged on most cars and motor vehicles driven within the Congestion Charge Zone in central London between specified hours. Introduced on 17 February 2003 under Mayor Ken Livingstone, it was one of the first and largest congestion charging schemes in the world, modelled in part on Singapore's Electronic Road Pricing system, and is administered by Transport for London (TfL).

Operation

Drivers must pay a daily charge (raised in steps from £5 at introduction to £15 in 2020) for each day their vehicle travels within the zone during charging hours. The zone is enforced by automatic number-plate recognition cameras. Residents receive a large discount, and certain vehicles — including buses, emergency vehicles, and (at various times) ultra-low-emission vehicles — are exempt or discounted. Non-payment results in penalty charge notices.

Effects and expansion

In its first years, the charge reduced traffic entering the zone by roughly 15–30 percent, cut congestion substantially, and raised net revenue (by law devoted to London's transport) of over £100 million a year. A western extension added in 2007 was removed at the end of 2010 after a change of mayor. The charge zone later interacted with wider schemes: the Low Emission Zone, the Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ, introduced 2019 and later expanded across Greater London), and a temporary widening of hours during the COVID-19 period. The London scheme influenced congestion pricing proposals worldwide, including those eventually adopted in Stockholm, Milan, and New York City.
