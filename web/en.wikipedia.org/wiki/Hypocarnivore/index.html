Hypocarnivore

A hypocarnivore is an animal whose diet consists of less than 30% meat, with the remainder made up of plant material such as fruit, leaves, roots, fungi, and other non-animal foods. The term belongs to a scheme classifying animals by the proportion of meat in their diet: hypocarnivores eat less than 30% meat, mesocarnivores eat 30–70%, and hypercarnivores eat more than 70%.

Examples

Many animals popularly called "carnivores" because of their taxonomic order (Carnivora) are in fact hypocarnivores. Examples include most bears — such as the American black bear and the grizzly bear — whose diets are dominated by vegetation, berries, nuts, and insects, with meat varying seasonally. The kinkajou is an almost entirely frugivorous member of Carnivora. Raccoons, coatis, and many canids such as the maned wolf also fall into this category. Humans, with traditional mixed diets, are sometimes described as hypocarnivores or mesocarnivores depending on the population studied.

Morphology

Hypocarnivores generally retain the simple digestive tract of their carnivorous ancestors — a relatively short gut unsuited to fermenting large volumes of cellulose — but show dental adaptations toward grinding: broader molars with flatter cusps, and reduced emphasis on the shearing carnassial teeth that dominate in hypercarnivores. In the fossil record, dietary category can be inferred from tooth shape, jaw mechanics, and stable isotope analysis.

Ecological role

Because they exploit abundant plant resources while remaining able to take animal prey, hypocarnivores are often ecologically flexible generalists. Many are important seed dispersers, consuming fruit and depositing seeds across their home ranges.
