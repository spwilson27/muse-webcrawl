Coastal defence ship

Coastal defence ships (sometimes called coast defence ships or coastal battleships) were warships built for the purpose of coastal defence, most popular in the period from the 1860s to the 1920s and built mainly by smaller naval powers. They were typically smaller and cheaper than true battleships, with shallower draught, heavier armour and armament relative to their size, limited speed, and short cruising range — all acceptable compromises for vessels intended to operate near their own coasts, in littoral waters, rather than with an ocean-going battle fleet.

Characteristics

The type descended from the monitors and coastal ironclads of the 1860s. Typical coastal defence ships displaced between 3,000 and 8,000 tons, carried two to four heavy guns of up to 283 mm (11 in) calibre in turrets, and were protected by a full armoured belt, yet made only 15–17 knots and had modest coal endurance. Their shallow draught suited them for the archipelagos and skerries of the Baltic and Scandinavian coasts, where they could shelter among islands and use knowledge of local waters against a stronger invader.

Operators

The most enthusiastic users were the Scandinavian navies. Sweden built a long series of pansarskepp ("armoured ships"), culminating in the three Sverige-class ships of 1915–1924, which remained in service into the 1950s. Norway operated the Tordenskjold and Eidsvold classes; the two Eidsvold-class ships were sunk by German destroyers at Narvik in 1940. Denmark's Herluf Trolle class and the large Niels Juel also belonged to the type. Finland's Väinämöinen and Ilmarinen served in the Winter War and Continuation War; Ilmarinen was lost to a mine in 1941 with heavy loss of life. The Netherlands built several coastal defence ships (such as De Zeven Provinciën) for home waters and the East Indies, Greece bought the ex-American pre-dreadnoughts Kilkis and Lemnos, and Thailand commissioned the Japanese-built Thonburi class in the 1930s, which fought the French at the Battle of Ko Chang in 1941.

Decline

After the First World War few new coastal defence ships were laid down: aircraft and submarines increasingly took over the coastal defence role, and the Washington Naval Treaty era favoured cruisers for limited budgets. The surviving ships mostly served through the Second World War in secondary roles and were scrapped in the 1950s and 1960s.
