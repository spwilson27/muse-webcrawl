Lyon is a city in east-central France, the prefecture of the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region, situated at the confluence of the Rhône and Saône rivers. It is the third-largest city in France and a major centre for banking, chemicals, pharmaceuticals and biotechnology, as well as a historic capital of gastronomy.

History

Founded by the Romans in 43 BC as the colony of Lugdunum, Lyon became the capital of the Gauls and one of the most important cities of the western Roman Empire; emperors Claudius and Caracalla were born there. In the Renaissance, Lyon flourished as a banking and printing centre and through trade fairs, and its silk industry — organised around the canut weavers of Croix-Rousse — dominated the city's economy in the 18th and 19th centuries. The canuts' revolts of 1831 and 1834 were among the earliest industrial workers' uprisings. During the Second World War Lyon was a centre of the French Resistance, led in part by Jean Moulin.

Geography and sights

The city is built around two hills — Fourvière ("the hill that prays", crowned by its 19th-century basilica) and Croix-Rousse ("the hill that works") — and the Presqu'île peninsula between the rivers. The old town (Vieux Lyon), with its Renaissance courtyards and covered passageways (traboules), the Roman theatres of Fourvière, and the modern Confluence district are part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site inscribed in 1998.

Culture

Lyon is celebrated as France's gastronomic capital, home of Paul Bocuse and of the traditional bouchon bistros serving dishes such as quenelles and andouillette. The Lumière brothers pioneered cinema here in 1895, commemorated by the Institut Lumière, and the Fête des Lumières each December draws millions of visitors. Olympique Lyonnais is the city's leading football club.
