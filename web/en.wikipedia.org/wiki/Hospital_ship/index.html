Hospital ship

Hospital ships are painted white and marked with red crosses under the Geneva Conventions.

A hospital ship is a vessel designated for the primary function of a floating medical treatment facility or hospital. Most are operated by the military forces of various countries, and under the Geneva Conventions they enjoy protected status in wartime: they must be painted white, marked with large red crosses, notified to belligerents, and used exclusively for caring for the sick and wounded, on pain of losing that protection.

History

Dedicated hospital ships appeared in the age of sail — the Royal Navy used them from the 17th century — and became prominent in the Crimean War and American Civil War. Converted liners served in vast numbers in both world wars; the sinking of HMHS Britannic by a mine in 1916 is among the best known losses. Post-war examples include the United States Navy's Mercy-class vessels, converted supertankers with a thousand beds, and China's Peace Ark.

Legal status

The Second Geneva Convention of 1949 governs hospital ships. They may not be attacked or captured, must render assistance to the wounded of all nationalities, may not be used for military purposes or hamper the movements of combatants, and may be searched by belligerents to verify compliance.

Civilian use

Charitable organisations also operate hospital ships, such as Mercy Ships' vessels, which deliver surgical care to port communities in the developing world, and hospital ships serve in disaster relief and humanitarian missions.
