Wild turkey

The wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) is an upland game bird native to North America, one of two extant turkey species and a member of the order Galliformes. It is the ancestor of the domestic turkey. Although native to North America, the turkey probably received its English name from the mistaken belief that it came from Turkey.

Description

Adult wild turkeys have long dark, iridescent plumage, a large fan-shaped tail, long reddish-orange to greyish-blue legs, and a bald, brightly coloured head. Males, called toms or gobblers, are substantially larger than females and sport a "beard" of coarse modified feathers on the chest. Despite their bulk — wild turkeys can weigh over 11 kg (24 lb) — they are agile, surprisingly fast fliers and runners.

Distribution and conservation

Wild turkeys range across the United States, Mexico, and southern Canada, inhabiting forests, woodlands, and mixed habitats. By the early 20th century, overhunting and habitat loss had reduced their numbers to as few as 100,000 birds. Conservation efforts — funded largely by hunting licences and excise taxes on sporting equipment, and based on trapping wild birds for reintroduction — restored the population to several million, making the wild turkey a celebrated conservation success story.

Behaviour

Turkeys are omnivorous ground feeders, eating acorns, nuts, seeds, fruits, insects, and small reptiles and amphibians. They roost in trees at night. Males are polygamous, attracting females with gobbling calls and elaborate strutting displays in spring.
