Diffusion model

In machine learning, diffusion models, also known as diffusion probabilistic models or score-based generative models, are a class of latent variable generative models. A diffusion model consists of three major components: a forward process (or diffusion process) that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data, a learned reverse process that progressively removes the noise to reconstruct data from pure noise, and a sampling procedure. They are used commercially by DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion and Midjourney.

Overview

Training data are corrupted stepwise over many timesteps according to a fixed noise schedule until they become indistinguishable from isotropic Gaussian noise. A neural network — commonly a U-Net — is trained to predict the noise (or the score) at each step, so that sampling can start from random noise and iteratively denoise into a new data sample. Conditioning, for example on a text prompt via cross-attention and CLIP embeddings, turns the model into a text-to-image generator; latent diffusion models perform the process in a compressed latent space to reduce cost.

History

The idea originated with Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015), drawing on non-equilibrium statistical physics. It was popularised by Ho, Jain and Abbeel's denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM, 2020) and subsequent work on score-based models and improved samplers such as DDIM, and from 2022 diffusion models surpassed GANs as the leading approach to image synthesis.
