Galaxy supercluster

A supercluster is a large group of smaller galaxy clusters or galaxy groups; they are among the largest known structures in the universe. The Milky Way is part of the Local Group galaxy group (which contains more than 54 galaxies), which in turn is part of the Virgo Cluster; the Virgo Cluster is part of the Laniakea Supercluster. The large size and low density of superclusters means that they, unlike clusters, expand with the Hubble expansion.

Structure

Superclusters typically span several hundred million light-years and contain tens of thousands of galaxies grouped into clusters and filaments separated by vast, nearly empty voids. Their shapes are irregular, often elongated chains or flattened sheets, and they form part of the cosmic web — the largest-scale organisation of matter in the universe. Because their densities are only modestly above the cosmic mean, superclusters are generally not gravitationally bound as a whole, though their constituent clusters may be.

Notable superclusters

  • Laniakea Supercluster — defined in 2014 by the flow lines of galaxies toward the Great Attractor; contains the Local Group, roughly 100,000 galaxies, and spans about 520 million light-years.
  • Virgo Supercluster — the older, smaller definition centred on the Virgo Cluster, now regarded as a lobe of Laniakea.
  • Shapley Supercluster — one of the most massive nearby concentrations, in the direction of Centaurus.
  • Horologium-Reticulum, Perseus-Pisces, Coma and Hercules superclusters — other prominent nearby structures mapped by redshift surveys.

Discovery and mapping

The existence of clustering beyond galaxy clusters was recognised from the work of de Vaucouleurs in the 1950s; large redshift surveys such as the CfA survey (which revealed the "Great Wall" in 1989) and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey mapped superclusters in three dimensions.
