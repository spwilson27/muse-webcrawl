Judgment (law)

A judgment is the formal final determination of the rights and obligations of the parties in a lawsuit, entered by a court. A judgment resolves the dispute and determines obligations, including whether a party must pay damages or perform (or refrain from) particular acts. In U.S. federal practice, "judgment" as defined in Rule 54(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure includes any decree and any order from which an appeal lies.

Types

  • Default judgment — for failure to plead or defend (Rule 55).
  • Summary judgment — Rule 56, where there is no genuine dispute of material fact.
  • Judgment as a matter of law / directed verdict, and judgment notwithstanding the verdict.
  • Consent judgment, declaratory judgment, and judgments on the pleadings (Rule 12(c)).

Entry and effect

Judgment is entered by the clerk, often on a separate document under Rule 58, starting appeal deadlines. A final judgment is generally res judicata — it bars relitigation of the same claim — and may be enforced by execution, garnishment or lien. Judgments may be appealed, or challenged by motion for new trial or relief from judgment under Rule 60(b) for mistake, newly discovered evidence, fraud, or voidness. A settlement usually avoids judgment.

Criminal judgments

In criminal cases, judgment is the pronouncement of conviction and sentence after a guilty plea or verdict.
