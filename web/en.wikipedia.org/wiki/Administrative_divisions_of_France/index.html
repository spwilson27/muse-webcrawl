Administrative divisions of France

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The administrative divisions of France form a hierarchical system of territorial units through which the French Republic is governed. France is a unitary state, but its subnational entities — regions, departments and communes — are decentralised territorial collectivities (collectivités territoriales) with elected councils and defined powers, alongside deconcentrated services of the central state headed by prefects.

Regions

Since the territorial reform that took effect on 1 January 2016, metropolitan France is divided into 13 regions, with five additional overseas regions (Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana, Réunion and Mayotte), for a total of 18. Regions are responsible for areas such as economic development, regional transport, lycées (secondary schools) and vocational training. The largest region by area is Nouvelle-Aquitaine; the most populous is Île-de-France, which contains Paris.

Departments

Below the regions are the departments, created in 1790 during the French Revolution. There are 101 departments, including five overseas departments which double as regions. Each is administered by an elected departmental council and a prefect representing the state, and is identified by a number used in postal codes and vehicle registration plates — 75 for Paris, for example. Departments manage social welfare, collèges (lower secondary schools), departmental roads and fire services.

Arrondissements, cantons and communes

Departments are subdivided into arrondissements (about 330), which group communes for state administration, and cantons, used mainly as electoral constituencies. The base unit is the commune, of which there are roughly 35,000 — more than in any other European country. Communes range from Paris, with over two million inhabitants, to villages with only a handful of residents, and are run by a mayor and municipal council responsible for civil registration, primary schools, local roads and town planning.

Overseas collectivities

Beyond the overseas regions and departments, France includes overseas collectivities such as French Polynesia, New Caledonia (a special-status collectivity), Saint Pierre and Miquelon, Wallis and Futuna, and Saint Martin and Saint Barthélemy, as well as the French Southern and Antarctic Lands.
