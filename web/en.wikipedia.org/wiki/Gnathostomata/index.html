Gnathostomata (from Greek gnathos, "jaw", and stoma, "mouth") is the group of vertebrates with jaws. It includes all familiar vertebrates: cartilaginous fishes (sharks, rays and chimaeras), bony fishes, and the tetrapods — amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals — totalling more than 60,000 living species, over 99% of all vertebrates. Gnathostomes are distinguished from the jawless fishes (lampreys and hagfish, the Agnatha) by jaws derived evolutionarily from the frontmost pharyngeal (gill) arches.

Characteristics

Beyond jaws, gnathostomes share a suite of innovations: paired appendages (fins or limbs) supported by girdles, three semicircular canals in the inner ear (rather than two), teeth with dentine and enamel in most groups, and an adaptive immune system based on immunoglobulins and T-cell receptors. Jaws transformed feeding, enabling active predation and manipulation of food, and are often described as one of the key innovations in vertebrate evolution.

Evolution

The earliest gnathostomes appear in the late Ordovician to Silurian fossil record (c. 460–440 million years ago), initially known from scales and fragments. The armoured placoderms dominated Devonian seas, and recent fossil finds from China, such as Entelognathus, have reshaped understanding of how the modern jawed-vertebrate body plan — including the marginal jaw bones of bony fishes and land vertebrates — was assembled. The two living great clades, Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes, had diverged by the late Silurian. See also vertebrate and chordate.
