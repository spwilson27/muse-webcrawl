The Trifid Nebula (catalogued as Messier 20 or M20, and as NGC 6514) is an H II region in the north-west of the constellation Sagittarius. It is an unusual combination of an open cluster of stars, an emission nebula, a reflection nebula, and a dark nebula. The name "Trifid" refers to the three dark dust lanes that appear to divide the bright emission portion of the nebula into three lobes.

Characteristics

The nebula lies about 4,100 light-years from the Sun (estimates range roughly from 2,700 to 5,200 light-years) and spans around 40 light-years across. The reddish emission nebula is ionized by a hot, massive multiple star system at its centre (HD 164492), whose ultraviolet light excites the surrounding hydrogen gas. To the north lies a distinctly blue reflection nebula, lit by a separate group of bright stars whose light is scattered by dust grains — the colour difference arises because the illuminating stars are cooler than the emission region's central star. The dark lanes are dense Bok globules and a radial "spoke" of dust silhouetted against the glowing gas.

Star formation

The Trifid is a stellar nursery: infrared observations have revealed hundreds of young stellar objects, protostars, and evaporating gaseous globules within it. In 2005, NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope images showed the nebula as a dynamic environment in which radiation from the massive central stars triggers new generations of star formation in the surrounding cold clouds, with dozens of embryonic stars embedded in the dust.

Observation

M20 is a popular target for amateur astronomers in summer skies of the northern hemisphere (winter in the southern). Visible in binoculars as a hazy patch near the brighter Lagoon Nebula (M8) about 2° away, it resolves in small telescopes into its emission and reflection components, with the trisecting dark lanes requiring darker skies and moderate aperture. It was discovered by Charles Messier on 5 June 1764, and the "trifid" description is credited to John Herschel.
