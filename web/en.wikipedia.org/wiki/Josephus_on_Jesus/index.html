Josephus on Jesus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian whose works mention Jesus.

The extant works of the first-century Romano-Jewish historian Josephus include two passages referring, or possibly referring, to Jesus of Nazareth. The best known is the Testimonium Flavianum in Book 18 of the Antiquities of the Jews, written around AD 93–94. A second, shorter passage in Book 20 mentions "James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ".

The Testimonium Flavianum

The Testimonium Flavianum, in the form found in surviving Greek manuscripts, reads in part:

About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if indeed one ought to call him a man. For he was one who performed surprising deeds and was a teacher of such people as accept the truth gladly. He won over many Jews and many of the Greeks. He was the Christ. And when, upon the accusation of the principal men among us, Pilate had condemned him to a cross, those who had first come to love him did not cease.

The passage is famous and controversial. Most scholars hold that it contains an authentic core written by Josephus but that it was later altered or expanded by Christian copyists; a minority regard it as wholly interpolated, and a smaller minority defend it as entirely authentic. Evidence cited in the debate includes a tenth-century Arabic version quoted by Agapius of Hierapolis and a Syriac version in Michael the Syrian, both of which lack some of the more explicitly Christian phrases.

The James passage

In Book 20 of the Antiquities, Josephus describes how the high priest Ananus convened a council and executed "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James". Unlike the Testimonium, this passage is widely regarded as authentic: it is brief, incidental, and consistent with Josephus's style, and it is quoted by Origen in the third century.

Scholarly significance

Because Josephus was a non-Christian Jewish author writing within living memory of Jesus's lifetime, the passages are among the most discussed pieces of extra-biblical evidence for the historical Jesus, and they feature prominently in studies of first-century Judea and Christian origins.
