Chrestomathy


Placeholder image: a chrestomathy volume

A chrestomathy (from Greek khrēstomatheia, "desire of learning", from khrēstos, "useful", and mathein, "to learn") is a selection of passages from an author or literature, assembled for the purpose of study — most often the study of a language. A chrestomathy typically presents extracts in the target language, often graded in difficulty and supplied with notes, glossaries and grammatical apparatus.

Use

Chrestomathies have been standard tools for learning classical and less commonly taught languages since antiquity: Proclus wrote a Chrestomathy summarising the Greek epic cycle, and the form was revived in the 18th and 19th centuries for languages such as Arabic, Sanskrit, Armenian and the Slavic languages. A famous English-language example is H. L. Mencken's A Mencken Chrestomathy (1949), a selection of the author's own writings.

In comparative philology, a "chrestomathy" for a language family or a dead language may be the principal published corpus from which students learn to read its texts.

Related forms

The chrestomathy differs from an anthology in its pedagogical purpose: selections are chosen for their value in learning the language or literature, not only for literary merit. A reader or coursebook for language learners is a modern descendant of the form.
