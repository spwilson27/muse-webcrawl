Mountain warfare is warfare conducted in mountains or similarly rough terrain. It is one of the most demanding forms of combat: steep slopes, narrow passes, extreme weather, thin air at altitude, and poor transport infrastructure constrain movement, supply, communications, and medical evacuation, while the terrain favours the defender.

Characteristics

In mountainous country, control of the high ground and of passes and valley roads is usually decisive. Vehicles are road-bound, artillery ranges and observation are distorted by the terrain, and units often depend on pack animals, porters, helicopters, and cableways for supply. Cold, snow, avalanches, and altitude sickness routinely cause more casualties than enemy action; historically, the Italian Front of the First World War saw enormous losses to avalanche and frostbite in the Alps.

History

Famous episodes of mountain warfare include Hannibal's crossing of the Alps in 218 BC, the campaigns of the Napoleonic Wars in the Alps and Pyrenees, the First World War fighting between Italy and Austria-Hungary along the Isonzo and in the Dolomites, the Caucasus and Italian campaigns of the Second World War, the Indo-Pakistani fighting in Kashmir including the Kargil War of 1999 at altitudes above 5,000 metres, and the Soviet and NATO wars in Afghanistan's Hindu Kush.

Mountain troops

Many armies maintain specialised mountain infantry trained in climbing, skiing, and survival, such as the German Gebirgsjäger, the French Chasseurs Alpins, the Italian Alpini — the oldest mountain infantry formation, founded in 1872 — and the United States Army's 10th Mountain Division.
