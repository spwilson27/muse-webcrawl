Heracleion (Ancient Greek: Ἡράκλειον), also known by its Egyptian name Thonis (Θῶνις), was an ancient Egyptian port city near the Canopic mouth of the Nile, about 32 km northeast of Alexandria. For centuries it was Egypt's principal port for trade with the Greek world, before being eclipsed by Alexandria. Its ruins, submerged beneath Abu Qir Bay, were rediscovered in 2000 by underwater archaeologist Franck Goddio.

History

Legend held that Heracles visited the city — hence its Greek name — and that Paris and Helen of Troy sheltered there before the Trojan War. Thonis-Heracleion flourished from the 6th to the 4th century BC: as Egypt's official port of entry, it levied customs on Greek and Phoenician shipping at the mouth of the Canopic branch of the Nile. Its great temple of Amun (Khonsu to the Greeks, equated with Heracles) was a centre of religious festivals, including the "mysteries of Osiris" procession in which a ceremonial boat carried the god from Heracleion to nearby Canopus.

The city's importance declined after Alexander the Great founded Alexandria in 331 BC, though it remained inhabited into late antiquity.

Submersion and rediscovery

Built on the soft, unstable clays of the Nile Delta, Heracleion slowly sank as a result of soil liquefaction, subsidence, sea-level rise and seismic events; by the end of the 8th century AD it lay beneath the sea, eventually under some 10 metres of water and sediment.

Goddio's team, working with the Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities from 2000, mapped the city and raised spectacular finds: colossal statues of Hapy and of a Ptolemaic queen, a black granite stele inscribed with a decree of Nectanebo I — a near-duplicate of the famous Canopus Decree — gold coins and jewellery, ritual instruments, and dozens of shipwrecks, including ceremonial barques. Many artefacts were exhibited internationally in the touring exhibition "Osiris, Egypt's Sunken Mysteries."
