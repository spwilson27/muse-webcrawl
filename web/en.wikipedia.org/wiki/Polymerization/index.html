Polymerization

In polymer chemistry, polymerization (American English), or polymerisation (British English), is a process of reacting monomer molecules together in a chemical reaction to form polymer chains or three-dimensional networks. There are many forms of polymerization, and different systems exist to categorize them. Polymerization is the fundamental process by which plastics, synthetic rubbers, and many other materials are produced, as well as the process by which biological macromolecules such as proteins, DNA, and cellulose are assembled in nature.

Classification

Polymerizations are conventionally divided into two broad mechanistic classes:

Step-growth polymerization

In step-growth polymerization, bifunctional or multifunctional monomers react to form first dimers, then trimers, longer oligomers, and eventually long polymer chains. Any two molecules present in the system can react with each other at any time, and high molecular weights are reached only at very high conversions. Condensation polymerization is a form of step-growth polymerization in which a small molecule, often water, is eliminated at each step; examples include the formation of nylon and polyester. Polyaddition, in which monomer units add without by-product elimination (as in polyurethane formation), is also a step-growth process.

Chain-growth polymerization

In chain-growth polymerization, unsaturated monomer molecules add onto the growing polymer chain one at a time through an active centre, which may be a free radical, a cation, an anion, or a coordination complex. Growth occurs only at the active centre, so high-molecular-weight polymer forms immediately even at low monomer conversion. Important examples include the free-radical polymerization of ethylene to polyethylene and of styrene to polystyrene, ionic polymerizations, and Ziegler–Natta coordination polymerization used for polypropylene.

Kinetics and molecular weight

The rate and resulting molecular weight distribution of a polymerization depend on temperature, initiator or catalyst concentration, monomer concentration, and the relative rates of initiation, propagation, chain transfer, and termination. In living polymerization, termination and transfer are absent, so chains grow uniformly and narrow molecular-weight distributions are obtained; such techniques underpin block copolymer synthesis and modern controlled radical methods such as ATRP and RAFT polymerization.

Industrial methods

Industrial polymerizations are carried out in bulk (mass), solution, suspension, or emulsion. Emulsion polymerization, used for latex paints and synthetic rubber latices, offers good heat removal and high molecular weights. Interfacial polymerization, conducted at the boundary between two immiscible phases, is used for polycarbonates and aramids.

History

The term "polymer" was introduced by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1833, and Hermann Staudinger's macromolecular hypothesis of the 1920s established that polymers consist of very long covalent chains, a view that earned him the 1953 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Wallace Carothers at DuPont systematized condensation polymerization in the 1930s, leading to nylon; Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta won the 1963 Nobel Prize for coordination catalysts.
