Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants

Stockholm ConventionSigned: 22 May 2001
Location: Stockholm, Sweden
Effective: 17 May 2004
Parties: more than 180
Depositary: Secretary-General of the United Nations

The Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants is an international environmental treaty, signed on 22 May 2001 in Stockholm and effective from 17 May 2004, that aims to eliminate or restrict the production and use of persistent organic pollutants (POPs).

History

In 1995, the Governing Council of the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) called for global action on POPs — chemical substances that persist in the environment, bioaccumulate through the food web, and pose risks to human health and ecosystems. Negotiations through an Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee concluded with the adoption of the convention in Stockholm in 2001.

Provisions

Parties must prohibit or phase out chemicals listed in Annex A (elimination), restrict those in Annex B (including DDT, permitted for disease-vector control), and reduce unintentional releases of Annex C substances such as dioxins and furans. The convention initially covered twelve chemicals known as the "dirty dozen," including aldrin, chlordane, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, hexachlorobenzene, mirex, toxaphene, PCBs, and hexachlorobutadiene. A POPs Review Committee evaluates candidate chemicals, and the Conference of the Parties has since added many more substances, including PFOS, PFOA, endosulfan, and chlorpyrifos.

Implementation

Parties prepare National Implementation Plans and report on measures taken. The Global Environment Facility serves as the financial mechanism. The convention operates jointly with the Basel and Rotterdam conventions under a synergies process, with a joint secretariat in Geneva.
