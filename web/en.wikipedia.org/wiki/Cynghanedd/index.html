Cynghanedd

Cynghanedd
Literally"Harmony" (Welsh)
TraditionWelsh strict-metre poetry
FeaturesConsonant correspondence (chime), internal rhyme, alliteration
Used inAwdl, cywydd, englyn and other strict metres

Cynghanedd (Welsh for "harmony"; pronounced roughly kung-HAN-eth) is the fundamental concept of sound arrangement within one line of Welsh strict-metre poetry. It is a system of internal rhyme, alliteration, and consonantal correspondence so intricate that the consonants before the main stress in one part of a line must be answered by the same consonants, in the same order, in another part of the line.

Types

  • Cynghanedd groes ("cross-harmony") — the line is divided by a caesura, and the consonants of the first section are echoed exactly in the second.
  • Cynghanedd draws ("across-harmony") — part of the consonant sequence is echoed across the caesura, with a middle section left unanswered.
  • Cynghanedd sain ("sound-harmony") — the line falls into three sections, with internal rhyme joining the first two and consonantal echo joining the last two.
  • Cynghanedd lusg ("trailing harmony") — the penultimate syllable of the first part rhymes internally with the line's final syllable, without full consonantal correspondence.

History

Cynghanedd was perfected by the court poets of medieval Wales, notably the Gogynfeirdd and the Poets of the Nobility such as Dafydd ap Gwilym, and it remains obligatory in the strict metres competed for at the National Eisteddfod. Masters of the art spend years perfecting technique; the system is widely regarded as unique to Welsh.
