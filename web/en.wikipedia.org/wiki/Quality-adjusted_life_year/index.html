Quality-adjusted life year

The quality-adjusted life year (QALY) is a generic measure of disease burden used in health economics to assess the value of medical interventions. One QALY corresponds to one year of life lived in perfect health. Health states considered worse than death can be assigned negative values, and death is scored zero.

QALYs combine length and quality of life into a single index.

Calculation

QALYs are calculated by multiplying the duration spent in a health state by a utility value for that state, anchored at 1 for perfect health and 0 for death. Utility values are usually elicited from the general public or patients using methods such as the time trade-off, the standard gamble, or standardised questionnaires such as the EQ-5D. For example, ten years lived at a utility of 0.6 yields six QALYs.

Use in health policy

The QALY is central to cost-utility analysis: interventions are compared by their incremental cost per QALY gained. Bodies such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in England use cost-per-QALY thresholds — conventionally in the region of £20,000–£30,000 — when deciding which treatments the health service should fund.

Criticism

Critics argue that QALYs can disadvantage elderly people and those with chronic disabilities, whose maximum achievable "quality" is lower; that utilities may not capture all that matters about a treatment; and that the measure rests on strong assumptions about the separability of length and quality of life. Alternatives and complements include the disability-adjusted life year (DALY) and equal-value life years gained.
