Topological quantum field theory

In gauge theory and mathematical physics, a topological quantum field theory (or topological field theory or TQFT) is a quantum field theory which computes topological invariants. While TQFTs were invented by physicists, they are also of mathematical interest, being related to, among other things, knot theory and the theory of four-manifolds in algebraic topology, and moduli spaces in algebraic geometry.

The modern connection between physics and geometry was pioneered by Albert Schwarz, Edward Witten, Michael Atiyah and others in the late 1970s and 1980s. In 1988 Witten proposed a topological twist of supersymmetric gauge theory — "topological Yang–Mills theory" — and used it to give a physical interpretation of Donaldson theory. The same year, Atiyah axiomatized the notion of a TQFT.

Atiyah's axioms

A TQFT is a functor from the category of cobordisms to the category of vector spaces: to each closed oriented (n−1)-manifold it assigns a vector space, and to each cobordism between such manifolds it assigns a linear map between the corresponding vector spaces, satisfying functoriality and normalization conditions. In this respect a TQFT is a machine that converts topological data into algebraic data, from which topological invariants can be extracted.

Examples

  • Chern–Simons theory in three dimensions, studied by Witten, whose expectation values of Wilson loops reproduce the Jones polynomial and its generalizations, the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants.
  • Donaldson–Witten theory, a twist of N=2 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory in four dimensions computing Donaldson invariants.
  • Gromov–Witten theory, arising from the topological A-model of the string worldsheet.
  • Seiberg–Witten theory, a twist of N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory with matter.

Applications

TQFTs have found applications in condensed matter physics, where topological phases of matter — such as fractional quantum Hall states — are described by effective TQFTs, and in topological quantum computing, where braiding of anyons, described by TQFT amplitudes, performs quantum gates.
