Genesis flood narrative

The Genesis flood narrative (chapters 6–9 of the Book of Genesis) is the Hebrew version of the universal flood myth also found in Mesopotamian literature. It tells of God's decision to return the world to watery chaos and start again, sparing only Noah, his family, and specimens of every animal aboard an ark. After the waters recede, God covenants never again to destroy the earth by flood, setting the rainbow as its sign.

The narrative

Seeing human wickedness — and the corruption associated with the Nephilim, offspring of the "sons of God" and human women — God resolves to blot out mankind but finds Noah righteous. God instructs Noah to build an ark of gopher wood, 300 cubits long, and to take aboard his family and pairs of all animals (in one strand of the text, seven pairs of clean animals). Rain falls for forty days and nights and the fountains of the deep burst open; the waters prevail for 150 days. The ark comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat. Noah sends out a raven and then a dove, which returns with an olive leaf. On leaving the ark Noah offers sacrifice, and God makes a covenant with all living creatures, blessing Noah's sons and permitting the eating of meat.

Composition

Scholars of the documentary hypothesis note interwoven strands — traditionally the Yahwist (J) and Priestly (P) sources — visible in doubled episodes, differing numbers of animals, and distinct divine names and chronologies. The narrative closely parallels the flood story of Utnapishtim in the Epic of Gilgamesh and the older Atrahasis epic, including the release of birds and the sacrifice after landing; such parallels suggest the biblical account was shaped within a shared ancient Near Eastern flood tradition.

Interpretation and influence

Jewish and Christian tradition treats Noah as a model of obedience; the New Testament and the Qur'an (where Nuh is a prophet) retell the story. The narrative is central to flood geology and young-Earth creationism, whose adherents read it as literal history and have searched for the ark on Mount Ararat; mainstream geology finds no evidence for a global flood. The story's themes — judgment, survival, covenant and renewal — have made it one of the most retold tales in world culture, from medieval mystery plays to modern films.
