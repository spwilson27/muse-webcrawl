Twin Earth thought experiment

The Twin Earth thought experiment is a philosophical thought experiment introduced by the American philosopher Hilary Putnam in his 1975 paper "The Meaning of 'Meaning'". It is one of the most influential arguments for semantic externalism, the view that the meanings of words and the contents of mental states are not determined solely by what is inside a speaker's head, but depend in part on the external environment and on the speaker's linguistic community.

Twin Earth is molecule-for-molecule identical to Earth except for the chemical composition of its "water".

The thought experiment

Putnam asks us to imagine a planet, Twin Earth, that is exactly like Earth in every respect except one: the liquid that fills its oceans and lakes, falls as rain, and is called "water" by its inhabitants is not H2O but a chemically different compound, abbreviated XYZ. XYZ is indistinguishable from water at ordinary temperatures and pressures: it tastes the same, quenches thirst, and behaves identically under all everyday observation. The people on Twin Earth ('Twin Earthlings') speak a language identical to English, and their word "water" refers to XYZ.

Now consider Oscar, an ordinary Earthling in 1750 — before the development of modern chemistry, when nobody on either planet knew the chemical composition of water. Oscar on Earth and his molecular duplicate, Twin Oscar, on Twin Earth are in identical internal states: they have the same beliefs, sensations, and dispositions concerning "water". Yet, Putnam argues, when Oscar says "water" he refers to H2O, while Twin Oscar refers to XYZ. Their words — and hence the contents of their thoughts — differ in meaning even though nothing inside their heads differs. Putnam's famous conclusion: "'meanings' just ain't in the head!"

The argument

The thought experiment supports two conclusions. First, semantic externalism: the extension (reference) of a natural kind term like "water" is fixed partly by the actual nature of the samples in the speaker's environment, together with a relation of "sameness of liquid" that speakers need not be able to specify. Second, Putnam proposed that meaning is best analysed as a vector of components including syntactic markers, semantic markers, a stereotype, and the extension; the extension itself is not determined by an individual's psychological state.

A related component of Putnam's account is the division of linguistic labour: ordinary speakers can use terms like "gold" or "elm" successfully because their linguistic community includes experts who can distinguish the kinds, even though individual speakers may not know the distinguishing criteria.

Influence and responses

Twin Earth has been enormously influential in the philosophy of language and the philosophy of mind. It motivated analogous thought experiments, such as Tyler Burge's "arthritis" case, which extended externalism from natural kind terms to social and linguistic factors. Critics have objected in various ways: some argue that the intuition about Twin Oscar is theory-laden; others (internalists) contend that what differs between Oscar and Twin Oscar is not the meaning or content of their states but only the external referent, proposing notions such as "narrow content" to capture the shared internal component. The debate shaped subsequent work on content externalism, two-dimensional semantics, and the metaphysics of mind.
