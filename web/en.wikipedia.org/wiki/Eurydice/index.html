In Greek mythology, Eurydice (Ancient Greek: Εὐρυδίκη, "wide justice") was a nymph, sometimes identified as an Auloniad or dryad, and the wife of the musician Orpheus. Her story — of death on her wedding day and Orpheus's descent into the underworld to recover her — is one of the most famous myths of antiquity, retold by Virgil in the Georgics and by Ovid in the Metamorphoses.

Myth

On the day of her wedding to Orpheus, Eurydice was pursued (in Virgil's version) by the shepherd Aristaeus; fleeing through the grass, she stepped on a venomous serpent, was bitten, and died. The grief-stricken Orpheus descended alive into Hades, where his music so moved Hades and Persephone that they agreed to let Eurydice follow him back to the living world — on the single condition that he not look back at her until both had reached the upper air. At the threshold of the light, fearing he was being deceived, Orpheus turned; Eurydice, still in the darkness, was lost to him forever, uttering a final farewell.

In later culture

The myth has inspired countless works: Gluck's opera Orfeo ed Euridice (1762), Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607), Offenbach's operetta Orpheus in the Underworld, Rilke's poem "Orpheus. Eurydice. Hermes", Sarah Ruhl's play Eurydice (2003), and the musical Hadestown (2019). Eurydice is also the namesake of the asteroid 75 Eurydike.
