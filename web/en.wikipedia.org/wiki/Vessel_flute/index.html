Vessel flute

A vessel flute is a type of flute with a large, compact, enclosed resonating chamber, in contrast to the tubular body of most flutes. The pitch produced by a vessel flute depends principally on the volume of the chamber and the total area of the open finger holes, rather than on the length of a tube; opening more holes raises the pitch regardless of their position. The best-known vessel flute is the ocarina.

Vessel flutes: ocarinas and globular flutes of clay

Acoustics

The air chamber of a vessel flute behaves as a Helmholtz resonator. Because pitch is determined by chamber volume and hole area, vessel flutes can be made in almost any shape — round, animal-shaped, or sculptural — without changing their tuning, a freedom exploited by instrument makers across many cultures. The trade-off is a more limited range and less uniform tone than tubular flutes, and cross-fingering is often needed for chromatic notes.

Types and history

Vessel flutes are among the oldest instruments known. The Chinese xun, an egg-shaped clay flute documented for over seven thousand years, is blown at the top and has five or six finger holes. Globular flutes appear in pre-Columbian Mesoamerican cultures, often moulded as animals or figures. The modern transverse ocarina was developed in the 19th century by Giuseppe Donati of Budrio, Italy, and became a popular folk and novelty instrument worldwide; variants include the English pendant ocarina and multi-chamber ("double" and "triple") ocarinas that extend the range. Vessel flutes also include gemshorn-like and pottery whistles, and whistling vessels from Andean cultures in which air displaced by liquid produces the sound.
