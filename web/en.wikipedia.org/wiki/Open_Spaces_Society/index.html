The Open Spaces Society is a British charity and amenity society dedicated to protecting commons, village greens, other open spaces, and public paths in England and Wales for public enjoyment. Founded in 1865 as the Commons Preservation Society, it is Britain's oldest national conservation organisation.

History

The society was founded amid Victorian concern that enclosure and development were extinguishing common land around London and other growing cities. Early supporters included John Stuart Mill, Octavia Hill, and Lord Eversley (George Shaw Lefevre), who served as a leading figure for decades. The society campaigned to save commons such as Hampstead Heath, Epping Forest, and Wimbledon Common from enclosure, using litigation as well as parliamentary lobbying; its work contributed to the Commons Act 1876 and the legal framework for metropolitan commons. In 1895 three of its members — Octavia Hill, Robert Hunter, and Hardwicke Rawnsley — went on to found the National Trust. The society took its present name in 1910 when it merged with the National Footpaths Preservation Society.

Work

The Open Spaces Society advises local communities and individuals on the law of commons, greens, and rights of way; campaigns against the loss or misuse of open spaces; responds to legislative proposals; and supports applications to register land as town or village greens and to record lost footpaths. It publishes the magazine Open Space and a range of advisory literature. Its casework includes opposing encroachments on commons and defending public access to paths threatened with closure or diversion.
