Testicular torsion occurs when the spermatic cord, from which the testicle is suspended within the scrotum, twists and cuts off the blood supply to the testicle. It is a urological emergency: if the blood flow is not restored within a few hours, the testicle can be permanently damaged or lost.

Signs and symptoms

The classic presentation is sudden onset of severe pain in one testicle, often accompanied by scrotal swelling, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, and a high-riding testicle lying in an abnormal transverse position. The cremasteric reflex is typically absent on the affected side. Onset may follow physical activity, trauma, or occur during sleep, when cremasteric contractions are common.

Causes and mechanism

Most cases in adolescents are intravaginal, made possible by the "bell clapper" deformity, in which the tunica vaginalis attaches high on the spermatic cord so the testicle can rotate freely like a clapper in a bell. Extravaginal torsion, in which the whole cord and tunica twist together, occurs mainly in newborns. Venous outflow is obstructed first, causing congestion and oedema, followed by arterial occlusion and ischaemia.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is primarily clinical; colour Doppler ultrasound showing absent or reduced blood flow supports it, but imaging must not delay surgery when suspicion is high. Salvage rates are around 90–100% if detorsion occurs within six hours, falling steeply thereafter. Definitive treatment is emergency surgical exploration, untwisting of the cord, and orchiopexy — fixation of both testes to prevent recurrence. Manual detorsion (usually rotating the testicle outward, "opening the book") may be attempted while surgery is arranged. Differential diagnoses include epididymitis, torsion of the appendix testis, and incarcerated inguinal hernia.

Epidemiology

Testicular torsion affects roughly 1 in 4,000 males under 25 per year, with peaks in neonates and adolescents aged 12–18.
