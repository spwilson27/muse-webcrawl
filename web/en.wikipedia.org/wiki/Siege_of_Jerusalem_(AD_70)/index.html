The Siege of Jerusalem in AD 70 was the decisive event of the First Jewish–Roman War. The Roman army, commanded by Titus (the son and heir of the emperor Vespasian), besieged and captured the city, which was held by rival Jewish rebel factions. The siege ended with the sacking of Jerusalem and the destruction of the Second Temple — one of the pivotal catastrophes in Jewish history, commemorated by the fast of Tisha B'Av and still mourned in Jewish liturgy.

Background

The revolt began in AD 66; after early successes, the Roman general Vespasian reconquered Galilee and most of Judaea before departing to seize the imperial throne in 69. Jerusalem, swollen with Passover pilgrims and refugees, was torn by civil war among the Zealots of John of Gischala, the forces of Simon bar Giora, and the followers of Eleazar ben Simon, who between them burned much of the city's food stocks.

The siege

Titus arrived with four legions (V Macedonica, XII Fulminata, XV Apollinaris, and X Fretensis) and auxiliaries — perhaps 70,000 men — in April 70. The Romans breached the third (outer) wall in May and the second wall shortly after. When assaults on the Antonia Fortress and Temple Mount stalled, Titus built a circumvallation wall around the entire city, starving the population; famine inside became horrific, as described by the historian Josephus, our principal source. The Antonia fell in July; on the ninth of Av (August), according to tradition, the Temple was set ablaze during the fighting — whether on Titus's orders or against them remains debated. The Upper City was stormed in September, the rebel leaders captured, and the city systematically demolished apart from three towers of Herod's palace and a stretch of wall.

Aftermath

Josephus claims 1.1 million died and 97,000 were enslaved — figures modern scholars consider inflated, though losses were plainly enormous. The Temple treasures, including the great menorah, were paraded in the triumph at Rome in 71 and depicted on the Arch of Titus. The war ended with the fall of Masada in 73/74. Jerusalem was later rebuilt as the Roman colony of Aelia Capitolina, and the destruction of AD 70 ended Jewish sovereignty in the land for nearly two millennia.
