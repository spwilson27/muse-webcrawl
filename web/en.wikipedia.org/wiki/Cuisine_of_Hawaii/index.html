The cuisine of Hawaii incorporates a range of traditional foods and beverages from the Hawaiian Islands. In the history of Hawaii, its island location and wave after wave of immigration from various nations and ethnic groups have resulted in a local cuisine that combines traditional Hawaiian ingredients with those brought by settlers from the United States, Asia, Europe, and Latin America.

History

Polynesian voyagers brought plants and animals to the islands, including taro, sweet potato, coconut, banana, sugarcane, chickens, pigs, and dogs. Native Hawaiians cultivated taro for poi and cooked pork and fish in earth ovens called imu. The luau feast tradition grew from these practices.

From the late 18th century, contact with Europeans and Americans introduced cattle, goats, and new crops. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, plantation laborers arrived from China, Japan, Portugal, Puerto Rico, Korea, and the Philippines, each bringing ingredients and techniques that fused into a distinctive local food culture.

Traditional Hawaiian food

Staples of the traditional diet include poi (pounded taro), kalua pig, lau lau (pork or fish wrapped in taro leaves), lomi salmon, chicken long rice, haupia (coconut pudding), and poke — seasoned cubes of raw fish. Poke shops today offer dozens of variations over rice.

Local food

The modern "local" cuisine includes the plate lunch — two scoops of rice, macaroni salad, and an entrée such as teriyaki beef, chicken katsu, or kalua pig. Spam became a beloved ingredient after the Second World War; spam musubi remains an iconic snack. Saimin, a noodle soup influenced by Chinese, Japanese and Filipino cooking, is served throughout the islands. The loco moco — rice topped with a hamburger patty, fried egg, and gravy — is another emblematic dish.

Hawaii Regional Cuisine

In 1991 twelve chefs, including Sam Choy, Roy Yamaguchi, and Alan Wong, founded the Hawaii Regional Cuisine movement, promoting locally grown ingredients fused with the flavors of Hawaii's ethnic groups.
