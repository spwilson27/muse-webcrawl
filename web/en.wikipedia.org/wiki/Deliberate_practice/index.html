Deliberate practice

Deliberate practice is a structured, purposeful form of practice aimed at improving performance, characterised by focused attention, specific goals just beyond one's current ability, immediate informative feedback, and repetition with refinement. Psychologist K. Anders Ericsson proposed it as the central mechanism behind expert performance.

Ericsson's research

In a landmark 1993 study with Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, violinists at a Berlin music academy who had accumulated roughly 10,000 hours of deliberate practice by age 20 outperformed less-accomplished peers—an origin of the popular "10,000-hour rule" popularised by Malcolm Gladwell in Outliers (2008), which Ericsson criticised as oversimplified: the figure was an average, the practice must be deliberate, and teachers and starting age matter.

Characteristics and debate

Unlike mere repetition or playful practice, deliberate practice is effortful, not inherently enjoyable, and usually designed by a teacher with clear targets and error correction, building refined mental representations. Meta-analyses (e.g. Macnamara et al., 2014) found deliberate practice explains a substantial but far from total share of variance in performance—about 26% in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, and much less in professions—indicating roles for talent, working memory and opportunity. Ericsson responded that many studies measured practice loosely, not true deliberate practice.
