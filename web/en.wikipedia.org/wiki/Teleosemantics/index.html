Teleosemantics

Teleosemantics is a programme in philosophy of mind and philosophy of language that seeks to explain mental content — what beliefs, desires and perceptions are about — in terms of biological function, understood through natural selection. On a teleosemantic account, a mental representation means what it does because representing that state of affairs is its evolved proper function.

Motivation

Teleosemantics aims to naturalise intentionality: to show how representation, truth and error can exist in a purely physical world. Function-based theories promise to solve the problem of misrepresentation: a frog's visual mechanism, selected for detecting flies, can misfire at a BB pellet, because its content is fixed by what it was selected to indicate, not by whatever happens to trigger it.

Major versions

Ruth Millikan's "biosemantics" grounds content in the proper functions of both representation-producing and representation-consuming systems, developed in Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories (1984). Karen Neander defended a simpler producer-side version; David Papineau developed a related account linking content to desire satisfaction and biological purpose. Fred Dretske's indicator semantics, with its appeal to learning history, is a close relative.

Objections

Critics raise the disjunction problem (whether selection history is fine-grained enough to fix content), the Swampman thought experiment (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person created by accident would lack selection history, yet seemingly has thoughts), and worries that evolutionary function is too indeterminate to underwrite precise contents.
