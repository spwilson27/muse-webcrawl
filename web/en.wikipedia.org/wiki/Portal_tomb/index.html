Portal tomb

Portal tomb
PeriodNeolithic, c. 3800–3200 BC
DistributionIreland, Wales, Cornwall
TypeMegalithic chamber tomb

Portal tombs are a type of Neolithic megalithic tomb found principally in Ireland, and also in Wales and Cornwall. They are characterised by a single chamber formed by two tall portal stones at the entrance, side stones, and a single enormous capstone, often resting at an angle. They are commonly called dolmens in popular usage.

Description

A typical portal tomb consists of an upright pair of entrance stones (the portal) supporting one end of a giant roof slab or capstone, the other end resting on the back stone or on the ground, giving the chamber a characteristic sloping profile. Many examples stand over 4 metres high with capstones weighing tens of tonnes. The tomb was originally covered by a cairn or mound of stones and earth, most of which have since been removed, leaving the dramatic stone skeleton visible today.

Distribution and examples

More than 170 portal tombs are known in Ireland, concentrated in the north and south-east. Famous examples include Poulnabrone on the Burren, County Clare — excavation of which produced the remains of more than 30 individuals — Browneshill in County Carlow, with one of the largest capstones in Europe, and Kilclooney More in County Donegal. Welsh examples are called cromlechi (cromlechs); Pentre Ifan in Pembrokeshire is the best known.

Interpretation

Portal tombs were built by early farming communities between about 3800 and 3200 BC and were communal burial places, containing cremated and unburnt human remains, pottery, flint tools and personal ornaments. Their prominent siting, often near water and routeways, suggests they also served as territorial markers and foci for ritual.
