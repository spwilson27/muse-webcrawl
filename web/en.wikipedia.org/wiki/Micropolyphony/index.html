Micropolyphony

Micropolyphony is a musical texture developed by the Hungarian composer György Ligeti in the late 1950s and 1960s. It consists of a dense web of many individual melodic lines — often dozens of separate parts — moving in canon at slightly different speeds and intervals, so closely packed that the individual lines cannot be heard; instead they fuse into a slowly evolving mass of sound.

Technique

Ligeti described the technique as a fabric so finely woven that its threads are imperceptible: the listener hears the whole texture, its harmony and timbre gradually transforming, rather than the counterpoint itself. Because the lines are offset by tiny intervals, micropolyphony produces shimmering clusters and gradual shifts of colour rather than discernible melody.

Works

The technique is central to Apparitions (1959), Atmosphères (1961), the Requiem (1963–65), Lux Aeterna (1966) and Lontano (1967). Excerpts from Atmosphères, the Requiem and Lux Aeterna were used in Stanley Kubrick's film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), bringing micropolyphony to a mass audience. Related sound-mass techniques appear in the work of Krzysztof Penderecki and other composers of the period, though Ligeti's canonic method is distinctive.
