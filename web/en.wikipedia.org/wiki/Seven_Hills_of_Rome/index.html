Seven hills of Rome

The seven hills of Rome (Italian: Sette colli di Roma) are the hills on or about which the ancient city of Rome was built, lying east of the river Tiber. The traditional list comprises the Aventine, Caelian, Capitoline, Esquiline, Palatine, Quirinal and Viminal hills. They form the heart of the modern city and have been central to Roman mythology, history and identity since antiquity.

The hills

The Palatine is the most famous, the legendary site of Romulus's founding of the city and later the location of the imperial palaces. The Capitoline housed the great Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus and served as Rome's citadel and religious centre. The Aventine was associated with the plebeians, who seceded there in the fifth century BC, while the Quirinal, Viminal and Esquiline were largely residential in the imperial period, and the Caelian was known for wealthy villas.

History

The hills were originally occupied by separate settlements, which gradually coalesced into a single city between the eighth and sixth centuries BC, commemorated in the Septimontium festival. The Servian Wall of the fourth century BC enclosed most of them. Because later building filled the valleys and raised the ground level, the hills are less distinct today than in antiquity; other Roman eminences such as the Janiculum and Vatican Hill lie west of the Tiber and are not counted among the canonical seven.
