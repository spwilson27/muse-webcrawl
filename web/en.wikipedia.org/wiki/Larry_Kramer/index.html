Larry Kramer

Larry Kramer
Born June 25, 1935, Bridgeport, Connecticut
Died May 27, 2020 (aged 84), New York City
Occupation: playwright, novelist, activist
Known for: The Normal Heart; co-founding GMHC and ACT UP

Larry Kramer (June 25, 1935 – May 27, 2020) was an American playwright, novelist and pioneering gay rights and AIDS activist. He co-founded the Gay Men's Health Crisis (GMHC) in 1981 and the direct-action group ACT UP in 1987, and his play The Normal Heart (1985) became one of the defining works of the AIDS era.

Early career

Kramer was born in Bridgeport, Connecticut, and educated at Yale University. He worked in the film industry, writing the screenplay for D. H. Lawrence's Women in Love (1969), which earned him an Academy Award nomination, and the musical Lost Horizon (1973), a notorious flop. His 1978 novel Faggots, a satirical portrait of promiscuous gay life on Fire Island and in New York, was controversial within the gay community for its moralizing tone but became a bestseller and a lasting point of debate.

AIDS activism

When the disease later named AIDS emerged in 1981, Kramer was among the first to respond: meetings at his apartment led to the founding of GMHC, the first community-based AIDS service organization. Frustrated by what he saw as governmental indifference, media silence and the caution of GMHC itself, he published the incendiary 1983 essay "1,112 and Counting" and in 1987 helped found ACT UP (AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power), whose confrontational demonstrations transformed drug approval and research policy in the United States. His polemics famously targeted officials including Anthony Fauci, with whom he later forged an unlikely friendship and working relationship.

Writing

The Normal Heart, Kramer's autobiographical play about the early years of the epidemic, premiered at the Public Theater in 1985, was revived on Broadway in 2011 (winning the Tony Award for Best Revival of a Play), and was adapted into an Emmy-winning HBO film in 2014. His later works include the play The Destiny of Me (1992) and the vast historical novel The American People (2015–2020), which presents an alternative, gay-centred history of the United States. Kramer, who lived with HIV and received a liver transplant in 2001, died of pneumonia in New York City in May 2020.
