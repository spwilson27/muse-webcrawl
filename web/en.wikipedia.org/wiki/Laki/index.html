Laki

Laki or Lakagígar (Icelandic for "Laki craters") is a volcanic fissure system in southern Iceland, part of the volcanic system centred on the Grímsvötn volcano beneath the Vatnajökull ice cap. It is famous for its eruption of 1783–84, one of the largest and most consequential lava eruptions in recorded history.

The 1783–84 eruption

On 8 June 1783 a fissure about 25 km (15.5 mi) long opened, with some 130 craters erupting explosively before settling into effusive activity. Over eight months the eruption produced an estimated 14–15 km³ of basaltic lava — the largest lava flow known from a single historical eruption — covering some 565 km², and released enormous quantities of sulphur dioxide and fluorine gases.

Consequences

In Iceland, the poisonous haze (móðuharðindin, the "Mist Hardships") killed roughly half the island's livestock and, through famine and disease, about a quarter of its human population — some 9,000 to 10,000 people. The volcanic haze spread across the Northern Hemisphere: in Europe, a hot, hazy summer in 1783 was followed by a severe winter, crop failures and excess mortality, and Benjamin Franklin was among those who speculatively linked the "dry fog" to the Icelandic eruption. Effects on global climate and harvests have been traced as far as Egypt and India.

Geology

Laki lies within Iceland's eastern volcanic zone, on the divergent boundary between the North American and Eurasian plates. The fissure trends northeast–southwest, broadly parallel to the Eldgjá fissure, which erupted in the 10th century.
