Medical school in the United States

Medical school in the United States is a four-year graduate program leading to the Doctor of Medicine (M.D.) or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (D.O.) degree. Admission generally requires an undergraduate degree and completion of premedical coursework in biology, chemistry, physics, and related subjects, plus a competitive score on the Medical College Admission Test (MCAT).

Curriculum

The traditional curriculum divides into two phases:

PhaseYearsContent
Pre-clerkship1–2Basic sciences: anatomy, biochemistry, physiology, pathology, pharmacology, microbiology, and introduction to clinical skills
Clerkship3–4Clinical rotations in internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, obstetrics and gynecology, psychiatry, family medicine, and electives

Many schools have moved to integrated, systems-based curricula with earlier clinical exposure and pass/fail grading in the pre-clerkship phase.

Licensing examinations

Students take the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE): Step 1 after the basic science years (now scored pass/fail), Step 2 CK during the clinical years, and Step 3 during residency. Osteopathic students take the COMLEX-USA series, and many also take the USMLE.

Residency and the Match

In the final year, students apply to residency programs through the National Resident Matching Program (the Match), which pairs applicants and programs by ranked preference lists. Residency lasts three to seven years depending on specialty, and fellowship may follow.

Accreditation and cost

M.D. programs are accredited by the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME); D.O. programs by the Commission on Osteopathic College Accreditation. Tuition is high — often exceeding $60,000 per year at private schools — and the average graduate carries substantial educational debt, a recurring policy concern.
