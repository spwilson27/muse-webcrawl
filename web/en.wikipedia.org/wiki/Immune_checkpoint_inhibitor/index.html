Immune checkpoint inhibitor

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are a class of drugs — typically monoclonal antibodies — that block checkpoint proteins made by some types of immune system cells, such as T cells, and some cancer cells. These proteins normally help keep immune responses in check, but when blocked, T cells can kill cancer cells more effectively. Checkpoint inhibitors are a major form of cancer immunotherapy.

Targets and drugs

The best-studied checkpoints are CTLA-4 and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. Ipilimumab, approved in 2011 for melanoma, was the first checkpoint inhibitor, targeting CTLA-4. PD-1 inhibitors include pembrolizumab and nivolumab; PD-L1 inhibitors include atezolizumab, durvalumab and avelumab. A third checkpoint, LAG-3, was added to clinical use with relatlimab in 2022. These drugs are now approved for many cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, bladder cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma and head and neck cancers.

Mechanism

Tumours exploit checkpoint pathways to evade immune attack. By blocking the inhibitory interaction between T-cell receptors and their ligands on tumour and antigen-presenting cells, checkpoint inhibitors restore anti-tumour T-cell activity. James P. Allison and Tasuku Honjo received the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering cancer therapy by inhibition of negative immune regulation.

Side effects

Because they release brakes on the immune system, checkpoint inhibitors can cause immune-related adverse events — inflammation of the colon, lungs, liver, skin, pituitary and thyroid glands — which are managed with corticosteroids and treatment interruption. Only a subset of patients responds durably, and biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and tumour mutational burden help predict benefit.
