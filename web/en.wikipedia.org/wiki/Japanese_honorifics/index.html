Japanese honorifics

The Japanese language makes extensive use of honorifics (keigo), a system of polite and respectful speech that reflects the relative status of speaker, listener and person spoken about. Honorifics include polite verb forms, respectful (sonkeigo) and humble (kenjōgo) vocabulary, and suffixes attached to names.

Name suffixes

The most common suffixes are -san, a general polite title roughly equivalent to Mr/Ms; -sama, a more respectful form used for customers, honoured guests and deities; -kun, used for boys and male juniors; -chan, an affectionate diminutive for children and close friends; -senpai and -kōhai for senior and junior colleagues; and -sensei for teachers, doctors and masters of a field. Omitting any suffix (yobisute) implies great familiarity or contempt. Family and business titles such as shachō (company president) are also used as forms of address.

Keigo

Beyond titles, verbs have honorific and humble counterparts: for example "to eat" is taberu in plain speech, meshiagaru when speaking respectfully of another's eating, and itadaku when humbly describing one's own. The polite desu/masu style is the default in most formal and business settings, while plain forms are used among intimates.
