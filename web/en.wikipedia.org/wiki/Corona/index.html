Corona

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The Sun's corona streaming outward during a total solar eclipse, seen as a pearly crown]
The Sun's corona streaming outward during a total solar eclipse, seen as a pearly crown

A corona is the outermost layer of a star's atmosphere, extending millions of kilometres into space as an irregularly shaped plasma halo. The Sun's corona — visible to the naked eye only during a total Solar eclipse as a pearly crown — is paradoxically far hotter (1–3 million kelvin) than the solar surface (5,800 K), a mystery at the frontier of solar physics. It is the source of the Solar wind and of Coronal mass ejections.

Structure and heating

The corona is sculpted by the Sun's magnetic field into streamers, loops and dark coronal holes (sources of fast solar wind). Why it is hundreds of times hotter than the surface — the 'coronal heating problem' — is attributed to magnetic reconnection and wave dissipation, on scales down to 'nanoflares'; NASA's Parker Solar Probe has flown through the corona itself since 2021, sampling it directly.

Observing

Eclipses revealed the corona's shape changing with the solar cycle; coronagraphs (artificial eclipses) and space telescopes (SOHO, SDO) now watch it continuously. Its emission lines once suggested a new element, 'coronium' — actually iron stripped of half its electrons (Fe XIV), evidence of the million-degree temperatures.
