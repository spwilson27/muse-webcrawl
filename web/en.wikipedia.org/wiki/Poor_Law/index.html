Poor Law

The Poor Law in England and Wales refers to the body of legislation governing the relief of poverty from the sixteenth century until the emergence of the modern welfare state after the Second World War. Under the Poor Law, each parish was responsible for its own poor, funded by a local property tax (the poor rate) and administered by overseers of the poor. The system was codified by the Poor Relief Act 1601 (the "Old Poor Law") and radically reformed by the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 (the "New Poor Law"), which organised parishes into unions run by boards of guardians and made relief in the workhouse the centrepiece of provision.

Origins and the Old Poor Law

Medieval poor relief had been provided mainly by the Church, monasteries, and private charity. The dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII, population growth, inflation, and enclosure created pressures that Tudor governments met with statutes against vagrancy and, after experiments including the Norwich census of the poor, with the comprehensive Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601. The Act obliged every parish to levy a rate and appoint overseers to relieve the "impotent" poor (the aged, sick, and children), set the able-bodied to work, and apprentice pauper children. The Settlement Act 1662 tied entitlement to a legal settlement in a parish, leading to removals of paupers to their parish of origin.

From 1795 many southern parishes adopted the Speenhamland system of topping up wages from the rates according to family size and the price of bread. Critics including Thomas Malthus blamed such allowances for encouraging dependency and depressing wages, and rising costs after the Napoleonic Wars drove demands for reform.

The New Poor Law of 1834

The Royal Commission of 1832–34, influenced by Edwin Chadwick and Nassau Senior, recommended the principles of less eligibility — the workhouse pauper's condition should be less desirable than that of the lowest independent labourer — and the workhouse test for able-bodied relief. The 1834 Act created a central Poor Law Commission (later the Poor Law Board) and grouped some 15,000 parishes into about 600 Poor Law unions, each with a workhouse administered by an elected board of guardians. Outdoor relief to the able-bodied was to be abolished, though in practice it remained common, especially in the industrial north, where the reform met fierce resistance in the Anti-Poor Law movement.

Later development and decline

During the later nineteenth century the system broadened: workhouse infirmaries became an important source of hospital care for the poor, and boarding-out, cottage homes, and other alternatives developed for children. The Local Government Act 1929 abolished the boards of guardians and transferred their functions to local authorities, and the National Assistance Act 1948 formally repealed the Poor Law, replacing it with the modern welfare state founded on social insurance (the Beveridge Report of 1942) and the National Health Service.

Scotland and Ireland

Scotland had a separate system under the Poor Law (Scotland) Act 1845, with parochial boards and no legal right to relief for the able-bodied. Ireland received a workhouse-based system under the Irish Poor Law Act 1838, which was severely strained by the Great Famine of 1845–52.
