Wallis product

In mathematics, the Wallis product is an infinite product giving an expression for π/2, published by John Wallis in his Arithmetica Infinitorum (1656):

π/2 = (2/1 · 2/3) · (4/3 · 4/5) · (6/5 · 6/7) · … = ∏n=1..∞ (2n/(2n−1)) · (2n/(2n+1)) = ∏n=1..∞ 4n²/(4n² − 1)

Wallis arrived at the product by interpolation while seeking the area under curves of the form (1−x²)n — in effect, computing the integral of sinn x over [0, π/2]. The product converges slowly, but it was historically important as one of the first infinite products in mathematics and as a forerunner of later work on factorials and the gamma function.


Convergence of the Wallis product to π/2

Derivation

A standard derivation considers the integrals In = ∫0π/2 sinn x dx. Integration by parts yields the recurrence In = ((n−1)/n) In−2, from which closed forms for even and odd n follow. Since I2n+1 ≤ I2n ≤ I2n−1 and the ratio of consecutive terms tends to 1, the squeeze theorem gives the Wallis product in the limit.

Connections

The product is closely related to Stirling's approximation for factorials, and an equivalent form appears in the evaluation of the sinc function product ∏ cos(x/2n). In 2015 a version of the Wallis product was unexpectedly found in calculations of the quantum mechanical energy levels of the hydrogen atom.
