Proanthocyanidins are a class of polyphenolic compounds found in many plants. Chemically, they are oligomeric flavonoids built from flavan-3-ol units such as catechin and epicatechin, and are the principal components of the condensed tannins (as opposed to the hydrolysable tannins). They are called proanthocyanidins because acid treatment breaks them down to yield coloured anthocyanidins.

Chemistry

Proanthocyanidins consist of chains of flavan-3-ol units linked mainly through C4→C8 or C4→C6 interflavan bonds (B-type), sometimes with an additional ether bond (A-type). Chains range from dimers and trimers to large polymers with degrees of polymerisation of 50 or more. The most studied include procyanidins (from catechin/epicatechin), prodelphinidins (from gallocatechins) and propelargonidins.

Sources

Rich dietary sources include grape seeds and skins, red wine, cocoa and dark chocolate, cranberries, blueberries, apples, pears, cinnamon and the bark of maritime pine (Pinus pinaster), sold as the extract Pycnogenol. Proanthocyanidins give unripe fruit its astringent taste and help defend plants against herbivores and pathogens.

Biological activity

Proanthocyanidins are powerful antioxidants in vitro. Cranberry proanthocyanidins with A-type linkages have been studied for their ability to inhibit adhesion of uropathogenic Escherichia coli to urinary tract walls. Larger polymers are poorly absorbed in the gut and are broken down by the microbiota into smaller phenolic acids; the health significance of many marketed claims remains under investigation.

Analysis

Proanthocyanidin content is commonly measured by the DMACA and vanillin assays, by acid-butanol cleavage, and by chromatography after thiolysis or phloroglucinolysis, which reveal chain length and subunit composition.
