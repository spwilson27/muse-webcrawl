Shortwave radio

Shortwave radio is radio transmission using shortwave (SW) frequencies — the high frequency (HF) band, from about 3 to 30 MHz. Radio waves in this band can be reflected or refracted by the ionosphere, a layer of charged particles in the upper atmosphere, allowing them to travel far beyond the horizon, often over intercontinental distances. This "skywave" or "skip" propagation makes shortwave uniquely suited to long-distance communication.

History

Radio amateurs in the early 1920s discovered that the then-neglected short wavelengths could achieve transatlantic contacts with low power, contrary to the belief that only long waves were useful for distance. Commercial and government services quickly moved in: shortwave carried international broadcasting (the BBC Empire Service began in 1932), transoceanic telephony before undersea cables and satellites, maritime and aeronautical communication, and diplomatic and military traffic. During the Cold War, stations such as Voice of America, Radio Free Europe, and Radio Moscow used shortwave to reach audiences behind the Iron Curtain, and jamming was common.

Uses

  • International broadcasting — news and cultural programming directed at foreign audiences.
  • Amateur radio — the HF amateur bands support worldwide contacts and contests.
  • Utility stations — maritime, aviation (VOLMET and oceanic air traffic control), military, and emergency communications.
  • Time signals — stations such as WWV (United States) and CHU (Canada) broadcast continuous time announcements.
  • Numbers stations — unidentified broadcasts of spoken digits, widely believed to carry messages to intelligence operatives.
  • Over-the-horizon radar and ionospheric research.

Propagation and decline

Shortwave reception depends on time of day, season, and the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle, since solar activity controls ionospheric ionization. Higher frequencies are generally usable by day and lower frequencies by night. Satellite broadcasting, the internet, and streaming have led many international broadcasters to cut shortwave services since the 2000s, though shortwave remains valued in regions with limited internet access, during disasters, and for its independence from local infrastructure.
