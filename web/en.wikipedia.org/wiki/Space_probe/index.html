A space probe is an uncrewed spacecraft that leaves Earth's orbit (or is sent on a trajectory away from Earth) to explore outer space and celestial bodies, returning data by radio telemetry. Space probes have flown by, orbited, landed on or sampled every planet in the Solar System, as well as the Moon, asteroids, comets and dwarf planets; five probes are on trajectories leaving the Solar System entirely.

History

The era began with the Soviet Luna programme: Luna 1 (1959) was the first probe to escape Earth's gravity and pass the Moon, and Luna 2 first impacted the Moon. NASA's Mariner 2 reached Venus in 1962, Mariner 4 returned the first close images of Mars in 1965, and Pioneer 10 and 11 reconnoitred Jupiter and Saturn in the 1970s. The twin Voyagers (1977) conducted the "Grand Tour" of the outer planets. Later landmarks include Galileo at Jupiter, Cassini–Huygens at Saturn and Titan, the Mars rovers, Rosetta's comet landing, New Horizons at Pluto and Arrokoth, and sample returns from asteroids by Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS-REx.

Design

Probes combine a power source (solar arrays, or radioisotope thermoelectric generators where sunlight is weak), a high-gain antenna for communication with networks such as NASA's Deep Space Network, scientific instruments (cameras, spectrometers, magnetometers, particle detectors), and autonomous fault protection, since light-delay makes real-time control impossible beyond the Moon.

Interstellar probes

Voyager 1 and 2 crossed into interstellar space in 2012 and 2018 respectively, continuing to return data on the heliosphere's boundary. Both carry the Golden Record, a phonograph record of sounds and images of Earth.
