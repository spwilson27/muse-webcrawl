Enunciation

In phonetics and speech, enunciation is the act of pronouncing words and sounds clearly and distinctly, so that speech is intelligible to a listener. It concerns the precision with which the speech organs — lips, tongue, teeth, palate and jaw — form individual sounds, and is closely related to, but distinct from, pronunciation (which concerns the accepted pattern of sounds in a language or dialect) and articulation.

Enunciation and related terms

Pronunciation describes which sounds and stress patterns a speaker uses; enunciation describes how clearly they are produced. Poor enunciation — mumbling or slurring — can make even correctly pronounced speech hard to follow. Actors, broadcasters, public speakers and singers train enunciation with exercises such as tongue twisters, exaggerated mouth movements and reading aloud, and speech-language pathologists treat enunciation difficulties in therapy.

In linguistics

In the theory of language associated with Émile Benveniste, "enunciation" (énonciation) has a technical sense: the individual act of producing an utterance, as opposed to the utterance itself (énoncé). Benveniste analysed how pronouns such as "I" and "you" and deictic expressions such as "here" and "now" refer only within the act of enunciation, a distinction influential in discourse analysis and literary theory.
