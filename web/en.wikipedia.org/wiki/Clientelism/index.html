Clientelism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clientelism is a social and political order in which relations of personal exchange — patrons providing goods, jobs, protection or access to the state in return for clients' political support and loyalty — structure access to power and resources. Political scientists distinguish traditional clientelism (dyadic, hierarchical, durable ties between big men and dependents) from its modern mass form, in which parties and brokers distribute particularistic benefits — jobs, contracts, welfare access, local projects — in exchange for votes, monitored through party machines and neighbourhood brokers.

Analysis and debate

Clientelism is the shadow organisation of many democracies: it solves poor voters' urgent problems credibly (a broker who delivers is believable in ways programme promises are not), gives parties durable electoral machines, and systematically under-provides public goods (schools and sewers benefit non-supporters too) while corroding accountability — citizens approach the state as supplicants trading votes, not rights-holders. Research debates its persistence under development and secret ballots (harder monitoring pushes brokers toward turnout buying and relational, long-term exchange rather than spot vote-buying), its ethnic and local-regional strongholds, and reform levers: universalistic welfare design, civil-service insulation, ballot secrecy enforcement, and digital payments that bypass brokers — with the warning that digitised welfare can recentralise clientelism in the ruling party's data rather than abolish it.
