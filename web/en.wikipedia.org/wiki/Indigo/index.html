Indigo

Indigo
Spectral region: Blue–violet
Wavelength: approx. 425–450 nm
Named as a rainbow colour by Isaac Newton

Indigo is a colour in the visible spectrum between blue and violet, with a wavelength of roughly 425–450 nanometres. It is named after the indigo dye derived from plants of the genus Indigofera. Isaac Newton included indigo as one of the seven colours of the rainbow when he divided the spectrum, by analogy with the seven notes of the musical scale; many modern colour scientists regard indigo as lying within the blue–violet range rather than as a separate spectral colour.

The dye

Indigo dye is one of the oldest dyes used for textile colouring, with evidence of use in ancient Egypt, India, China and Peru. It is a distinctive deep blue vat dye, insoluble in water, which must be reduced to a soluble leuco form before dyeing and then oxidises in air to the familiar blue. Indigo was a major plantation crop and trade good in the colonial era and is culturally inseparable from denim and blue jeans. In 1897 BASF brought synthetic indigo to market following Adolf von Baeyer's elucidation of its structure; today nearly all indigo is synthetic, and most of it dyes denim yarn.

Uses and symbolism

Beyond textiles, indigo and its derivatives appear in food colouring (indigo carmine), as a pigment, and in organic electronics. The colour carries varied symbolic associations, from denim workwear to the "indigo children" of New Age belief.
