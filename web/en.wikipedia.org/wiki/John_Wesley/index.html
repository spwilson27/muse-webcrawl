John Wesley (17 June 1703 – 2 March 1791) was an English cleric, theologian, and evangelist who was a leader of a revival movement within the Church of England known as Methodism. The societies he founded laid the basis for the contemporary Methodist Church. Wesley traveled throughout the British Isles, preaching outdoors to crowds who were largely outside the reach of the parish churches, and organized converts into societies, classes, and bands governed by the Methodist discipline.

Early life and education

Wesley was born in Epworth, Lincolnshire, the fifteenth child of Samuel Wesley, an Anglican rector, and Susanna Wesley, whose rigorous, pious household deeply shaped him. As a child he was famously rescued from a fire in the rectory — an event he interpreted as divine providence, calling himself "a brand plucked out of the burning." He was educated at Charterhouse and Christ Church, Oxford, where in 1729 he took over leadership of the "Holy Club," a group of students (including his brother Charles and George Whitefield) whose methodical devotion earned them the nickname "Methodists."

Conversion and revival

Wesley served as a missionary in the colony of Georgia (1735–37), an experience he regarded as a failure. On 24 May 1738, at a Moravian society meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, he felt his heart "strangely warmed" while hearing Luther's preface to Romans read, and gained an assurance of salvation that transformed his ministry. The following year, at Whitefield's urging, he began open-air field preaching to miners and workers, and founded the first Methodist society in Bristol. His organizational genius — lay preachers, itinerancy, class meetings, and annual conferences — turned scattered converts into a disciplined, enduring movement.

Theology

Wesleyan (Wesleyan-Arminian) theology emphasizes prevenient grace, justification by faith, the possibility of Christian perfection or entire sanctification in this life, and the "means of grace." Wesley split with Whitefield over predestination and later with the Calvinistic Methodists, defending Arminian doctrines in his sermons and the Arminian Magazine. He summarized his ethics in the famous rule: "Do all the good you can, by all the means you can, in all the ways you can, in all the places you can, at all the times you can, to all the people you can, as long as ever you can." He opposed slavery, publishing Thoughts upon Slavery (1774).

Later years, death, and legacy

In 1784 Wesley ordained Thomas Coke as superintendent for America, effectively beginning a separate Methodist church there, though Wesley himself remained an Anglican priest all his life. He traveled some 250,000 miles on horseback and preached more than 40,000 sermons. He died in London on 2 March 1791. Methodism today claims tens of millions of adherents worldwide in its various denominations, and Wesley is commemorated in the calendars of several churches.
