Trade (occupation)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trade is a skilled occupation that requires particular training, knowledge and manual ability. A person engaged in one of the trades is called a tradesperson (also tradesman or tradeswoman). Trades are distinguished from professions, which are typically entered through university education and regulated by professional bodies, and from unskilled labour. Examples of trades include carpenter, electrician, plumber, pipefitter, welder, machinist, mechanic, stonemason, plasterer, painter and decorator, roofer, glazier and blacksmith.

Training

Entry into a trade is traditionally by apprenticeship, in which a trainee (apprentice) works under the supervision of an experienced tradesperson (a journeyman, and after further experience a master craftsman) while completing classroom instruction, often at a technical college or trade school. In Germany and other countries with the dual education system, apprenticeships combine paid employment with part-time schooling and lead to recognised qualifications. In many jurisdictions trades such as electrician and plumber require licensing, both to protect consumers and to meet safety codes.

History

Trades descend from the crafts organised in medieval guilds, which controlled training, quality and prices, and certified masters. The industrial revolution factory system eroded some crafts while creating new ones — millwrights, fitters and electricians among them. Trade unions grew substantially out of the organisation of skilled trades, beginning with craft unions in the nineteenth century. In modern labour statistics, the "skilled trades" remain a distinct category, and shortages of tradespeople are periodically reported in construction and manufacturing sectors.

Trades and the professions

The distinction between a trade and a profession is social as much as technical: tradespeople are typically paid wages or charge fees for manual services, learn mainly through supervised practice, and are licensed (where required) by government rather than self-governing professional bodies. Nevertheless many trades demand deep theoretical knowledge — of mathematics, physics, drawing and regulations — and skilled tradespeople can command earnings comparable to or exceeding those of many professionals.

Common trades

  • Construction trades: carpenter, electrician, plumber, bricklayer, stonemason, plasterer, roofer, glazier, painter and decorator, HVAC technician
  • Mechanical and metal trades: mechanic, millwright, machinist, welder, boilermaker, sheet-metal worker, blacksmith, tool and die maker
  • Service trades: baker, butcher, hairdresser, tailor, locksmith, linesman (lineworker), printer
