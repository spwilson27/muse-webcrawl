Reciprocating engine

A reciprocating engine, also often known as a piston engine, is a heat engine that uses one or more reciprocating pistons to convert high temperature and high pressure into a rotating motion. This article describes the common features of all types. The main types are the internal combustion engine, used extensively in motor vehicles; the steam engine, the mainstay of the Industrial Revolution; and the niche application Stirling engine.

Principle of operation

In a reciprocating engine, expanding gas pushes a piston within a cylinder. The piston's linear motion is converted into rotational motion by a connecting rod and crankshaft. Combustion engines repeat a cycle of strokes — intake, compression, power and exhaust in a four-stroke engine, or a combined cycle in two strokes. Valves or ports admit the fuel–air mixture or steam and discharge the exhaust.

Configurations

Pistons may be arranged in many configurations, including inline, V, flat (boxer), radial and opposed-piston arrangements. Engines are characterised by cylinder count, displacement, bore and stroke. Historically, steam engines dominated stationary power and rail and marine transport before being displaced by turbines and internal combustion engines, which in turn dominate road transport.
