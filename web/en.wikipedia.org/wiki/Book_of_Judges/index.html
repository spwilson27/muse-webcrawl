The Book of Judges (Hebrew: ספר שופטים, Sefer Shoftim) is the seventh book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. It covers the period between the death of Joshua and the rise of the monarchy, when Israel was led by charismatic military deliverers called "judges" (shoftim).


Samson, the last of the major judges

Content and pattern

The book is framed by a repeating cycle: Israel "does evil in the sight of the LORD" by worshipping other gods; God delivers them to oppressors; the people cry out; God raises a judge who delivers them; the land has rest until the judge dies and the cycle resumes. The major judges are Othniel, Ehud (who assassinated the Moabite king Eglon), Deborah (with Barak, and Jael who killed Sisera), Gideon (who defeated Midian with 300 men), Jephthah (whose vow cost his daughter), and Samson, whose exploits against the Philistines fill four chapters. Shorter notices cover Shamgar, Tola, Jair, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon.

Ending and themes

The final chapters (17–21) abandon the cycle for two dark appendices — Micah's idol and the migration of Dan, and the outrage at Gibeah with the ensuing civil war against Benjamin — punctuated by the refrain "In those days there was no king in Israel; every man did what was right in his own eyes." The book's trajectory from order to chaos builds the Deuteronomistic case for kingship, while many individual stories are thought to preserve much older tribal traditions.

Composition

Traditionally attributed to Samuel, Judges is regarded by scholars as part of the Deuteronomistic history, shaped in the 7th–6th centuries BC from older heroic tales, lists and songs — the Song of Deborah (ch. 5) being among the oldest texts in the Bible.
