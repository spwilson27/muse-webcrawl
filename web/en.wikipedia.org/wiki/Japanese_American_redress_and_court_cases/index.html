Japanese American redress and court cases

[Image: Signing of the Civil Liberties Act, 1988]

The Japanese American redress movement sought official acknowledgment and compensation from the United States government for the incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II. Under Executive Order 9066, signed in 1942, some 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry — most of them American citizens — were forcibly removed from the West Coast and confined in camps. The movement culminated in the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, which granted a formal apology and $20,000 payments to surviving incarcerees.

Early court cases

During the war, the Supreme Court decided several challenges to the removal and curfew orders. In Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) and Korematsu v. United States (1944), the Court upheld the government's actions, while in Ex parte Endo (1944) it held that concededly loyal citizens could not continue to be detained. Decades later, researchers uncovered evidence that the government had suppressed reports contradicting claims of military necessity, leading to the convictions being vacated through coram nobis proceedings in the 1980s.

The redress campaign

In the 1970s, Japanese American community organizations including the Japanese American Citizens League campaigned for redress. In 1980 Congress created the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, whose 1983 report, Personal Justice Denied, concluded that the incarceration had resulted from "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership" rather than military necessity.

Civil Liberties Act of 1988

Signed by President Ronald Reagan on 10 August 1988, the Civil Liberties Act provided a formal national apology and authorized payments of $20,000 to each surviving person who had been incarcerated. Payments began in 1990, accompanied by a signed letter of apology from the President. More than 82,000 individuals ultimately received redress.

Legacy

The redress movement is regarded as a landmark in American civil rights history and a model of reparative legislation achieved through community organizing and litigation. In 2018, the Supreme Court's decision in Trump v. Hawaii formally repudiated the Korematsu ruling as gravely wrong.
