La Venta is an archaeological site of the pre-Columbian Olmec civilization located in the present-day Mexican state of Tabasco. Built on an island in a coastal swamp on the Tonalá River, La Venta was the greatest Olmec centre during its florescence between about 900 and 400 BC, succeeding the earlier centre of San Lorenzo.

Layout and monuments

The site is laid out along a precise north–south axis. Its dominant feature is the Great Pyramid (Complex C), a huge fluted earthen cone about 33 metres high and one of the earliest monumental pyramids in Mesoamerica. North of the pyramid, Complex A contained elaborate ritual deposits: "Massive Offerings" of thousands of serpentine blocks buried in pits, mosaic pavements of serpentine arranged in abstract jaguar-mask designs, and rich tombs of basalt columns containing jade figurines and ornaments.

Sculpture

La Venta is famous for its monumental stone sculpture, including four of the celebrated Olmec colossal heads — basalt portraits up to about 3 metres tall, each thought to represent a ruler — and several "altars" (probably thrones) carved with figures emerging from cave-like niches holding were-jaguar infants. Much of the sculpture was deliberately buried or mutilated when the site was abandoned around 400 BC.

Excavation

The site was reported in 1925 by Frans Blom and Oliver La Farge, and excavated in the 1940s by Matthew Stirling and later by teams from the Smithsonian and the University of California, Berkeley. Many of the finest monuments were moved for safekeeping to the Parque Museo La Venta in Villahermosa and the Museo Regional de Antropología Carlos Pellicer.
