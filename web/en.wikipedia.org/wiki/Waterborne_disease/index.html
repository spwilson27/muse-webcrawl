Waterborne disease

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Waterborne diseases are conditions caused by pathogenic micro-organisms transmitted through contaminated water — drunk, used for washing or food preparation, or contacted recreationally. They include cholera, typhoid fever, dysentery (bacterial and amoebic), hepatitis A and E, poliomyelitis, and diarrhoeal diseases caused by rotavirus, E. coli, Campylobacter and the parasite Giardia. Diarrhoeal disease remains one of the leading killers of children under five worldwide.

History

The waterborne route was first demonstrated in 1854, when the physician John Snow traced a London cholera outbreak to the Broad Street pump and had its handle removed — a founding moment of epidemiology, decades before germ theory. Nineteenth-century sanitary reform (sewers, filtration, and later chlorination) then nearly eliminated cholera and typhoid from wealthy cities, one of public health's greatest triumphs. Cholera itself, carried from the Ganges delta in seven pandemics since 1817, still causes an estimated 1.3–4 million cases a year, with outbreaks after disasters and wars (Haiti 2010, Yemen from 2016).

Prevention and scale

Prevention is summarised as WASH — safe water, sanitation and hygiene. About two billion people use a drinking-water source contaminated with faeces, according to WHO/UNICEF monitoring; boiling, chlorination, filtration, safe storage, handwashing with soap, and vaccines (against cholera, typhoid, rotavirus and polio) are the main defences. Oral rehydration therapy — a simple glucose-and-salts solution — has saved tens of millions of lives from diarrhoeal dehydration and is considered one of the most important medical advances of the 20th century.
