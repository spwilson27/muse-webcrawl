Mating system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mating system is a way in which a group of animals is structured in relation to sexual behaviour — in particular, which males mate with which females, how many mates each sex has, and how mates are acquired and (in some species) how long associations between mates last. The term is used in behavioural ecology and evolutionary biology, and parallel concepts apply to plant mating systems, which concern patterns of pollen transfer and self- versus cross-fertilisation.

Principal types

  • Monogamy — one male mates with one female. Social monogamy (a pair bond with shared territory or offspring care) is common in birds, though genetic studies show extra-pair matings often make it distinct from genetic monogamy. Serial monogamy involves successive pairings across seasons.
  • Polygyny — one male mates with several females, as in harems defended by a male, or resource-defence systems where males control territories attracting multiple females. It is the most common polygamous system in mammals.
  • Polyandry — one female mates with several males, as in jacanas and some pipefish; rare overall.
  • Polygynandry — both sexes have multiple mates within a group.
  • Promiscuity — mating without pair bonds or exclusivity, with little mate association beyond copulation.
  • Lek mating — males gather at display grounds (leks) where females choose among them; males provide no resources or care.

Determining factors

The mating system a species evolves depends on the distribution of resources and receptive mates in space and time, the economics of defending them, the need for biparental care (which favours monogamy, as in most birds), and the operational sex ratio. These influences are summarised in ideas such as the polygyny threshold model and Emlen and Oring's framework linking mating systems to the environmental potential for polygamy. Mating systems drive sexual selection, influencing sexual size dimorphism, weaponry, ornaments and sperm competition.

In humans

Human societies permit a range of marriage systems — monogamy, polygyny (historically the most widely permitted), and rarely polyandry — regulated by law and custom, while actual mating behaviour varies further; anthropologists distinguish these social arrangements from the underlying mating system studied biologically.
