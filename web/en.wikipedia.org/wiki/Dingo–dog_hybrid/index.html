Dingo–dog hybrid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dingo–dog hybrid is a hybrid cross between a dingo and a domestic dog. Interbreeding between dingoes and feral or free-roaming domestic dogs has occurred since European colonisation of Australia brought domestic dogs into contact with wild dingo populations. Hybridisation is regarded as one of the principal threats to the survival of the dingo as a distinct population, because backcrossing over generations dilutes dingo characteristics — a process sometimes called "genetic swamping".

Identification

Pure dingoes are generally identified by a combination of skull measurements, coat colour (typically ginger, though black-and-tan and other colours occur), a single annual breeding season, and behavioural traits. Hybrids may show dog-like features such as patchy, brindle, or black coats, barking vocalisations (pure dingoes rarely bark), and the ability to breed twice a year. Coat colour alone is unreliable, and modern assessments increasingly rely on DNA testing.

Prevalence and debate

Studies using microsatellite DNA testing have suggested that hybrids make up a large proportion of the wild canid population in south-eastern Australia, while animals in central and northern Australia remain mostly pure. The extent of hybridisation — and even whether "pure" dingoes persist — has been debated; some genomic research indicates that dog ancestry in wild populations is lower than earlier morphological studies implied.

Management

Because dingoes are protected in some Australian states and declared pests in others, hybridisation complicates management: control programs target wild dogs and hybrids as predators of livestock, while conservation programs seek to protect genetically pure dingo populations, for example on K'gari (Fraser Island).
