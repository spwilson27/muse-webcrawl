Herbicide resistance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herbicide resistance is the inherited ability of a plant to survive and reproduce following exposure to a dose of herbicide normally lethal to the wild type. Resistance arises when repeated use of herbicides with the same mode of action selects for naturally resistant individuals, whose offspring come to dominate the weed population.

Extent

Resistance was first reported in the 1950s–1970s with triazine herbicides. The International Herbicide-Resistant Weed Database records hundreds of unique cases of resistance across dozens of herbicide modes of action and more than 250 weed species. The problem intensified with reliance on glyphosate in genetically modified herbicide-tolerant crops: glyphosate-resistant Palmer amaranth, waterhemp, and ryegrass have become major agronomic pests in the Americas and Australia.

Mechanisms

  • Target-site resistance — mutation of the herbicide's target enzyme (e.g., ALS or EPSPS) so the chemical no longer binds; also gene amplification of the target (e.g., EPSPS amplification in Palmer amaranth).
  • Non-target-site resistance — enhanced metabolic detoxification (via cytochrome P450s, GSTs), reduced translocation, or sequestration of the herbicide.

Management

Recommended strategies include rotating and mixing herbicides with different modes of action, integrating non-chemical control (tillage, cover crops, crop rotation, harvest weed-seed control), and cleaning machinery to limit spread. Researchers warn that no new herbicide mode of action has been commercialised in decades, making stewardship of existing chemistry critical.
