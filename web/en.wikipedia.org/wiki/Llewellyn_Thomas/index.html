Llewellyn Hilleth Thomas (21 October 1903 – 20 April 1992) was a British-American physicist and applied mathematician. Educated at Cambridge, where he worked in the circle around Ralph Fowler, he moved in 1929 to Ohio State University and spent much of his later career with IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center and at North Carolina State University.

Contributions

Thomas is best known for three results that bear his name. In 1926 he explained the anomalous fine structure of atomic spectra by the Thomas precession: a relativistic precession of an electron's spin as it orbits the nucleus, which supplies the factor of one-half missing from naive spin–orbit coupling calculations — a result reached just after the introduction of electron spin by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit. In 1927 he independently developed, at the same time as Enrico Fermi, the Thomas–Fermi model, a statistical treatment of the electron cloud in atoms that became a foundation of density functional thinking. In numerical mathematics, the Thomas algorithm (1949) is the standard fast method for solving tridiagonal systems of equations. He also worked on ballistics, shock waves and early electronic computing, and helped establish university computing centres during the 1950s. Thomas was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1958.
