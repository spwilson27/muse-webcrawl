Network science is an academic field which studies complex networks such as telecommunication networks, computer networks, biological networks, cognitive and semantic networks, and social networks, considering distinct elements or actors represented by nodes (or vertices) and the connections between the elements or actors as links (or edges). The field draws theories and methods from graph theory, statistical mechanics, data mining and information visualisation, and it grew rapidly from the late 1990s as large network data sets became available.

History

The mathematical study of networks began with Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution to the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, the founding work of graph theory. In the twentieth century, sociologists developed social network analysis, while Erdős and Rényi introduced the theory of random graphs. Stanley Milgram's small-world experiment (1967) suggested that any two people are connected by a surprisingly short chain of acquaintances — popularised as "six degrees of separation". Mark Granovetter's 1973 paper "The Strength of Weak Ties" showed the importance of loosely-knit connections in spreading information.

The modern era began with the Watts–Strogatz model of small-world networks (1998) and the Barabási–Albert model of scale-free networks generated by growth and preferential attachment (1999), which explained the power-law degree distributions found in many real networks.

Network measures

  • Degree — the number of links incident to a node.
  • Centrality — measures of node importance, including betweenness, closeness and eigenvector centrality (of which Google's PageRank is a variant).
  • Clustering coefficient — the extent to which a node's neighbours are also connected to each other.
  • Path length — the average number of steps along shortest paths between node pairs.
  • Community structure — the division of a network into densely connected groups, detected by algorithms such as modularity optimisation.

Applications

Network science is applied to epidemiology (modelling disease spread over contact networks), infrastructure resilience, systems biology (protein–protein interaction and metabolic networks), neuroscience (connectomics), economics and the study of the World Wide Web and online social platforms.
