Shigellosis

Shigellosis (historically also called bacillary dysentery) is an infection of the intestines caused by Shigella bacteria. Symptoms generally start one to two days after exposure and include diarrhoea, fever, abdominal pain, and feeling the urge to pass stools even when the bowels are empty. The diarrhoea may be bloody. Symptoms typically last five to seven days, though it may take several months before bowel habits return to normal.

Cause and transmission

Shigellosis is caused by four species of Shigella: S. dysenteriae, S. flexneri, S. boydii, and S. sonnei. The bacteria are spread by exposure to infected faeces, via contaminated food or water, contact with a contaminated object, flies, or direct person-to-person contact. The infectious dose is extremely low — as few as 10 to 100 organisms can cause disease — which makes person-to-person spread especially efficient. After ingestion, the bacteria invade the epithelial cells of the colon, multiply intracellularly, and spread from cell to cell, provoking intense inflammation, ulceration, and the characteristic bloody, mucoid stools. S. dysenteriae type 1 produces Shiga toxin, which can cause haemolytic uraemic syndrome.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis can be confirmed by stool culture or molecular tests. Treatment is primarily supportive: rest and adequate fluids by mouth to replace losses. Antibiotics can shorten the illness and reduce shedding, but growing antimicrobial resistance — including extensively drug-resistant S. sonnei — increasingly complicates therapy, and antibiotics are generally reserved for severe cases or high-risk patients. Antimotility agents are discouraged, as they may worsen the disease.

Epidemiology and prevention

Shigellosis occurs worldwide, but the burden is greatest in low- and middle-income countries, where it is a major cause of diarrhoeal death in young children. Estimates of annual deaths range from about 100,000 to over 200,000, most of them in children under five. Outbreaks also occur in high-income countries, in childcare centres, among travellers, and among men who have sex with men. Prevention is by proper hand washing, safe water, and sanitation; there is currently no licensed vaccine, although several candidates are in development. The genus is named after Kiyoshi Shiga, who identified S. dysenteriae during a Japanese dysentery epidemic in 1897.
