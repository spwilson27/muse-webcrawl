Weyl tensor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In differential geometry and general relativity, the Weyl tensor (or Weyl curvature tensor), named after Hermann Weyl, is a measure of the curvature of a spacetime that captures the part of the Riemann curvature tensor not determined by the local distribution of matter and energy. It describes tidal forces and gravitational waves — curvature that can exist even in vacuum.

Definition

The Riemann tensor decomposes into the Weyl tensor plus terms built from the Ricci tensor and scalar curvature. In four dimensions the Weyl tensor Cabcd has the same symmetries as the Riemann tensor but is completely traceless: all of its contractions vanish. In dimensions two and three the Weyl tensor vanishes identically; in dimensions four and higher it is the conformal part of the curvature.

Properties and significance

Because its trace-free part is unaffected by the local energy-momentum tensor, a non-zero Weyl tensor in empty space — for instance around a black hole or in a plane gravitational wave — produces relative acceleration between nearby freely falling particles without changing their total volume. Spacetimes are classified by the algebraic structure of their Weyl tensor in the Petrov classification. Roger Penrose's Weyl curvature hypothesis proposes that the Weyl tensor at the Big Bang was near zero, offering a possible geometric origin for the low entropy of the early universe and hence for the arrow of time.
