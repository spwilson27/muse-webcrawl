Montane grasslands and shrublands

Montane grasslands and shrublands are a biome defined by the World Wildlife Fund. The biome includes high-elevation (montane and alpine) grasslands and shrublands around the world, often above the tree line, where cold temperatures, wind, and thin soils prevent the growth of closed forest.

Settings

Representative regions include the páramo of the northern Andes, the puna of the central Andes, the alpine grasslands of the Ethiopian Highlands and East African mountains (with giant groundsel and lobelia rosettes), the subalpine heathlands of Mount Kinabalu and New Guinea, the Tibetan Plateau steppe, and the alpine tussock grasslands of New Zealand's Southern Alps. Tropical examples experience "summer every day, winter every night", with nightly frost even near the equator.

Flora and fauna

Plants are typically low-growing — tussock grasses, cushion plants, dwarf shrubs, and giant rosette plants such as the Andean frailejones (Espeletia) — adaptations to frost, drought, wind, and intense ultraviolet light. Animals include grazing ungulates such as vicuña and alpaca in the puna, mountain tapirs, spectacled bears, and high-elevation specialist birds such as the Andean condor.

Human use and threats

These ecosystems store water in soils and wetlands vital to downstream cities, and are used for livestock grazing and potato cultivation. They are threatened by overgrazing, burning, agricultural conversion, mining, and climate change, which is shifting the conditions that define them upslope.
