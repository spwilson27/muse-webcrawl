Snake venom

Snake venom is a highly toxic saliva containing over 20 different compounds, mostly proteins and polypeptides, used by venomous snakes to subdue prey and for self-defence. A complex mixture of proteins, enzymes and various other substances, venom can cause significant medical symptoms and death in humans.

Composition

Snake venoms consist largely of secreted toxins including neurotoxins, which attack the nervous system; hemotoxins, which disrupt blood clotting; cytotoxins, which destroy tissue; and cardiotoxins, which affect the heart. Enzymes such as phospholipase A2 and metalloproteinases are also common.

Evolution and function

Venom evolved in snakes primarily to immobilise and begin digesting prey. Venom composition varies greatly between species and even between populations, influenced by diet. Venomous snakes are found mainly in the families Elapidae, Viperidae, Atractaspididae and some Colubridae.

Medical significance

Snakebite envenoming kills an estimated 81,000 to 138,000 people per year worldwide, mainly in Africa, Asia and Latin America. The specific treatment is antivenom, produced by immunising animals such as horses with small doses of venom. Venom components are also studied as sources of drugs, including the ACE inhibitor captopril, derived from a Brazilian pit viper toxin.
