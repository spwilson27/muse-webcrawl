Good and evil

In most contexts, the concept of good denotes the conduct that should be preferred when posed with a choice between possible actions. Good is generally considered to be the opposite of evil and is of interest in the study of ethics, morality, religion, and philosophy. The specific meaning and etymology of the term and its associated translations among ancient and contemporary languages show substantial variation in inflection and meaning, depending on circumstances of place, history, religion, or philosophical context.

In religion

Many religions frame good and evil as cosmic forces or moral absolutes. Zoroastrianism presents a dualism between Ahura Mazda (good) and Angra Mainyu (evil). In the Abrahamic traditions, good is associated with obedience to God and evil with sin, raising the problem of evil: how an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God permits suffering — the question addressed by theodicies. Eastern traditions such as Buddhism and Hinduism more often analyse harmful action in terms of ignorance, karma, and suffering rather than metaphysical evil.

In philosophy

Philosophers dispute whether good is an objective property (moral realism), a projection of human attitudes (expressivism), or a term whose meaning varies by culture (moral relativism). Plato's Form of the Good, Aristotle's eudaimonia, Kant's categorical imperative, and utilitarianism's "greatest happiness" are classic attempts to ground the good. Meta-ethics asks what "good" means at all; G. E. Moore argued it is indefinable, and that defining it by natural properties commits the naturalistic fallacy.
