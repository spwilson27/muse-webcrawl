Homoousion

Homoousion (Greek: ὁμοούσιον, from ὁμός, "same", and οὐσία, "being" or "substance") is a Christian theological term meaning "of the same substance". In its adjectival form, homoousios, it was adopted by the First Council of Nicaea in 325 to affirm that Jesus Christ, the Son, is of the same substance as God the Father — fully God, not a created being. In English the doctrine is usually expressed as the Son being "consubstantial" with the Father, as in the Nicene Creed: "God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father".

The Arian controversy

The term stood at the centre of the Arian controversy. Arius taught that the Son was created by the Father and therefore of a different (or at most similar) substance, summarised in the slogan "there was when he was not". Athanasius of Alexandria championed homoousios as the only formula that excluded Arianism, arguing that salvation required the Son to be fully divine. The opposing compromise formula homoiousios ("of similar substance") differed by a single letter — the iota that gave rise to the phrase "not an iota of difference".

History and use

The word had earlier Gnostic and philosophical usages and had been condemned in a different sense at Antioch in 268 against Paul of Samosata, which made some bishops wary of it; at Nicaea it was pressed by the emperor Constantine's advisers and the Western bishops. After decades of controversy, the homoousian position prevailed at the Council of Constantinople in 381, where it was extended to the Holy Spirit, and it remains the touchstone of Trinitarian orthodoxy in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant churches. In the revised English translation of the Roman Missal (2011), the Latin consubstantialem Patri is rendered "consubstantial with the Father", replacing the older "one in Being with the Father".
