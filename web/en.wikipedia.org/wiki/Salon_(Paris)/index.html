The Paris Salon: paintings hung to the ceiling in the great annual exhibition

The Salon, or rarely the Paris Salon (French: Salon de Paris), beginning in 1667 was the official art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris. Between 1748 and 1890 it was arguably the greatest annual or biennial art event in the Western world, and admission to it — selected by a conservative jury — was essential to a successful artist's career in France.

History

The first exhibition was held in 1667 to display the work of the Academy's members; from 1737 the Salon became a regular public event in the Salon Carré of the Louvre, with explanatory booklets (livrets) and, increasingly, published art criticism — Denis Diderot's Salons (1759–1781) founded the genre. After the Revolution the exhibition was opened to all artists, but under the restored academic system of the 19th century the jury's hierarchy of genres privileged history painting over landscape and modern-life subjects.

The Salon and the avant-garde

Rejection by the Salon jury became a badge of honour for innovators. After mass rejections in 1863, Napoleon III authorised the Salon des Refusés, where Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe caused a sensation. The Impressionists bypassed the Salon with their own exhibitions from 1874. In 1881 the state handed the Salon to the Société des Artistes Français; rival exhibitions — the Salon d'Automne (1903), where Fauvism erupted in 1905, and the Salon des Indépendants — dispersed its authority.

Legacy

The Salon's crowded hanging style, prize system (including the Prix de Rome), and role as arbiter of taste defined the 19th-century art world; the term "salon" spread to exhibitions elsewhere and even to academic conferences in other fields.
