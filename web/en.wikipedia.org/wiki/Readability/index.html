Readability

Readability is the ease with which a reader can understand a written text. It depends on the complexity of the vocabulary and syntax, the presentation and typography, and the reader's own knowledge and motivation. Since the 1920s, researchers have developed readability formulas that estimate comprehension difficulty from surface features such as sentence length and syllables per word.

Common formulas

  • The Flesch Reading Ease score (1948) rates text on a 100-point scale; its companion Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level converts difficulty into a U.S. school grade.
  • The Gunning fog index estimates the years of education needed to understand a text on first reading.
  • The SMOG grade and Coleman–Liau index use polysyllable counts and characters per word respectively.

Applications and criticism

Readability scores are used in education to match books to readers, in government and health care to ensure public documents are accessible, and in some jurisdictions as legal requirements for insurance policies and official forms. Critics note that formulas ignore cohesion, structure, interest and background knowledge, and that mechanically shortening sentences can produce choppy prose without improving comprehension. Nevertheless, the tests remain widely used as rough guides for plain-language editing.
