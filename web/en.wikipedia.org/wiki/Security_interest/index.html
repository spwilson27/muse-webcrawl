Security interest

Security interest
FieldProperty law, commercial law
Governing law (U.S.)Uniform Commercial Code, Article 9
CollateralPersonal property, fixtures, intangible assets
RelatedLien, mortgage, pledge, charge

A security interest is a legal claim on collateral pledged by a borrower to secure a debt or other obligation. It gives the secured party (usually a lender) the right, upon default, to take and sell the collateral to satisfy the obligation. Security interests allow borrowers to obtain credit at lower cost because the lender's risk is reduced by recourse to identifiable assets.

United States law

In the United States, security interests in personal property are governed by Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), adopted in some form by every state. A security interest attaches (becomes enforceable against the debtor) when value has been given, the debtor has rights in the collateral, and the debtor has authenticated a security agreement describing the collateral. It is perfected — made effective against third parties — most commonly by filing a financing statement (UCC-1) in a public registry, by the secured party's possession of the collateral, or automatically for certain purchase-money security interests in consumer goods. Priority among competing claimants is generally determined by the rule "first to file or perfect."

On default, a secured party may repossess collateral without breach of the peace and dispose of it in a commercially reasonable manner, applying the proceeds to the debt; any surplus goes to the debtor, and the debtor usually remains liable for a deficiency. Secured claims receive special treatment in bankruptcy, typically ranking ahead of unsecured creditors up to the value of the collateral.

Other jurisdictions

English law historically recognized several distinct devices — the mortgage, charge, pledge, and contractual lien — categorized as fixed or floating charges, with registration requirements under companies legislation. Canada and New Zealand consolidated these under Personal Property Security Acts modelled partly on Article 9, and many civil-law jurisdictions have adopted similar functional, registry-based systems in secured-transactions reforms.
