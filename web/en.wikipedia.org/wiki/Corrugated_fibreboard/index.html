Corrugated fibreboard

[Image placeholder: corrugated board structure]

Corrugated fibreboard, commonly called corrugated cardboard, is a paper-based material consisting of a fluted corrugated sheet (the "medium") glued between one or two flat linerboards. It is widely used in the manufacture of shipping containers and packaging because it combines light weight with considerable rigidity and stacking strength, especially in the direction perpendicular to the flutes.

Corrugated paper was patented in England in 1856 for use as a liner in tall hats; its use for wrapping and boxes developed in the 1870s–1890s, and the first machine for producing corrugated board was built in 1874. Modern production uses a corrugator line in which the medium is formed into flutes over heated corrugating rolls and bonded to the liners with a starch-based adhesive.

Structure and grades

Flutes are designated by letter: common profiles include A-flute (about 4.8 mm thick), B-flute (about 3.2 mm), C-flute (about 4.0 mm) and the finer E- and F-flutes used for retail packaging. Board may be single-face, single-wall (three layers), double-wall (five layers) or triple-wall (seven layers). Strength is measured by tests such as the edge crush test (ECT) and the Mullen burst test. Corrugated fibreboard is highly recyclable and is recovered at high rates in most industrialised countries.
