A book review is a form of literary criticism in which a book is merely described (summary review) or analyzed based on content, style, and merit (critical review). Book reviews are published in newspapers, magazines, dedicated review journals, academic journals, and online; they may be written by professional critics, scholars, or — in trade publications such as Kirkus Reviews and Publishers Weekly — anonymously by staff reviewers for the book trade.

Functions

Reviews serve several audiences at once: readers deciding what to buy or borrow, librarians and booksellers selecting stock, and publishers gauging reception. A review typically summarizes the book's argument or plot, evaluates its quality and significance, and situates it among comparable works. Trade reviews appear before publication, based on advance reading copies, and can influence bookstore orders and library purchasing.

History

Reviewing grew with the periodical press in the 18th century, when journals such as the Monthly Review (1749) and Critical Review (1756) made reviewing a profession. The 19th and 20th centuries saw prominent review supplements and quarterlies; the founding of the New York Review of Books in 1963 and the London Review of Books in 1979 extended the form toward the long review-essay. Since the 2000s, reader reviews on retail and social platforms (Amazon, Goodreads) and video and blog criticism have vastly expanded who reviews, prompting debate about the authority of professional critics.

Criticism of reviewing

Reviewing is periodically accused of logrolling, conflicts of interest, reviewer bias, and overreliance on star ratings and blurbs. Scholarly book reviews, by contrast, are a form of post-publication peer evaluation and are governed by academic norms.
