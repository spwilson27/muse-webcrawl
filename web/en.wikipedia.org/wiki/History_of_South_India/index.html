History of South India

The history of South India covers the southern part of the Indian subcontinent, roughly the region south of the Vindhya range, including the modern states of Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana. Its history is distinguished by the early Tamil Sangam literature, the great temple-building dynasties of the Cholas, Cheras, and Pandyas, the Deccan empires of the Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas, the Vijayanagara Empire, and lucrative maritime trade with Rome, Arabia, and Southeast Asia.

Prehistory and early history

South India has been inhabited since the Palaeolithic era, and its Neolithic ashmound cultures of the Deccan were among the earliest farming communities in India. By the early centuries BCE, the region participated in the Iron Age megalithic culture, and ports on both coasts traded with the Roman world — Roman coins and amphorae have been found at sites such as Arikamedu.

Sangam age

The Sangam literature of ancient Tamilakam, composed roughly between 300 BCE and 300 CE, describes the three crowned dynasties — the Cheras, Cholas, and Pandyas — and a society of ports, poets, and warrior chiefs. The period saw flourishing trade in pepper, pearls, and textiles, described in Greco-Roman sources such as the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea.

Medieval kingdoms

From the 6th century CE, the Pallavas of Kanchipuram pioneered rock-cut and structural stone temple architecture at Mahabalipuram. In the Deccan, the Chalukyas of Badami and their successors, the Rashtrakutas, built the cave temples of Badami, Aihole, Pattadakal, and Ellora. The imperial Chola Empire (9th–13th centuries) reached its height under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I, whose navy campaigned as far as Srivijaya in Southeast Asia and whose Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur (1010 CE) is a masterpiece of Dravidian architecture. Later powers included the Hoysalas, the Kakatiyas, and the Pandyas of Madurai.

Vijayanagara and the Deccan sultanates

The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646), founded by Harihara and Bukka, became the dominant power of the south, patronising the splendid capital at Hampi. Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) was its greatest ruler. The empire's power was shattered at the Battle of Talikota (1565) by an alliance of the Deccan sultanates — Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmadnagar, Bidar, and Berar — which themselves fell to the Mughal Empire in the 17th century.

Colonial period

European trading companies — Portuguese, Dutch, French, and English — established coastal enclaves from the 16th century. The Carnatic Wars of the 18th century saw Britain defeat France for supremacy in the south. Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan resisted the British East India Company through four Anglo-Mysore Wars until Tipu's death at Seringapatam in 1799. The south was organised into the Madras Presidency and princely states such as Travancore, Cochin, Hyderabad, and Mysore.

Modern period

South India played a major role in the Indian independence movement and, after 1947, in the linguistic reorganisation of states (1956), which created states along language lines: Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam. The region later became known for Dravidian politics in Tamil Nadu, land reform in Kerala, and — from the 1990s — the rise of Bengaluru and Hyderabad as global technology centres.
