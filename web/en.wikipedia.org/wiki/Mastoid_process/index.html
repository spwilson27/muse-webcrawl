Mastoid process

The mastoid process is a conical prominence projecting from the undersurface of the mastoid portion of the temporal bone of the skull, located just behind the external ear. Its name derives from its breast-like shape (Greek mastos, "breast"). It can be felt as a hard bony bump behind the earlobe.

Structure

The mastoid process is largely hollow, containing a system of interconnected air-filled cavities called mastoid air cells, which communicate with the middle ear cavity through the mastoid antrum and aditus. The process is poorly developed at birth — one reason the facial nerve is relatively superficial and vulnerable in infants — and enlarges during early childhood as the air cells expand and the neck muscles that attach to it begin to act on it. Its size varies considerably between individuals and is generally larger in males, making it a feature used in forensic anthropology to estimate the sex of skeletal remains.

Muscle attachments

The process provides attachment for several muscles of the neck and head, including the sternocleidomastoid, the posterior belly of the digastric (which grooves its medial surface as the mastoid notch), the splenius capitis and the longissimus capitis. These muscles act in turning and flexing the head.

Clinical significance

Because the mastoid air cells communicate with the middle ear, infections of the middle ear (otitis media) can spread into them, causing mastoiditis, an inflammation that presents with pain, swelling and tenderness behind the ear. Before antibiotics, mastoiditis was a common and dangerous complication, sometimes spreading to the brain or the sigmoid sinus; surgical removal of infected bone (mastoidectomy) may still be required in severe cases. The process is also a landmark in skull-base surgery and for cochlear implant placement.
