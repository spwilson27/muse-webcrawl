Sixtus Affair

The Sixtus Affair was a failed attempt at secret peace negotiations between Austria-Hungary and France during the First World War, conducted in 1917 through Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma, an officer in the Belgian army and brother-in-law of the Austro-Hungarian emperor Charles I. Its public exposure in April 1918 destroyed the credibility of Austria-Hungary's foreign minister, Count Ottokar Czernin, and bound Vienna more tightly to Germany.

The mission

Through his mother-in-law connections and with the knowledge of Empress Zita, Charles I authorised his brothers-in-law, Princes Sixtus and Xavier of Bourbon-Parma, to carry a secret letter to French president Raymond Poincaré in March 1917. The letter, handwritten by Charles, pledged that he would "use all means" and his "personal influence" to support France's "just claims" to Alsace-Lorraine, promised Belgium's restoration, and spoke sympathetically of Serbia. Meetings in Switzerland and Paris followed, and the initiative initially extended to soundings with Britain and Italy — but Italy's territorial demands on Austria made any agreement impossible, and the talks quietly lapsed over the summer of 1917.

Exposure and consequences

In April 1918 Czernin publicly boasted that France had sought negotiations on terms Austria could reject, provoking French premier Georges Clemenceau to publish Charles's secret letter. Charles denied its authenticity, but the affair forced Czernin's resignation, humiliated the emperor, and led Germany to impose the humiliating Spa agreements of May 1918, subordinating Austria-Hungary still further. The episode convinced the Allies that Austria-Hungary could not break from Berlin, hardening their policy toward the Habsburg monarchy in the war's final year.
