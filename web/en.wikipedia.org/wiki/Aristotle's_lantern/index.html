Aristotle's lantern

[Image: Aristotle's lantern — The jaw apparatus of a sea urchin, showing the five teeth and supporting ossicles]

Aristotle's lantern is the name for the jaw or chewing apparatus of most sea urchins. It was first described by Aristotle in his History of Animals, who likened its shape to that of a horn lantern with the panes of horn left out — the five calcareous teeth converging like the frame of the lamp.

Anatomy

The lantern is a complex musculo-skeletal structure situated inside the test (shell) at the centre of the oral (lower) surface. It consists of about 40 calcareous ossicles, of which the principal pieces are five large interlocking pyramids (the hemipyramids) that bear the five continually growing teeth; the teeth emerge through the mouth and are used to scrape algae from rocks, graze seagrass, or even excavate burrows in rock and coral. Muscles protrude and retract the lantern and move the teeth, allowing the urchin to bite and chew. In sand dollars and heart urchins the lantern is reduced or modified, and in some groups it is absent in adults.

Function and development

The lantern develops after metamorphosis from the larval stage and grows throughout the urchin's life; the teeth, composed of magnesium-rich calcite, are continually replaced as they wear. The apparatus is a principal reason for the ecological importance of urchins as grazers controlling algal growth on reefs.
