A zoom lens is a type of camera lens whose focal length — and therefore angle of view — can be varied continuously within a specified range, as opposed to a fixed-focal-length prime lens. Zoom lenses allow a photographer to change composition without moving position or swapping lenses, and are standard on cameras from compacts to cinema systems.

Design

A true zoom (parfocal) lens maintains focus while its focal length changes, achieved by moving groups of lens elements along the optical axis in coordinated paths. Simpler "varifocal" lenses shift focus when zoomed and must be refocused. Modern designs use multiple element groups, aspherical elements, and low-dispersion glass to control aberrations across the range. The ratio between longest and shortest focal lengths is called the zoom ratio; consumer "superzoom" lenses exceed 10×, typically at the cost of maximum aperture and image quality.

History

Early zoom designs appeared in the early twentieth century, with the Zoomar lens of the 1940s–50s bringing zoom to television and cinema. The Voigtländer Zoomar of 1959 was an early zoom for still photography. Zoom lenses became dominant in the 1980s and 1990s as computer-aided design improved their quality, and standard zooms such as 24–70 mm and 70–200 mm became professional staples.

Applications

Zoom lenses are used in photography, cinematography, television broadcasting, surveillance, and projection. In cinema and broadcast, parfocal zooms permit the "zoom shot" and the combined dolly-zoom effect popularised by Alfred Hitchcock's Vertigo.
