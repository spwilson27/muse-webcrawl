Monel
TypeNickel–copper alloy
Principal elementsNickel (52–67%), copper, iron, manganese
Notable gradesMonel 400, Monel K-500

Monel is a group of nickel alloys, primarily composed of nickel (up to 67%) and copper, with small amounts of iron, manganese, carbon, and silicon. It is a trademark of Special Metals Corporation. Monel was created by Robert Crooks Stanley of the International Nickel Company (INCO) and patented in 1906; the name is a truncated form of that of company president Ambrose Monell.

Properties

Monel is stronger than pure nickel and is especially resistant to corrosion by seawater, rapidly flowing brackish water, and many acids, including hydrofluoric acid. It is fabricable, weldable, and resistant to pitting and stress-corrosion cracking. Monel 400 is the standard grade, while the age-hardenable Monel K-500 offers greater strength and hardness through the addition of aluminium and titanium.

Applications

Monel is used in marine engineering (propeller shafts, pumps, valves and seawater piping), chemical and hydrocarbon processing, oil production, heat exchangers, and musical instrument strings — notably for bass guitar and early electric guitar strings. It was famously used for the roofing of the original Pennsylvania Station in New York and on the wristwatch-case-like nameplates of military identification tags.
