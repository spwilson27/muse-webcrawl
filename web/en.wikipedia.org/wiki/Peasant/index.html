Peasant

Peasants formed the backbone of agrarian economies for millennia.

A peasant is a member of a traditional class of farmers, either labouring on land they own or working as agricultural labourers on land owned by others, especially in a pre-industrialised society. Peasants typically practise subsistence agriculture, producing chiefly for their own household's needs, with any surplus going to rents, taxes, or the market.

Characteristics

Historians and anthropologists describe peasant societies through features such as the household as the basic unit of production, a mixture of farming with crafts and seasonal labour, strong village community structures, and a subordinate position relative to a landowning or state elite that extracts surplus through rent, tribute, or taxation. The word derives from Old French paisant — a countryman, from pais, countryside — ultimately from Latin pagus, a rural district (the same root as "pagan").

History

Peasantries appeared with the Neolithic adoption of agriculture and formed the great majority of the population in most civilisations until industrialisation. In feudal Europe many peasants were serfs, bound to the land; elsewhere peasants might be free tenants or communal landholders. Peasant revolts — such as the English Peasants' Revolt of 1381 or the German Peasants' War of 1524–25 — periodically shook agrarian states. In the 20th century, peasant movements were central to revolutions in Russia, China, Mexico, and Vietnam, while collectivisation and industrialisation shrank the peasantry drastically. Smallholder farmers remain numerous in much of Africa and Asia today, and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Peasants was adopted in 2018.
