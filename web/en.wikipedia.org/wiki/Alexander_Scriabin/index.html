Alexander Nikolayevich Scriabin (6 January 1872 – 27 April 1915) was a Russian composer and virtuoso pianist. Initially influenced by Chopin and Liszt, he developed an increasingly atonal and mystical musical language, becoming one of the most innovative — and controversial — figures in Russian music before the First World War.

Life

Scriabin studied at the Moscow Conservatory, graduating in 1892 alongside his rival Sergei Rachmaninoff. An injury to his right hand, sustained through over-practising, led him to compose works for the left hand alone, including the Prelude and Nocturne, Op. 9. He toured widely as a pianist and, after a period teaching at the Moscow Conservatory, lived largely in Western Europe before returning to Russia in 1909.

Music and mysticism

Scriabin's ten piano sonatas trace a path from lush late Romanticism to the compressed, ecstatic language of his late works. His orchestral masterpieces include The Poem of Ecstasy (Op. 54, 1908) and Prometheus: The Poem of Fire (Op. 60, 1910), the latter written for orchestra with a part for a colour organ projecting hues corresponding to the music — Scriabin, who experienced synesthesia, assigned colours to keys. Influenced by Theosophy and his own grandiose philosophy, he planned a never-completed multi-sensory ritual work, the Mysterium, intended to be performed in the Himalayas and to usher in a new world. His late harmony, built on the so-called "mystic chord" of fourths, anticipates later atonal practice. He died in Moscow in 1915 of septicaemia from a boil on his lip.
