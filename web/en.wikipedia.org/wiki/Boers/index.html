Boers (Afrikaans: Boere, "farmers") are the descendants of the free burghers who settled in the eastern Cape Colony frontier in Dutch South Africa in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, speaking a language that evolved into Afrikaans. The Boers developed a distinct frontier culture — pastoral, Calvinist, and fiercely independent — and in the nineteenth century founded the Boer republics of the interior. In modern usage the term often overlaps with "Afrikaner," though some prefer "Boer" to stress descent from the republic-era farming population as distinct from the Cape-based "Afrikaner" identity.

History

From 1652 the Dutch East India Company settled farmers ("vrijburgers") near Cape Town; augmented by German soldiers and French Huguenot refugees after 1688, they expanded inland as trekboers, semi-nomadic stock farmers living beyond effective colonial administration. Conflicts with the Xhosa on the eastern frontier and with British rule after Britain seized the Cape (1795, definitively 1806) — over language policy, missionary influence, and the abolition of slavery in 1834 — led to the Great Trek of the 1830s–40s, in which Voortrekkers established republics in the interior.

The Boers repelled Britain in the First Boer War (1880–81) but were defeated in the Second Boer War (1899–1902), after which their republics were absorbed into the Union of South Africa (1910). Boer generals such as Louis Botha and Jan Smuts then became South African statesmen; other Boers formed the backbone of the National Party that instituted apartheid in 1948.

Culture

Traditional Boer life centered on the farm, family, and the Dutch Reformed Church, with commandos — mounted citizen militias — as its military institution. Symbols such as the ox-wagon, the Day of the Covenant (commemorating the 1838 Battle of Blood River), and the Voortrekker Monument in Pretoria occupy an important place in Boer/Afrikaner memory. Afrikaans, long dismissed as a "kitchen Dutch," was standardized and became an official language in 1925.
