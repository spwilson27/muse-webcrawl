Overview

Cartography is the study and practice of making maps — the graphic representation of geographic space. It joins the science of geodesy, which fixes the positions being mapped, to design, because every map must select, simplify and symbolise a world too rich to show in full. A map is always an argument about what matters.

The craft is ancient: Babylonian clay tablets carry town plans and a diagrammatic world map, and Greek scholars from Anaximander to Eratosthenes and Ptolemy founded mathematical cartography, with latitude, longitude and projections.

From manuscript to satellite

Medieval Europe produced symbolic mappaemundi and, in the Mediterranean, strikingly accurate portolan sailing charts; the Renaissance printed atlases of Mercator and Ortelius made maps a commercial product. State surveys of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries mapped whole nations by triangulation. The twentieth century added aerial photography and then digital geographic information systems; today most cartography is done in software, and the most-read maps are web maps rendered on demand — still, underneath, an exercise in projection, generalisation and honest symbol design.
