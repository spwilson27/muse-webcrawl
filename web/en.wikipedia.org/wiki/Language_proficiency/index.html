Language proficiency is the ability of an individual to use a language for communicative purposes. Proficiency is usually described across the four skills of listening, speaking, reading, and writing, and encompasses accuracy, fluency, vocabulary range, and pragmatic appropriateness. It is distinguished from language aptitude (the capacity to learn) and from knowledge about a language; someone may be highly proficient without formal study, or well informed about grammar without speaking fluently.

Assessment frameworks

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), published by the Council of Europe, grades proficiency in six levels: A1 and A2 (basic user), B1 and B2 (independent user), and C1 and C2 (proficient user), with "can-do" descriptors for each skill. In the United States, the ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines describe levels from Novice to Distinguished, and the Interagency Language Roundtable (ILR) scale, used by the US government, runs from 0 (no proficiency) to 5 (functionally native).

Testing

Standardized tests operationalize proficiency for education, employment, and immigration decisions. Widely used examinations include IELTS and TOEFL for English, DELF/DALF for French, the Goethe-Zertifikat for German, HSK for Chinese, and JLPT for Japanese. Test validity is a subject of research: critics note that high-stakes tests measure test-taking constructs that may not fully reflect real-world communicative ability, a concern addressed by frameworks such as Bachman's model of communicative language ability.

Proficiency and bilingualism

Proficiency in bilingual and multilingual speakers is typically uneven across languages and domains — a speaker may read a heritage language well but speak it haltingly. Researchers such as François Grosjean argue proficiency should be understood relative to the uses each language serves in a person's life rather than against a monolingual native-speaker ideal.
