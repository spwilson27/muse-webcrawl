In a musical composition, a chord progression or harmonic progression (informally chord changes, changes, or simply "the progression") is a succession of chords. Chord progressions are the foundation of harmony in much of Western music, from Bach chorales to jazz standards and popular songs: they establish or contradict a tonality, and they structure melodies built on top of them.

Notation

In classical theory, chords built on scale degrees are labelled with Roman numerals: uppercase for major, lowercase for minor (for example, I–IV–V in major). Jazz musicians use charts of chord symbols, while the Nashville Number System uses Arabic numerals to allow instant transposition. In popular music, progressions are often described by looping cycles of two, three, or four chords.

Common progressions

  • ii–V–I — the fundamental cadential progression of jazz, driving strongly to the tonic.
  • I–V–vi–IV (and its rotations, such as vi–IV–I–V, the "sensitive female chord progression") — underpinning hundreds of pop songs.
  • Twelve-bar blues — the I–IV–V framework of blues, rock and roll, and rhythm and blues.
  • Andalusian cadence — i–♭VII–♭VI–V in minor, associated with flamenco.
  • Pachelbel progression — the I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V sequence of Pachelbel's Canon.
  • '50s progression (I–vi–IV–V) — the doo-wop changes.
  • Rhythm changes — the 32-bar AABA progression derived from Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm."

Function and voice leading

In tonal theory, chords are grouped into tonic, subdominant (predominant), and dominant functions; progressions typically move from tonic stability through tension to dominant instability and back, with the dominant seventh resolving to the tonic at cadences. Smooth voice leading — moving each voice by the smallest possible interval — gives progressions their sense of inevitability, while substitutions (such as the tritone substitution) allow harmonic colour without changing function.
