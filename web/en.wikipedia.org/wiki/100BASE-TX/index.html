100BASE-TX

100BASE-TX runs over two pairs of Category 5 unshielded twisted-pair cable.

100BASE-TX is the most common form of Fast Ethernet, providing data transmission speeds of 100 Mbit/s over twisted-pair cabling. It was introduced in 1995 as part of the IEEE 802.3u standard and became the dominant wired local area network technology of the late 1990s, succeeding 10BASE-T.

Technical details

100BASE-TX uses two of the four pairs in Category 5 (or better) unshielded twisted-pair (UTP) cable: one pair for transmitting and one for receiving, terminated with 8P8C (RJ-45) connectors. The maximum segment length is 100 metres (328 ft). Signalling uses the 4B5B line code combined with MLT-3 encoding, a scheme adapted from the ANSI X3T9.5 standard developed for FDDI over copper (CDDI). The resulting symbol rate is 125 MBaud.

Operation

Like other Ethernet physical layers of its era, 100BASE-TX originally operated in half-duplex mode using CSMA/CD when connected through a hub, but with the widespread adoption of network switches it is normally run in full-duplex mode, allowing simultaneous 100 Mbit/s transmission in both directions. Autonegotiation allows a port to select the fastest mode supported by both link partners.

Related standards

Sibling standards in the 802.3u family include 100BASE-T4, which runs over four pairs of Category 3 cable, and 100BASE-FX, which operates over optical fibre. 100BASE-TX was itself largely superseded in new installations by 1000BASE-T (Gigabit Ethernet), which uses all four pairs of Category 5e cable but remains backward compatible with 100BASE-TX equipment.
