Philippe Barbier

Born: Philippe Antoine Barbier, March 2, 1848, Luzy, France
Died: September 18, 1922, Bandol, France
Field: Organic chemistry
Known for: Barbier reaction

Barbier's name reaction paved the way for Grignard reagents.

Philippe Antoine Barbier (2 March 1848 – 18 September 1922) was a French organic chemist best known for the Barbier reaction, published in 1899, in which an alkyl halide reacts with a carbonyl compound in the presence of a metal such as magnesium to form a new carbon–carbon bond in a single step.

Career

Barbier studied in Paris and worked under Berthelot and Friedel before taking a professorship at the University of Lyon, where he spent most of his career. His research ranged over terpene chemistry and the synthesis of alcohols and hydrocarbons. His student Victor Grignard developed the two-step version of Barbier's method — preforming organomagnesium halides, the Grignard reagents — and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912, an award whose omission of Barbier was long remarked upon.

Legacy

The Barbier reaction remains a standard method in organic synthesis, and its modern variants using metals such as zinc, indium, and samarium are valued for their tolerance of water and functional groups.
