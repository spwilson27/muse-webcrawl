False-belief task

The false-belief task, also called the Sally–Anne test, is a psychological test used to assess a child's development of theory of mind — the ability to attribute beliefs to others that may differ from one's own knowledge of reality. It was developed by Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith in 1985, building on work by Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner.

Procedure

In the classic Sally–Anne version, a child is shown two dolls. Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves the room. Anne then moves the marble to her own box. When Sally returns, the child is asked where Sally will look for her marble. A child who answers "the basket" understands that Sally holds a false belief; a child who answers "the box" responds based on their own knowledge of the marble's actual location.

Findings

Typically developing children usually pass the task around age four to five. Baron-Cohen, Leslie, and Frith found that most children with autism, and a minority of typically developing and Down syndrome controls, failed the task, a result that motivated the "mindblindness" theory of autism. Later research using anticipatory-looking measures has suggested that infants may show implicit sensitivity to false beliefs much earlier, sparking debate about whether the classic task underestimates young children's competence or taps different abilities.
