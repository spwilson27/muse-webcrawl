Hausdorff paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hausdorff paradox is a paradox in mathematics named after Felix Hausdorff. It states that there exists a countable subset C of the sphere S2 such that the remainder, S2 ∖ C, can be partitioned into three disjoint sets A, B1 and B2 such that A, B1 and B2 are all congruent to one another, and both A and the union B1 ∪ B2 are congruent to the whole remainder — that is, the sphere minus a countable set can be "doubled" by rotations alone.

Hausdorff published the result in 1914 in his book Grundzüge der Mengenlehre. The paradox relies essentially on the axiom of choice: the sets involved cannot be Lebesgue measurable, so the paradox does not contradict the behaviour of ordinary volume.

Statement and proof sketch

The construction uses the free group on two generators, realised as a group of rotations of the sphere. The rotation group SO(3) contains a free subgroup F on two generators σ and τ. The free group admits a paradoxical decomposition: it splits into pieces that can be reassembled, using left multiplication by group elements, into two copies of the whole group. Acting on the sphere, this decomposition descends to the sphere minus the countable set of points fixed by non-identity elements of F, because all other points have trivial stabiliser and their orbits are in bijection with the group itself.

Relation to the Banach–Tarski paradox

The Hausdorff paradox was the key step toward the Banach–Tarski paradox (1924), which extended the construction from the sphere's surface to the solid ball: using the Hausdorff decomposition together with the fact that the countable exceptional set can be absorbed on the ball, Banach and Tarski showed a ball can be partitioned into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls identical to the original.
