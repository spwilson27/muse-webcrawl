Oxford

Oxford is a city in England, the county town of Oxfordshire, situated about 56 miles (90 km) north-west of London at the confluence of the River Thames (known locally as the Isis) and the River Cherwell. With a population of roughly 162,000, it is best known as the home of the University of Oxford, the oldest university in the English-speaking world, whose colleges and honey-coloured stone buildings have earned Oxford the nickname "the city of dreaming spires", coined by the poet Matthew Arnold.

History

Oxford began as a Saxon settlement at a ford for oxen across the Thames — hence Oxenaforda, "ford of the oxen" — and is first recorded in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle for 911 AD. A fortified burh under Alfred the Great's successors, it was repeatedly burned during Danish invasions. Teaching existed in Oxford by 1096, and the university grew rapidly after 1167, when Henry II banned English students from attending the University of Paris. The university's development brought frequent friction with the town, culminating in the St Scholastica Day riot of 1355. Oxford was a royalist stronghold and Charles I's capital during the English Civil War. In the 20th century the city also became an industrial centre: William Morris (later Lord Nuffield) built his motor works at Cowley, which produced Morris and later Mini cars, and remains a major BMW Mini plant.

The University

The University of Oxford is a collegiate university comprising 39 constituent colleges and a range of academic departments. It regularly ranks among the top universities in the world and has educated dozens of British prime ministers, numerous heads of state, and many Nobel laureates. Its institutions include the Bodleian Library, one of the oldest libraries in Europe; the Ashmolean Museum, Britain's first public museum (opened 1683); the Oxford University Press, the world's largest university press; and the Sheldonian Theatre and Radcliffe Camera, landmarks of the historic centre.

Economy and culture

Beyond the university, Oxford's economy includes publishing (Oxford University Press, Wiley-Blackwell), car manufacturing at Cowley, a growing science and technology sector centred on science parks around the city, healthcare through the John Radcliffe Hospital, and tourism attracted by the university's architecture and literary associations — from Lewis Carroll and J. R. R. Tolkien to Inspector Morse. Oxford Brookes University gives the city a second university. The city is also famous for rowing: the annual Boat Race against Cambridge starts in London, but Oxford's own regattas, including Summer Eights, dominate the Isis in term time.

Transport

Oxford railway station offers services to London Paddington and Marylebone, and the city is linked to the capital by the M40 motorway and frequent coach services. The historic centre restricts car use with some of the earliest park-and-ride schemes in Britain and, since 2022, a Zero Emission Zone pilot in the city centre.
