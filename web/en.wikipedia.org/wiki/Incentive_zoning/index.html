Incentive zoning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Incentive zoning is a system of land-use regulation used by municipalities, most notably in the United States, in which developers are rewarded with additional building rights — typically extra floor area or height beyond what zoning rules would normally allow — in exchange for providing public benefits such as plazas, affordable housing, childcare facilities, or transit improvements.

History

The technique was pioneered by New York City in its 1961 Zoning Resolution, which allowed developers a floor-area bonus for providing a publicly accessible plaza at street level. The policy produced hundreds of "privately owned public spaces" (POPS) across the city. Its first conspicuous product was the plaza of the Seagram Building model applied citywide; later audits, including a major study published in 2000, found that many of the spaces were poorly maintained, obstructed, or effectively closed to the public, prompting tightened design and enforcement standards.

Criticism and use elsewhere

Critics argue that incentive zoning can amount to selling public planning authority, yielding windfall density without commensurate benefit, and that the amenities provided are often of low value relative to the bonus granted. Supporters respond that it harnesses private development to deliver public goods at no direct cost to taxpayers. Variants are used in cities such as Seattle, San Francisco, Chicago, and Toronto, and related mechanisms underlie inclusionary zoning programmes for affordable housing.
