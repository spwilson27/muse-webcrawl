Beatification

Tradition: Roman Catholic Church
Title conferred: Blessed
Next stage: Canonization

Beatification (from Latin beatus, "blessed", and facere, "to make") is a recognition accorded by the Catholic Church of a deceased person's entrance into Heaven and capacity to intercede on behalf of individuals who pray in their name. A person who is beatified is given the title "Blessed" and may be venerated locally, within the orders or dioceses associated with them; beatification is the third of the four steps in the process of canonization.

Process

The stages are: Servant of God, Venerable (recognition of heroic virtue or martyrdom), Blessed, and Saint. Ordinarily, beatification requires proof of one miracle attributed to the candidate's intercession after death — except for martyrs, who may be beatified by recognition of their martyrdom alone. The cause is investigated by the diocese and then by the Vatican's Dicastery for the Causes of Saints; the pope approves the decree, and the beatification ceremony is usually celebrated in the place associated with the blessed's life, often by a papal representative.

History

In the early Church, local churches honoured their martyrs and confessors; from the 10th century the approval of cults was progressively reserved to bishops and then to the papacy. Pope Urban VIII's decrees of 1634 and the codification by Benedict XIV in the 18th century established the modern procedure. Pope John Paul II greatly increased the number of beatifications and in 2005 Benedict XVI restored the practice of ceremonies presided over by a cardinal rather than the pope himself.
