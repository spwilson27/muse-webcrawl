Substituted amphetamine

Substituted amphetamines are a class of compounds based upon the amphetamine structure, formally alpha-methylphenethylamine. The class includes a wide range of pharmacological agents: stimulants, empathogens and hallucinogens among them. Amphetamine itself is the parent compound, and members of the class are produced by adding substituents to its phenethylamine skeleton — on the phenyl ring, the alpha or beta carbons, or the terminal nitrogen.

Structure

The amphetamine core consists of a phenyl ring joined by a two-carbon side chain to an amine, with a methyl group on the alpha carbon. Substitutions on the aromatic ring (at the 2-, 3-, 4- or 5-positions, commonly methoxy or methylenedioxy groups) tend to shift activity toward serotonergic and hallucinogenic effects, while N-substitutions and alpha-ethyl homologues modify stimulant potency and duration.

Examples

Well-known members include methamphetamine (N-methylamphetamine), MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxy-N-methylamphetamine), MDA, 4-fluoroamphetamine, cathinone and its derivatives from the khat plant, and the prodrug lisdexamfetamine. The class also encompasses many compounds developed as research chemicals and "designer drugs". The related substituted cathinones share the beta-keto modification of the same skeleton.

Pharmacology

Most substituted amphetamines act as monoamine releasing agents or reuptake inhibitors at the dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin transporters (DAT, NET and SERT). The relative balance of activity at these transporters largely determines whether a compound is predominantly a stimulant, an empathogen-entactogen or a psychedelic, though several also act directly at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.

Legal status

Many substituted amphetamines are controlled substances internationally under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971, and national analogue legislation in several countries extends controls to structurally related compounds not individually listed.
