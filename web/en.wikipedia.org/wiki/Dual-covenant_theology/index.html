Dual-covenant theology

The two covenants: Sinai and the New Covenant

Dual-covenant theology is a Christian theological position, held by a minority of Christians, which holds that the Mosaic covenant between God and the Jewish people remains valid and salvific alongside the New Covenant established through Jesus Christ. Under this view, Jews do not need to convert to Christianity to be in right relationship with God, because God's covenant with Israel, made at Mount Sinai, was never revoked.

Background

The position emerged principally in the aftermath of the Holocaust, when many Christian theologians reconsidered traditional supersessionism — the doctrine that the Church has replaced Israel as God's covenant people. Franz Rosenzweig's philosophy, developed earlier in the twentieth century, is often cited as a precursor: Rosenzweig argued that Judaism and Christianity represent two valid, complementary paths to God.

Main claims

Advocates typically argue from passages such as Romans 11, in which Paul insists that "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" with respect to Israel, and from the enduring promises made to Abraham. Some proponents describe Judaism and Christianity as two distinct covenants intended for two distinct peoples — the Mosaic covenant for Jews and the covenant in Christ for Gentiles.

Criticism

Most Christian denominations reject dual-covenant theology, holding that salvation is universally through Christ and that the New Testament applies the promises of the Old Covenant to the Church. Critics within evangelicalism argue the view contradicts the New Testament's mission to Jews as well as Gentiles. Some Jewish critics also regard the idea with ambivalence, seeing it as a Christian theological construct that still frames Judaism in Christian categories. The Roman Catholic Church's 2015 document "The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable" affirmed that God's covenant with the Jewish people was never revoked, while stopping short of fully articulating a dual-covenant doctrine.
