East–West Schism
Date: Traditionally 1054
Parties: Roman Catholic Church / Eastern Orthodox Church
Issues: Papal authority, Filioque

The East–West Schism, also the Great Schism, is the break of communion between the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, conventionally dated to 1054, which divided Christianity into Western Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy.

Causes

Long cultural, linguistic and political divergence between Latin West and Greek East, disputes over papal primacy, the Filioque clause added to the Nicene Creed in the West, clerical celibacy and liturgical practices all contributed. In 1054 papal legate Humbert of Silva Candida excommunicated Patriarch Michael Cerularius in Hagia Sophia, and retaliatory excommunications followed.

Aftermath

Reunion attempts at Lyons (1274) and Florence (1439) failed; the sack of Constantinople in the Fourth Crusade (1204) deepened hostility. In 1965 Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual excommunications, and dialogue continues, though communion is not restored. See also Ecumenical Patriarchate.
