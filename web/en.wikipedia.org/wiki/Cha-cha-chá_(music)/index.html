Cha-cha-chá (music)

Cha-cha-chá is a genre of Cuban music and the partner dance that developed with it. It emerged in the early 1950s from the danzón-mambo tradition and became an international ballroom phenomenon. The name is onomatopoeic, imitating the shuffling sound of the dancers' feet in the distinctive triple step, or the rattle of the güiro.

Ballroom cha-cha is one of the five Latin dances of International Latin competition.

Origins

The genre is credited to the Cuban charanga violinist and composer Enrique Jorrín, who in the early 1950s modified the danzón's rhythm, introducing a syncopated figure in which the dancers' shuffling steps on the fourth beat produced the "cha-cha-chá" sound. His composition La engañadora (1951) is generally considered the first cha-cha-chá. Dancers in Havana's social clubs demanded a slower, more accessible alternative to mambo, and the new rhythm spread rapidly through Cuba, Mexico and the United States.

Characteristics

Cha-cha-chá is written in 4/4 time. In authentic Cuban style the music is played by a charanga francesa — flute, violins, piano, bass, timbales and güiro — or by big bands such as those of Tito Puente and Pérez Prado, whose recordings carried the genre worldwide. The dance is built on a basic step pattern of rock steps and chassés, with action counted "two, three, cha-cha-chá".

International popularity

Standardised for ballroom competition in England and France, the cha-cha (the name was shortened in English) became one of the five dances of the International Latin programme. Hit recordings and film appearances in the 1950s made it a fixture of popular dance floors, and its rhythms influenced rock and roll and later pop music.
