Andalusian classical music refers to the classical music traditions of the Maghreb — Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya — that preserve and develop the court music of medieval Muslim Spain (al-Andalus). After the fall of Granada in 1492 and the expulsion of the Moriscos, refugee musicians carried the repertoire to North African cities, where distinct regional schools — Moroccan al-āla, Algerian schools of Algiers (san'a), Tlemcen (gharnati) and Constantine, and Tunisian and Libyan ma'lūf — have maintained it ever since.

The nūba

The repertoire is organised into nūbāt (singular nūba): large suites of vocal and instrumental pieces unified by a mode (ṭabʿ) and unfolding through a fixed sequence of movements in contrasting rhythms, accelerating toward the close. Tradition attributes a system of twenty-four nūbāt, one for each hour of the day, to the 9th-century Córdoban musician Ziryāb; eleven substantially complete nūbāt survive in Morocco and fewer elsewhere. Texts are drawn from classical Arabic and Andalusi poetry, including muwashshaḥ and zajal forms.

Ensemble and performance

Orchestras combine bowed strings, oud, rabab (a spike fiddle that often leads), qanun, nay and frame drums, with the singers forming a chorus. Performance practice was transmitted orally by master musicians; notation and recording projects in the 20th century, together with festivals such as the Fes Festival of World Sacred Music, have supported preservation.
