In economics, a public good is a good that is both non-excludable—it is impossible or prohibitively costly to prevent anyone from enjoying it—and non-rivalrous—one person's enjoyment does not diminish the amount available to others. National defence is the standard example: protecting one resident of a country protects all, and no resident can be excluded from the protection. Paul Samuelson gave the concept its modern formal treatment in "The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure" (1954), defining a "collective consumption good" for which each individual's consumption equals the total quantity supplied, in contrast to a private good, whose total is divided among consumers. Because public goods can be enjoyed without payment, markets tend to under-provide them: each person has an incentive to free-ride on others' contributions, so public goods are a classic case of market failure and a principal economic rationale for government provision and taxation.

Most real goods are impure: roads are non-rivalrous until they congest; a lighthouse beam is non-excludable, though Ronald Coase's famous 1974 study showed that English lighthouses were for centuries financed by port fees levied on beneficiaries—a caution against assuming that only the state can supply them. Knowledge is the economically most important near-public good: an idea used by one firm is undiminished for others, which underlies the economics of research, patents and open science.

The free-rider problem and optimal provision

Samuelson showed that efficiency requires the sum of individuals' marginal rates of substitution to equal the marginal rate of transformation (the "Samuelson condition"), in contrast to the private-good rule that each person's MRS equal the MRT; but no decentralised price mechanism elicits the honest preference revelations needed to apply the rule, because understating one's valuation lowers one's tax share while barely reducing provision. The free-rider problem is thus built into the concept. Responses studied by economists include Lindahl pricing (personalised tax shares, impractical at scale), mechanism design (Clarke–Groves taxes that make honesty a dominant strategy), assortative and conditional cooperation observed in laboratory public-goods games (contributions typically start at 40–60 percent of the social optimum and decay with repetition unless punishment of free-riders is possible), and Elinor Ostrom's work on polycentric governance, which documented communities successfully managing common-pool resources through local rules—blurring the line between the public-good and common-pool cells and qualifying the presumption that provision requires either markets or a central state.

Global public goods

The framework extends naturally to goods whose benefits cross borders: climate stability, eradication of infectious disease (smallpox eradication is the paradigm success), pandemic surveillance, financial stability, peacekeeping and the open trading system. Global public goods suffer free-riding in an acute form because there is no world government to tax and compel; their supply depends on treaties, hegemonic provision, and clubs of the willing, and their chronic under-supply—climate mitigation above all—is a central problem of international political economy.

Criticism

Critics in the public-choice tradition note that declaring a good "public" does not show that politicians and bureaucracies will supply the right quantity, since governments have their own failures of information and incentive. Others observe that excludability is partly a technological and legal choice (encryption made broadcast television a club good; congestion pricing makes roads excludable), so the categories are moving targets rather than natural kinds. The concept nevertheless remains foundational in public economics, welfare economics and the theory of the state.
