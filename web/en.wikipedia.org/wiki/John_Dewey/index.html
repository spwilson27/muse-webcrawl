John Dewey (October 20, 1859 – June 1, 1952) was an American philosopher, psychologist, and educational reformer. A leading figure of the philosophical school of pragmatism and a founder of functional psychology, he is regarded as one of the most prominent American scholars of the first half of the 20th century, and his thought profoundly influenced education, social reform, and democratic theory.

Philosophy

Dewey's "instrumentalism" treated ideas as tools for solving problems arising in experience, and knowledge as the product of active inquiry rather than passive contemplation of fixed truths. Rejecting dualisms of mind and body, theory and practice, he developed a naturalistic metaphysics of experience in works such as Experience and Nature (1925), and a theory of inquiry in Logic: The Theory of Inquiry (1938). With Charles Sanders Peirce and William James, he stands among the classical pragmatists.

Education

At the University of Chicago (1894–1904) Dewey founded the Laboratory School to test his ideas. Against rote, authoritarian schooling he argued that education is "a process of living and not a preparation for future living": children learn by doing, through collaborative problem-solving continuous with democratic life. Democracy and Education (1916) remains his most influential book; his ideas underlie progressive education, though he later criticized its excesses in Experience and Education (1938).

Public life

Dewey taught at Columbia University from 1904 to 1939, lectured in China (1919–1921) during the May Fourth era, helped found the ACLU and the NAACP, and chaired the 1937 commission of inquiry into the Moscow Trials charges against Leon Trotsky, which found them baseless. He remained a prolific public intellectual into his nineties.
