Akvavit

Akvavit or aquavit (Latin: aqua vitae, "water of life") is a distilled spirit produced principally in Scandinavia, where it has been made since the 15th century. It is distilled from grain or potatoes and flavoured, as required by European Union regulation, predominantly with caraway or dill. Akvavit typically contains 37.5–45% alcohol by volume and is an essential feature of the Scandinavian drinking culture, consumed especially at festive gatherings such as Christmas and Midsummer, usually as a chilled shot accompanying pickled herring and other smörgåsbord dishes.

Production and varieties

The earliest known reference to aquavit is in a letter of 1531 from the Danish Lord of Bergenhus castle, Eske Bille, to the Archbishop of Norway, describing a spirit that "helps for all kinds of illness". Danish akvavits, such as Aalborg Taffel, are usually clear and strongly caraway-flavoured; Norwegian akevitt is characteristically matured in oak sherry casks — most famously Linie Aquavit, which is shipped across the equator ("the line") in barrels aboard Wilhelmsen ships, the voyage mellowing the spirit. Swedish akvavit brands include O.P. Anderson and Skåne Akvavit. Icelandic brennivín, flavoured with caraway, is a close relative.

Traditions

Akvavit is drunk from small tulip-shaped glasses, often in a single draught, accompanied by toasting songs (snapsvisor) in Sweden and Denmark. Norwegian akevitt matured in oak is more often sipped alongside beer, particularly at Christmas with rich pork dishes. See also Brennivín and distilled beverage.
