Adyghe (Адыгабзэ, Adyğabze), also known as West Circassian, is a Northwest Caucasian language spoken by the Adyghe (Circassian) people, chiefly in the Republic of Adygea and neighbouring regions of southern Russia, and by large Circassian diaspora communities in Turkey, Jordan, Syria, and elsewhere. Together with Kabardian (East Circassian) it forms the Circassian branch of the family.

Phonology

Adyghe is famous for its extremely large consonant inventory and small vowel system. Depending on the dialect, it has between 50 and 60 consonants — including ejectives, pharyngealised and labialised consonants, and (in the Abzakh dialect) a rare lateral ejective affricate — contrasted with as few as two or three phonemic vowels, whose quality is largely conditioned by surrounding consonants.

Grammar

The language is polysynthetic and ergative, with verbs that agree with subject, direct object, and indirect object and can incorporate a wide array of prefixes expressing location, direction, and benefaction. Nouns mark a small set of cases, including ergative and oblique forms, and word order is typically subject–object–verb.

Dialects and writing

Major dialects include Temirgoy (the basis of the literary standard), Abzakh, Bzhedug, and Shapsug. A literary standard was created after the Russian Revolution, initially in Latin script (1927) and from 1938 in a Cyrillic-based alphabet of 66 letters. Adyghe is co-official with Russian in Adygea, though UNESCO classifies it as vulnerable, as transmission in the diaspora weakens.
