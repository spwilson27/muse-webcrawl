Northwest Coast art

RegionPacific Northwest Coast of North America
PeoplesHaida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth, Coast Salish and others
Key mediaCedar wood carving, weaving, argillite, metalwork, painting
Signature formsTotem poles, masks, bentwood boxes, Chilkat blankets, crest art (formline)

Northwest Coast art is the distinctive artistic tradition of the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, extending from the Alaska Panhandle through British Columbia to Washington State. It is renowned for its monumental totem poles, transformation masks, carved house posts and a highly formalised graphic vocabulary known as formline, used to depict crests — hereditary designs representing clan histories, spirits and ancestors.

Formline

Formline design is the characteristic two-dimensional style of northern Northwest Coast art. It is built from flowing, swelling-and-tapering lines ("formlines"), ovoids, U-forms and split-U forms arranged to fill a shape — an animal, a crest figure, a box — within a strictly organised composition that fills the field without empty space. Traditional colours are black (primary formline), red (secondary) and blue-green (tertiary).

Objects and media

Artists worked in red and yellow cedar, carving totem poles, memorial posts, house frontal poles, masks, rattles, bowls, and steamed-and-bent kerfed wooden boxes. Women wove cedar-bark regalia and the famous Chilkat blankets of mountain-goat wool. Haida artists carved black argillite slate for the tourist trade from the early 19th century, and silversmiths engraved bracelets and spoons with crest designs. The potlatch feast system, at which crests were displayed and validated, drove much artistic production.

Suppression and revival

Colonial bans on the potlatch (in Canada, 1885–1951) and the removal of art to museums worldwide disrupted transmission, though artists such as Charles Edenshaw (Haida) maintained the tradition. From the 1960s a major renaissance, led by artists and teachers such as Bill Reid, Bill Holm (whose 1965 analysis codified formline principles), Freda Diesing and Robert Davidson, revived monumental carving and trained new generations.
