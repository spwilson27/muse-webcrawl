The Peruvian anchoveta (Engraulis ringens), known in Spanish as anchoveta, is a species of anchovy found in the cold, nutrient-rich waters of the Humboldt (Peru) Current off the Pacific coast of South America. It supports what is frequently the world's largest single-species fishery, with annual catches that have at times exceeded 8–10 million tonnes.

Biology

Anchoveta are small, silvery schooling fish that feed directly on plankton, an unusually short food chain that — combined with the intense upwelling of the Humboldt Current — allows an enormous biomass. They typically live about three years and spawn mainly in austral winter and summer. Anchoveta are in turn the key prey of seabirds (the guano-producing cormorants, boobies, and pelicans), sea lions, and larger fish such as bonito and jack mackerel.

The fishery

The industrial fishery developed in the 1950s; by the early 1970s Peru was landing over 12 million tonnes in a single year, most of it reduced to fishmeal and fish oil for export as animal feed. The fishery famously collapsed in 1972–73 under the combined pressure of overfishing and a strong El Niño, which warms the coastal waters and drives the fish deeper and further south; catches fell below 2 million tonnes. Collapses and closures have recurred with subsequent El Niño events, including 1982–83 and 1997–98.

Since the 1990s Peru's fisheries institute IMARPE has managed the stock with scientific surveys, seasonal closures (vedas), and, from 2009, individual vessel quotas. Only a small fraction of the catch is used for direct human consumption, though Peruvian campaigns promote anchoveta as an inexpensive, highly nutritious food fish.

Ecological importance

Anchoveta are considered a keystone species of the Humboldt Current ecosystem: fluctuations in their abundance cascade through the entire food web, famously affecting the guano bird populations whose droppings were once a major Peruvian export industry in the nineteenth century.
