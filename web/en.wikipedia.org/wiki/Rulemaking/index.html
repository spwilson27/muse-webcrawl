Rulemaking

In administrative law, rule-making is the process that executive and independent agencies use to create, or promulgate, regulations. In general, legislatures first set broad policy mandates by passing statutes, then agencies create more detailed regulations through rulemaking.

United States process

In the United States, the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 governs federal rulemaking. Under "notice and comment" rulemaking, an agency publishes a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) in the Federal Register, allows a period for public comments, and then publishes a final rule, often accompanied by a statement of basis and purpose responding to significant comments. Rules are ultimately codified in the Code of Federal Regulations.

Types of rules

Agencies issue legislative (substantive) rules, which have the force of law; interpretive rules, which explain how the agency understands existing law; and procedural rules governing the agency's own operations. Major rules may be subject to review by the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs and can be overturned by Congress under the Congressional Review Act.

Judicial review

Final agency rules are subject to judicial review; courts may set aside rules that are arbitrary and capricious, contrary to statute, or adopted without required procedure.
