Syncope (phonology)

In phonology and historical linguistics, syncope is the loss of an unstressed vowel within a word, typically from a medial syllable. It is a common type of sound change and a frequent source of consonant clusters and shortened word forms. Syncope contrasts with apocope (loss of a final vowel or syllable), aphaeresis (loss at the beginning of a word), and elision more generally. (For the medical condition, see syncope.)

Syncope deletes an unstressed vowel from the interior of a word.

Examples

English exhibits syncope in everyday pronunciations such as family pronounced "fam'ly", every as "ev'ry", chocolate as "choc'late", and camera as "cam'ra". Historically, syncope shaped many languages: in the development from Latin to French, unstressed internal vowels were widely lost (e.g. Latin calidum → French chaud); in Old Irish, syncope of vowels in even-numbered syllables was a systematic and morphologically important process; and in the history of English, syncope contributed to forms like lord from Old English hlāford (from hlāfweard). In Arabic dialects, syncope of short vowels in open syllables is pervasive, and it plays a major role in the phonology of many other language families.

Mechanism and analysis

Syncope typically targets vowels in unstressed, often open, syllables, where short duration makes them vulnerable to deletion, especially in fast or casual speech before the change becomes phonologised. In metrical and optimality-theoretic analyses it is often described as a strategy for satisfying stress or foot-structure requirements. Orthographic syncope is sometimes indicated with an apostrophe, as in poetic contractions.
