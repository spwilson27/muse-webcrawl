Silencer (genetics)

TypeCis-regulatory DNA element
FunctionRepression of gene transcription

In genetics, a silencer is a DNA sequence capable of binding transcription regulation factors, called repressors, thereby preventing or reducing the transcription of a gene. Silencers are cis-acting regulatory elements, typically between 20 and 2,000 base pairs long, and are the functional counterparts of enhancers: where enhancers increase the rate of transcription, silencers decrease it. A silencer may lie upstream or downstream of a gene, within an intron, or in an untranslated region, and generally acts regardless of orientation.

Mechanism

When a repressor protein binds a silencer, it can inhibit transcription in several ways: by recruiting corepressor complexes and chromatin-modifying enzymes such as histone deacetylases, producing a compact, inactive chromatin state; by interfering with the assembly of the basal transcription machinery at the promoter; or by competing with activators for overlapping binding sites. DNA looping can bring distant silencers into physical proximity with the promoter they regulate.

Examples

Silencer elements have been characterised in many genes. In the human CD4 locus, a silencer in the first intron ensures CD4 is switched off in cytotoxic (CD8) T cells. The RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST/NRSF) binds the RE1 silencer to repress neuronal genes in non-neuronal tissues. In yeast, silencers such as the HML and HMR elements establish heritable heterochromatin ("silent" mating-type loci) through Sir proteins — giving rise to the term "silencing" for epigenetic repression more broadly.

Silencers in development and disease

Because silencers help restrict gene expression to the right cells and times, their disruption can cause disease: deletions or mutations of silencer elements can lead to inappropriate gene activation, while genome-wide association studies have mapped many disease-associated variants to non-coding regions enriched for silencers and other regulatory elements. Large-scale projects such as ENCODE have annotated candidate silencer regions across the human genome, although silencers remain less completely catalogued than enhancers.
