Boogie rock

Boogie rock
Stylistic originsBlues rock, boogie-woogie, country, rock and roll

Typical instrumentsElectric guitar, bass, drums, piano

Boogie rock is a genre of rock music that developed in the late 1960s, built on the driving, repetitive twelve-bar blues patterns and shuffling rhythms of boogie-woogie. It favours groove and riff over complexity: songs are typically constructed around a single insistent guitar or piano riff, with extended jams and a danceable beat.

Characteristics

Boogie rock emphasises a relentless, swinging rhythm section, slide or riffing guitars, and often honky-tonk piano. Lyrics are usually simple and celebratory, dealing with the road, drinking and good times. Where mainstream blues rock explored virtuosity and dynamics, boogie rock pursued hypnotic repetition, making it a staple of bars, festivals and stadium shows.

History and artists

American bands such as Canned Heat — whose "On the Road Again" and "Going Up the Country" were hits in 1968 — pioneered the style, drawing on John Lee Hooker's one-chord boogie. In the 1970s ZZ Top, Foghat, George Thorogood and the Destroyers, and Bachman–Turner Overdrive carried the sound to arena success. In Britain, Status Quo became the archetypal boogie rock band, building a decades-long career on twelve-bar riffs from "Down Down" onwards; Australia's AC/DC also drew heavily on boogie structures in their early work.

Though rarely critically fashionable, boogie rock proved durable: its riffs underlie much hard rock and pub rock, and its leading acts continued to fill arenas long after the genre's commercial peak.
