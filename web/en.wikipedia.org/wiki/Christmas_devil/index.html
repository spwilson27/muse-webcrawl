Christmas devil

A Christmas devil is a figure of folklore associated with the Christmas season who serves as a dark counterpart or punishing companion to Saint Nicholas or Santa Claus. Whereas the gift-bringer rewards well-behaved children, the Christmas devil threatens, frightens or punishes the naughty — often with chains, switches, rods or sacks in which misbehaving children are said to be carried away. Such figures are most prominent in the folklore of Central and Eastern Europe but occur in traditions across the continent and beyond.

Regional figures

Krampus

The best-known Christmas devil is Krampus, a horned, fur-covered figure of Alpine folklore in Austria, southern Germany, Hungary, Slovenia, Croatia and northern Italy. On Krampusnacht (December 5), the eve of the feast of Saint Nicholas, Krampus accompanies the saint, rattling chains and bells and menacing children who have behaved badly. Modern Krampus runs (Krampuslauf) draw large crowds.

Knecht Ruprecht and companions of Saint Nicholas

In much of Germany, Saint Nicholas is accompanied by Knecht Ruprecht, a stern servant who questions children about their prayers and behaviour. Related companions include Hans Trapp in Alsace, the Père Fouettard ("Father Whipper") in France and Luxembourg, and Zwarte Piet in the Low Countries.

Belsnickel and others

Belsnickel, a ragged, switch-carrying figure of the Palatinate, was carried to Pennsylvania by German immigrants. In Iceland the tradition is different: children are visited by the thirteen Yule Lads, sons of the ogress Grýla, who was said to devour naughty children. In Greece the kallikantzaroi are goblin-like creatures abroad during the Twelve Days of Christmas.

Origins and interpretation

Folklorists trace such figures to pre-Christian winter customs, masking traditions and mumming plays, later absorbed into the Christian festive calendar around the feasts of Saint Nicholas and the Nativity. The devils function as instruments of social discipline and as ritual embodiments of winter darkness, in contrast to the generosity and light associated with the saint.

In modern culture

Christmas devils, especially Krampus, have enjoyed a revival in popular culture since the late twentieth century, appearing in parades, greeting cards, films (including the 2015 horror-comedy Krampus) and seasonal merchandise, sometimes detached from their original disciplinary role.
