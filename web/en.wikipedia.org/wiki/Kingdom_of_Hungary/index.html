The Kingdom of Hungary was a monarchy in Central Europe that existed for nearly a millennium, from the coronation of Stephen I in 1000 (or 1001) until 1918, and again as a regency under Miklós Horthy from 1920 to 1946. For most of its history it was a medium-sized multi-ethnic state controlling the Carpathian Basin, and from 1867 to 1918 it formed one half of the Austro-Hungarian Empire as the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen.

Medieval kingdom

Stephen I, of the Árpád dynasty, was crowned with a crown traditionally said to have been sent by the Pope and organised Hungary as a Christian kingdom of counties administered by royal officials. His successors endured the catastrophic Mongol invasion of 1241–42, after which Béla IV rebuilt the kingdom with stone castles. Under the Angevin kings Charles Robert and Louis the Great in the 14th century, Hungary became a major European power, ruling at times Croatia, and briefly exerting influence over Naples, Poland, and the Balkans. Matthias Corvinus (r. 1458–1490) made Buda a Renaissance court and fielded the standing Black Army, but after his death the kingdom weakened, and the Ottoman victory at Mohács (1526) killed King Louis II and split the country three ways: Royal Hungary under the Habsburgs, the Ottoman-occupied centre, and the Eastern Hungarian Kingdom that became Transylvania.

Habsburg era and dual monarchy

The Habsburg reconquest culminating at Vienna (1683) and the recapture of Buda (1686) reunified Hungary under the dynasty, amid grievances over taxation and religion that fuelled Rákóczi's War of Independence (1703–1711). The failed revolution of 1848–49 was followed by the Compromise of 1867, which made Hungary an equal partner in Austria-Hungary. In the dualist era Budapest grew explosively — united from Buda, Pest, and Óbuda in 1873 — while Magyarisation policies strained relations with the kingdom's Slovaks, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, and others.

Dissolution and aftermath

Defeat in the First World War dissolved the monarchy in 1918. The Treaty of Trianon (1920) stripped Hungary of about two-thirds of its territory and population, leaving millions of ethnic Hungarians in neighbouring states — a national trauma that shaped interwar politics under Regent Horthy's formally restored kingdom (1920–1946), alignment with Nazi Germany, and the catastrophe of 1944–45. The monarchy was formally abolished on 1 February 1946, when the Second Hungarian Republic was proclaimed.
