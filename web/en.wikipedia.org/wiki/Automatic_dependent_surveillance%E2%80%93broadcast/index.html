Automatic dependent surveillance–broadcast

Automatic dependent surveillance–broadcast (ADS-B) is a cooperative surveillance technology for tracking aircraft: an aircraft broadcasts its own position, altitude, velocity and identification about once per second, and any suitably equipped receiver — on the ground or in another aircraft — can pick up the signal.

The term is one of a family of "automatic dependent surveillance" techniques, in which the aircraft automatically reports data derived from its own on-board navigation systems. ADS-B differs from ADS-C (contract), used over oceans, where reports are sent by satellite or radio data link at intervals agreed with a specific ground station.

The aircraft's navigation system — almost always GNSS — supplies position and velocity. The transponder or a dedicated transmitter formats these into standard messages and broadcasts them. In the 1090ES implementation the transmissions ride on the Mode S transponder's extended squitter; receivers decode the 112-bit messages to extract the airborne position reports.

Because the broadcasts are plain and open, low-cost software-defined radio receivers have made global crowdsourced flight tracking possible, feeding services that display live traffic maps. The same openness means the system provides no authentication, and researchers have demonstrated that false ADS-B targets can be injected with modest equipment, a known limitation that next-generation links seek to address.

Applications

Beyond air traffic control, ADS-B data is used for flight tracking by airlines and the public, search and rescue support, traffic displays in the cockpit (ADS-B In), surface movement guidance at airports, and space-based surveillance: receivers hosted on the Iridium NEXT satellite constellation provide ADS-B coverage over oceans and polar regions where ground stations cannot be built.
