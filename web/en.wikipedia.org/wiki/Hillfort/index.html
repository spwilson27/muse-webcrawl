Hillfort

[Image: An Iron Age hillfort: banks and ditches following the contours of a hill]

Overview

A hillfort is a type of earthwork fortification built on elevated ground, used from the Bronze Age into the early medieval period, characterised by one or more lines of ramparts (banks and ditches, sometimes stone or timber-laced) that follow the contours of a hill. Britain and Ireland hold thousands — Maiden Castle in Dorset is the largest in Britain — and related forms occur across Europe (oppida, castros, burgen) and worldwide (pā in New Zealand, kraal hilltop sites in southern Africa).

Interpretation

Hillforts were once read simply as defensive refuges, but excavations show many were permanently occupied settlements — centres of craft, storage, trade, ritual and tribal assembly (as at Danebury, intensively excavated by Barry Cunliffe) — as much as strongholds. Their ramparts were also statements of communal labour and power. In the British Iron Age they were largely superseded after the Roman conquest, though many Welsh and Scottish sites were refortified in the early middle ages.
