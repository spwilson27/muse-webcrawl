FamilyPhocidae

The Ladoga seal (Russian: Ладожская нерпа; Pusa hispida ladogensis) is a freshwater subspecies of the ringed seal found only in Lake Ladoga in northwestern Russia. It became isolated in the lake roughly 8,000–11,000 years ago, after the retreat of the continental ice sheet cut the basin off from the sea as the land rebounded.

Description

Adults are smaller than marine ringed seals, typically 100–130 cm long and 30–50 kg in weight. The coat is dark grey to nearly black, usually with lighter marbled markings rather than the distinct rings of its marine relatives. The seals haul out on rocks and islands in summer and live under and on the lake ice in winter, maintaining breathing holes and giving birth in snow lairs on the ice.

Population and threats

Hunting reduced the population from perhaps 20,000 in the early twentieth century to a few thousand by the 1980s; it has since recovered to an estimated 5,000–8,000 animals, though figures remain uncertain. Remaining threats include drowning in fishing nets, disturbance, pollution, and reduction of winter ice cover due to climate change. The Ladoga seal is listed in the Red Data Book of Russia and is assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.

Relationship to other freshwater seals

Along with the Saimaa ringed seal of Finland and the Baikal seal, the Ladoga seal is one of the very few seal populations living exclusively in fresh water. Genetic studies show the Ladoga population descends from ringed seals that colonised the lake from the Baltic basin after the last glaciation.
