Echolocation, also called biosonar, is a physiological process for locating objects by means of reflected sound: an animal (or a device) emits a sound pulse and listens to the returning echo, extracting from its delay, loudness and spectral content the distance, direction, size, shape and even surface texture of objects in the environment. Distance follows directly from the echo's travel time, since range = (speed of sound × delay) / 2.

Physical principles

Short, broadband pulses give fine range resolution, because two objects can be told apart only if their echoes do not overlap; many echolocators therefore use brief clicks or frequency-swept (FM) chirps a few milliseconds long. Higher frequencies give finer detail and narrower beams but attenuate faster in air, which is why most animal echolocation is ultrasonic and operates over short ranges. Constant-frequency (CF) components, used by horseshoe bats and by dolphins' whistling relatives among sonar systems, excel at detecting Doppler shifts — allowing the echolocator to measure a target's relative velocity and even the flutter of insect wings. A moving echolocator must compensate for Doppler shifts in returning echoes; horseshoe bats famously lower their emitted frequency in flight so that echoes return within the sharp tuning of their cochlea ("acoustic fovea").

Neural processing

The auditory systems of echolocators are highly specialised. In bats, delay-tuned neurons in the auditory cortex respond to specific combinations of pulse and echo, forming a neural map of target range; in dolphins, echoes received through the fat-filled lower jaw are processed with microsecond precision. Experiments show that experienced echolocators can judge range to within centimetres and discriminate echo differences of less than a microsecond in arrival time.

Human echolocation

Some blind people have learned to echolocate actively, producing tongue clicks and interpreting the echoes to detect obstacles, buildings and even object shapes; documented experts such as Daniel Kish navigate and ride bicycles by this means. Brain-imaging studies show that echoes are processed in the visual cortex of early-blind echolocators, an example of cross-modal plasticity. Passive listening to ambient sound reflections ("facial vision", historically misattributed to skin pressure) is a weaker, related phenomenon available to many sighted people too.

Technology

Echolocation in animals inspired and parallels human technologies: sonar applies the same principle underwater (its development predates the discovery of animal biosonar, which it in turn helped researchers understand), radar substitutes radio waves for sound, and medical ultrasound imaging builds pictures from echoes in tissue. Roboticists and engineers continue to study bat and dolphin sonar for compact navigation and sensing systems.
