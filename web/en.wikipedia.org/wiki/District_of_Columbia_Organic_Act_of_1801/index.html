District of Columbia Organic Act of 1801

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The District of Columbia Organic Act of 1801 was an organic act enacted by the United States Congress on 27 February 1801 that placed the District of Columbia under the direct control of Congress, in accordance with the District Clause of the US Constitution. It officially recognised the city of Washington and divided the district into two counties: Washington County on the Maryland side of the Potomac River and Alexandria County on the Virginia side.

Provisions

The act provided that the laws of Maryland and Virginia would continue in force in the respective parts of the district until Congress provided otherwise, and it established a system of courts and presidentially appointed justices of the peace. It did not provide the district with any form of self-government or representation: residents of the district, who had previously voted in Maryland or Virginia elections, lost the right to vote for members of Congress and, until the Twenty-third Amendment in 1961, for President. It also did not incorporate the city of Washington; Congress chartered the city separately in 1802, giving it an elected council and a mayor appointed by the President.

Consequences

The 1801 act is the origin of the district's distinctive constitutional position: governed by Congress, without voting representation in it. The portion south of the Potomac, Alexandria County, was retroceded to Virginia in 1846–47, largely over economic neglect and the slave trade issue, leaving the district in its present boundaries. Later organic acts in 1871 and 1878 reorganised the district's territorial government; limited home rule came only with the District of Columbia Home Rule Act of 1973.
