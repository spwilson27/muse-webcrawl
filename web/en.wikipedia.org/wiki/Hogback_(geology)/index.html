Hogback (geology)

A hogback ridge formed by steeply dipping strata

In geology and geomorphology, a hogback or hog's back is a long, narrow ridge or a series of hills with a narrow crest and steep slopes of nearly equal inclination on both flanks. Typically, the term is restricted to a ridge created by the differential erosion of outcropping, steeply dipping (greater than 30°–40°), homoclinal sedimentary rock strata. One side of a hogback (its "backslope") is typically a dip slope, formed by the outcrop of a resistant stratum of rock; the other side (its "front slope") is an escarpment (scarp face). The more gently sloping ridge formed by the differential erosion of less steeply dipping strata is called a cuesta.

Formation

Hogbacks form where sedimentary strata have been tilted strongly, often at the flanks of uplifts or along fault zones. Softer rock layers erode more rapidly, leaving the resistant layers standing out as sharp-crested ridges. Because the strata dip steeply, both slopes of the ridge are steep and roughly symmetrical, distinguishing hogbacks from cuestas, whose strata dip at gentler angles.

Examples

Prominent hogbacks include the Dakota Hogback along the Front Range of Colorado, the hogbacks of the San Rafael Swell in Utah, and the chalk Hog's Back in Surrey, England, along which the A31 road runs west of Guildford. Hogback ridges are common features in folded and faulted sedimentary terrains worldwide.
