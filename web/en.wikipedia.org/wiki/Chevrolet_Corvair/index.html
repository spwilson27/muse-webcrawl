Chevrolet Corvair

The Chevrolet Corvair is a rear-engined, air-cooled compact car manufactured by Chevrolet for model years 1960–1969. The only American-designed, mass-produced passenger car with a rear-mounted, air-cooled flat-six engine, it was offered in an unusually wide range of body styles — sedans, coupes, convertibles, station wagons, vans and pickups — and total production exceeded 1.8 million.

Development

Championed by Chevrolet general manager Ed Cole, the Corvair was conceived as an import-fighter against the Volkswagen Beetle, borrowing its rear-engined, air-cooled layout but with a larger six-cylinder engine and unit-body construction. The sporty Monza coupe of 1960 was an unexpected hit and is credited with helping to inspire the Ford Mustang. Turbocharged Monza Spyder (1962) and later Corsa models made the Corvair one of the first production cars offered with a turbocharger.

Controversy

The first-generation car's swing-axle rear suspension could produce oversteer if tyre pressures were not maintained as specified. Ralph Nader's 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed opened with a chapter attacking the Corvair, making it a symbol of automotive safety reform, although a 1972 U.S. government study concluded the early Corvair's handling was comparable to its contemporaries. The second generation (1965–69) received a fully independent rear suspension modelled on the Corvette's.

Legacy

Sales collapsed after 1965 amid the publicity and competition from the Mustang; production ended on May 14, 1969. The Corvair remains a cult classic, and its name blends "Corvette" and "Bel Air".
