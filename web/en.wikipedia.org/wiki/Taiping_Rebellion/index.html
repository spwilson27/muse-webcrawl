Taiping Rebellion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Taiping troops in the Yangtze valley, from a contemporary illustration]

The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), also known as the Taiping Civil War, was a massive civil war in Qing China and one of the deadliest conflicts in history, with death-toll estimates commonly between 20 and 30 million. Led by Hong Xiuquan, a failed examination candidate who believed himself the younger brother of Jesus Christ, the Taiping ('Great Peace') movement rose in Guangxi, swept up the Yangtze, and established the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom with its capital at Nanjing (renamed Tianjing) from 1853. It was destroyed only after a decade of war by regional armies raised by Qing loyalists such as Zeng Guofan, with limited foreign assistance.

The Heavenly Kingdom

Hong's God Worshipping Society fused his heterodox Christianity with social radicalism: common treasuries, land equalisation (in the programme, if rarely in practice), bans on opium, footbinding and prostitution, and separate women's camps and examinations. The Taiping took Nanjing in 1853 and nearly marched on Beijing; their northern expedition failed. In 1856 the kingdom tore itself apart in the Tianjing Incident, when Hong ordered the slaughter of the Eastern King Yang Xiuqing and tens of thousands of his followers — a purge from which Taiping leadership never recovered.

Suppression and legacy

With the regular Qing forces decayed, the court authorised Han officials to raise provincial armies: Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army, Li Hongzhang's Huai Army and Zuo Zongtang's forces — funded by new commercial taxes (lijin) — slowly strangled the kingdom, with the Ever Victorious Army under Charles 'Chinese' Gordon assisting around Shanghai and Suzhou. Nanjing fell in July 1864 amid massacre and Hong's death (by illness or suicide) weeks before. The rebellion devastated China's richest region, weakened the Qing irreversibly while empowering regional militarists, and remains debated as proto-revolutionary inspiration (honoured by the PRC) or theocratic catastrophe.
