The Canadian Caper was the joint covert rescue by the government of Canada and the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) of six American diplomats who had evaded capture during the seizure of the United States embassy in Tehran, Iran, on 4 November 1979, at the outset of the Iran hostage crisis. The diplomats were sheltered by Canadian embassy staff for 79 days before being exfiltrated from the country under Canadian passports and a cover story that they were a Canadian film crew scouting locations for a science-fiction movie.

Background

When Iranian students stormed the U.S. embassy in Tehran on 4 November 1979, taking 52 Americans hostage, six diplomats — Robert Anders, Cora Lijek, Mark Lijek, Joseph Stafford, Kathleen Stafford and Henry Schatz — slipped out a side door and evaded capture. After hiding for several days, including at the British embassy and Anders' home, they were taken in by Canadian ambassador Ken Taylor and by Canadian immigration officer John Sheardown and his wife Zena, who sheltered them in their own homes at considerable personal risk.

The government of Prime Minister Joe Clark, through Foreign Minister Flora MacDonald, authorised in a secret Order in Council — the first secret Order in Council passed by a Canadian government in peacetime — the issue of genuine Canadian passports in the diplomats' aliases, together with forged Iranian visas. The passports were flown to Tehran by diplomatic courier.

The cover story and exfiltration

The CIA, which had been planning an exfiltration, devised the cover: the six would pose as a Canadian film crew from a fictional production company, "Studio Six Productions," scouting locations in Iran for a science-fiction film called Argo, based on Roger Zelazny's novel Lord of Light. CIA officer Tony Mendez, a specialist in disguise and exfiltration, set up the fake company in Hollywood — complete with offices, trade-press advertising and a script, using artwork by comics artist Jack Kirby prepared for an earlier unproduced adaptation — and flew to Tehran to coach the diplomats in their roles.

On Sunday, 27 January 1980, the six, accompanied by Mendez and a second CIA officer, passed through Mehrabad Airport in Tehran using the Canadian passports and departed on a Swissair flight to Zurich. The Canadian embassy in Tehran was closed the same day, and Taylor and his staff left Iran.

Revelation and aftermath

The operation was kept secret even after the escape, to protect the remaining 52 hostages. It was revealed on 29 January 1980 by Jean Pelletier of the Montreal newspaper La Presse, who had learned of the story weeks earlier but held it. Canada received enormous gratitude in the United States: American flags flew across the country, and Taylor was made a member of the Order of Canada and awarded the U.S. Congressional Gold Medal. The CIA's role was classified until 1997, when it was declassified on the agency's 50th anniversary; Mendez received the CIA's Trailblazer Award.

In popular culture

The escape formed the basis of the 1981 television film Escape from Iran: The Canadian Caper and of Ben Affleck's 2012 film Argo, which won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Argo was criticised in Canada for minimising the Canadian role — portraying Taylor largely as a landlord and attributing the plan overwhelmingly to the CIA — prompting Affleck to adjust the film's postscript. Historians note that in reality the Canadians housed the diplomats for the entire period, conceived much of the operation on the ground, and produced the indispensable passports and documentation, while the CIA supplied the film-crew cover and exfiltration expertise.
