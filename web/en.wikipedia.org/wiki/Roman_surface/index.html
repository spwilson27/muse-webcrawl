Roman surface

The Roman surface (also called the Steiner surface) is a self-intersecting mapping of the real projective plane into three-dimensional space. It was first studied by Jakob Steiner during his stay in Rome in 1844, hence its name. It is notable as one of the simplest immersions of the projective plane and appears in algebraic geometry as a quartic surface.

Parametrization

The surface can be parametrized using spherical coordinates with angles θ (longitude) and φ (latitude): x = r² cos φ cos θ sin θ, y = r² sin φ cos φ, z = r² cos θ sin φ cos φ. Equivalently, it satisfies the implicit equation x²y² + y²z² + z²x² − r²xyz = 0 in three-dimensional space.

Properties

The Roman surface has three double lines meeting at a triple point, six pinch points (Whitney singularities), and tetrahedral symmetry. It features three lobes or "ears" extending outward. Because the projective plane is non-orientable, its image in three-space necessarily intersects itself; the Roman surface is one of the most symmetric such realizations. Related models include the cross-cap and Boy's surface, the latter being an immersion without pinch points.
