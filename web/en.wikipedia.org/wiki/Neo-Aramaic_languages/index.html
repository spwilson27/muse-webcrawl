Neo-Aramaic languages

The Neo-Aramaic languages are the modern vernacular descendants of Aramaic, a Semitic language with a documented history of some 3,000 years. They are spoken by Assyrian Christians, Mandaeans, and some Jewish communities whose homelands lie in northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran, and northeastern Syria, and by large diasporas in Europe, North America, and Australia. All Neo-Aramaic varieties are endangered, many severely, following the Assyrian genocide of 1915 and subsequent persecution and emigration.

Classification

Linguists conventionally divide Neo-Aramaic into four groups:

  • Western Neo-Aramaic — spoken in the Syrian villages of Maaloula, Bakh'a, and Jubb'adin near Damascus; the only survivor of the western branch of Aramaic.
  • Central Neo-Aramaic — including Turoyo, spoken in the Tur Abdin region of Turkey, and the extinct Mlahso.
  • Northeastern Neo-Aramaic (NENA) — a large, diverse group of Christian and Jewish dialects across the Mosul plain, the Hakkari mountains, and Lake Urmia, including varieties ancestral to modern Assyrian Neo-Aramaic (Suret) and Chaldean Neo-Aramaic.
  • Neo-Mandaic — spoken by Mandaeans of Iraq and Iran, closely tied to the classical Mandaic liturgical language.

Features

Modern varieties differ greatly from Classical Syriac and from one another in phonology, morphology, and vocabulary, having absorbed heavy influence from Arabic, Kurdish, Turkish, and Persian. Many have restructured the old tense system into new aspect-based forms built on participles, and ergative alignment survives in parts of NENA. Written Neo-Aramaic uses forms of the Syriac alphabet, with a 19th–20th-century literary standard based largely on the Urmia dialect, while spoken dialects diverge so much that mutual unintelligibility between groups is common.

Status

Estimates of total speakers range from several hundred thousand to around a million, but transmission to children has collapsed across most of the homeland and much of the diaspora. Language documentation projects, diaspora schools, and media in Suret aim to preserve the languages, several of which have only a handful of elderly speakers remaining.
