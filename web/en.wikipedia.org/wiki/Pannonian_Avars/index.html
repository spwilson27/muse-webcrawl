The Pannonian Avars (also called the European Avars) were an alliance of several groups of Eurasian nomads of various origins who established the Avar Khaganate, which spanned the Pannonian Basin and considerable areas of Central and Eastern Europe from the late 6th to the early 9th century. They arrived from the Eurasian Steppe, driven westward by the expansion of the Göktürks, and settled in the former Roman province of Pannonia after destroying the Gepids with Lombard help in 567–568.

Origins

The Avars first appeared in European sources in 557–558, when an embassy reached the Byzantine emperor Justinian I in Constantinople. Byzantine writers such as Menander Protector and Theophylact Simocatta recorded their arrival. Scholars have long debated whether the European Avars were the genuine Rouran (the "true Avars" of Inner Asia) or "pseudo-Avars" — an unrelated confederation that adopted the prestigious Avar name. Their material culture, including stirrups, lamellar armour and distinctive belt fittings, displays clear steppe characteristics and major Byzantine and Germanic influences.

History

Under Khagan Bayan I (562–602), the Avars conquered the Gepid kingdom in alliance with the Lombards, occupied the whole Carpathian Basin, and subjugated large Slavic populations. They repeatedly raided the Balkans and extorted enormous tribute from the Byzantine Empire. In 626, in alliance with the Sasanian Persians and Slavic auxiliaries, they laid siege to Constantinople, but the attack failed — a turning point that triggered revolts among their subject peoples, including the Slavs under Samo.

During the 7th and 8th centuries the khaganate contracted but remained the dominant power of the middle Danube. In the 790s Charlemagne launched a series of campaigns against them (791–803); Frankish and Bulgar attacks shattered the khaganate, and the immense treasure hoard accumulated in the khagan's fortified camp, the Ring (hring), was carried off to Aachen. Remnant Avar groups survived into the early 9th century before being absorbed by Slavs, Bulgars and Magyars.

Society and culture

Avar society was organised around the khagan, a paramount ruler with both military and sacral authority. Archaeology has uncovered tens of thousands of Avar-period graves in Hungary, Slovakia, Austria and Romania, rich in horse burials, cast bronze belt sets and gold jewellery. The Avars are credited with introducing or popularising the stirrup in Europe, which transformed mounted warfare.
