Atropa belladonna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atropa belladonna, commonly known as belladonna or deadly nightshade, is a poisonous perennial herbaceous plant in the nightshade family Solanaceae, native to Europe, North Africa and Western Asia. Its foliage and glossy black berries contain tropane alkaloids — atropine, scopolamine and hyoscyamine — that are extremely toxic to humans.

Description

The plant grows to about 1.5 m tall with ovate leaves and dull purple, bell-shaped flowers about 2 cm long, followed by sweet-tasting black berries roughly the size of cherries. It favours calcareous soils in shaded, disturbed ground.

Toxicity

All parts are poisonous; ingestion of even a few berries can be fatal, especially to children. The alkaloids block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing the anticholinergic syndrome: dilated pupils, dry flushed skin, rapid heartbeat, delirium and hallucinations. The specific antidote is physostigmine.

History and uses

The name belladonna ("beautiful lady") is said to derive from the Italian Renaissance practice of using its juice to dilate women's pupils. Historically it was used as a poison, an anaesthetic ingredient and a folk medicine. Its alkaloid atropine remains an essential medicine, used in ophthalmology and to treat bradycardia and organophosphate poisoning.
