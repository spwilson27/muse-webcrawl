1890 Australian maritime dispute

Date16 August – 6 November 1890
LocationAustralian colonies and New Zealand
MethodsStrikes, picketing, boycotts
ResultDefeat for the unions
PartiesMaritime Council and allied unions vs. shipowners, pastoralists and colonial governments

The 1890 Australian maritime dispute, commonly known as the maritime strike, was a major industrial conflict in the Australian colonies and New Zealand between August and November 1890. Beginning as a dispute over union membership in the shipping industry, it spread into a general confrontation between organised labour and employers backed by colonial governments. At its height some 50,000 workers were on strike, including seamen, wharf labourers, shearers and coal miners. The strike ended in defeat for the unions, and its failure was a decisive catalyst for the formation of political labour parties in Australia, most notably the Australian Labor Party.

Background

Australian trade unions had grown rapidly during the long economic boom of the 1880s. The immediate cause of the dispute was the demand by the Marine Officers' Association for the right to affiliate with the Melbourne Trades Hall Council; the shipowners, organised in the Steamship Owners' Association, refused to recognise the affiliation and demanded the officers withdraw from the trades council. When officers refused, they were stood down, and the Maritime Council — representing seamen, wharf labourers and other waterfront unions — called a strike on 16 August 1890.

Course of the strike

The strike spread from Sydney and Melbourne to ports throughout the colonies and to New Zealand. Employers organised "free labour" from non-union workers, protected by police and, in several colonies, by special constables and military forces enrolled under the colonial governments. The pastoralists' associations joined the employers' offensive by demanding freedom of contract in the shearing industry, leading shearers to strike in support.

There were violent clashes at a number of ports, and strike funds — sustained by levies and public donations — were steadily exhausted. The union leadership, divided over strategy and unable to win recognition, gradually accepted defeat. Workers returned to work on employers' terms through October and early November, and the strike officially ended on 6 November 1890.

Consequences

The maritime strike, followed by the defeat of the 1891 shearers' strike and the 1894 shearers' strike, convinced many trade unionists that industrial action alone could not protect their interests. Labor Electoral Leagues were formed in New South Wales and other colonies in 1891; Labor candidates won a balance-of-power bloc in the New South Wales Legislative Assembly that year, and within a decade labour parties contested elections across the colonies. The dispute is therefore regarded as a founding event of the Australian labour movement's political wing. The Australian Council of Trade Unions and histories of the period treat the 1890s strikes as the moment Australian unionism turned decisively towards parliamentary representation.
