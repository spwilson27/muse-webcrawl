Joint replacement

SpecialtyOrthopaedic surgery
Common sitesHip, knee, shoulder, elbow, ankle
Other namesArthroplasty (joint replacement)

Joint replacement is a procedure of orthopaedic surgery, also known as replacement arthroplasty, in which an arthritic or dysfunctional joint surface is replaced with an orthopaedic prosthesis. It is commonly performed for severe joint pain or disability, most often caused by osteoarthritis, when less invasive treatments have failed.

Procedures

The most common procedures are total hip replacement and total knee replacement, of which well over a million are performed annually worldwide. The surgeon removes the damaged cartilage and bone and fits metal, ceramic or polyethylene components, fixed with bone cement or by press-fit surfaces that encourage bone ingrowth. Shoulder, elbow, wrist, ankle and finger joints may also be replaced; in partial (hemi-) replacement only one side of the joint is resurfaced.

Outcomes and risks

Modern prostheses commonly last 15–25 years, and the operation typically relieves pain and restores mobility; hip replacement has been described as "the operation of the century". Risks include infection, dislocation, blood clots, nerve injury, loosening and wear of the components, leg-length inequality and periprosthetic fracture, and many patients eventually require revision surgery. Recovery involves early mobilisation and physiotherapy.
