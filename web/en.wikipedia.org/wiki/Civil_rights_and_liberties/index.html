Civil rights and liberties

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil rights and civil liberties are guarantees of equal social opportunity and equal protection under the law, regardless of race, religion or other personal characteristics. Civil liberties are protections against government action — such as freedom of speech, religion and assembly, and due process rights — while civil rights concern equal treatment and freedom from discrimination, often requiring government action to secure them. The distinction varies by country and author; in the United States both trace to constitutional provisions, especially the Bill of Rights and the Reconstruction Amendments.

History

The modern conception descends from Enlightenment natural-rights theory and eighteenth-century declarations, expanded through struggles: abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, labour rights, and the American civil rights movement (1954–1968), whose campaigns against segregation produced the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965). Comparable movements won equality legislation in many countries, and international law now prohibits discrimination through treaties policed (imperfectly) by courts and UN bodies.

Contemporary issues

Debates continue over the scope of religious exemptions, affirmative action, voting rules, digital privacy and surveillance, hate speech, disability rights, and the rights of non-citizens — and over conflicts between rights themselves, which courts resolve through balancing and proportionality tests. Civil-liberties organisations (such as the ACLU, founded 1920, and Liberty in the UK) litigate and campaign across political lines.
