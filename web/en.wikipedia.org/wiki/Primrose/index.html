The primrose is a common name for flowering plants in the genus Primula, most often referring to Primula vulgaris, the common or English primrose, a perennial flowering plant in the family Primulaceae native to western and southern Europe, northwest Africa, and parts of southwest Asia.

Description

Primula vulgaris is a low-growing perennial, 10–30 cm tall, with a basal rosette of wrinkled, tongue-shaped leaves. The fragrant flowers, typically pale yellow (and occasionally white or pink), are 2–4 cm in diameter and are borne singly on short stalks from late winter to early spring. Wild plants are classic inhabitants of woodland clearings, hedgerows, and grassy banks.

Heterostyly

Primroses are famous in evolutionary biology for heterostyly: populations contain two flower forms, "pin" (long style, low anthers) and "thrum" (short style, high anthers), which promote cross-pollination by insects between forms. Charles Darwin studied the phenomenon in detail and published his findings in 1862, regarding it as a striking example of a mechanism favoring outcrossing. The trait is controlled by a supergene, whose genetic basis has been characterized in modern molecular studies.

Cultivation and uses

Primroses are widely grown as ornamental plants. Numerous cultivars and hybrids — including the colorful polyanthus group (hybrids of P. vulgaris with P. veris and P. juliae) — provide some of the most popular spring bedding plants in shades of yellow, red, pink, purple, and blue. Historically, primrose flowers and leaves were used in herbal medicine, and the flowers are edible, sometimes used in salads or to make primrose wine. The primrose is the county flower of Devon, and in British political tradition, 19 April ("Primrose Day") is associated with Benjamin Disraeli, whose favorite flower it was said to be.
