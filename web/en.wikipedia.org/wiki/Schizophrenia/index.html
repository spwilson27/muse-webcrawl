Schizophrenia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schizophrenia is a mental disorder characterised by disruptions in thought processes, perceptions, emotional responsiveness and behaviour. Its symptoms divide into positive (delusions, hallucinations — most often hearing voices — and disorganised speech), negative (flattened affect, social withdrawal, loss of motivation) and cognitive (impaired attention, working memory and executive function). It typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood and affects roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 300 people over a lifetime.

The name (Bleuler, 1908/1911, 'splitting of the mind') does not mean split personality. Causes are multifactorial: strong heritability (twin studies suggest ~80%), neurodevelopmental differences, and environmental factors including prenatal infection, obstetric complications, cannabis use in adolescence and urban upbringing. The dopamine hypothesis — supported by the mechanism of antipsychotic drugs — explains part, not all, of the picture.

Treatment and outcomes

Antipsychotic medicines (blocking dopamine D2 receptors) control positive symptoms; clozapine is uniquely effective in treatment-resistant cases. Psychosocial rehabilitation, supported employment and family interventions improve functioning. Outcomes vary widely: a substantial minority recover well, many manage chronic symptoms, and life expectancy is reduced by 10–20 years, largely through physical illness and suicide — a disparity services aim to close.

Stigma

Schizophrenia is among the most stigmatised diagnoses; media links to violence are disproportionate to risk, most of which is directed at the self. Early-intervention services (treating first episodes quickly) improve long-term prognosis.
