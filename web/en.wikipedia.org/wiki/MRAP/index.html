Mine-Resistant Ambush Protected (MRAP; pronounced EM-rap) is an American term for vehicles designed specifically to withstand improvised explosive device (IED) attacks and ambushes. The United States Department of Defense MRAP program motivated the funding of designs from a variety of vendors, and delivered roughly 12,000 MRAP vehicles by the program's peak in the late 2000s. Increases in the numbers of these vehicles available in Iraq and Afghanistan coincided with steep declines in coalition deaths from IEDs.

Design

The defining feature of an MRAP is the V-shaped hull, adapted from vehicles developed during the Rhodesian Bush War and by South Africa's defence industry (such as the Casspir). The sloped hull directs the force of an underbody mine or IED blast outward and away from the crew compartment, which is additionally armored, raised high off the ground, and fitted with blast-attenuating seats and restraints. Windows use thick ballistic glass and crew cells are designed as survivable capsules. The trade-offs are high weight, a tall centre of gravity that increases rollover risk, poor off-road performance relative to tracked vehicles, and limited bridge and road compatibility.

Categories and vehicles

The US program procured vehicles in three broad categories: Category I vehicles for urban combat (such as the MaxxPro and Caiman), Category II for convoy and engineer missions (such as the Cougar H and RG-33), and Category III for mine clearing (the Buffalo). The lighter MRAP All-Terrain Vehicle (M-ATV) was developed from 2009 to give better mobility in Afghanistan's terrain. The Joint Light Tactical Vehicle (JLTV) program subsequently sought to combine MRAP-level protection with Humvee-like mobility.

History and disposition

Following the Iraq War's escalating IED campaign, Secretary of Defense Robert Gates made MRAP the Pentagon's top acquisition priority in 2007 in an accelerated program costing roughly $50 billion in total. Casualty rates per IED incident fell markedly as MRAPs replaced up-armored Humvees on patrol routes. After the drawdowns from Iraq and Afghanistan, thousands of surplus vehicles were transferred to allied nations, sold at discount, scrapped, or given to US law enforcement agencies through the 1033 program — transfers that attracted public criticism as part of the debate over police militarization.
