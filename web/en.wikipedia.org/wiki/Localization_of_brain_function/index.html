Localization of brain function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Functional areas of the cerebral cortex

Localization of brain function (or functional localization) is the doctrine that specific mental and behavioural functions are carried out by specific regions of the brain, in opposition to holistic theories holding that the cortex works as an undifferentiated whole. It is a foundational — though continuously qualified — principle of neuroscience, neurology and neuropsychology.

History and evidence

The idea was advanced in a crude form by Franz Joseph Gall's phrenology in the early nineteenth century, which correctly insisted that the cortex is functionally differentiated while wrongly mapping faculties onto skull bumps. Scientific localization began with Paul Broca's 1861 report of the patient "Tan", whose frontal lesion abolished articulate speech (Broca's area), followed by Carl Wernicke's identification of a posterior temporal region for language comprehension (1874), Fritsch and Hitzig's electrical stimulation of motor cortex in dogs (1870), and David Ferrier's and John Hughlings Jackson's clinical-experimental work. Korbinian Brodmann's cytoarchitectonic map (1909) and Wilder Penfield's stimulation maps of motor and sensory "homunculi" in the 1930s–50s entrenched the localist picture. Karl Lashley's experiments on maze learning in rats, however, seemed to show "mass action" and equipotentiality, and modern evidence balances the two views: primary sensory and motor areas are tightly localized, while higher cognition depends on distributed networks — large-scale brain networks such as the default mode network — identified with lesion studies, functional MRI, EEG and transcranial magnetic stimulation. The modern synthesis treats function as localized in networks rather than in single centres, and the developing and injured brain can show substantial reorganisation (neuroplasticity) that older doctrine did not anticipate.
