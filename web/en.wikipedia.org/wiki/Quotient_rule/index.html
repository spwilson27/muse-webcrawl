Quotient rule

In calculus, the quotient rule is a method of finding the derivative of a function that is the ratio of two differentiable functions. Let h(x) = f(x) / g(x), where both f and g are differentiable and g(x) ≠ 0. The quotient rule states that the derivative of h(x) is:

h′(x) = [ f′(x) g(x) − f(x) g′(x) ] / [ g(x) ]²

Examples

A basic example: the derivative of (x² − 2) / (x + 1) can be computed by applying the rule with f(x) = x² − 2 and g(x) = x + 1, yielding [ 2x(x + 1) − (x² − 2) ] / (x + 1)² = (x² + 2x + 2) / (x + 1)². Another common example is the derivative of tan(x) = sin(x)/cos(x), which simplifies to sec²(x).

Proofs

Proof using limits and the product rule

The quotient rule can be derived by writing f(x)/g(x) = f(x) · 1/g(x) and applying the product rule together with the chain rule for the reciprocal function, whose derivative is −g′(x)/g(x)². Combining terms over the common denominator g(x)² gives the result.

Proof using implicit differentiation

Let y = f(x)/g(x), so that f(x) = y·g(x). Differentiating both sides with the product rule gives f′(x) = y′g(x) + y g′(x). Substituting y = f/g and solving for y′ yields the quotient rule.

Higher-order derivatives

Higher derivatives of quotients can be computed by repeated application of the rule, though the expressions grow quickly in complexity; a general formula analogous to the Leibniz rule for products exists but is seldom used in practice.
