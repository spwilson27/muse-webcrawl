Algebraic curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an affine algebraic plane curve is the zero set of a polynomial in two variables. A projective algebraic plane curve is the zero set in the projective plane of a homogeneous polynomial in three variables. More generally, an algebraic curve is an algebraic variety of dimension one. The theory of algebraic curves is one of the oldest and richest parts of algebraic geometry, connecting algebra, geometry, analysis and number theory.

Plane curves

An affine plane curve over a field k is defined by an equation f(x, y) = 0, where f is a polynomial in two variables; the degree of f is the degree of the curve. Lines are curves of degree one, conic sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas and degenerate cases) are curves of degree two, and cubic curves — studied by Newton — include elliptic curves in the nonsingular case. Points where the gradient of f vanishes are singular points; a curve with no singular points is called smooth or nonsingular.

Riemann surfaces and genus

Over the complex numbers, a smooth projective algebraic curve is the same thing as a compact Riemann surface. A fundamental invariant is the genus g, the number of "holes" of the surface: a projective line has genus 0, an elliptic curve genus 1, and a smooth plane curve of degree d has genus (d − 1)(d − 2)/2. The Riemann–Roch theorem relates the dimensions of spaces of meromorphic functions on the curve to its degree and genus, and is a cornerstone of the theory.

Applications

Elliptic curves play a central role in modern number theory — they were key to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem — and in cryptography, where the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem on elliptic curves underlies elliptic-curve cryptography. Higher-genus curves appear in coding theory (algebraic geometry codes) and string theory.
