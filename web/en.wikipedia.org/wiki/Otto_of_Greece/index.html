Otto (Greek: Όθων, romanized: Óthon; 1 June 1815 – 26 July 1867) was a Bavarian prince who became the first king of the modern Kingdom of Greece, reigning from his election in 1832 until his deposition in 1862. The second son of Ludwig I of Bavaria, he was chosen as king by the Great Powers under the London Conference of 1832 following Greece's war of independence from the Ottoman Empire.

Reign

Otto arrived at Nafplion in February 1833, ruling at first through a Bavarian regency council. In 1834 the capital was transferred to Athens, which was rebuilt with neoclassical palaces and public buildings. His absolute rule provoked mounting opposition: the revolution of 3 September 1843 forced him to grant a constitution and convene a national assembly, making Greece a constitutional monarchy. Otto, a Catholic ruling an Orthodox country, never converted, and he and Queen Amalia remained childless, fuelling succession concerns.

Deposition and exile

Fiscal difficulties, interference by the protecting powers and Otto's entanglement in the Crimean War brought opposition to a head; an armed uprising in October 1862 deposed him while he was touring the provinces. Otto and Amalia left Greece aboard a British warship, famously resolving to return one day. He lived out his exile in Bamberg, Bavaria, wearing Greek dress and observing Greek customs until his death in 1867. He was succeeded by the Danish prince who became George I.
