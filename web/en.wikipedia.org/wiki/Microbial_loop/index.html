Microbial loop

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Microbial loop
FieldMarine ecology; biological oceanography
Term coined1983, by Farooq Azam and colleagues
Key componentsDissolved organic matter, bacteria, flagellates, microzooplankton

The microbial loop describes a trophic pathway in aquatic environments in which dissolved organic carbon (DOC) — which would otherwise be lost from the food web — is returned to higher trophic levels through its incorporation into bacterial biomass, which is then consumed by flagellates, ciliates and other microzooplankton and passed on to larger organisms. The concept, formalised by Farooq Azam and co-workers in 1983, revolutionised understanding of marine food webs.

Process

Phytoplankton release a substantial fraction of their photosynthetically fixed carbon as dissolved organic matter through leakage, exudation, viral lysis and sloppy feeding by zooplankton. Heterotrophic bacteria take up this DOC and convert it into particulate biomass. Bacterivorous protists, especially heterotrophic nanoflagellates, graze the bacteria and are themselves eaten by ciliates and small zooplankton such as copepod nauplii, thus re-entering the "classical" grazing food chain of phytoplankton → zooplankton → fish.

Significance

The microbial loop is a major pathway of carbon and nutrient cycling in the oceans: in oligotrophic waters the great majority of primary production passes through bacteria. Microbial remineralisation regenerates inorganic nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, iron) that sustain further photosynthesis, while the additional trophic transfers in the loop also dissipate energy as respiration. Related concepts include the viral shunt, in which viral lysis diverts microbial biomass back into the dissolved pool.
