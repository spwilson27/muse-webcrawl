Manfred (Italian: Manfredi di Sicilia; c. 1232 – 26 February 1266) was the last king of Sicily from the Hohenstaufen dynasty, reigning from 1258 until his death at the Battle of Benevento. An illegitimate son of the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II and Bianca Lancia, he first ruled the kingdom as regent for his young nephew Conradin, then took the crown himself. A celebrated patron of poetry and learning, Manfred became the papacy's chief adversary in Italy, and his fall ended effective Hohenstaufen power in the south.

Early life and regency

Manfred was raised at his father's brilliant court in Sicily and studied at Paris and Bologna. In Frederick II's will he was made Prince of Taranto and regent of Sicily for his half-brother Conrad IV, and retained after Conrad's death in 1254 as regent for the infant Conradin. Beset by invasions sponsored by Pope Innocent IV and his successors, who claimed Sicily as a papal fief, Manfred alternately submitted and fought, at one point fleeing to the Saracens of Lucera before defeating the papal army at Foggia in December 1254.

Kingship

In 1258, on a (false) report of Conradin's death, Manfred had himself crowned king at Palermo. Though the report proved untrue, he kept the throne, arguing that the kingdom needed an adult ruler. His rule was energetic and relatively popular: he founded the city of Manfredonia, patronised the Sicilian School of poetry that had flourished at his father's court, translated works from Greek and Arabic, and built an alliance network among the Ghibelline cities of northern Italy, culminating in the victory of his allies at Montaperti (1260), which briefly gave him dominance from Sicily to Tuscany.

Fall and death

Pope Urban IV and then Clement IV offered the Sicilian crown to Charles of Anjou, brother of Louis IX of France. Charles invaded in 1265, and on 26 February 1266 the armies met at Benevento. Deserted by some of his barons, Manfred fought bravely and was killed in the battle. Because he had died excommunicate, his body was denied burial in consecrated ground; it was buried beside the bridge at Benevento, later exhumed and cast beyond the kingdom's border. His widow Helena and their children were imprisoned by Charles, and his young sons spent decades in captivity.

Legacy

Manfred has been treated sympathetically by posterity. Dante placed him in the Divine Comedy among the late-repentant in Purgatory (Purgatorio, Canto III), where Manfred describes his wounds and declares that God's mercy extends beyond the Church's excommunication. His daughter Constance married Peter III of Aragon; through her claim, Aragon conquered Sicily after the Sicilian Vespers of 1282.
