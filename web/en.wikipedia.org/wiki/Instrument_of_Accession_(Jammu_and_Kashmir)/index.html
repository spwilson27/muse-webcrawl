Instrument of Accession (Jammu and Kashmir)

The Instrument of Accession of Jammu and Kashmir is the legal document executed by Maharaja Hari Singh, ruler of the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, on 26 October 1947, by which the state acceded to the Dominion of India. It was accepted by Lord Mountbatten, Governor-General of India, on 27 October 1947. The accession, made as tribal fighters from Pakistan invaded the state, became the legal basis of India's claim to Kashmir and a central element of the Kashmir conflict between India and Pakistan.

Background

Jammu and Kashmir was the largest princely state in British India, with a Muslim-majority population ruled by a Hindu Dogra maharaja. When British paramountcy lapsed on 15 August 1947, Hari Singh did not accede to either dominion and instead sought to remain independent, signing a standstill agreement with Pakistan; India did not conclude such an agreement. In October 1947, Pashtun tribesmen from Pakistan's North-West Frontier, supported by Pakistan, invaded the state and advanced toward Srinagar. The Maharaja appealed to India for military assistance.

Execution and acceptance

India maintained that it could not intervene in a state that had not acceded. V. P. Menon flew to Srinagar, and the Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, in the same standard form used by other princely states, acceding on defence, external affairs and communications. Mountbatten accepted the accession the following day, adding in his covering letter that, once law and order had been restored, the question of the state's accession should be settled by a reference to the people. Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar on 27 October, beginning the Indo-Pakistani War of 1947–1948.

Terms

The instrument specified that the terms could not be varied by amendment of the Indian Independence Act or the Government of India Act 1935 unless accepted by the Maharaja in a supplementary instrument, and that nothing in it committed him to accept any future constitution of India. Clause 7 preserved the ruler's sovereignty in other matters, and the accession did not oblige the state to accept laws beyond the three acceded subjects without further ratification. These provisions later underpinned Article 370 of the Constitution of India, which granted Jammu and Kashmir special autonomous status until most of its operative provisions were rendered inoperative in August 2019.

Controversy

Pakistan disputes the validity and circumstances of the accession, questioning the timing and authority of the signing, and arguing that it conflicted with the standstill agreement and the wishes of the majority population. Historians have also debated the precise chronology of the signature and the Indian troop airlift. India maintains that the accession was complete, unconditional in law and final, and that the promised reference to the people was an offer by Mountbatten rather than a condition of the instrument itself.
