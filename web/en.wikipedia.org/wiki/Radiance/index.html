In radiometry, radiance is the radiant flux emitted, reflected, transmitted or received by a given surface, per unit solid angle per unit projected area. Radiance is used to characterise diffuse emission and reflection of electromagnetic radiation, and describes how much power will be received by an optical system looking at the surface from a given angle of view.

The SI unit of radiance is the watt per steradian per square metre (W·sr−1·m−2). The analogous photometric quantity, weighted by the sensitivity of the human eye, is luminance, measured in candela per square metre ("nits").

Definition

Radiance L is defined by:

L = ∂²Φ / (∂Ω ∂A cos θ)

where Φ is the radiant flux, Ω is the solid angle, A is the surface area, and θ is the angle between the surface normal and the specified direction.

Properties

A fundamental property of radiance is its conservation along a ray in a lossless medium (more precisely, the quantity L/n², where n is refractive index, is invariant). This is why an extended source does not appear brighter when viewed through an ideal telescope: increased flux is offset by increased image size. For a Lambertian surface — an ideal diffuse reflector or emitter — radiance is independent of viewing angle, and is related to the radiant exitance M by L = M/π. A perfect blackbody at temperature T emits radiance described by Planck's law, integrated over all directions and wavelengths as the Stefan–Boltzmann law.

Applications

Radiance is central to computer graphics rendering (where it is the quantity computed by the rendering equation), remote sensing (satellite instruments report top-of-atmosphere radiance that is converted to surface reflectance or temperature), and optical design.
