Robert Clive

Major-General Robert Clive, 1st Baron Clive (29 September 1725 – 22 November 1774), also known as Clive of India, was the first British Governor of the Bengal Presidency. Clive has been widely credited for laying the foundation of the British East India Company (EIC) rule in Bengal. He began as a writer (the term used then in India for an office clerk) for the EIC in 1744 and established Company rule in Bengal by winning the Battle of Plassey in 1757. In return for supporting the Nawab Mir Jafar as ruler of Bengal, Clive was granted a jagir of £30,000 per year which was the rent the EIC would otherwise pay to the Nawab for their tax-farming concession. When he left India in January 1767 he had a fortune of £180,000 (equivalent to £26 million in 2023) which he remitted through the Dutch East India Company.

Early life

Clive was born at Styche Hall, near Moreton Say, Shropshire, on 29 September 1725 to Richard Clive, a lawyer and minor landowner, and Rebecca Gaskell. He was expelled from several schools and was remembered as a troublemaker. At eighteen he was sent to India as a "writer" (clerk) in the service of the East India Company and arrived at Fort St. George, Madras, in 1744.

First tour of India

During the Carnatic Wars, Clive distinguished himself at the defence of Arcot in 1751, holding the fort against a much larger force for fifty days. His reputation secured him rapid promotion. After the loss of Calcutta to Siraj ud-Daulah in 1756, Clive was sent north with Admiral Charles Watson; together they retook the city in January 1757.

Battle of Plassey

On 23 June 1757, Clive's force of about 3,000 men defeated the Nawab's army of some 50,000 at Plassey, in large part because Mir Jafar, the Nawab's commander, had secretly agreed to defect. The victory gave the Company effective control of Bengal, the richest province of Mughal India.

Later career and death

Clive returned to India as governor of Bengal in 1765, after the Battle of Buxar, and obtained the diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa from the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. He reformed the Company's administration and attempted to curb corruption, including his own officials' acceptance of presents. Back in England he faced parliamentary inquiries into his fortune and conduct; he defended himself vigorously and was vindicated in 1773. Suffering from ill health and depression, he died on 22 November 1774, reportedly by his own hand.

Legacy

Clive is a controversial figure: celebrated in Britain as the founder of British India, he is criticised in India for the exploitation and misgovernment that followed Company rule, including the Bengal famine of 1770. His statue stands outside the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office in London and in Shrewsbury.
