Progressive education, or educational progressivism, is a pedagogical movement that began in the late 19th century and has persisted in various forms to the present. It arose as a reaction against the rote memorisation and rigid discipline of traditional schooling, emphasising learning through experience, the interests of the child, problem solving, and preparation for democratic life.

History

Early influences include Pestalozzi, Froebel (founder of the kindergarten), and Herbart. In the United States, Francis Parker applied progressive methods at the Cook County Normal School, and philosopher John Dewey gave the movement its theoretical foundation in works such as The School and Society (1899) and Democracy and Education (1916). Dewey's Laboratory School at the University of Chicago (1896–1904) tested his ideas in practice. The Progressive Education Association was founded in 1919; William Heard Kilpatrick popularised the project method.

Characteristics

Progressive classrooms typically emphasise integrated curricula rather than separate subjects, learning by doing, collaborative group work, education for democratic citizenship, attention to the child's interests and developmental needs, and assessment through projects and observation rather than standardised tests alone. Related approaches include Montessori education, Waldorf education, and constructivist teaching.

Criticism

Critics have argued that progressive methods underemphasise factual knowledge, academic rigour, and teacher authority, and debates between progressives and traditionalists ("essentialists", later E. D. Hirsch's cultural literacy movement) recur throughout 20th- and 21st-century education policy. See also John Dewey, Constructivism, and Child-centred education.
