Straight-six engine

The straight-six engine (also referred to as an inline-six engine; abbreviated I6 or L6) is a piston engine with six cylinders arranged in a straight line along the crankshaft. A straight-six engine has perfect primary and secondary engine balance, resulting in fewer vibrations than other configurations of six cylinders. It is widely used in automobiles, trucks, boats, and industrial equipment.

Design

The straight-six shares its layout with the straight-four but adds two cylinders; its inherent balance comes from the pistons' paired arrangement, with firing intervals of 120 degrees of crank rotation providing even power delivery. The design is long, however, which complicates packaging in front-wheel-drive cars with transversely mounted engines, and long crankshafts can suffer torsional vibration, addressed by harmonic dampers and firing-order design.

History and applications

Manufactured since the early 1900s (Spyker built an early example in 1903), the straight-six became the standard American car engine from the 1920s through the 1970s and dominated British and European premium cars. Famous examples include the Jaguar XK engine, the BMW straight-sixes, the Toyota 2JZ, the Chrysler Slant Six, and the AMC/Jeep 4.0 litre engine. From the 1990s many manufacturers switched to the more compact V6, but the 2010s–2020s saw renewed interest in inline-sixes — by Mercedes-Benz, Mazda, and Stellantis — owing to balance, simplicity of turbocharging, and shared architecture with inline-fours. Diesel straight-sixes remain standard in heavy trucks and marine applications.
