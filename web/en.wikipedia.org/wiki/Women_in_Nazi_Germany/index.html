Women in Nazi Germany

Women in Nazi Germany were subject to the doctrines of Nazism implemented by the Nazi Party, which actively excluded women from senior public roles and sought to confine them to the roles of mother and wife. Nazi policy toward women rested on the slogan Kinder, Küche, Kirche ("children, kitchen, church"), and women were encouraged to leave the workforce, marry, and produce children deemed racially valuable to the Volk.

Policy before the war

From 1933, women were pushed out of professions such as law, medicine and civil service. Marriage loans were offered to couples on condition that the wife gave up employment, and mothers of large families were decorated with the Cross of Honour of the German Mother. Girls were channelled into the League of German Girls (Bund Deutscher Mädel), the female counterpart of the Hitler Youth, which trained them in domestic skills and ideological loyalty.

Education and employment

A quota limited women to ten percent of university places. Nevertheless, as rearmament and then the Second World War created labour shortages, the regime reversed course and pressed women into factories and auxiliary service, contradicting its earlier ideology. By 1944, millions of German women were working in war industries, and many served as auxiliaries (Wehrmachthelferinnen) in anti-aircraft and signals roles.

Women and the Holocaust

While the regime cast women as apolitical, research since the 1990s has shown that German women participated in and enabled the crimes of the regime — as nurses involved in the T4 euthanasia programme, as secretaries administering deportations, as guards in concentration camps, and as wives and administrators in the occupied eastern territories.
