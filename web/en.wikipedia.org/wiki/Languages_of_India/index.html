Languages of India

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

India is one of the most linguistically diverse countries in the world. The 2011 census recorded speakers of 121 languages each with more than 10,000 speakers, and rationalised more than 19,500 raw returns of mother tongues. Indian languages belong mainly to four families: Indo-Aryan (spoken by about 78% of the population), Dravidian (about 20%), and smaller Austroasiatic and Sino-Tibetan groups, concentrated in central, eastern and northeastern regions.

Official status

The Constitution of India designates Hindi in the Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, with English continuing as an associate official language. The Eighth Schedule lists 22 scheduled languages, including Bengali, Telugu, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu, Gujarati, Malayalam, Kannada, Odia, Punjabi, Assamese and Sanskrit. States are free to adopt their own official languages, and India was reorganised largely along linguistic lines beginning with the States Reorganisation Act of 1956.

Major languages

By first-language speakers, the largest are Hindi (over 520 million including many counted varieties), Bengali (about 97 million), Marathi, Telugu, Tamil, Gujarati, Urdu, Kannada, Odia and Malayalam. Sanskrit, though the mother tongue of few, remains a liturgical and scholarly language of great cultural importance. English functions as a pan-Indian language of higher education, law and commerce.

Scripts

Most major Indian languages are written in scripts derived from Brahmi: Devanagari (Hindi, Marathi, Nepali), Bengali–Assamese, Gurmukhi (Punjabi), Gujarati, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam scripts. Urdu is written in a Perso-Arabic script (Nastaliq), and some languages, such as Santali, have their own scripts (Ol Chiki).

Language policy and issues

Language policy — including the three-language formula in schools, the status of Hindi, and demands for classical-language status (granted to Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Odia, among others) — remains politically sensitive. Hundreds of smaller languages, many of them tribal, are endangered and the subject of documentation efforts.
