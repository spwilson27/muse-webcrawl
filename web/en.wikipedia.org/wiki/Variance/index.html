Variance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Two distributions with the same mean, one narrow and one wide]

In probability theory and statistics, variance is the expected value of the squared deviation from the mean: Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]. It measures how spread out a distribution is. Its square root is the standard deviation σ, which shares the units of the data and is the more commonly reported figure.

The identity Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² simplifies computation. For independent variables, variances add under summation — the reason errors and risks combine by root-sum-of-squares.

Uses

Variance and covariance are the machinery of least squares, ANOVA (analysis of variance — partitioning total variance among sources), portfolio theory (Markowitz, 1952: return versus variance as risk), and quality control. The sample variance divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) to remain unbiased.

Chebyshev's inequality bounds tail probabilities using only mean and variance (e.g. at most 1/4 of values lie beyond 2σ), while the normal distribution's tighter 95% rule shows what extra assumptions buy.
