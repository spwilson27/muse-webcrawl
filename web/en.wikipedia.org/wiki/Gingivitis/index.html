Gingivitis

Gingivitis is a non-destructive disease that causes inflammation of the gums (gingiva). The most common form, plaque-induced gingivitis, results from bacterial biofilm (dental plaque) accumulating at and below the gum margin, producing redness, swelling and bleeding on brushing or gentle probing. Gingivitis is the mildest and most prevalent form of periodontal disease, affecting the majority of adults to some degree, and it is fully reversible with effective oral hygiene; if untreated, however, it can progress in susceptible individuals to periodontitis, in which inflammation destroys the supporting tissues and bone of the teeth.

Signs and symptoms

Healthy gums are pale pink and firm; in gingivitis they become red or purplish, swollen and glossy, lose their stippled texture, and bleed readily when brushed, flossed or probed. The condition is usually painless, so many people are unaware of it, though halitosis (bad breath) and a bad taste may occur. Unlike periodontitis, there is no loss of attachment or bone — the inflammation is confined to the soft tissue and resolves completely once plaque is removed.

Causes and modifying factors

The essential cause is dental plaque: when oral hygiene lapses, the microbial biofilm at the gumline matures and provokes an inflammatory response within days to weeks — classically demonstrated in the experimental gingivitis studies of Löe and colleagues (1965), in which healthy volunteers developed gingivitis after 2–3 weeks without brushing, which then resolved when cleaning resumed. The response is exaggerated by hormonal changes (puberty, pregnancy, oral contraceptives), systemic diseases such as diabetes, smoking, vitamin C deficiency (scurvy), and certain drugs (phenytoin, ciclosporin, calcium-channel blockers) that cause gingival enlargement. Specific forms include necrotising ulcerative gingivitis (painful, punched-out ulcers of the gum papillae, associated with stress, smoking and immunosuppression) and desquamative gingivitis linked to mucocutaneous diseases such as lichen planus.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on appearance and bleeding on probing; radiographs show no bone loss. Treatment consists of professional removal of plaque and calculus (scaling) and sustained personal plaque control by twice-daily brushing with fluoride toothpaste and interdental cleaning. Antiseptic mouthwashes containing chlorhexidine may help short-term. With these measures inflammation normally resolves within one to two weeks.
