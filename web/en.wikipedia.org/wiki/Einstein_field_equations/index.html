Einstein field equations

In the general theory of relativity, the Einstein field equations (EFE) describe how the curvature of spacetime is determined by the matter and energy within it. Published by Albert Einstein in 1915, the equations form the core of general relativity: they relate the geometry of a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold to the distribution of mass–energy it contains. As John Archibald Wheeler summarised it, "spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."

Form of the equations

The equations are commonly written as

Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c4) Tμν

where Gμν is the Einstein tensor, which encodes the curvature of spacetime; Λ is the cosmological constant; gμν is the metric tensor; G is Newton's gravitational constant; c is the speed of light; and Tμν is the stress–energy tensor describing the density and flux of energy and momentum. The left side represents curvature, the right side the matter and energy content.

Nature and solutions

The EFE are a system of ten coupled, nonlinear partial differential equations for the components of the metric tensor. Because of their nonlinearity, exact solutions are known only under assumptions of symmetry. Famous solutions include the Schwarzschild solution (1916) for a spherically symmetric mass, which predicts black holes; the Kerr solution (1963) for a rotating mass; and the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric used in physical cosmology to describe an expanding universe.

Weak-field limit and tests

In the limit of weak gravity and slow motion the equations reduce to Newton's law of universal gravitation, so general relativity reproduces Newtonian predictions where they apply. Their distinctive predictions — the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, the deflection of light by the Sun, gravitational redshift, frame-dragging, and gravitational waves — have all been confirmed by observation, most recently by the direct detection of gravitational waves in 2015 and the imaging of black-hole shadows by the Event Horizon Telescope.

History

Einstein arrived at the final form of the equations in November 1915 after years of work with the mathematician Marcel Grossmann, presenting them to the Prussian Academy of Sciences. David Hilbert derived equivalent equations from an action principle at almost the same time. Einstein added the cosmological constant in 1917 to permit a static universe, later abandoning it; it was revived in the late 1990s to account for the accelerating expansion of the universe, interpreted as dark energy.
