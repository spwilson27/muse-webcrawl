Chronophotography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chronophotography is a photographic technique that captures several phases of a moving subject in a single image or a rapid series of images, in order to study motion. Developed in the 1870s and 1880s, it bridged the gap between still photography and cinema, and it profoundly influenced both the science of biomechanics and modern art.

Pioneers

In 1878 Eadweard Muybridge photographed a galloping horse at Palo Alto, California, using a battery of cameras triggered in sequence, settling the question of whether all four hooves leave the ground at once (they do). His later Animal Locomotion studies at the University of Pennsylvania (1884–87) produced thousands of motion sequences of humans and animals.

The French physiologist Étienne-Jules Marey pursued a scientific method he called the "graphic method" and, from 1882, used his chronophotographic gun — a camera shaped like a rifle — to record birds in flight and other movements on a single plate, later on roll film. Marey aimed at analysis rather than pictures: he dressed subjects in black suits with white lines along the limbs to extract the geometry of gait. His assistant Georges Demenÿ also contributed significantly, photographing speech movements for the deaf.

Influence

Chronophotography laid technical groundwork for motion pictures: Marey's roll-film chronophotographs of 1888–90 anticipated cinematography. Its overlapping figures also inspired modernist art — Marcel Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2 (1912) and Italian Futurist works by Balla and Bragaglia directly echo its visual language. Today the technique survives in high-speed photography, motion capture, and sports science.
