Licchavis of Vaishali

The Licchavis were an ancient Indo-Aryan clan of north-eastern India whose capital was the city of Vaishali (Vaiśālī), in present-day Bihar. In the early first millennium BCE they formed the leading clan of the Vajjian confederacy (Vrijji/Vajji), one of the sixteen mahājanapadas of ancient India, and were notable for their republican form of government, in which power was shared among an assembly of clan chiefs rather than concentrated in a king.

Government

Buddhist and Jain texts describe the Licchavis as governed by an assembly of some 7,707 rājās (chiefs), who met in the assembly hall at Vaishali to deliberate on policy. Executive authority was exercised by elected officers, including a president or chief. The Buddha is said to have praised the Licchavis' concord and their observance of traditional customs, and the principles he recommended to them were later held up as models for the Buddhist monastic order (sangha).

History

The Licchavis were closely connected with the lives of both Mahāvīra, the Jain teacher, who was born at Kundagrama near Vaishali, and the Buddha, who visited Vaishali many times and announced his approaching death in its vicinity. The confederacy came into conflict with the expansionist kingdom of Magadha; after a long struggle, King Ajātaśatru of Magadha defeated the Vajjians in the fifth or fourth century BCE, using diplomacy to sow dissension among them. In later centuries a Licchavi clan rose to power in Nepal, where the Licchavi kingdom flourished from roughly the fourth to the eighth century CE.
