Corpus Christi

Corpus Christi (Latin for “Body of Christ”) is a Western Christian solemnity celebrating the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist. Formally titled the Solemnity of the Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ, it is observed by the Catholic Church, some Anglican churches, and some Lutheran churches on the Thursday (or, in many countries, the Sunday) after Trinity Sunday.

History

The feast originated in the Diocese of Liège in the 13th century, inspired by the visions of the Augustinian nun Juliana of Liège, who reported a recurring vision of the Church under a full moon with one dark spot, interpreted as the absence of a feast devoted to the Blessed Sacrament. The feast was celebrated locally in 1246 and, after the reported Eucharistic miracle of Bolsena, Pope Urban IV extended it to the universal Church in 1264 with the bull Transiturus. Thomas Aquinas composed the office for the feast, including the hymns Pange lingua, Lauda Sion, and Adoro te devote.

Observance

The most distinctive feature of the day is the procession: a consecrated host is placed in a monstrance and carried through the streets, often under a canopy, with streets decorated by flowers and, in places such as Valencia and parts of Germany and Poland, elaborate flower carpets. Corpus Christi is a public holiday in many predominantly Catholic countries, including Austria, Brazil, Poland, Spain (locally), and parts of Germany and Switzerland.

In other traditions

The feast was abolished in England during the Reformation but has been restored in many Anglo-Catholic parishes. Some Lutheran churches in Scandinavia retained it. In medieval England and elsewhere, the day was also associated with the performance of mystery plays by craft guilds, including the famous Corpus Christi cycles of York and Chester.
