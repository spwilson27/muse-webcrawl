Emerging infectious disease

An emerging infectious disease (EID) is an infectious disease whose incidence has increased recently (in the past 20 years) and could increase in the near future. The majority of pathogens causing emerging diseases are zoonotic, originating in animals. Sometimes the term is also applied to a re-emerging disease, a disease that was once in decline but has reappeared, often in a drug-resistant form.

Contributing factors

Factors in the emergence of new diseases include ecological and environmental change, expansion of human populations into new areas, international travel and commerce, microbial adaptation and evolution, breakdown of public health measures, and changes in human susceptibility to infection. Land-use change and climate change are thought to alter contact between humans, wildlife, and vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks.

Examples

Well-known emerging diseases include HIV/AIDS, Ebola virus disease, SARS (2002–2004), Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), Zika fever, Lyme disease, and COVID-19, caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, which produced a pandemic beginning in 2019. Re-emerging diseases include tuberculosis with multidrug-resistant strains and measles in regions where vaccination coverage has fallen.

Surveillance and response

The World Health Organization and national agencies such as the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention maintain surveillance systems to detect outbreaks early. Responses include epidemiological investigation, development of diagnostics, vaccines, and therapeutics, quarantine and travel measures, and risk communication. The One Health approach coordinates human, animal, and environmental health sectors to anticipate zoonotic spillover.
