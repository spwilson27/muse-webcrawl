Sumerian (Sumerian: 𒅴𒂠 eme-gir15, "native tongue") is the language of ancient Sumer, spoken in southern Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq) from at least the fourth millennium BC. It is the earliest known written language, first attested in the Uruk period around 3100 BC, and the first for which a substantial literature survives. Sumerian is a language isolate: it is not demonstrably related to any other known language, living or dead.

History

Sumerian was gradually replaced as a vernacular by the Semitic language Akkadian during the third millennium BC and had died out as a mother tongue by around 2000–1800 BC. It nonetheless survived for nearly two thousand more years as the language of religion, scholarship and literature in Babylonia and Assyria, taught in scribal schools (the edubba) much as Latin was in medieval Europe. Its decipherment in the nineteenth century, through the work of scholars such as Edward Hincks and Henry Rawlinson, founded the field of Assyriology.

Grammar

Sumerian is an agglutinative language: grammatical elements are strung together as chains of prefixes and suffixes around a verbal root. It is ergative–absolutive in alignment, marking the subject of a transitive verb differently from the subject of an intransitive verb. Nouns belong to two genders — animate and inanimate — and the language makes heavy use of compound verbs and a large inventory of verbal affixes encoding person, number, tense-aspect and direction.

Writing

Sumerian was written in cuneiform, wedge-shaped signs impressed into wet clay with a reed stylus. The system began as pictographic accounting tokens and developed into a mixed logographic–syllabic script with several hundred signs, many having multiple readings. Surviving texts include the world's earliest literature, such as the poems ancestral to the Epic of Gilgamesh, hymns, law codes, proverbs and school exercises.
