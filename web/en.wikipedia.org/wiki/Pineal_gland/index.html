The pineal gland (also called the pineal body or epiphysis) is a small endocrine gland in the brain of most vertebrates. In humans it is a reddish-grey, pinecone-shaped structure about 5–8 mm long, located in the epithalamus near the centre of the brain. Its principal function is the production of melatonin, a hormone that modulates sleep patterns and circadian rhythms.

Function

The pineal gland receives information about light and darkness from the retina via the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus. In darkness it converts serotonin to melatonin and releases it into the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid, promoting sleepiness; light suppresses production. Melatonin also helps regulate seasonal rhythms and the timing of puberty in many animals.

Structure

The gland consists mainly of pinealocytes, which secrete melatonin, supported by glial cells. With age it often accumulates calcium deposits known as corpora arenacea ("brain sand"), which make it a useful landmark in radiographs of the skull.

History and cultural significance

The pineal gland was the last of the endocrine glands to have its function understood. The philosopher René Descartes famously proposed it as the principal seat of the soul and the point of the brain and the immaterial mind — a view now of only historical interest. Some other vertebrates have light-sensitive pineal organs, and the gland is evolutionarily related to the "third eye" of some reptiles and amphibians.
