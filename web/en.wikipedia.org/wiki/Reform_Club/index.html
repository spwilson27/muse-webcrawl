Reform Club

The Reform Club is a private members' club, now primarily social, on the south side of Pall Mall in central London. Founded in 1836 by Edward Ellice, it was established for the new MPs created by the Great Reform Act of 1832, together with Whig and Radical peers and gentlemen, as a liberal political headquarters. Today it is one of the grandest of the surviving London clubs, and admits both men and women.

The building

The clubhouse, opened in 1841, was designed by Sir Charles Barry — architect of the Houses of Parliament — in the style of an Italian Renaissance palazzo, modelled on the Palazzo Farnese in Rome. Its central feature is a large galleried saloon under a glazed roof. In the 1840s and 1850s, under its celebrated chef Alexis Soyer, the club's kitchens were among the most famous in Europe.

History

The club was a centre of Liberal politics through the 19th century, associated with the Liberal Party that emerged from the Whigs. In the 20th century its political character faded; it amalgamated with the ladies' Lyceum Club in 1981 and began admitting women on equal terms. Its membership is no longer linked to a party.

In fiction

The Reform Club is the starting and finishing point of Phileas Fogg's journey in Jules Verne's Around the World in Eighty Days (1873), where Fogg makes his famous wager with fellow members. It also appears in works by authors such as Graham Greene and in the BBC series featuring the club as a meeting place of the establishment.
