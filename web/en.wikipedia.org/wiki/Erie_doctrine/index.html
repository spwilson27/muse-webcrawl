Erie doctrine

The Erie doctrine is a binding principle of United States federal law derived from Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), in which the Supreme Court held that federal courts exercising diversity jurisdiction must apply state substantive law and federal procedural law. Justice Brandeis's opinion declared: "There is no federal general common law."

Background

Erie overruled Swift v. Tyson (1842), under which federal courts had applied a general common law in diversity cases, producing forum shopping and inconsistent outcomes between state and federal courts in the same state.

Application

Later cases refined the doctrine: Guaranty Trust v. York (outcome-determinative test), Byrd v. Blue Ridge (balancing), and Hanna v. Plumer (1965), under which valid Federal Rules of Civil Procedure govern when they directly conflict with state practice. The Rules of Decision Act and the Rules Enabling Act provide the statutory framework.

Significance

The doctrine is a cornerstone of American federalism and civil procedure teaching, limiting federal judicial lawmaking and ensuring uniformity within each state.
