Remilitarisation of the Rhineland
Date7 March 1936
LocationRhineland, Germany
ActionGerman troops enter the demilitarised Rhineland
OutcomeViolation of the Treaties of Versailles and Locarno; no military response from France or Britain

The remilitarisation of the Rhineland took place on 7 March 1936, when German military forces entered the Rhineland, which had been designated as a demilitarised zone under the Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno Treaties. The move was a direct violation of those treaties and represented a significant gamble by Adolf Hitler, who ordered the operation despite the misgivings of his generals; German troops had orders to retreat if the French army advanced.

Background

Articles 42–44 of the Treaty of Versailles forbade Germany from fortifying or stationing troops on the left bank of the Rhine or within 50 km of its right bank. The Locarno Treaties of 1925, freely negotiated by Germany, reaffirmed the demilitarisation and the Franco-German and Belgo-German frontiers.

The operation

Using the ratification of the Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance as a pretext, on the morning of 7 March 1936 Hitler announced the repudiation of the Locarno Treaties and German forces — at first only a small number of battalions, later built up to around 30,000 men — marched into the zone to be welcomed by cheering crowds.

Reactions

France, in the midst of political turmoil and an election campaign, appealed to the League of Nations but did not mobilise; French military estimates greatly exaggerated German strength. Britain was unwilling to act, with much of British opinion holding that Germany was merely "going into its own back garden". The League formally condemned the violation but took no action. The episode is generally seen as a turning point: it ended the Locarno system, emboldened Hitler, and shifted the strategic balance, allowing Germany to fortify its western frontier (the Westwall) and freeing it to act in the east.
