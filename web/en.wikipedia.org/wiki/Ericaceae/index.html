Ericaceae

Common heather (Calluna vulgaris), one of the best-known members of the heath family.

The Ericaceae are a family of flowering plants, commonly known as the heath family or heather family, found most commonly in acidic and infertile growing conditions. The family is large, with about 4,250 known species spread across 124 genera, making it the 14th most species-rich family of flowering plants. Well-known members include the cranberry, blueberry, bilberry, lingonberry, huckleberry, azalea, rhododendron, and heather.

Description

The Ericaceae consist of herbs, dwarf shrubs, shrubs, and trees. Their leaves are usually evergreen, alternate or whorled, simple and without stipules. Flowers are hermaphrodite and show a considerable range of form; in many genera the corolla is bell- or urn-shaped. The fruit may be a berry, capsule, or drupe.

Distribution and ecology

The family is widespread, absent only from Antarctica, parts of the lowland tropics, and the high Arctic. Ericaceae are characteristic components of heathlands and acidic woodlands and bogs. Most members form symbiotic associations with mycorrhizal fungi (ericoid mycorrhiza), which help the plants obtain nutrients from poor, acidic soils.

Taxonomy

The family is placed in the order Ericales. Modern classifications based on molecular phylogenetics include the former families Empetraceae, Epacridaceae, Monotropaceae, Prionotaceae, and Pyrolaceae within Ericaceae, and recognize eight subfamilies, including Ericoideae, Vaccinioideae, and Monotropoideae.

Uses

Many species are grown as ornamental plants, particularly rhododendrons and azaleas. Economically important fruit crops include cranberry, blueberry, and lingonberry. Some members, such as species of Kalmia and Rhododendron, contain grayanotoxins and are toxic to livestock and humans.
