Fundamental theorem of calculus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A curve with its accumulated area growing beside it, differentiation and integration drawn as inverse arrows]

The fundamental theorem of calculus is the theorem linking the two central operations of calculus: it states that differentiation and integration are inverse processes. In its first form, the integral of a function, accumulated up to a point, has that function as its derivative; in its second, a definite integral can be computed from any antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints.

The theorem turned integration from laborious limit computations into a table lookup — find an antiderivative — and is the reason calculus works as a practical tool. Newton and Leibniz both saw the connection in the 1670s–80s; rigorous proofs came with 19th-century analysis.

Meaning

Intuitively: the total accumulation of a quantity is built from its instantaneous rates, and changes in the accumulation recover the rate. Stock and flow, distance and speed, area and height are all instances.

Generalisations of the theorem — Green's, Stokes' and the divergence theorem — are milestones of multivariable calculus.
