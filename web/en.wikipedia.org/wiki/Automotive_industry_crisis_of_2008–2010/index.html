Automotive industry crisis of 2008–2010

The automotive industry crisis of 2008–2010 was a global financial downturn in the automotive industry, part of the wider Great Recession. The crisis primarily affected the American automobile manufacturers, with European and Asian makers also suffering substantial losses. Record high oil prices in mid-2008 collapsed demand for the large trucks and SUVs on which the American "Big Three" depended, and the ensuing credit crunch prevented many consumers from financing new vehicles.

United States bailouts

In late 2008 General Motors and Chrysler sought emergency federal assistance; Ford, having borrowed heavily before the credit markets froze, declined a bailout but supported aid for its rivals to protect the shared supplier base. The United States government provided bridge loans through the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), and in 2009 both Chrysler and General Motors passed through government-managed Chapter 11 bankruptcy reorganizations, emerging within weeks as restructured companies. The Auto Industry Financing Program ultimately disbursed about $80 billion.

Effects and recovery

US light vehicle sales fell from over 16 million units in 2007 to about 10.4 million in 2009, the lowest level in decades. Manufacturers closed plants, discontinued brands including Pontiac, Saturn, Hummer and Mercury, and cut hundreds of thousands of jobs. Sales recovered steadily through the following decade, and the US Treasury ultimately recovered most of its outlay. Governments in Europe and Canada also supported their industries, and scrappage schemes such as the American "Cash for Clunkers" program temporarily boosted demand.
