Russian nobility

The Russian nobility (Russian: дворянство, dvoryanstvo) was a privileged estate of the Russian Empire and, before it, of the Tsardom of Russia. It arose from the medieval service class of retainers and boyars and by the 18th century formed the empire's landowning and office-holding elite. Membership conferred exemption from taxation and corporal punishment, the right to own serfs, and preferential access to state service and education.

The nobility's status was systematised by Peter the Great, whose Table of Ranks (1722) tied noble status to service in the military, civil administration or court, and by Catherine the Great's Charter to the Nobility (1785), which confirmed corporate rights and provincial noble assemblies. Nobles were registered in provincial genealogy books according to the origin of their title.

Origins

The early Russian nobility grew out of the retinues (druzhina) of the Kievan and Muscovite princes. The boyars, descendants of old princely and landowning families, stood at the top, while lesser servitors held land (pomestie) conditionally on military service. Under Ivan IV the oprichnina shattered many old families, and the Time of Troubles further reshuffled the elite.

The Table of Ranks

Peter I's Table of Ranks divided military, civil and court service into fourteen parallel grades. Reaching the eighth civil rank (or the fourteenth military rank before later reforms) conferred hereditary nobility, making service rather than birth the formal gateway to the estate. This opened the nobility to commoners of talent while binding the old aristocracy to the state.

Charter to the Nobility

Catherine II's Charter of 1785 guaranteed nobles personal inviolability, freedom from compulsory service (already granted by Peter III in 1762), the right to travel abroad, and exclusive rights to own populated estates. Each province maintained a noble assembly headed by an elected marshal of nobility.

Decline and abolition

The emancipation of the serfs in 1861 undermined the economic basis of the landed nobility, and many estates passed to merchants and peasants. After the February Revolution of 1917 the Provisional Government abolished estates, and the Bolshevik Decree on the Abolition of Estates and Civil Ranks (November 1917) formally ended the nobility's legal existence. Many noble families emigrated; others were repressed during the Red Terror and the Soviet period.
