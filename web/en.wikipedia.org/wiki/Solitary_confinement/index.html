Solitary confinement

Solitary confinement is a form of imprisonment in which an incarcerated person lives in a single cell with little or no contact with other people. Prisoners in solitary are typically confined for 22 to 24 hours a day, with meals delivered through a slot in the door and limited exercise alone in a small yard or cage. It is used variously as a punishment for rule violations, as "administrative segregation" for people deemed dangerous, and as protective custody. The practice has drawn sustained criticism from human-rights bodies, and international standards — the UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (the Nelson Mandela Rules, 2015) — define solitary beyond 15 consecutive days as "prolonged" and prohibit it, regarding it as potentially amounting to torture or cruel treatment.

A typical segregation cell.

History

Solitary confinement was systematised in the early 19th century in the United States, where the Quaker-inspired "Pennsylvania system" at Eastern State Penitentiary (opened 1829) kept prisoners alone at all times, in the belief that silence and solitude would induce penitence and reform. Charles Dickens, visiting in 1842, condemned the regime as cruel and destructive. Most systems abandoned total isolation by the late 19th century, but long-term segregation returned on a large scale from the 1980s with the construction of "supermax" prisons such as USP Marion and ADX Florence in the United States, designed to hold prisoners in near-total isolation for years.

Effects

Psychiatric research beginning with studies by Stuart Grassian and Craig Haney documents a characteristic syndrome associated with isolation: anxiety, depression, paranoia, hallucinations, cognitive disturbance, hypersensitivity to stimuli, and elevated rates of self-harm and suicide. Studies also show suicide is markedly more common among prisoners who have spent time in solitary. Reform advocates argue the practice worsens mental illness and post-release outcomes; correctional officials argue it is needed to manage violence and protect staff and other prisoners.

Use around the world

Many European systems limit disciplinary segregation to days or a few weeks. In the United States, estimates by the Bureau of Justice Statistics have suggested roughly 4–5% of state and federal prisoners are held in solitary on any given day — tens of thousands of people. Litigation and legislation in the 2010s and 2020s reduced its use in a number of jurisdictions, and the US federal system adopted limits, though practices vary widely. Norway's system, by contrast, makes minimal use of isolation and is often cited by reformers.
