Shape notes
TypeMusic notation system
OriginNew England, late 18th century
Key textsThe Sacred Harp (1844)

Shape notes are a music notation system designed to facilitate congregational and community singing. In shape-note singing, each scale degree is given a distinct note-head shape as well as a syllable name, so that singers can sight-read hymns in four-part harmony without instrumental accompaniment or formal training. The tradition is strongest in the rural American South, where "singing schools" and all-day singings from books such as The Sacred Harp remain active.

Systems

Two families of systems exist. Four-shape (fasola) systems use the syllables fa, sol, la and mi repeated across the scale, a method with roots in the Guidonian hexachord. Seven-shape systems assign a different shape and syllable to each degree of the major and minor scale (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti), and were widely used in gospel songbooks of the late 19th and 20th centuries.

History

The first American shape-note tunebook, The Easy Instructor (1801), by William Smith and William Little, introduced shaped noteheads to teach singing in New England. The practice flourished in the South and West through singing schools run by itinerant masters. B. F. White and E. J. King's The Sacred Harp (1844) became the most enduring four-shape book; its singings, organised around a hollow square of singers with a rotating leader, continue today across the United States and abroad.

Practice

At a Sacred Harp singing, participants first "sing the shapes" — performing the notes using the syllable names — before singing the words. The singing is loud, fast and unaccompanied, with emphasis on participation rather than performance, and singers typically take a midday break for "dinner on the grounds".
