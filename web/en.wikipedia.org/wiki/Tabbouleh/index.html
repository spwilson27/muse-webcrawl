Tabbouleh (Arabic: تبولة, romanised tabbūla) is a Levantine salad made mostly of finely chopped parsley, with tomatoes, mint, onion, soaked uncooked bulgur, and seasoned with olive oil, lemon juice, salt and sweet pepper. Some variations add lettuce or use semolina instead of bulgur. It is traditionally served as part of a mezze in the Eastern Mediterranean and the Arab world, often scooped up with leaves of romaine lettuce.

History and etymology

The name derives from the Arabic word taabil, meaning seasoning or dip. Edible herbs known as qaḍb were part of regional diets in the mountains of Lebanon and Syria in the medieval period, and dishes of chopped herbs with wheat developed there over centuries. Tabbouleh is strongly associated with Lebanon, where it is considered a national dish, and with Syria; from the Levant it spread throughout the Middle East and, with Lebanese and Syrian emigration, worldwide.

Preparation

In the Levantine style, parsley dominates—the salad is a parsley salad with wheat, rather than a wheat salad with parsley. Flat-leaf parsley is chopped very finely, combined with a small quantity of fine bulgur softened in water or tomato juice, diced tomatoes, chopped mint and scallions (or onion), and dressed with lemon juice and olive oil. The mixture rests briefly so the bulgur absorbs the juices. In Turkey, a related salad, kısır, reverses the proportions, using much more bulgur plus tomato or pepper paste.

Variations and records

Regional variations use pomegranate seeds in parts of Syria and Turkey, and some modern adaptations substitute quinoa or couscous for bulgur to make the dish gluten-free. Lebanon has repeatedly produced record-setting giant bowls of tabbouleh; in 2009 a team of chefs in Beirut prepared a bowl weighing more than 4,300 kilograms, recognised as a world record, amid a public dispute with Israel over the cultural ownership of dishes such as tabbouleh and hummus.
