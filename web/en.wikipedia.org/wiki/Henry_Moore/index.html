Henry Spencer Moore (30 July 1898 – 31 August 1986) was an English artist, widely regarded as the most important British sculptor of the 20th century. He is best known for his monumental semi-abstract bronze sculptures of reclining figures and mother-and-child forms, pierced by holes and hollows, which are located around the world as public art.

Early life and formation

Moore was born in Castleford, Yorkshire, the son of a coal miner. After war service — he was gassed at the Battle of Cambrai in 1917 — he studied at Leeds School of Art and the Royal College of Art in London. Influenced by the direct carving ethos of Brâncuși, by pre-Columbian Mexican sculpture (notably the Chac Mool reclining figures) and African art, he made his first reclining figure in 1929 and won growing recognition through the 1930s, associating with Barbara Hepworth and the Unit One group.

War art and international fame

As an official war artist during the Blitz, Moore produced the famous Shelter Drawings of Londoners sleeping in Underground stations, which made him a household name. Victory in the sculpture prize at the 1948 Venice Biennale launched his international career. Major commissions followed: the Reclining Figure for UNESCO headquarters in Paris (1958), Knife Edge Two Piece in London, works for the Lincoln Center in New York and universities and civic sites worldwide. His home and studios at Perry Green, Hertfordshire, became the Henry Moore Foundation after his death.

Style

Moore's forms derive from the human figure, bones, shells and the Yorkshire landscape; he spoke of the hole as having "a mysterious fascination", giving sculpture a three-dimensional power independent of mass. Working from drawings and small maquettes scaled up with assistants, he became exceptionally wealthy for an artist, yet lived modestly and used his fortune to endow the Foundation for the support of sculpture.
