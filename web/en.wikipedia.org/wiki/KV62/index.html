KV62 is the standard Egyptological designation for the tomb of the pharaoh Tutankhamun in the Valley of the Kings, near Luxor, Egypt. Found almost intact in 1922 by Howard Carter, funded by George Herbert, 5th Earl of Carnarvon, it is the most famous tomb discovery in archaeology and the only royal Egyptian tomb found with its grave goods substantially undisturbed.

Layout

For a royal tomb KV62 is small and architecturally simple: a stairway and sloping corridor lead to an antechamber, with an annexe, a decorated burial chamber — the only room with painted walls, showing scenes from the Amduat and the Opening of the Mouth ceremony — and a "Treasury" chamber beyond. The modest scale suggests the tomb was originally intended for a non-royal person, possibly the official Ay, and was pressed into service when Tutankhamun died unexpectedly as a teenager, around 1323 BCE.

Discovery and contents

On 4 November 1922 Carter's workmen uncovered a step cut into the bedrock below the tomb of Ramesses VI. On 26 November Carter peered through a breach in the sealed doorway and, asked if he could see anything, replied "Yes, wonderful things". Systematic clearance took a decade. The tomb contained more than 5,000 objects, including the nested shrines and solid-gold coffin of the king, his famous gold funerary mask, the throne, chariots, beds, weapons, clothing, food and the mummies of two stillborn daughters. An iron dagger blade was later shown to be made of meteoritic iron.

Later history

The treasures are now housed principally in the Grand Egyptian Museum in Giza. The tomb itself remains open to visitors, with a replica nearby. Speculation about hidden chambers, popularised by a 2015 theory that Nefertiti might lie behind the burial chamber walls, was largely settled by ground-penetrating radar surveys in 2018, which found no evidence of voids. The "curse of the pharaohs" legend that followed Lord Carnarvon's death in 1923 remains a staple of popular culture.
