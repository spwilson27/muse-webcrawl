Bogomilism

Bogomilism (Bulgarian: богомилство; Macedonian: богомилство; Serbian: богумилство) was a Christian neo-Gnostic, dualist religious movement founded in the First Bulgarian Empire by the priest Bogomil during the reign of Tsar Peter I in the 10th century. It most probably arose in what is today the region of Macedonia.

The Bogomils were dualists: they believed that God had two sons, the elder Satanail and the younger Michael. The visible, material world was created by Satanail, while the invisible, spiritual world was created by God. They rejected the Orthodox Church hierarchy, church buildings, the cross, icons, sacraments and the Old Testament (except the Psalms and the Prophets in some accounts), relying instead on prayer—chiefly the Lord's Prayer—and on a community of the "perfect" who practised asceticism.

History

Bogomilism spread through the Balkans and into the Byzantine Empire, where emperors such as Alexios I Komnenos persecuted it; the Bogomil leader Basil the Physician was burned at the stake in Constantinople around 1111. From the Balkans, related dualist teachings influenced the Patarenes of Italy and, most famously, the Cathars of southern France, although the precise links between the movements remain debated by historians.

In Bosnia, the medieval Bosnian Church was long accused by Catholic and Orthodox neighbours of Bogomil sympathies, a thesis popularised in the 19th century but substantially revised by later scholarship. Bogomil communities survived in Bulgaria until the Ottoman conquest of the Balkans in the 14th–15th centuries, when the movement gradually disappeared, with some adherents converting to Islam or returning to Orthodoxy.
