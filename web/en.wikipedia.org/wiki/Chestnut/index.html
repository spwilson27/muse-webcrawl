The chestnuts are the deciduous trees and shrubs of the genus Castanea, in the beech family Fagaceae, and the nuts they produce. The genus contains eight or nine species native to temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. The four main species are the sweet (or European) chestnut C. sativa, the American chestnut C. dentata, the Chinese chestnut C. mollissima, and the Japanese chestnut C. crenata. Chestnuts are not closely related to the horse chestnut (genus Aesculus), whose seeds are toxic, nor to the water chestnut.

Description

Chestnut trees can grow very large and long-lived; some European specimens are estimated to be over a thousand years old. The leaves are simple, ovate-lanceolate, with coarsely toothed margins. The flowers are catkins appearing in late spring or summer. The nuts develop inside a sharply spiny cupule (burr) that splits open when ripe, typically releasing one to three shiny brown nuts with a pointed tip (the remnant of the flower).

Uses

Chestnuts are unusual among nuts for their high starch and water content and low fat, making them nutritionally closer to a cereal. They are eaten roasted — a classic winter street food in Europe and East Asia — boiled, or ground into flour for breads, cakes, and the Corsican pulenda. Candied chestnuts (marrons glacés) and chestnut purée are specialities. Historically, chestnut flour was a staple "bread tree" food in mountainous Mediterranean regions. The timber is durable and rich in tannins, valued for posts, beams, and furniture, and the bark and wood were important sources of vegetable tannin.

Blight and restoration

The American chestnut was once a dominant tree of eastern North American forests, but the introduced fungal chestnut blight (Cryphonectria parasitica), first noticed in 1904, killed an estimated three to four billion trees within decades, effectively eliminating it as a canopy species, though root systems still send up sprouts. Breeding programmes cross American with blight-resistant Chinese chestnuts, and research into transgenic blight-resistant trees aims to restore the species. European and Asian chestnuts also face ink disease and gall wasps.
