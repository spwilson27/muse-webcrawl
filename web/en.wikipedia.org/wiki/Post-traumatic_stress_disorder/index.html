Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a mental and behavioural disorder that develops after exposure to a traumatic event such as sexual assault, warfare, traffic collisions, child abuse, domestic violence, or other threats to a person's life or well-being. Symptoms may include disturbing thoughts, feelings, or dreams related to the events; mental or physical distress in response to trauma-related cues; attempts to avoid such cues; changes in the way a person thinks and feels; and increased arousal and reactivity. Symptoms must last more than a month and cause significant impairment to be diagnosed.

History

Descriptions of post-traumatic symptoms date back to antiquity, but modern study began with "railway spine" and "soldier's heart" in the 19th century, "shell shock" in the First World War, and "combat fatigue" in the Second. The diagnosis was introduced into the DSM-III in 1980, largely as a result of research and advocacy concerning Vietnam veterans and, later, survivors of sexual violence. ICD-11 also recognises complex PTSD (CPTSD), involving additional disturbances in self-organisation following prolonged or repeated trauma.

Causes and risk factors

Most people exposed to trauma do not develop PTSD. Risk is higher with interpersonal violence (especially sexual violence and torture), prior trauma, childhood adversity, lack of social support, and additional post-event stressors. Biological findings include heightened amygdala reactivity, altered medial prefrontal regulation, and dysregulation of stress-hormone (HPA axis) systems.

Treatment

First-line treatments are trauma-focused psychotherapies: cognitive processing therapy (CPT), prolonged exposure (PE), trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, and eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR). Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors — sertraline and paroxetine are FDA-approved — are the main pharmacological options. Prevention research has examined psychological debriefing (now discouraged) and early targeted interventions for high-risk individuals.

Epidemiology

The World Health Organization's World Mental Health Surveys estimate a cross-national lifetime prevalence of about 3.9%, with higher rates in high-income countries and among conflict-affected populations. Rates among veterans of recent wars and among emergency responders are substantially elevated.
