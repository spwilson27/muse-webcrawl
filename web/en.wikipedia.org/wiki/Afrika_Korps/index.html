The Afrika Korps (German: Deutsches Afrikakorps, DAK) was the German expeditionary force sent to North Africa during the Second World War. Formed in February 1941 to rescue Germany's Italian ally, whose offensive into Egypt had collapsed in Operation Compass, it was commanded for most of its existence by Erwin Rommel, whose aggressive generalship in the desert earned him the nickname "Desert Fox" and made the corps famous. Originally built around the 5th Light Division and the 15th Panzer Division, it later formed the core of Panzer Army Africa alongside Italian formations.

Campaign

Rommel landed at Tripoli in February 1941 and immediately counter-attacked, driving the British back across Cyrenaica and besieging Tobruk. Through 1941–42 the front swung back and forth in the "desert war": Crusader relieved Tobruk, Rommel struck again, and in June 1942 he captured Tobruk and pursued the Eighth Army deep into Egypt, raising fears for the Suez Canal. Halted at the First Battle of El Alamein (July 1942) and checked at Alam el Halfa, the Afrika Korps — starved of fuel and supplies by Allied air and naval interdiction from Malta — was decisively beaten at the Second Battle of El Alamein (October–November 1942) by Montgomery's Eighth Army. After the Allied Torch landings in Morocco and Algeria, the Axis forces were compressed into Tunisia; Rommel won a sharp victory over inexperienced US troops at Kasserine Pass in February 1943, but the Axis bridgehead collapsed and the Afrika Korps surrendered in May 1943, some 130,000 German and 120,000 Italian troops becoming prisoners.

Legacy

The Afrika Korps acquired an outsized reputation for professional skill and, in popular memory, for a comparatively "clean" war detached from the atrocities of the Eastern Front — a reputation historians have qualified, noting among other things the presence of SS and police units in its theatre and the persecution of Tunisian Jews under the occupation. Its tropical uniforms, palm-and-swastika insignia and the song "Lili Marleen", beloved of both sides, remain enduring images of the desert war.
