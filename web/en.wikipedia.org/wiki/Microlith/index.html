Microlith

Microlith
MaterialFlint, chert, obsidian and other fine-grained stone
Typical sizeAbout 1–5 cm in length
PeriodsUpper Palaeolithic, Epipalaeolithic, Mesolithic (in places into the Neolithic and Bronze Age)

A microlith is a small stone tool, usually made of flint or chert and typically around 1 to 5 centimetres long, shaped into pointed or geometric forms. Microliths were produced across large parts of the Old World from the later Upper Palaeolithic onward and are a hallmark of the Mesolithic. They were generally hafted — glued or bound into wooden or bone handles — to form composite tools such as arrows, spears, harpoons and sickles.

Production

Microliths were commonly made by snapping narrow blades struck from prepared cores using the microburin technique: a notch was worked into a blade, which was then snapped at the notch to produce a sharp segment that could be retouched into the desired shape. Common forms include points, crescents (lunates), triangles, trapezes and backed bladelets. The surviving blunt microburin waste pieces are a diagnostic byproduct of the technique.

Distribution and chronology

Two broad families are recognised: laminar microliths, associated with the end of the last Ice Age, and geometric microliths, characteristic of the Mesolithic and Epipalaeolithic. Microlithic industries occur from Europe and North Africa through the Levant (Natufian and Kebaran cultures), across South Asia, in sub-Saharan Africa — where small backed tools have very early origins, as at Pinnacle Point in South Africa — and in Australia, where backed artefacts became widespread in the Holocene. Microliths persisted into the Neolithic and even the Bronze Age in some regions, where they continued to tip arrows and serve as sickle inserts.

Function

Use-wear and residue studies show microliths served mainly as the cutting and piercing elements of composite hunting weapons; sets of microliths could be mounted in a row as barbs, and damaged segments were easily replaced — a maintainable technology well suited to mobile hunter-gatherers.
