Pleural cavity

The pleural cavity, also known as the pleural space, is the potential space between the two layers of the pleura — the visceral pleura covering the lungs and the parietal pleura lining the thoracic cavity. In health it is not a real open space but a sealed compartment containing only a few millilitres of pleural fluid spread as a thin film, typically 0.1–0.2 mL per kilogram of body mass. The pressure inside the cavity is subatmospheric, around −5 cm H2O at rest, becoming more negative during inspiration.

Anatomy

There are two pleural cavities, one for each lung, and they are completely separate from one another, divided by the mediastinum. Each cavity extends from the apex of the lung in the root of the neck down to the diaphragm. The parietal pleura forms recesses — the costodiaphragmatic and costomediastinal recesses — where its surfaces meet at acute angles; the lung does not fill these recesses except in deep inspiration, and the costodiaphragmatic recess is the most dependent part of the cavity, where pleural fluid first accumulates.

Pleural fluid

Pleural fluid is continuously formed by filtration from the systemic capillaries of the parietal pleura and drained mainly through lymphatic stomata in the parietal pleura, particularly over the diaphragm and mediastinum. The fluid acts as a lubricant, reducing friction between the lung and chest wall, and its surface tension couples the two pleural surfaces so the lung follows the movements of the thorax. Balance between formation and absorption is essential: disruption on either side produces a pleural effusion.

Clinical significance

  • Pleural effusion: excess fluid, classified as transudate or exudate by Light's criteria; causes include heart failure, pneumonia, malignancy, and pulmonary embolism.
  • Pneumothorax: air entering the cavity, from trauma, ruptured blebs, or iatrogenic causes, allowing the lung to collapse under its own elastic recoil.
  • Haemothorax and empyema: blood or pus in the pleural space, usually requiring tube drainage.
  • Procedures: thoracentesis (diagnostic or therapeutic aspiration), chest tube thoracostomy, and pleurodesis (obliteration of the space) are performed through or upon the pleural cavity.
