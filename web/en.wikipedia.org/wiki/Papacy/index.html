The papacy is the office and jurisdiction of the pope, the bishop of Rome and head of the worldwide Catholic Church. The pope is regarded by Catholics as the successor of Saint Peter, to whom, according to Catholic teaching, Christ gave primacy among the apostles. The papacy is one of the most enduring institutions in the world and has had a prominent part in world history.

History

The bishops of Rome gradually acquired a primacy of honour, and then of jurisdiction, in the early Church, grounded in Rome's association with the apostles Peter and Paul and in texts such as Matthew 16:18 ("you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church"). In the Middle Ages the papacy became a great temporal power: popes ruled the Papal States in central Italy, crowned emperors, launched the Crusades and contested authority with kings in the Investiture Controversy. Periods of crisis included the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377) and the Western Schism, healed at the Council of Constance. The Reformation of the 16th century broke much of northern Europe away from Rome.

Modern papacy

In 1870 the First Vatican Council defined the dogma of papal infallibility, while Italian unification ended the popes' temporal rule; the Lateran Treaty of 1929 settled the "Roman Question" by creating Vatican City State. The Second Vatican Council (1962–65) reformed the Church's liturgy and its relations with other Christians and religions. The pope is elected by the College of Cardinals in a conclave held in the Sistine Chapel.

Claims and criticism

The extent of papal authority — supremacy and infallibility — remains a principal point of division between the Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Churches (which accept only a primacy of honour) and Protestant churches (which reject papal authority). Within Catholicism the papacy functions as a visible centre of unity for some 1.4 billion faithful.
