Sound change

Sound change is any systematic change in the pronunciation of a language over time, a central object of study in historical linguistics. Sound changes are typically regular, affecting all words in a given phonetic environment — the Neogrammarian hypothesis of the regularity of sound change.

Types

Changes include assimilation (sounds becoming more alike, e.g. Latin in- + possibilis → impossibilis), dissimilation, lenition (weakening, as in intervocalic voicing), fortition, metathesis (reordering, as in Old English brid → bird), elision, epenthesis, vowel shifts and chain shifts, in which one sound moves into the space vacated by another.

Famous examples

Grimm's law described the High German and Germanic consonant shifts from Proto-Indo-European (voiceless stops became fricatives: *p → f, as in Latin piscis vs English fish); Verner's law explained apparent exceptions by accent. The Great Vowel Shift (c. 1400–1700) raised and diphthongised English long vowels. The High German consonant shift distinguishes German Pfund from English pound.

Mechanisms

Explanations include articulatory ease, misperception by listeners (Ohala), social identity and contact. Lexical diffusion argues some changes spread word by word. Sound change underlies the comparative method used to reconstruct proto-languages, essential for languages such as Welsh, whose history shows systematic mutations.
