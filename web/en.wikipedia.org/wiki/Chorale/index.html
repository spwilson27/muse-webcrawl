Chorale

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A chorale is the name of several related musical forms originating in the music genre of the Lutheran hymn. A chorale is a melody — often associated with a metrical sacred text — harmonised in four parts, and traditionally sung by a congregation in the Lutheran church. In English the term can also refer to a choral ensemble or choir.

History

Chorales emerged from the Reformation in the 16th century. Martin Luther and his followers wrote melodies and borrowed existing tunes — including plainsong and popular songs — so that congregations could sing in the vernacular. Composers such as Johann Walter and later Michael Praetorius provided harmonised settings.

Bach and the four-part chorale

The genre reached a high point in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach, whose more than 400 surviving four-part chorale harmonisations close most of his church cantatas and form the emotional core of his Passions. Bach's chorale style has become the standard model for teaching harmony and voice leading.

Chorale prelude

A related form is the chorale prelude, a short organ piece based on a chorale melody, played before the congregation sang the hymn. Composers of the North German Baroque, including Buxtehude, and Bach himself, developed the form extensively.
