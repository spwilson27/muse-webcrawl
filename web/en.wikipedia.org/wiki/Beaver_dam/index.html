Beaver dam

A beaver dam or beaver impoundment is a dam built by beavers to create a pond in which they can build their lodge and protect themselves from predators. The pond also provides underwater access to food stores and building material during winter. Beaver dams are built from trees felled by the beavers, branches, vegetation, rocks and mud.


A beaver dam and the pond it creates

Construction

Beavers begin by diverting or narrowing a stream, driving sticks and branches into the streambed and weaving them together, then piling on mud, stones and further vegetation. The animals work mostly at night and repair damage promptly — the sound of running water is thought to stimulate building behaviour. Dams range from small structures a metre across to the largest known, in Wood Buffalo National Park in Alberta, Canada, which stretches about 850 metres and is visible from space.

Ecological effects

Beaver ponds create wetlands that support a wide range of wildlife, store water, slow streamflow, trap sediment and can mitigate flooding and drought downstream. Beaver activity is a classic example of ecosystem engineering, and the beaver is regarded as a keystone species in many North American and Eurasian ecosystems. Reintroduction programmes, such as those in Britain, cite these effects as benefits, though dams can also flood farmland, roads and forestry, prompting management measures such as pond levellers ("beaver deceivers").
