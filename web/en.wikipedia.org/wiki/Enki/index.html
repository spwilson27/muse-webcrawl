Enki (Sumerian: "Lord of the Earth," or "Lord below"), known in Akkadian as Ea, is the Mesopotamian god of wisdom, crafts, magic, incantations and the sweet subterranean waters of the Abzu. He is the friendliest of the great gods toward humankind — creator and protector of people, patron of sages and exorcists, and the god whose cleverness repeatedly saves the world from the sterner decrees of Enlil. His city is Eridu, by tradition the first city of Sumer, and his temple the E-Abzu, "House of the Deep."

Myths

Enki is the keeper of the me, the divine decrees that govern civilisation — kingship, crafts, music, sexual arts, lamentation and dozens more. In Inanna and Enki, the goddess gets him drunk at a banquet and sails away with the me to her own city of Uruk; the sobered Enki's attempts to retrieve them fail, and the gifts of civilisation stay with Inanna. In Enki and the World Order he organises the cosmos, filling the Tigris and Euphrates with life, assigning domains to the gods and decreeing the fates of lands. In Enki and Ninhursag, set in pure Dilmun, he fathers generations of plant deities and is healed of eight ailments by eight new gods. Above all, in the flood tradition — the Sumerian flood story, the Atrahasis epic and the Gilgamesh epic — it is Enki who, bound by oath not to warn humankind directly, speaks to the reed wall of the flood hero's house and so betrays Enlil's secret, enabling Ziusudra / Atrahasis / Utnapishtim to build the boat and preserve life. He also rescues Inanna's corpse from the underworld with creatures fashioned from the dirt beneath his fingernails, and in Enuma Elish (as Ea) he slays Apsu and begets Marduk.

Cult and character

As Ea, god of incantation and the āšipu (exorcist-priest), Enki was among the most invoked gods in everyday religion: rituals, medical texts and magical series such as Maqlû and Šurpu call on him and his son Asalluhi/Marduk to undo witchcraft and disease. His symbols — the flowing vase, streams with leaping fish, the goat-fish (the origin of Capricorn) and the ram-headed staff — are among the most recognisable in Mesopotamian art, appearing on cylinder seals from the Akkadian period onward. Scholars have noted his unusual profile as a trickster-benefactor: wise, lustful, drunken on occasion, but consistently on humanity's side.
