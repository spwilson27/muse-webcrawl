Electroacoustic music is a genre of Western art music in which composers use technology both to access sounds and to transform them in composition. It encompasses music made from recorded environmental and instrumental sounds (the French musique concrète), music generated purely by electronic oscillators (the German elektronische Musik), and later computer music, live electronics and sound art. The term became current in the 1950s as the two founding schools converged in studios at Paris (RTF/GRM) and Cologne (WDR).

History

Precursors include the Futurists' noise instruments, early electronic instruments such as the theremin and ondes Martenot, and Edgard Varèse's vision of "organised sound". In 1948 Pierre Schaeffer broadcast his first études from manipulated phonograph records, coining musique concrète. In Cologne from 1951, Herbert Eimert and Karlheinz Stockhausen pursued composition built from sine tones; Stockhausen's Gesang der Jünglinge (1955–56) fused electronic and recorded human sounds. Studios followed worldwide — at Columbia-Princeton, NHK Tokyo, RAI Milan, and the BBC Radiophonic Workshop — and composers such as Luciano Berio, Iannis Xenakis, Pauline Oliveros and Delia Derbyshire extended the field.

Practice

Electroacoustic works may be "fixed media" played back over loudspeakers (often diffused live through orchestras of speakers, the acousmatic tradition based on Schaeffer's idea of the sound object and reduced listening), or they may combine live performers with electronics in real time. Techniques include tape splicing, speed and pitch manipulation, filtering, granulation, spectral processing and spatialisation. The field supports its own institutions, festivals and academic programmes and overlaps with film sound, installation art and experimental electronic music.
