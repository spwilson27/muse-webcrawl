Angiogenesis inhibitor

An angiogenesis inhibitor is a substance that inhibits angiogenesis, the growth of new blood vessels. Because tumors need to establish a blood supply to grow beyond a small size and to metastasize, blocking angiogenesis is a strategy in cancer treatment ("anti-angiogenic therapy"), first championed by the surgeon-scientist Judah Folkman in 1971. Angiogenesis inhibitors are also used in ophthalmology to treat neovascular ("wet") age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy.

Mechanisms

Drugs in this class interfere with angiogenic signaling at different points. Some are monoclonal antibodies that bind vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), such as bevacizumab, or its receptor, such as ramucirumab. Others are small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors that block VEGF receptors and related pathways inside endothelial cells, including sunitinib, sorafenib and pazopanib. Endogenous inhibitors of angiogenesis include angiostatin, endostatin and thrombospondin.

Medical uses

Bevacizumab (Avastin), approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2004 for metastatic colorectal cancer, was the first clinically available angiogenesis inhibitor for cancer. Anti-VEGF agents such as ranibizumab and aflibercept are injected into the eye to treat wet macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema, transforming the prognosis of those diseases. Thalidomide and its derivatives, originally infamous sedatives, were found to have anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory activity and are used in multiple myeloma.

Side effects and limitations

Because VEGF signaling also maintains normal blood vessels, adverse effects include hypertension, bleeding, impaired wound healing, proteinuria and, rarely, gastrointestinal perforation. Tumors can develop resistance by switching to alternative growth factors, so anti-angiogenic drugs are usually combined with chemotherapy or immunotherapy rather than used alone.
