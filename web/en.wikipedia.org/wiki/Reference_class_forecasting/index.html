Reference class forecasting

Reference class forecasting or comparison class forecasting is a method of predicting the future by looking at similar past situations and their outcomes. It is a form of taking an "outside view" on a planned project, in contrast to the "inside view" that builds a forecast from the project's own details. The method was developed from the work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky on the planning fallacy and was formalised for major projects by Bent Flyvbjerg and colleagues.

The method

Reference class forecasting proceeds in three steps: (1) identify a reference class of similar past projects broad enough to be statistically meaningful but narrow enough to be comparable; (2) establish the empirical distribution of outcomes for that class (for example, percentage cost overrun or demand shortfall); and (3) position the planned project within that distribution, adjusting the project-specific forecast so that its implied percentile matches the risk the decision-maker is willing to accept — for example, applying an "optimism bias uplift" sufficient to give an 80% chance of staying within budget.

Origins and evidence

Kahneman and Tversky observed that forecasters anchor on a project's internal narrative and neglect base rates — the planning fallacy. Flyvbjerg's datasets of hundreds of transport projects found systematic cost underestimation (averaging around 28% for rail, in constant prices) and demand overestimation, across countries and decades, which he attributes jointly to optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation. The American Planning Association endorsed reference class forecasting in 2005, and the UK Treasury's Green Book has required optimism-bias adjustments informed by reference classes since 2003.

Applications and criticism

The method has been applied to rail and road schemes, dams, tunnels, IT systems, defence procurement, films and pharmaceutical development, and was used in planning elements of the Edinburgh Tram and London Crossrail business cases. Critics note the difficulty of defining an appropriate reference class for novel projects, the data burden, and the risk that uplift percentages become ritualised; proponents respond that even a crude outside view outperforms unaided inside-view estimates.
