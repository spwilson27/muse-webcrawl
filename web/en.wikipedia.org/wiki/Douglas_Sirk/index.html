Douglas Sirk (born Hans Detlef Sierck; 26 April 1897 – 14 January 1987) was a German film director best known for his Hollywood melodramas of the 1950s. Born in Hamburg, he began his career in Germany as a theatre director and made films for UFA before emigrating to the United States in 1937 to escape Nazi Germany. In Hollywood he anglicised his name and worked his way up from low-budget productions to become, at Universal-International, the leading director of glossy Technicolor melodramas.

Sirk's 1950s films — including Magnificent Obsession (1954), All That Heaven Allows (1955), Written on the Wind (1956), The Tarnished Angels (1957) and Imitation of Life (1959) — were commercially successful but dismissed by contemporary critics as glossy "women's pictures". His final Hollywood film, Imitation of Life, was among the biggest hits of his career.

Reputation and influence

From the 1970s onwards, Sirk was re-evaluated by critics and scholars, who recognised in his films a sophisticated, ironic critique of American middle-class values, conformity and repression, conveyed through elaborate mise-en-scène, saturated colour and mirrors. Directors such as Rainer Werner Fassbinder, Pedro Almodóvar and Todd Haynes (whose Far from Heaven is an explicit homage) have cited him as a major influence.
