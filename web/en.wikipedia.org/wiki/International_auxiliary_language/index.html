International auxiliary language

An international auxiliary language (sometimes acronymized as IAL or auxlang) is a language meant for communication between people from all different nations, who do not share a common first language. An auxiliary language is primarily a foreign language, while a natural language serving the same role—such as English, Spanish, French, or Mandarin—may also be described by the term.

Esperanto, the most widely spoken constructed auxiliary language, has its own flag and literature.

History

The idea gained prominence in the late 19th century. Volapük, published by Johann Martin Schleyer in 1879, attracted hundreds of thousands of learners before declining. In 1887, L. L. Zamenhof published Esperanto, which became the most successful IAL, with an enduring speech community, original literature, and annual world congresses. Subsequent projects attempted reform or alternatives: Ido (1907, a reformed Esperanto), Occidental/Interlingue (1922), Novial (1928), and Interlingua (1951), developed by the International Auxiliary Language Association using vocabulary common to major European languages.

Types

  • Schematic (a priori) languages, built from systematic inventories of roots and affixes, such as Solresol and Ro.
  • Naturalistic (a posteriori) languages, drawing vocabulary and grammar from existing languages, such as Interlingua and Latino sine flexione.
  • Zonal auxiliary languages, intended for a language family or region, such as Interslavic for Slavic speakers or Folkspraak for Germanic speakers.

Natural languages as auxiliaries

Historically, lingua francas such as Latin, Koine Greek, Arabic, Persian, French, and now English have filled the role without being designed for it. Constructed-language advocates argue that a neutral, easy, planned language avoids giving native speakers an unfair advantage; critics respond that natural languages dominate through network effects, and that no constructed language has achieved comparable adoption.
