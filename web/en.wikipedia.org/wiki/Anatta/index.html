Anattā (Pali) or Anātman (Sanskrit: अनात्मन्) is a central doctrine of Buddhism, usually translated "not-self" or "no-self". It holds that no unchanging, permanent self or soul (ātman) can be found in any phenomenon. Together with dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness) and anicca (impermanence), it is one of the three marks of existence.

Meaning

The doctrine does not simply deny that a person exists in a conventional sense; rather, it analyses the person into the five aggregates (skandhas) — form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness — and states that none of them, individually or collectively, is a permanent, independent self. The Buddha's discourse on the subject, the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, is traditionally regarded as the second sermon he preached and the one that led his first five disciples to awakening.

Contrast with Hindu thought

Anatta sets Buddhism apart from the Upanishadic Hindu teaching of ātman, an eternal inner self identical ultimately with Brahman. Buddhist texts instead explain continuity between lives through dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) and karma without positing an enduring soul that transmigrates.

Interpretations

Schools differ in emphasis. Theravada treats anatta as a universal characteristic to be realised through insight meditation. Mahayana traditions connect it with emptiness (śūnyatā), and some, such as tathāgatagarbha literature, speak in positive terms of Buddha-nature — a point of long-standing debate. The early Buddhist "Great Self" passages in some Mahayana sutras are interpreted by many scholars as skillful means rather than a reassertion of ātman.
