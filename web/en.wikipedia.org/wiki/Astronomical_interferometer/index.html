Astronomical interferometer

Arrays such as ALMA and the VLA combine many antennas into one virtual telescope.
Astronomical interferometer
TypeArray of separate telescopes combined coherently
PrincipleAperture synthesis; interference of signals from separated apertures
ResolutionSet by the longest baseline, not dish size
ExamplesVLA, ALMA, VLBA, CHARA Array, EHT, VLTI

An astronomical interferometer is an array of separate telescopes, antennas or mirror segments whose signals are combined to produce images with the angular resolution of a single instrument as large as the separation (baseline) between the elements. The technique, called aperture synthesis, was pioneered in radio astronomy and earned Martin Ryle a share of the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Principle

Waves arriving at separated apertures from a distant source travel slightly different path lengths; correlating the signals yields interference fringes whose visibility encodes the source's structure. Earth's rotation changes the projected baselines over hours, filling in the Fourier (uv) plane so an image can be reconstructed. Longer baselines give finer resolution: very-long-baseline interferometry (VLBI) links telescopes on different continents.

Major instruments

Radio and millimetre arrays include the Very Large Array (New Mexico), ALMA (Chile), the VLBA, MeerKAT and the planned Square Kilometre Array. In the optical and infrared, the CHARA Array and ESO's VLTI measure stellar diameters and image stellar surfaces. The Event Horizon Telescope, a planet-scale VLBI network, produced the first images of black hole shadows — M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022.
