Stockholm School

The Stockholm School (Swedish: Stockholmsskolan) is a school of economic thought associated with a group of Swedish economists working in Stockholm in the 1930s and 1940s. Building on the work of Knut Wicksell, its leading figures included Erik Lindahl, Gunnar Myrdal, Bertil Ohlin, and Erik Lundberg. Working largely independently of John Maynard Keynes, members of the school arrived at conclusions similar to those of Keynesian macroeconomics, and some of their analytical tools — above all the distinction between ex ante and ex post magnitudes — were distinctive contributions.

Key ideas

Because investment decisions are made on the basis of expectations, Lindahl and Myrdal stressed the difference between planned (ex ante) values of saving and investment and their realised (ex post) values. Ex post, saving and investment are necessarily equal by accounting identity; ex ante, they may diverge, and the process of adjustment — changes in income, prices, and expectations during a "period" — drives the dynamics of the economy. This period analysis, inherited from Wicksell and Austrian capital theory, made expectations and disequilibrium dynamics central topics and anticipated later work on sequence economies.

Policy and legacy

The Stockholm School was closely linked with Swedish economic policy. Ohlin and Myrdal advised the Social Democratic governments of the 1930s, advocating counter-cyclical fiscal policy before Keynes's General Theory (1936) had appeared; Ohlin later publicly claimed priority for the school in developing the theory of expansionary fiscal policy, a claim historians treat as partly justified. Myrdal and Ohlin both received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (1974 and 1977 respectively). The school's influence waned after the Second World War as Keynesian and then neoclassical synthesis ideas, developed internationally, displaced its distinctively dynamic methods, though Lindahl's intertemporal equilibrium concept and Lundberg's sequence models retained lasting influence.
