At-will employment

Most US offer letters and handbooks state that employment is at will.
At-will employment
JurisdictionUnited States (default rule in all states except Montana's modified regime)
MeaningEither party may end employment at any time, for any lawful reason, without notice
ExceptionsStatutory (anti-discrimination), public policy, implied contract, covenant of good faith (some states)

At-will employment is the default doctrine of United States labour law under which an employer may dismiss an employee for any reason — and without warning — as long as the reason is not illegal, and an employee is equally free to quit at any time. It applies where no contract, collective bargaining agreement or statute provides otherwise.

Origins

The doctrine is commonly traced to Horace Gray Wood's 1877 treatise on master and servant law, which stated the rule in sweeping terms; it displaced the older English presumption of year-long hiring during the industrial era and was entrenched by the courts by the early 20th century.

Exceptions

Courts and legislatures have carved out major limits: terminations may not violate anti-discrimination statutes (such as Title VII), retaliate for protected activity (whistleblowing, union organising, filing wage claims), or breach an implied contract created by handbook promises. A majority of states recognise a public-policy exception (e.g., firing an employee for refusing to commit an illegal act), and a minority impose a covenant of good faith. Montana's Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act of 1987 uniquely requires "good cause" after a probationary period. Federal and state WARN Acts additionally require advance notice of mass layoffs.

Criticism and comparison

Critics contrast at-will employment with the "just cause" dismissal protection standard in most other developed countries; defenders cite flexibility and the employee's symmetric freedom to quit.
