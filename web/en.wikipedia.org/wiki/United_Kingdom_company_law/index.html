United Kingdom company law

United Kingdom company law regulates corporations formed under the Companies Act 2006 and related legislation. It is the body of rules governing the formation, governance, finance and dissolution of companies in the United Kingdom, together with the judge-made principles of the common law and equity that continue to shape how companies are run. The central institution in the field is the registered company limited by shares, which since the nineteenth century has enjoyed separate legal personality and limited liability for its members.

History

Modern British company law begins with the Joint Stock Companies Act 1844, which first allowed companies to be incorporated by registration, and the Limited Liability Act 1855, which generalised limited liability. The Joint Stock Companies Act 1856 simplified the system into something recognisably modern: any group of people could register a company by filing a memorandum and articles of association.

The landmark case Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 confirmed that a duly registered company is a legal person entirely separate from its shareholders, even where one individual dominates it. Successive Companies Acts in 1929, 1948, 1967, 1980, 1985 and 1989 consolidated the law, before the Companies Act 2006 — one of the longest statutes ever passed by Parliament — restated and reformed it, following the Company Law Review launched in 1998.

Formation and legal personality

A company is formed by registering documents with Companies House, including a memorandum of association, articles of association and a statement of capital. On registration the registrar issues a certificate of incorporation, which is conclusive evidence that the company exists. Companies may be limited by shares, limited by guarantee, or unlimited; most trading companies are private companies limited by shares ("Ltd"), while public companies ("plc") may offer their shares to the public and must have a minimum allotted share capital of £50,000.

Because the company is a separate person, it owns its own assets, bears its own debts and can sue and be sued in its own name. The courts will only exceptionally "pierce the corporate veil", for example where the company is used to evade an existing obligation, as in Gilford Motor Co Ltd v Horne [1933] Ch 935, or where statute imposes liability in insolvency.

Corporate governance

Directors' duties

The Companies Act 2006 codified the duties owed by directors to their company in sections 171 to 177. Directors must act within their powers, promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, exercise independent judgment, exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence, avoid conflicts of interest, not accept benefits from third parties, and declare any interest in a proposed transaction. The duty to promote the company's success in section 172 requires directors to have regard to the long-term consequences of decisions, the interests of employees, relationships with suppliers and customers, the impact on the community and the environment, and the company's reputation.

Shareholders

Shareholders exercise power principally through resolutions at general meetings: ordinary resolutions pass by simple majority and special resolutions require 75 per cent. Minority shareholders are protected by the unfair prejudice remedy in section 994 of the Companies Act 2006 and by derivative claims under Part 11, which allow a member to pursue wrongs done to the company. Larger listed companies are also subject to the UK Corporate Governance Code, administered by the Financial Reporting Council on a "comply or explain" basis, and to the City Code on Takeovers and Mergers.

Capital and finance

Company law protects creditors by restricting the return of capital to shareholders. Dividends may only be paid out of accumulated realised profits less realised losses. A company may purchase its own shares only in accordance with Part 18 of the 2006 Act, and public companies are subject to additional capital maintenance rules derived originally from European Union law. Security interests created by companies — charges over their assets — generally must be registered at Companies House within 21 days or they are void against a liquidator and creditors.

Insolvency

Corporate insolvency is governed principally by the Insolvency Act 1986. A company that cannot pay its debts may enter administration, a collective procedure aimed at rescuing the company or achieving a better result for creditors than liquidation, or it may be wound up by the court or voluntarily. Liquidators can challenge antecedent transactions, including preferences and transactions at an undervalue, and directors may be liable for wrongful trading under section 214 if they continued trading when there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation. The Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 added a free-standing moratorium and a new restructuring plan procedure.
