1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯

The infinite series whose terms are the natural numbers, 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯, is also written with the summation notation as the sum of n from 1 to infinity. It is a divergent series: the sequence of its partial sums (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, …) grows without bound and does not tend to any finite limit.

The partial sums of the series are the triangular numbers, which grow quadratically.

Divergence

The n-th partial sum of the series is the triangular number n(n + 1)/2, which increases without bound as n increases. Because the sequence of partial sums does not converge, the series does not converge in the ordinary sense. Nevertheless, the series has many interesting properties and appears throughout mathematics and theoretical physics.

Ramanujan summation and the value −1/12

Using methods such as Ramanujan summation and analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function, the series can be assigned the finite value ζ(−1) = −1/12. This value does not mean that the series sums to −1/12 in the ordinary sense; rather, the zeta function at −1 is defined by analytic continuation, bypassing the divergent sum. The Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan wrote to G. H. Hardy that "if I tell you this you will at once point out to me the lunatic asylum", presenting the result −1/12 for the series in his famous first letter of 1913.

Applications

The value −1/12 arises naturally in theoretical physics, notably in the computation of the Casimir force and in bosonic string theory, where it fixes the critical dimension of the theory at 26 spacetime dimensions. Popular expositions of the −1/12 result have attracted widespread attention, along with criticism from mathematicians who emphasise that the assignment depends on the summation method used.
