Rosacea

Specialty: Dermatology

Symptoms: Facial redness, flushing, papules, pustules, visible blood vessels

Usual onset: Age 30–50

Rosacea is a long-term inflammatory skin condition that mainly affects the central face. It is characterised by persistent redness (erythema), episodes of flushing, small visible blood vessels (telangiectasia), and, in many people, acne-like papules and pustules. Unlike acne, rosacea does not produce blackheads, and it typically begins in adults between 30 and 50 years of age. Global prevalence is estimated at over 5 percent of adults, with fair-skinned people of Northern European ancestry most often affected, though it occurs in all skin types.

Signs and symptoms

Four broad presentations are recognised. Erythematotelangiectatic rosacea features flushing and persistent central facial redness. Papulopustular rosacea adds dome-shaped red papules and pustules. Phymatous rosacea involves skin thickening, most famously enlargement of the nose (rhinophyma), seen mostly in men. Ocular rosacea affects the eyes and eyelids, causing dryness, irritation, blepharitis, and in severe cases corneal inflammation that can threaten vision.

Causes and triggers

The exact cause is unknown. Current understanding implicates an overactive innate immune system — including abnormal processing of the antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin — neurovascular dysregulation, genetics, and microscopic Demodex mites that normally live in facial skin. Common triggers that provoke flushing include sunlight, heat, alcohol, spicy food, hot drinks, exercise, wind, and emotional stress.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be controlled. Management begins with gentle skincare, daily sunscreen, and avoidance of personal triggers. Topical treatments include metronidazole, azelaic acid, and ivermectin; brimonidine and oxymetazoline gels temporarily constrict surface vessels to reduce redness. Oral doxycycline (often at sub-antimicrobial doses) is used for inflammatory lesions, and low-dose isotretinoin for severe disease. Laser and intense pulsed light therapy can reduce visible blood vessels, and surgery can remodel rhinophyma.
