Andreas Vesalius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564) was a Flemish physician and anatomist whose De humani corporis fabrica ('On the Fabric of the Human Body', 1543) revolutionised Anatomy and founded its modern study. Professor at Padua from age 23, Vesalius insisted on dissecting human bodies himself — then a menial task delegated to barber-surgeons — and showed that Galen's anatomy, canonical for 1,300 years, had been based largely on animals and contradicted human structure in over 200 places.

The Fabrica, published in the same year as Copernicus's De revolutionibus, paired meticulous text with large woodcut illustrations of dissected figures in landscape poses — among the most famous scientific images ever made, likely from Titian's workshop circle.

Career

Vesalius became physician to Emperor Charles V and Philip II of Spain, a court role that constrained his research. He died in 1564 on the island of Zakynthos returning from a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, in circumstances (shipwreck, illness) that remain partly obscure.

Legacy

By insisting observation outranked authority, Vesalius modelled the Scientific Revolution's method in medicine; his successors at Padua (Falloppio, Fabricius — Harvey's teacher) carried anatomical research toward Physiology.
