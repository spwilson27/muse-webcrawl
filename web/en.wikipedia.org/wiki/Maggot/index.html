Maggot

A maggot is the larva of a fly of the order Diptera, most commonly of the housefly and blow fly families. Maggots are legless, soft-bodied and typically whitish, tapering toward the head; they feed on decaying organic matter, carrion or living tissue, and pass through several instars before pupating in a hardened case called a puparium.

Biology

Female blow flies lay eggs on carcasses or wounds, and the hatching larvae secrete digestive enzymes that liquefy tissue externally before ingestion. Because their development rate depends predictably on temperature, maggot age is used in forensic entomology to estimate the time since death.

Uses

Maggots have a long history in medicine: maggot debridement therapy, in which sterile larvae of the green bottle fly clean dead tissue from chronic wounds, is approved in several countries and has regained popularity with antibiotic resistance. Maggots are also used as fishing bait and as animal feed.

Pests

Some species, such as the screwworm, parasitise living animals and cause serious agricultural losses; others contaminate food. The eradication of screwworm from North America using the sterile insect technique is a celebrated success of applied entomology.
