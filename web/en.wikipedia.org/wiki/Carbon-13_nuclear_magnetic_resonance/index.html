Carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance

Image placeholder: A typical 13C NMR spectrum.

Carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (most commonly known as 13C NMR spectroscopy or sometimes simply carbon NMR) is the application of NMR spectroscopy to carbon-13, the only naturally occurring carbon isotope with a nuclear spin (spin = ½).

Principles

Because 13C has a natural abundance of only about 1.1% and a smaller gyromagnetic ratio than 1H, 13C NMR is far less sensitive than proton NMR, requiring more concentrated samples or longer acquisition. Spectra are referenced to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm, and chemical shifts span a wide range of roughly 0–220 ppm, so signals for individual carbon atoms are usually well separated.

Techniques

Routine spectra are recorded with broadband proton decoupling, collapsing each chemically distinct carbon environment to a single peak; the number of peaks therefore counts distinct carbon environments in the molecule. The complementary DEPT experiment distinguishes CH3, CH2, and CH groups by polarisation transfer. Unlike proton NMR, peak integrals in standard 13C spectra are not quantitative because of varying relaxation times and nuclear Overhauser enhancements.

Applications

13C NMR is a cornerstone of organic structure determination, used with proton NMR and mass spectrometry to establish molecular skeletons, functional groups, and symmetry in natural products, pharmaceuticals, and polymers.
