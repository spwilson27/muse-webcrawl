Chorus

In ancient Greek theatre, a chorus (Greek: χορός, khorós) was a homogeneous group of performers who commented in a collective voice on the dramatic action. The chorus performed by singing, dancing and narrating, and formed a bridge between actors and audience. The word survives in the "orchestra" (dancing-place) of the Greek theatre and in modern terms for singing groups.

Origins

Greek drama grew out of choral lyric — above all the dithyramb, a hymn to Dionysus performed by a circular chorus of fifty. According to tradition, drama began when Thespis separated a single performer from the chorus to speak as a character; the chorus nevertheless remained the dominant element in early plays of Aeschylus, in which it could even be the protagonist, as in The Suppliants.

Composition and performance

In classical tragedy the chorus numbered twelve, later fifteen, members, led by a koryphaios who could speak individually in dialogue. Comedy used a chorus of twenty-four. Chorus members were citizen amateurs trained for months at the expense of a wealthy sponsor (the choregos). They entered the orchestra with a marching song (the parodos), delivered choral odes (stasima) between episodes, and departed with an exodos. Famous choruses include the elders of Argos in the Oresteia, the Theban elders of Antigone, the women of Trachis, and the comic choruses of wasps, birds and frogs in Aristophanes.

Dramatic functions

The chorus sets the scene and gives background, voices communal norms and fears, reacts emotionally to events, asks the questions the audience would ask, and provides musical interludes that structure the play. Because it rarely intervenes effectively — as with the Argive elders who witness but cannot prevent murder — it can embody the limits of collective wisdom. Aristotle, in the Poetics, urged that the chorus be treated as one of the actors, integrated into the whole, rather than as an excuse for detachable songs.

Decline and legacy

From the fourth century BC the chorus's role shrank: in New Comedy it merely provided interludes marked "of the chorus" in the text. The device was revived in various forms by Seneca, Renaissance drama, opera, and modern playwrights such as T. S. Eliot, and survives as the narrator and ensemble commenting devices of contemporary theatre.
