Lanternfish (or myctophids, family Myctophidae) are small mesopelagic fish found in oceans worldwide. Named for their rows of light-producing organs called photophores, they are among the most abundant vertebrates on Earth, with an estimated global biomass of 1.8–16 billion tonnes, and form a crucial link in oceanic food webs.

Biology

Each species has a unique arrangement of photophores along its belly and flanks — a pattern used for species recognition, signalling, and camouflage by counter-illumination, matching downwelling light so the fish casts no silhouette. Many also have luminous scales, headlights, or sexually dimorphic caudal light organs. Lanternfish undertake one of the largest animal migrations on the planet: each night they rise from depths of 300–1,500 m to feed on zooplankton near the surface — the deep scattering layer detected by sonar — and descend again before dawn, transporting carbon into the deep ocean in the process.

Ecological importance

Lanternfish are the principal prey of many squid, tuna, billfish, sharks, seals, penguins, and other seabirds. Despite their abundance they are little fished commercially (some are taken for fishmeal and oil), and scientists study them as indicators of ocean health and as a possible, carefully debated, future food resource.
