Operation Torch / Operation Neptune

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This page treats Operation Neptune — the naval and amphibious assault phase of the Normandy landings of June 1944 — in the context of Operation Torch, the Anglo-American invasion of French North Africa in November 1942. Torch was the Allies' first great amphibious undertaking of the European war, and the hard lessons learned there made Neptune possible.

Neptune in brief

Operation Neptune was the code name for the cross-Channel assault phase of Operation Overlord. On 6 June 1944 (D-Day), under the overall naval command of Admiral Sir Bertram Ramsay, an armada of nearly 7,000 vessels — including 1,213 warships and over 4,000 landing craft — put some 160,000 troops ashore on five beaches in Normandy (Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword), supported by airborne landings on the flanks and by the artificial Mulberry harbours towed across the Channel in the following days. It remains the largest seaborne invasion in history.

What Torch taught Neptune

Torch, on 8 November 1942, landed American and British troops at Casablanca, Oran and Algiers. It exposed serious deficiencies: landing craft crews were inexperienced, beaches became congested, naval gunfire support was poorly coordinated, and the political handling of Vichy French forces nearly derailed the operation. In the nineteen months between Torch and Neptune, the Allies built the specialised landing ships and craft used in Normandy, created beach organisation units, rehearsed assault divisions, developed amphibious tanks and obstacle-clearing vehicles, and established the combined planning machinery that produced Neptune's detailed naval plan. Ramsay himself had planned the naval side of the earlier operations, and applied at Normandy the administrative lessons of North Africa on a vastly larger scale.
