Operation Torch was the Allied invasion of French North Africa during the North African campaign of the Second World War. On 8 November 1942, some 107,000 American and British troops, carried by more than 500 ships, landed at Casablanca, Oran and Algiers in the Vichy French territories of Morocco and Algeria. It was the first major American ground offensive against the Axis in the European-African theatre and the largest amphibious operation yet attempted.

The landings

The operation, commanded by Lieutenant General Dwight D. Eisenhower, depended on uncertainty about whether Vichy French forces would resist. Resistance varied: it was fierce at Casablanca and Oran, including a naval battle off Casablanca, but negligible at Algiers, where a local coup by the French resistance seized key points hours before the landings. Within days Admiral François Darlan, the Vichy commander-in-chief who happened to be in Algiers, ordered a ceasefire and brought French North Africa over to the Allied side — a deal that proved politically controversial.

Aftermath

Germany responded by occupying Vichy France (Case Anton) and rushing troops into Tunisia. The Torch landings placed Axis forces in North Africa between two Allied armies, and the ensuing Tunisia campaign ended in May 1943 with the surrender of some 250,000 Axis soldiers. The operation also served as a rehearsal for the later invasions of Sicily, Italy and Normandy.
