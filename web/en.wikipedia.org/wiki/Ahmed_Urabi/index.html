Ahmed Urabi (31 March 1841 – 21 September 1911), also known as Urabi Pasha, was an Egyptian army officer and nationalist who led the Urabi revolt against the Khedive Tewfik and European — particularly British and French — domination of Egypt. A fellah (peasant) by birth who rose through the ranks, he is often regarded as the first leader of modern Egyptian nationalism.

Early career

Urabi was born in a village near Zagazig in the Nile Delta and educated at Al-Azhar. He joined the army as a conscript and rose unusually quickly for a native Egyptian in an officer corps dominated by Turco-Circassians, reaching the rank of colonel. Discrimination against Egyptian-born officers, and Egypt's growing debt and foreign financial control, radicalised him and his fellow officers.

The Urabi revolt

In 1881 Urabi led a demonstration of officers at Abdeen Palace demanding the dismissal of the war minister and the creation of a national assembly. Confronted by the Khedive, he is famously said to have declared, "We are not slaves and shall not be enslaved." He became Minister of War in the nationalist government of 1882 and the effective ruler of Egypt under the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians".

Defeat and exile

After riots in Alexandria, Britain bombarded the city in July 1882 and landed an expeditionary force. Urabi's army was routed at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir in September 1882. He was tried for rebellion, sentenced to death, and the sentence commuted to exile in Ceylon (Sri Lanka), where he lived for nearly two decades. Pardoned in 1901, he returned to Egypt and died in Cairo in 1911.

Legacy

Although the British occupation of Egypt lasted until the 1950s, Urabi's stand at Abdeen Palace made him an enduring symbol of Egyptian resistance to foreign domination, later celebrated by the leaders of the 1952 revolution.
