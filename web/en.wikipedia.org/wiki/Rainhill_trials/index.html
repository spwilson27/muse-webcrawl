The Rainhill trials were an important competition run from 6 to 14 October 1829, to test George Stephenson's argument that locomotives would have the best motive power for the nearly-completed Liverpool and Manchester Railway (L&MR). Ten locomotives were entered, of which five were able to compete, running along a 1 mile (1.6 km) length of level track at Rainhill, in Lancashire (now Merseyside).

The locomotive designed by George Stephenson and his son Robert Stephenson, working with Henry Booth, Rocket, won the £500 prize and the contract. The trials attracted large crowds — some estimates put the spectators at 10,000 to 15,000 — and were extensively covered by the press, making them one of the first great public spectacles of the railway age.

Background

The directors of the L&MR were undecided whether to use stationary steam engines, hauling trains by cable, or locomotives along the 31-mile line. George Stephenson, the line's engineer, advocated of locomotives, proposed a competition to settle the question. The rules required each engine to haul a load of three times its own weight at a speed of at least 10 mph (16 km/h), to complete a course equivalent to a return journey between Liverpool and Manchester, to consume its own smoke, and to weigh no more than six tons.

The competitors

  • Rocket — George and Robert Stephenson with Henry Booth. The only locomotive to complete all its runs. Its multi-tubular boiler and blast pipe made it far more efficient than its rivals; it reached 30 mph (48 km/h).
  • Sans Pareil — Timothy Hackworth. Completed the course but was overweight at entry and eventually cracked a cylinder.
  • Novelty — John Ericsson and John Braithwaite. Light and fast, and a crowd favourite, but its bellows and boiler joints failed repeatedly.
  • Perseverance — Timothy Burstall. Damaged en route to the trials; too slow and withdrawn.
  • Cycloped — Thomas Shaw Brandreth. Powered by a horse on a treadmill; not a steam locomotive and not a serious contender.

Outcome

Rocket was declared the winner, and the Stephensons received the contract to build locomotives for the line, which opened on 15 September 1830. Sans Pareil and Novelty were also purchased by the railway. The trials are generally regarded as the moment when the steam locomotive proved itself as the practical basis of the coming railway system; Rocket's design — multi-tube boiler, blast pipe, and direct drive from inclined cylinders — set the pattern for steam locomotives for the next century.
