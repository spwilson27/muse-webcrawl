William Willett

Born: 10 August 1856, Farnham, Surrey, England
Died: 4 March 1915, Chislehurst, Kent, England
Occupation: Builder
Known for: Promoting daylight saving time

William Willett (10 August 1856 – 4 March 1915) was a British builder and the leading promoter of daylight saving time, whose pamphlet The Waste of Daylight (1907) campaigned for advancing the clocks in summer so that evening daylight could be enjoyed by all.

The idea

Willett said the idea came to him while riding in Petts Wood, Chislehurst, early one summer morning, when he noticed how many blinds were still down though the sun was well up. His scheme proposed advancing clocks by 80 minutes in four steps of 20 minutes on successive Sundays in April, and reversing the change in September.

Campaign

Willett lobbied MPs, businesses, and local councils energetically, and a Daylight Saving Bill was introduced in Parliament in 1908 with support from figures including Winston Churchill. The bill was repeatedly debated but not passed in his lifetime; opponents included farmers worried about morning routines.

Legacy

Willett died of influenza in 1915. The following year, during the First World War, Germany adopted summer time to save fuel, and Britain followed with the Summer Time Act of 1916 — implementing his proposal in the simpler form of a one-hour shift. He is commemorated by a memorial sundial in Petts Wood.
