Radical constructivism

Radical constructivism is a theory of knowledge (epistemology) developed by Ernst von Glasersfeld. It proposes that knowledge is not passively received but actively constructed by the cognizing subject, and that the function of cognition is adaptive and serves the subject's organization of the experiential world, not the discovery of an objective ontological reality.

Principles

Von Glasersfeld summarized radical constructivism in two main principles:

  • Knowledge is not passively received either through the senses or by way of communication, but is actively built up by the cognizing subject.
  • The function of cognition is adaptive and serves the subject's organization of the experiential world, not the discovery of an objective reality.

Instead of "truth" in the correspondence sense, radical constructivism uses the notion of viability: a construction is viable if it fits within the constraints of the experiential world and allows the subject to achieve goals without running into obstacles.

History and influences

Radical constructivism draws on the work of Giambattista Vico, Jean Piaget's genetic epistemology, George Berkeley's idealism, and cybernetics, particularly Heinz von Foerster's second-order cybernetics. Von Glasersfeld coined the adjective "radical" to mark the break with the traditional view that knowledge must match an independent reality.

Implications for education

In education, radical constructivism underlies constructivist teaching methods: learners actively construct understanding, and teachers act as guides who model, coach, and provoke cognitive conflict rather than transmitting facts. Critics argue the theory skirts solipsism and relativism, charges von Glasersfeld rejected.
