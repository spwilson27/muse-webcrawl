Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards

Crash testing is central to compliance with the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards.

The Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) are regulations promulgated by the United States Department of Transportation's National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) that define minimum safety performance requirements for motor vehicles and for certain items of motor vehicle equipment sold in the United States. They are codified at Title 49 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 571.

Background

The standards arose from the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, passed amid growing public concern about road deaths, galvanised by Ralph Nader's book Unsafe at Any Speed. The first standards took effect in 1968 and covered such items as seat belts, energy-absorbing steering columns, and padded dashboards.

Structure

The FMVSS are organised in series: the 100 series covers crash avoidance (e.g. lighting, braking, electronic stability control), the 200 series covers crashworthiness (e.g. occupant protection, seat belts, airbags, roof crush resistance), and the 300 series covers post-crash protection (e.g. fuel system integrity and flammability of interior materials). Well-known standards include FMVSS 108 (lamps and reflective devices), 208 (occupant crash protection), and 126 (electronic stability control systems).

Compliance

Unlike the European type-approval system, the United States uses manufacturer self-certification: manufacturers certify that their vehicles comply with all applicable standards, and NHTSA tests vehicles purchased from dealers and may order recalls for non-compliance. The standards also govern imported vehicles, which is why many foreign-market models cannot be sold in the US without modification.
