AC power plugs and sockets connect devices to the alternating-current electricity supply in buildings and similar sites. Electrical plugs and sockets differ from one another in voltage and current rating, shape, size, and connector type; the differences arose historically as each country developed its own system, and around fifteen plug types remain in common use around the world. Travellers frequently require adapters between systems, and voltage differences may also require converters.

Safety

Modern designs incorporate safety features such as recessed sockets, insulated pin shanks, shutters over live contacts (notably the British Type G), grounding (earthing) pins, polarity, and fuses inside plugs. Socket outlets in many countries must accept only the correct plug, preventing insertion of foreign objects. Standards bodies such as the International Electrotechnical Commission classify and publish the characteristics of each national system.

Voltage and frequency

Plug type alone does not determine electrical compatibility: most of the world uses 220–240 V at 50 Hz, while the Americas and Japan use roughly 100–127 V at 60 Hz (or 50 Hz in eastern Japan). A plug adapter changes only the shape of the connection, not the voltage, so equipment not rated for the local supply may be damaged without a voltage converter.

Toward a world standard

The IEC 60906-1 standard, published in 1986, defines a compact three-pin 230 V plug intended as a future common standard; it has been adopted, with modifications, by Brazil (as the NBR 14136) and South Africa. Universal adoption has otherwise stalled because of the enormous installed base of existing sockets.
