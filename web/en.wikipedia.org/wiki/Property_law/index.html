Property law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Property law is the area of law that governs the various forms of ownership in real property (land) and personal property. Property rights give the holder entitlements in relation to a thing — typically to use, exclude others from, transfer and derive income from it — enforceable against the world at large. The law defines what counts as property, how it is acquired and transferred, and how competing claims are resolved.

Concepts and history

Legal traditions divide the field differently: common law developed estates in land and the split between law and equity (including trusts), while civil law works with a unitary concept of ownership and a closed list (numerus clausus) of real rights. Feudal tenures, enclosure, and colonial dispossession make property law inseparable from political history; modern systems register title (often in Torrens or deeds-registration systems), protect leases and mortgages, and regulate planning and expropriation, usually requiring public purpose and compensation. Intellectual property extends the idea to intangibles — copyrights, patents and trade marks — with its own justifications and limits.

Theory

Philosophical defences range from Locke's labour theory to utilitarian incentive arguments and Hegelian personhood accounts; critics from Proudhon onward have contested private property's distributive and social consequences. The "bundle of rights" metaphor, economists' analysis of externalities and transaction costs (Coase), and debates about the commons shape the field's theory.
