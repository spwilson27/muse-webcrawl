Álvaro Alberto da Motta e Silva (22 April 1889 – 31 January 1976), known as Admiral Álvaro Alberto, was a Brazilian naval officer, scientist and policymaker. He was the first president of the Brazilian Academy of Sciences' successor institutions in the postwar science establishment and founded the National Research Council (CNPq) in 1951, serving as its first president.

Álvaro Alberto graduated from the Naval School and later studied physical chemistry in Berlin, where he worked with leading chemists of the period. In the Brazilian Navy he pursued research on explosives and developed the safety standards for powder storage. He rose to the rank of vice-admiral and held teaching posts at the Naval School and the Polytechnic School of the University of Brazil.

Nuclear policy

Álvaro Alberto was the architect of Brazil's early nuclear policy. He represented Brazil on the United Nations Atomic Energy Commission in 1946–47, where he resisted the Baruch Plan's provisions that would have left uranium and thorium resources under foreign control, arguing instead for compensation and national ownership — positions based on Brazil's significant monazite sand and thorium reserves. He later negotiated cooperation agreements with West Germany for the acquisition of centrifuge enrichment technology in the 1950s.

Legacy

In his honour, Brazil's most prestigious scientific award, the Admiral Álvaro Alberto Prize, was named after him, as was the Brazilian Navy nuclear-propelled submarine programme's first planned vessel, the Álvaro Alberto (SN-BR). The CNPq remains the principal federal agency for funding scientific research in Brazil.
