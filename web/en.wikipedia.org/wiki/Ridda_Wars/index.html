The Ridda Wars (Arabic: حُرُوب ٱلرِّدَّة, Ḥurūb ar-Ridda, "Wars of Apostasy") were a series of military campaigns launched by the first caliph, Abu Bakr, against rebellious Arabian tribes in 632–633 CE, shortly after the death of the Islamic prophet Muhammad. Many tribes had considered their allegiance a personal pact with Muhammad and withheld the zakat tax or followed rival prophets on his death. Abu Bakr insisted that submission to God and the community could not lapse, and the campaigns reunited Arabia under Medina, setting the stage for the great Muslim conquests.

Background

During his final years Muhammad had united most of Arabia through alliance, tribute and conversion. His death in June 632 prompted tribes across the peninsula to renounce Islam or refuse taxes. Several rival prophetic claimants arose: Musaylima among the Banu Hanifa in Yamama, Tulayha of the Banu Asad, the prophetess Sajah, and al-Aswad al-Ansi in Yemen (killed shortly before Muhammad's death). Abu Bakr, elected caliph, rejected compromise over zakat, declaring he would fight those who separated prayer from almsgiving even over "a hobbling-cord".

Campaigns

After defeating raiders threatening Medina itself at Dhu al-Qassah and Dhu Khushub, Abu Bakr organised some eleven columns sent across Arabia. His ablest general, Khalid ibn al-Walid, defeated Tulayha at the Battle of Buzakha (September 632), subdued the Banu Tamim (in a campaign shadowed by the disputed killing of Malik ibn Nuwayra), and culminated in the bloody Battle of Yamama (December 632), where Musaylima's Banu Hanifa were crushed but many Qur'an memorisers (huffaz) among the Muslims were killed — prompting, by tradition, the first collection of the Qur'an under Abu Bakr and Umar. Other columns under Ikrima, Shurahbil and al-Muhajir subdued Oman, Mahra, Yemen and Bahrain, while Kindah resistance in Hadhramaut was suppressed by 633.

Aftermath

By 633 Arabia was politically united for the first time, under the authority of Medina. The wars bound tribal military energies into the caliphate and channelled them outward: the same armies and commanders, above all Khalid, immediately began the conquests of Sasanian Iraq and Byzantine Syria. Historians (notably Fred Donner) note that the "apostasy" framing reflects later tradition; many rebels had never been deeply integrated, and the wars were as much state-formation as religious reconquest.

Sources

The narrative sources are later Islamic histories — al-Tabari transmits extensive accounts from Sayf ibn Umar (whose reliability is debated) and others — and modern reconstructions vary in detail. The broad outline and chronology are broadly accepted.
