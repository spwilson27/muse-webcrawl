Liability (financial accounting)

In financial accounting, a liability is a present obligation of an entity arising from past transactions or events, the settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits. Liabilities are reported on the balance sheet and, together with equity, fund the entity's assets, in accordance with the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

Classification

Liabilities are generally classified by maturity:

  • Current liabilities — obligations expected to be settled within one year or the normal operating cycle, such as accounts payable, accrued expenses, short-term loans, unearned revenue, and the current portion of long-term debt.
  • Non-current (long-term) liabilities — obligations due beyond one year, such as bonds payable, long-term loans, lease liabilities, deferred tax liabilities, and pension obligations.

Recognition and measurement

Under IFRS and US GAAP, a liability is recognized when it is probable that an outflow of resources will be required and the amount can be measured reliably. Most liabilities are recorded at the amount owed; long-term obligations may be measured at amortized cost using the effective interest method, and some financial liabilities are carried at fair value. Contingent liabilities — possible obligations dependent on uncertain future events, such as pending lawsuits — are disclosed in the notes rather than recognized, unless the outflow becomes probable and estimable, when a provision is recorded.

Examples

Common liabilities include trade payables to suppliers, wages and taxes payable, bank overdrafts, mortgages, corporate bonds, customer deposits (for banks), deferred revenue from subscriptions paid in advance, and warranty provisions. Analysis of liabilities is central to assessing solvency and liquidity, through measures such as the current ratio and debt-to-equity ratio.
