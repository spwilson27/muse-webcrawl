Debridement

Debridement is the medical removal of dead, damaged or infected tissue to improve the healing potential of a wound. Dead tissue (such as eschar and slough) harbours bacteria, sustains biofilm, and physically obstructs healing, so debridement is a foundational step in the management of burns, pressure ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, traumatic wounds and necrotising infections.

Types

  • Surgical (sharp) debridement — excision with scalpel, scissors, curette or hydrosurgery, typically in an operating theatre or at the bedside; the fastest method and essential in emergencies such as necrotising fasciitis.
  • Mechanical debridement — wet-to-dry dressings, irrigation, or whirlpool; largely non-selective and now less favoured.
  • Enzymatic debridement — topical agents such as collagenase that digest necrotic tissue.
  • Autolytic debridement — moisture-retentive dressings that let the body's own enzymes liquefy dead tissue; slow but selective and painless.
  • Biological debridement — sterile maggots (maggot therapy), which consume necrotic tissue and disinfect the wound.

Considerations

Debridement must balance aggressiveness against preservation of viable tissue; in ischaemic limbs it is coordinated with vascular assessment and revascularisation. Repeated "maintenance" debridement is often needed in chronic wounds until the wound bed is clean and granulating.
