Industrial symbiosis

Industrial symbiosis is an approach to industrial organisation in which traditionally separate industries cooperate in a collective network to gain competitive advantage through the physical exchange of materials, energy, water and by-products. It is a central concept of industrial ecology and a practical strategy for the circular economy: the waste or residual stream of one process becomes the raw material of another.

Kalundborg

The best-known example is the eco-industrial park at Kalundborg in Denmark, which developed from the 1960s onward as a web of bilateral agreements between a power station, an oil refinery, a pharmaceutical and enzyme producer, a plasterboard factory, the municipality and others. The power station supplies surplus steam and fly ash; refinery gas and treated water are shared; fermentation sludge from pharmaceutical production is used as farm fertiliser; and desulfurisation gypsum feeds plasterboard manufacture.

Characteristics

Industrial symbiosis networks usually arise incrementally, driven by economic savings and resource scarcity rather than central planning, though programmes such as the UK's National Industrial Symbiosis Programme (NISP) have brokered exchanges at national scale. Key enabling conditions include geographic proximity, diversity of industries, trust between firms and a supportive regulatory framework.

Benefits and limits

Benefits include reduced waste disposal and raw-material costs, lower emissions and water use, and new revenue streams. Barriers include dependence on anchor tenants, regulatory classification of by-products as waste, and the logistics of transporting low-value bulk materials.
