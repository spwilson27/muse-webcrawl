Granular material

A granular material is a conglomeration of discrete solid, macroscopic particles characterized by a loss of energy whenever the particles interact (the most common example would be friction when grains collide). The constituents that compose granular material are large enough such that they are not subject to thermal motion fluctuations. Thus, the size of the constituents of granular material is an important characteristic, distinguishing them from a colloid or a powder in some definitions.

Examples of granular materials are snow, nuts, coal, sand, rice, coffee, corn flakes, salt, and bearing balls. Granular matter is commercially important in the processing of pharmaceuticals, foodstuffs, and construction materials.

Properties

Granular materials can behave in some respects like solids, in others like liquids, and in still others like gases — a peculiarity sometimes described as a distinct state of matter. A pile of sand maintains a shape and an angle of repose like a solid, yet it can be poured like a liquid and its grains can be suspended like a gas.

Important phenomena in granular physics include:

  • Segregation — under vibration or flow, particles of different sizes tend to separate, as in the Brazil nut effect.
  • Jamming — a granular assembly can abruptly transition from flowing to rigid under shear or compression.
  • Dilatancy — a densely packed granular material tends to expand in volume when sheared.
  • Force chains — stresses are transmitted through networks of contacting grains rather than uniformly.

Research

The physics of granular materials is an active field. Because thermal energy is negligible compared with the gravitational and contact energies of the grains, ordinary statistical mechanics does not directly apply, and granular systems are typically far from equilibrium. Applications range from the handling of industrial powders to the geophysics of avalanches and dunes.
