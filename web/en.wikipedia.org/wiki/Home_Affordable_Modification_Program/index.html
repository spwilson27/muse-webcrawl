Home Affordable Modification Program

HAMP was part of the Making Home Affordable initiative

The Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) was a US federal government program introduced in 2009 as part of the Making Home Affordable initiative under the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). It aimed to help homeowners at risk of foreclosure during the subprime mortgage crisis and the ensuing Great Recession by providing incentives to mortgage servicers and investors to modify the loans of struggling borrowers, reducing monthly payments to affordable levels.

Design

Under HAMP, participating servicers evaluated delinquent or at-risk borrowers for a trial period plan, during which the borrower made reduced payments for at least three months. Successful completion led to a permanent modification, typically achieved by reducing the interest rate (to as low as 2 per cent), extending the loan term to up to 40 years, and, if necessary, deferring part of the principal — following a standard "waterfall" intended to bring monthly payments down to 31 per cent of the borrower's gross monthly income. The programme paid servicers and investors incentive fees for completed modifications and provided some protection against losses.

Results and criticism

HAMP fell well short of the administration's initial goal of helping three to four million homeowners: roughly 1.6 million borrowers received permanent modifications, and a substantial proportion later re-defaulted. The programme was criticised by the Special Inspector General for TARP, Neil Barofsky, and by Congressional oversight bodies for poor servicer compliance, lost paperwork, and slow implementation. Defenders argued that HAMP nevertheless established standardised modification practices across the industry and helped stabilise housing markets. HAMP expired on 31 December 2016, along with the related Home Affordable Refinance Program (HARP).
