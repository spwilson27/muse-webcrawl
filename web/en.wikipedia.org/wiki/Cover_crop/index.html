Cover crop

A cover crop is a plant grown primarily to benefit the soil and subsequent crops rather than to be harvested. Cover crops are planted during fallow periods or between rows of a cash crop to manage soil erosion, fertility, water, weeds, pests, and biodiversity. They are a cornerstone of sustainable agriculture and have been used in various forms since antiquity.

A cover crop of cereal rye and clover.

Benefits

  • Erosion control — living cover protects soil from wind and rain between cash crops.
  • Nitrogen — leguminous covers such as clover, vetch, and alfalfa fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic bacteria, fertilising the following crop; grass covers scavenge leftover nitrate and prevent leaching.
  • Soil health — roots and residues add organic matter, improve soil structure and water infiltration, and feed soil organisms.
  • Weed and pest suppression — dense cover competes with weeds; some species release allelopathic compounds or act as trap crops and beneficial-insect habitat.

Common species and management

Widely used cover crops include cereal rye, oats, winter wheat, crimson clover, hairy vetch, radish (tillage radish), buckwheat, and sorghum-sudangrass. Before planting the cash crop, the cover is terminated by mowing, rolling-crimping, tillage, winter-kill, or herbicide. When a cover crop is ploughed under specifically for its fertiliser value it is called green manure.

Adoption

Use has grown steadily in the United States and elsewhere, supported by research showing yield stability and soil-carbon benefits, though farmers weigh seed and management costs and possible moisture competition against the gains.
