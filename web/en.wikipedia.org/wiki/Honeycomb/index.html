Honeycomb

A honeycomb is a mass of hexagonal prismatic wax cells built by honey bees in their nests to contain honey, pollen, and their brood of larvae and pupae. The cells are made of beeswax secreted by worker bees and are inclined slightly upward to retain liquid honey.

Geometry

The hexagonal tiling of the comb is a celebrated example of natural economy: in 1999 Thomas Hales proved the "honeycomb conjecture", that a hexagonal grid divides a surface into regions of equal area with the least total perimeter, hence uses the least wax for a given storage volume. Individual cells begin roughly circular and take their hexagonal form as the wax flows and walls settle, a subject of continuing physical study.

Structure and use

Combs hang vertically in the hive; bees cap ripe honey cells with wax, while brood cells receive a different capping. Beekeepers harvest combs for extracted or comb honey, and render the wax for candles, cosmetics and foundation sheets. Artificial foundation embossed with cell patterns guides bees to build regular, workable combs within frames.

Honeycomb structures in engineering

Engineers imitate the geometry in lightweight sandwich panels — aluminium or aramid honeycomb cores used in aircraft, racing cars and architecture — exploiting the structure's exceptional strength-to-weight ratio.
