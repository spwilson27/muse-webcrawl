Prism (optics)

A triangular prism disperses white light into its component colours.

An optical prism is a transparent optical element with flat, polished surfaces that refract light. At least one surface must be angled; the most familiar form is the triangular prism, made classically of glass. Prisms can be used to break light into its constituent spectral colours (a dispersive prism) or to reflect light, or to split light into components with different polarisations.

MaterialOptical glass, fused silica, fluorite, acrylic
Main usesSpectroscopy, binoculars, periscopes, cameras, lasers
Key phenomenonRefraction, dispersion, total internal reflection

History

Isaac Newton's experiments of 1666, published in his Opticks (1704), used a prism to show that white light is a mixture of colours and that the prism does not create the colours but separates them because each colour is bent by a different amount — his experimentum crucis refuted the prevailing theory that prisms modified light.

Types

  • Dispersive prisms: triangular prisms and compound designs (Amici, Pellin–Broca) for spectroscopy.
  • Reflective prisms: Porro prisms in binoculars and the pentaprism in SLR cameras exploit total internal reflection to fold the optical path and erect the image.
  • Polarising prisms: Nicol, Glan–Taylor, and Wollaston prisms separate orthogonal polarisations using birefringent crystals.
  • Beam-splitting prisms: partially silvered cubes divide a beam into two.
  • Deflecting prisms: Dove and wedge (Risley) prisms rotate or deviate a beam.

How it works

At each surface, Snell's law governs the bending of light; because the refractive index of glass varies with wavelength (dispersion), blue light is deviated more than red, spreading white light into a spectrum. The total deviation depends on the apex angle and the angle of incidence, passing through a minimum-deviation position used for precise refractive-index measurements.
