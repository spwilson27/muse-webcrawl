Liberalism in the United States

Liberalism in the United States is a political and moral philosophy based on concepts of unalienable rights of the individual. In its modern American usage, it combines elements of classical liberalism — civil liberties, the rule of law, free markets tempered by regulation — with a belief, consolidated during the twentieth century, that government should act to promote equality of opportunity and protect citizens from economic insecurity and private concentrations of power.

Origins

The American founding drew heavily on Enlightenment liberal thinkers such as John Locke and Montesieu on natural rights and separated powers. Through the nineteenth century, both major parties claimed a liberal inheritance of limited government and individual freedom, while liberal reform movements — abolitionism, women's suffrage, and labor regulation — pressed to extend rights to those excluded from them.

The modern synthesis

The Great Depression transformed American liberalism. Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal (1933–1939) made federal activism against unemployment, poverty, and financial instability the defining liberal program, abandoning strict laissez-faire while defending liberal democracy against fascism and communism. Harry Truman's Fair Deal, John F. Kennedy's New Frontier, and Lyndon B. Johnson's Great Society extended this settlement: Social Security expansion, Medicare and Medicaid, federal aid to education, and the landmark civil rights legislation of 1964–1965 that ended legal racial segregation.

Backlash and after

From the late 1960s, liberalism was challenged by the Vietnam War's divisions, stagflation, and a resurgent conservatism that won the presidency under Ronald Reagan in 1980. Self-described liberals became closely associated with the Democratic Party, advocating environmental protection, abortion rights, gun regulation, LGBTQ rights, and universal health coverage, while critics — including the "neoliberal" turn within Democratic politics in the 1990s — debated how far market mechanisms should be embraced. The term remains central to American political argument, often used pejoratively by opponents and reclaimed proudly by supporters.
