William Golding

Sir William Gerald Golding (19 September 1911 – 19 June 1993) was a British novelist, playwright, and poet, best known for his debut novel Lord of the Flies (1954), in which a group of schoolboys marooned on an island descend into savagery. He won the Booker Prize for Rites of Passage (1980) and the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1983.

Life

Golding was born in Cornwall and studied at Oxford, initially natural sciences before switching to English literature. He worked as a schoolteacher and served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, commanding a rocket-launching craft and taking part in the Normandy landings; his wartime experience of human cruelty deeply shaped his fiction. After the war he taught at Bishop Wordsworth's School in Salisbury while writing.

Works

Lord of the Flies was rejected by numerous publishers before Faber and Faber issued it in 1954; it became a modern classic and a staple of school syllabuses. Golding's later novels include The Inheritors (1955), about the last Neanderthals; Pincher Martin (1956); Free Fall (1959); The Spire (1964); and the sea trilogy beginning with Rites of Passage, later published collectively as To the Ends of the Earth. His work characteristically explores the darkness and "the defect of human nature", often through allegory and mythic patterns.

Golding was knighted in 1988. He died of heart failure at his home in Cornwall in 1993.
