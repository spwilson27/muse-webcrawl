Welding


Shielded metal arc welding (stick welding) is one of the oldest arc welding processes.

Welding is a fabrication process that joins materials, usually metals or thermoplastics, by using high heat to melt the parts together and allowing them to cool, causing fusion. It differs from lower-temperature joining techniques such as brazing and soldering, in which the base metal is not melted. In addition to melting the base material, a filler material is typically added to the joint to form a pool of molten material that cools to form a joint that can be stronger than the base material itself. Pressure may also be used, alone or with heat, to produce a weld.

Processes

Many different energy sources can be used for welding, including a gas flame, an electric arc, a laser, an electron beam, friction, and ultrasound. The most common arc welding processes include shielded metal arc welding (SMAW, or stick welding), gas metal arc welding (GMAW, commonly called MIG welding), gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG welding), flux-cored arc welding, and submerged arc welding. Other methods include resistance welding, oxy-fuel welding, laser beam welding, electron beam welding, and friction stir welding.

History

Forge welding, in which blacksmiths joined iron and steel by heating and hammering, dates to the Bronze and Iron Ages. Arc welding emerged in the late nineteenth century after the invention of the electric arc and the work of pioneers such as Nikolay Benardos and Stanisław Olszewski; it became industrially important during the two World Wars, particularly in shipbuilding.

Safety

Welding exposes workers to intense ultraviolet light, heat, sparks, and fumes. Safety measures include welding helmets with darkened filters, protective clothing, ventilation, and fire precautions. Precautions are also taken to avoid electric shock and exposure to hazardous gases such as ozone and hexavalent chromium compounds.
