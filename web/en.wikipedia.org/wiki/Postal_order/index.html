Postal order

A British postal order, a paper money order issued through the post office

A postal order or post office order is a type of money order usually intended for sending money through the mail. It is purchased at a post office and is payable at another post office to the named recipient. A small fee, known as poundage in the United Kingdom, is charged for the service. Postal orders were among the earliest widely used instruments for making small payments at a distance, predating modern electronic transfers by more than a century.

In the United States, the equivalent instrument is generally called a money order, while the term "postal order" has survived longest in the United Kingdom and in countries whose postal systems were modelled on the British one, including Ireland, India, and several Commonwealth nations.

History

The postal order system was introduced in the United Kingdom in 1881, growing out of the older money order service that the Post Office had operated since the eighteenth century. Money orders required the sender's funds to be advised between offices by letter or telegraph, which made them relatively expensive for small sums. The postal order simplified the process: the purchaser bought a printed order for a fixed denomination, paid a small poundage fee, and could send the order itself by post. The recipient then presented it at a post office and received cash.

The scheme was an immediate success. By the early twentieth century, hundreds of millions of postal orders were being issued each year in Britain alone, and the system was copied by postal administrations around the world. Postal orders became a standard way to pay for goods ordered by mail, to enter competitions, to pay small debts, and to send money to family members.

In the United Kingdom, postal orders were originally issued in fixed denominations, with adhesive postage stamps sometimes affixed to make up odd amounts. Recipients were required to sign the order and, for much of the system's history, to "cross" it so that it could only be paid into a bank account. Uncrossed orders could be cashed over the counter.

Modern British postal orders are printed on demand at the counter in any value, and are treated similarly to cheques: they can be paid into a bank account or, in some cases, cashed. Although the volume of business has fallen dramatically with the spread of bank transfers and online payments, postal orders remain available and are still used for purposes such as paying government fees, entering postal competitions, and making payments where the sender has no bank account.

Around the world

Many countries issued their own postal orders, often in distinctive designs that are now collected by philatelists and students of paper money. British postal orders were also overprinted or issued specifically for use in British colonies, protectorates, and military post offices overseas. Irish postal orders were issued after independence in denominations of the Irish pound and later the euro. India continues to issue Indian Postal Orders, which are widely used for paying application fees, including fees for information requests under the Right to Information Act.

Decline

The rise of personal cheques, debit cards, and electronic payment systems steadily eroded the postal order's role through the late twentieth century. Nevertheless, the instrument persists as a simple, prepaid, and widely accessible method of payment, particularly valued by people without bank accounts and in administrative contexts where a traceable paper payment is convenient.
