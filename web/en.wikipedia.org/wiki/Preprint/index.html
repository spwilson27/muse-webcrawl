In academic publishing, a preprint is a version of a scholarly or scientific paper that precedes formal peer review and publication in a peer-reviewed scholarly journal. Preprints are typically posted to public servers where they are freely available, allowing researchers to share findings quickly, claim priority, and solicit informal feedback before (or alongside) journal submission. The term has been in use since at least the 1960s, when physics institutes exchanged printed paper preprints by mail; today the distribution is almost entirely electronic.

History

The modern preprint culture took off with arXiv, founded in 1991 by Paul Ginsparg at Los Alamos National Laboratory for high-energy physics and later extended to mathematics, computer science, and other fields. The Social Science Research Network (SSRN) followed in 1994. Biology adopted preprints more slowly until the launch of bioRxiv in 2013, and medicine followed with medRxiv in 2019. The COVID-19 pandemic greatly accelerated preprint use in the life sciences, with tens of thousands of pandemic-related manuscripts posted, which both sped the sharing of results and raised concerns about unvetted findings reaching the public and the press.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages attributed to preprints include speed (dissemination in days rather than months), free access without paywalls, earlier feedback, visibility for early-career researchers, and a verifiable timestamp establishing priority of discovery. Disadvantages include the absence of formal peer review — preprints may contain errors that would have been caught in review — media coverage that fails to note their preliminary status, and the risk that findings are "scooped" or misused clinically before validation.

Preprint servers

  • arXiv — physics, mathematics, computer science, quantitative biology, statistics, economics
  • bioRxiv — life sciences
  • medRxiv — medical and health sciences
  • SSRN — social sciences and humanities
  • ChemRxiv — chemistry
  • EarthArXiv and PsyArXiv — discipline-specific servers

Most servers perform basic screening for plagiarism, offensive content and scientific soundness, but screening is not peer review. Preprints are generally assigned a digital object identifier (DOI) and can be cited; many journals now permit or encourage prior preprint posting, and funders increasingly allow preprints to be listed in grant applications.
