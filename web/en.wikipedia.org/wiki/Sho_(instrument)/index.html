Shō (instrument)

The shō (笙) is a Japanese free reed musical instrument consisting of 17 slender bamboo pipes, each fitted with a metal free reed, set into a lacquered wooden wind chest. It is one of the principal instruments of gagaku, the imperial court music of Japan, where it provides sustained chord clusters (called aitake) that form the harmonic foundation of the ensemble.

ClassificationFree reed aerophone
Related instrumentsSheng, saenghwang, khaen
DevelopedNara period, from the Chinese sheng

Description

The pipes of the shō vary in length and are arranged so that the instrument resembles a phoenix with folded wings when played. Of the 17 pipes, 15 sound and two are traditionally silent, retained for symmetry. The player exhales and inhales through a mouthpiece while opening and closing finger holes on the pipes, so the instrument can sound continuously. Because the metal reeds are sensitive to moisture, performers traditionally warm the instrument over a small brazier before and during performance to keep the reeds free of condensation.

History

The shō derives from the Chinese sheng, transmitted to Japan by the Nara period (710–794) and preserved since then in gagaku ensembles. Its technique and construction changed little over the centuries. In the later 20th and 21st centuries, performers such as Mayumi Miyata brought the shō into contemporary classical music, and composers including John Cage and Tōru Takemitsu wrote works for it.

Role in gagaku

In gagaku, the shō plays alongside the ryūteki flute and the hichiriki double-reed pipe, which carry the melody. The shō's slowly shifting chords, built from prescribed groupings of five or six notes, give the music its characteristic shimmering, ethereal texture.
