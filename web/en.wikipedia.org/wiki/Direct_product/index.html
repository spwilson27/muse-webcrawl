Direct product

In mathematics, one can often define a direct product of objects already known, giving a new one. This generalises the Cartesian product of underlying sets together with a suitably defined structure on the product set. Examples include the direct product of groups, rings, modules, topological spaces and other algebraic structures. In category theory the product is the corresponding categorical construction, characterised by a universal property.

Examples

The direct product of two groups is the group formed by their Cartesian product with componentwise multiplication. The product of two topological spaces carries the product topology, whose open sets are unions of products of open sets. Products of vector spaces and modules are likewise defined coordinate by coordinate.

Direct sum

For infinite families of objects, the direct product must be distinguished from the direct sum: in the direct sum of groups, modules or vector spaces, only finitely many components of each element may be non-zero (or non-identity), whereas the direct product allows arbitrary components. For finite families the two coincide in many categories.
