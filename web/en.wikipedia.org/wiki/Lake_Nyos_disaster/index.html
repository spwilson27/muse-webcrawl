The Lake Nyos disaster occurred on 21 August 1986, when a limnic eruption at Lake Nyos in northwestern Cameroon suddenly released approximately one million tons of carbon dioxide (CO2). The gas cloud initially rose at nearly 100 kilometres per hour (62 mph) and then, being denser than air, descended onto nearby villages, suffocating 1,746 people and 3,500 livestock within a zone up to 25 kilometres (16 mi) from the lake.

Background

Lake Nyos is a deep crater lake occupying a volcanic maar on the flank of an inactive volcano in the Oku volcanic field, along the Cameroon volcanic line. Magma beneath the lake leaks carbon dioxide into the water, where it dissolves in the deep, stable layers under high pressure. Over time, the bottom water can become saturated with CO2.

The eruption

On the evening of 21 August 1986, something — possibly a landslide, heavy rain, or earthquake — disturbed the lake's stratified waters, triggering a sudden overturn. The depressurised deep water released its dissolved gas in a self-sustaining chain reaction, producing a fountain of gas and water and a cloud of CO2 perhaps 100 metres thick that rolled down the surrounding valleys at up to 50 kilometres per hour. Because carbon dioxide is denser than air, the cloud hugged the ground, asphyxiating people and animals in the villages of Nyos, Kam, Cha and Subum.

Aftermath

Survivors reported symptoms consistent with carbon dioxide asphyxiation; many had blisters and respiratory damage. The disaster, together with a similar smaller event at Lake Monoun in 1984, led scientists to recognise limnic eruptions as a natural hazard. To prevent a recurrence, degassing columns were installed at Lake Nyos beginning in 2001; these siphon CO2-rich bottom water to the surface, allowing the gas to dissipate harmlessly at a controlled rate. A similar degassing programme was undertaken at Lake Kivu, on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which contains far larger quantities of dissolved gas.
