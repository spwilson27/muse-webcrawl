Due diligence

Due diligence involves a thorough investigation of a business or asset before signing a contract.

Due diligence is the comprehensive investigation, appraisal and evaluation of a business, person or asset that a reasonable party undertakes before entering into an agreement or transaction with another party. The term is most commonly associated with mergers and acquisitions, securities offerings and real estate purchases, but the underlying standard of care—that a party should investigate what it is buying and with whom it is dealing—applies broadly in law and business.

Origin of the term

The expression entered common usage through the United States Securities Act of 1933, which provided a "due diligence" defence for broker-dealers: a defendant could avoid liability for misstatements in a registration statement by showing that, after reasonable investigation, they had reasonable ground to believe the statements were true. Over time the phrase broadened from a legal defence into a general description of the investigative process itself.

Process

A typical commercial due diligence exercise examines financial statements and tax records, material contracts, intellectual property, litigation and regulatory exposure, environmental liabilities, information systems and human resources. Findings may lead to price adjustments, warranties and indemnities, conditions precedent, or abandonment of the transaction. In venture capital and private equity, due diligence also assesses the management team, market size and the target's business model.

Types

  • Financial due diligence – verification of earnings, assets, liabilities and cash flows.
  • Legal due diligence – review of contracts, title, compliance and disputes.
  • Commercial due diligence – market and competitive analysis.
  • Vendor due diligence – prepared by the seller to streamline a sale process.
  • Customer due diligence – identity verification under anti-money-laundering rules, related to know your customer requirements.
