Capital punishment in Singapore

Executions in Singapore are carried out by long-drop hanging at Changi Prison.

Capital punishment in Singapore is a legal penalty. It is applied principally for murder, drug trafficking, treason, terrorism-related offences and certain firearms offences. Executions are carried out by hanging at Changi Prison, usually at dawn on a Friday. Singapore has one of the highest per-capita execution rates in the world, and its use of the death penalty — particularly the mandatory death penalty for drug trafficking above threshold quantities — has drawn sustained criticism from human rights organisations and foreign governments, while domestic opinion polls have generally shown majority public support.

Legal framework

The Misuse of Drugs Act prescribes the death penalty for trafficking specified quantities of controlled drugs — for example, more than 15 grams of diamorphine (heroin), 30 grams of cocaine, or 500 grams of cannabis — with the burden historically placed on the accused in relation to presumptions of trafficking and knowledge. Amendments that took effect in 2013 gave judges limited discretion to impose life imprisonment instead for drug couriers who substantively assist the authorities or suffer from diminished responsibility.

Practice

Death sentences are subject to appeal to the Court of Appeal, and convicted persons may petition the President of Singapore for clemency, acting on the advice of the Cabinet; clemency is very rarely granted. Statistics published by the Singapore Prison Service and compiled by Amnesty International recorded dozens of executions in the 1990s and early 2000s, with numbers declining thereafter, a de facto pause in 2020–2021, and a resumption of executions in 2022.

Debate

The government defends capital punishment as an essential deterrent that keeps Singapore's drug and violent-crime rates low. Critics, including Amnesty International and the United Nations human rights bodies, argue that there is no conclusive evidence of a unique deterrent effect, that mandatory sentences remove judicial discretion, and that executions fall disproportionately on low-level couriers and foreign nationals — cases such as that of Van Tuong Nguyen (2005) and Nagaenthran K. Dharmalingam (2022) attracted international attention.
