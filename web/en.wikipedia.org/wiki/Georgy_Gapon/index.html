Georgy Gapon

Georgy Apollonovich Gapon (17 February 1870 – 10 April 1906) was a Russian Orthodox priest and popular labour leader who led the peaceful workers' procession to the Winter Palace in St Petersburg on Bloody Sunday, 22 January 1905 — the event that ignited the Russian Revolution of 1905 and fatally damaged the prestige of Tsar Nicholas II.

Early life

Gapon was born in the Ukrainian village of Beliki, the son of a village clerk and a former serf. He studied at the Poltava seminary and the St Petersburg Theological Academy, becoming a charismatic preacher known for working among the poor. Through contacts with the police official Sergei Zubatov's "police socialism" scheme, he was encouraged to found a workers' organization loyal to the Tsar.

The Assembly and the petition

In 1903–04 Gapon founded the Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers of St Petersburg, which grew to thousands of members with branches across the capital. When four members were dismissed from the Putilov steel works in December 1904, a strike spread through the city. Gapon organized a mass petition to the Tsar asking for an eight-hour day, fair wages, and ultimately a constituent assembly — and a peaceful procession, carrying icons and portraits of the Tsar, to present it at the Winter Palace.

Bloody Sunday

On 22 January 1905, troops fired on the unarmed marchers at several points in the city, killing and wounding hundreds. Gapon, led to safety by the Socialist Revolutionary Pyotr Rutenberg, cursed the Tsar and issued a call to arms: "There is no God any longer. There is no Tsar." The massacre destroyed the myth of the benevolent Tsar-father and triggered strikes, mutinies, and uprisings across the empire, forcing Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto conceding a parliament, the Duma.

Exile, return, and death

Gapon fled abroad, where he was feted by émigré revolutionaries before falling out with them. Pardoned under the October amnesty, he returned to Russia and resumed contact with the Okhrana, the tsarist secret police, apparently offering to betray revolutionaries in exchange for money. The Socialist Revolutionary leadership, informed by Rutenberg, condemned him; in April 1906 Rutenberg lured Gapon to a cottage at Ozerki outside St Petersburg, where he was hanged after a confrontation in which hidden SR comrades listened. Whether he was a police agent, a double-dealer, or a naive idealist remains debated.
