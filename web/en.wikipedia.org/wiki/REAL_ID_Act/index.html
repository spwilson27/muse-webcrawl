The REAL ID Act of 2005 is an Act of Congress that establishes federal standards for state-issued driver's licenses and identification cards acceptable for certain federal purposes, including boarding regulated commercial aircraft, entering nuclear power plants and accessing certain federal facilities. Enacted in response to a recommendation of the 9/11 Commission, it was attached to an emergency supplemental appropriations bill for the Iraq War and tsunami relief.

Provisions

The Act sets minimum document requirements for state licenses, including verification of identity and lawful status, a full-face digital photograph, security features, and machine-readable technology. Federal agencies may not accept, for official purposes, licenses from states that do not meet the standards. Compliant licenses are typically marked with a star in the upper portion of the card.

Implementation

The original compliance deadline of 2008 was repeatedly extended by the Department of Homeland Security over objections from state legislatures, some of which passed statutes or resolutions refusing implementation on federalism and privacy grounds. Enforcement for air travel finally began on 7 May 2025, after which travellers presenting non-compliant identification face additional screening and may be unable to fly. All 50 states, the District of Columbia and most territories eventually issued compliant credentials.

Criticism

Civil-liberties groups criticised the Act as creating a de facto national identity card and raising privacy and data-security concerns, while supporters argued it closes loopholes that allowed the September 11 hijackers to obtain state identification fraudulently.
