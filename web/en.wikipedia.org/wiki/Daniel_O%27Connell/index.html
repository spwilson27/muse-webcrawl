Daniel O'Connell

Daniel O'Connell (Irish: Dónall Ó Conaill; 6 August 1775 – 15 May 1847), often called the Liberator, was an Irish political leader in the first half of the 19th century. He campaigned for Catholic emancipation — the right for Catholics to sit in the Westminster Parliament — which he achieved in 1829, and later organised a mass movement for the repeal of the Acts of Union between Ireland and Great Britain.

Early life and career

O'Connell was born in County Kerry and educated in France and at Lincoln's Inn in London, becoming a successful barrister. Profoundly influenced by the violence of the 1798 rebellion, which he opposed, he committed himself to constitutional, non-violent mass politics.

Catholic emancipation

In 1823 O'Connell founded the Catholic Association, funded by a penny-a-month "Catholic rent" that made it one of the first mass political movements in Europe. His election as MP for County Clare in 1828 — though as a Catholic he could not take his seat — forced the government of the Duke of Wellington to pass the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, granting emancipation.

Repeal and final years

O'Connell then campaigned to repeal the Act of Union of 1801 and restore an Irish parliament, holding huge "monster meetings" in 1843, the largest at Tara. When the government banned his meeting at Clontarf, O'Connell cancelled rather than risk violence and was later imprisoned for sedition (the conviction was overturned). His influence waned amid the Great Famine's onset and a split with the Young Irelanders, who resented his strict pacifism. He died in Genoa in 1847 on a pilgrimage to Rome.
