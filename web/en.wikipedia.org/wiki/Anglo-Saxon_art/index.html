Anglo-Saxon art

Anglo-Saxon art is the art produced in England from the fifth century until the Norman Conquest in 1066. It is known especially for illuminated manuscripts, metalwork, ivory carving, stone sculpture and embroidery. Dense interlace, animal ornament and intricate pattern are recurring features.

Early period

Early Anglo-Saxon art is richly represented by grave goods from sites such as Sutton Hoo, where gold, garnet and enamel work combined Germanic animal styles with Mediterranean and Celtic influences. After the conversion to Christianity, monastic culture fostered manuscript illumination, including Insular gospel books produced in both English and Irish spheres.

Later developments

From the ninth to eleventh centuries, Viking raids and settlement disrupted production but also introduced new motifs. The Winchester School of illumination, stone crosses, ivory carvings and embroideries such as those recorded at Durham show a mature style. The Bayeux Tapestry, probably made in England soon after the Conquest, is often treated as a final flowering of Anglo-Saxon needlework.
