Franz Schmidt

BornDecember 22, 1874, Pressburg, Austria-Hungary (now Bratislava, Slovakia)
DiedFebruary 11, 1939 (aged 64), Perchtoldsdorf, Austria
OccupationComposer, cellist, pianist

Franz Schmidt (22 December 1874 – 11 February 1939) was an Austrian composer, cellist, and pianist, and one of the last major representatives of the late-Romantic symphonic tradition descending from Anton Bruckner.

Life

Schmidt was born in Pressburg (now Bratislava). He studied at the Vienna Conservatory, where his composition teacher was Robert Fuchs and where he also received some instruction from Bruckner. From 1896 to 1911 he was a cellist in the Vienna Court Opera orchestra (the Vienna Philharmonic), performing under Gustav Mahler among others. He later taught piano, cello, counterpoint, and composition at the Vienna Academy of Music, serving as its director in the 1920s.

Music

Schmidt's works include four symphonies, the operas Notre Dame (1904, after Victor Hugo) and Fredigundis (1922), two string quartets, piano and organ works, and the oratorio The Book with Seven Seals (1937), after the Book of Revelation, generally considered his masterpiece. His best-known single piece is the Intermezzo from Notre Dame. His style combines Brucknerian breadth with rich chromatic harmony and masterful counterpoint.

Schmidt's reputation suffered after the Second World War, partly because he accepted commissions during the Nazi era, including the cantata Deutsche Auferstehung celebrating the Anschluss. His music has since enjoyed renewed advocacy from conductors and recording labels.
