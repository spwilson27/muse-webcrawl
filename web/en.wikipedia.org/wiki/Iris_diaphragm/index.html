An iris diaphragm is a mechanism of thin, overlapping metal leaves (blades) that form an adjustable circular opening — an aperture — used to control the amount of light passing through an optical system. Named for its resemblance to the iris of the eye, it is the standard aperture mechanism in photographic lenses, and is also used in microscopes, telescopes, enlargers and lighting equipment.

Mechanism

Each blade is pivoted at its outer edge to a fixed ring and carries a pin engaged by a rotating ring. Rotating the control ring causes all the blades to swing simultaneously, expanding or contracting the central opening while keeping it approximately circular. The more blades the diaphragm has, the rounder the aperture remains at small openings; this influences the rendering of out-of-focus highlights (bokeh) in photographs. In modern cameras the blades are driven by an actuator under electronic control, snapping from a fully open viewing aperture to the selected working aperture at the instant of exposure; older lenses use a preset or fully manual ring.

Use in photography

In a camera lens the iris sets the f-number, governing both exposure and depth of field. Aperture markings on manual lenses are accompanied by a depth-of-field scale computed from the iris setting. In microscopes, an iris diaphragm in the condenser assembly controls the numerical aperture and contrast of the illumination; in theatrical and studio lighting, iris diaphragms fitted to spotlights vary the beam diameter.

History

Adjustable diaphragms of sliding plates and rotating discs (such as the Waterhouse stop) preceded the iris design. True iris diaphragms with multiple leaves appeared in cameras in the late 1850s — a design generally credited to Charles Harrison and Joseph Schnitzer (1860) — and became universal in lenses by the early 20th century, displacing interchangeable stops.
