Gekiga (劇画, "dramatic pictures") is a style of Japanese comics (manga) aimed at adult audiences, characterised by realistic artwork, cinematic storytelling and serious, often gritty subject matter. The term was coined in 1957 by Yoshihiro Tatsumi, who wanted a word distinct from "manga" ("whimsical pictures") for the darker stories he and his peers were drawing.

History

Gekiga emerged in the rental-book (kashi-hon) market of 1950s Osaka, where Tatsumi, Takao Saito, Masahiko Matsumoto and others formed the "Gekiga Workshop" (Gekiga Kōbō) in 1959. Its artists rejected the child-oriented style of mainstream manga, drawing on film noir, crime fiction and postwar social reality.

In the 1960s gekiga found outlets in adult magazines and in the avant-garde monthly Garo, which published Sanpei Shirato's Kamui and experimental work. In the 1970s gekiga writers such as Kazuo Koike (Lone Wolf and Cub, with Goseki Kojima) and creators of action series dominated popular seinen manga, while Tatsumi chronicled working-class life in works later translated as A Drifting Life.

Characteristics and influence

Gekiga typically features proportioned, detailed figures, heavy use of cinematic angles and pacing, and themes of crime, sexuality, politics and alienation. The movement permanently widened manga's audience to adults and influenced alternative comics in the West, where Tatsumi's short stories were promoted by Adrian Tomine and Drawn & Quarterly from 2005.
