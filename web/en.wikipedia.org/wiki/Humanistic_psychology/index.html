Humanistic psychology

Humanistic psychology is a psychological perspective that arose in the mid-20th century in reaction to two theories: Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theory and B. F. Skinner's behaviourism. Sometimes called the "third force" in psychology, it emphasises the inherent goodness of people, free will, self-efficacy and self-actualisation.

Origins

The movement was formalised with the founding of the Association for Humanistic Psychology in 1961 and the Journal of Humanistic Psychology. Its roots lie in existential and phenomenological thought, and in earlier work by figures such as Otto Rank and Karen Horney.

Major theorists

Abraham Maslow proposed the hierarchy of needs, culminating in self-actualisation, and studied "peak experiences". Carl Rogers developed client-centred (person-centred) therapy, emphasising empathy, congruence and unconditional positive regard. Rollo May brought existential psychology to the United States.

Therapy and influence

Humanistic approaches influenced counselling and psychotherapy practice, education, and organisational development. The movement later seeded positive psychology, which shares its focus on wellbeing and human flourishing but uses more experimental methods.

Criticism

Critics argue that humanistic concepts such as self-actualisation are vague and difficult to test empirically, and that the approach underplays unconscious processes and biological influences.
