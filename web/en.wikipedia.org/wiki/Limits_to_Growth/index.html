The Limits to Growth (often abbreviated LTG) is a 1972 report that discussed the possibility of exponential economic and population growth with finite supply of resources, studied by computer simulation. The study used the World3 computer model to simulate the consequences of interactions between the Earth and human systems.

Commissioned by the Club of Rome and carried out at MIT by a team led by Donella and Dennis Meadows, the book concluded that if the (then) present growth trends in world population, industrialisation, pollution, food production and resource depletion continued unchanged, the limits to growth on the planet would be reached sometime within the next 100 years, most probably resulting in a rather sudden and uncontrollable decline in both population and industrial capacity. It also concluded that it was possible to alter these trends and establish a condition of ecological and economic stability sustainable far into the future.

The World3 model

World3 is a system dynamics model tracking five interacting variables: population, industrial output, food production, non-renewable resources and persistent pollution. Its famous "standard run" shows overshoot and collapse in the mid-21st century; other runs, assuming stabilising policies, show equilibrium.

Conclusions

The authors explicitly denied that the runs were predictions: they were scenarios exploring the behaviour modes of a growing system in a finite world. The book's central message was that overshoot is the default behaviour of unmanaged growth, and that deliberate limits — stabilising population and capital, and improving efficiency — could avert collapse.

Reception and criticism

The book sold some 30 million copies in 30 languages and provoked intense debate. Economists criticised the model for omitting price mechanisms, substitution and technological adaptation, and for aggregating resources into a single stock; some early critics wrongly reported that it predicted resource exhaustion by the 1990s. Defenders note that later data comparisons (notably by Graham Turner, 2008 and 2014) found observed trends broadly tracking the standard run.

Updates

The authors published Beyond the Limits (1992) and Limits to Growth: The 30-Year Update (2004), arguing that humanity had already overshot some limits. The report helped launch modern sustainability discourse and influenced discussions of ecological economics and planetary boundaries.
