United States Bill of Rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States Bill of Rights comprises the first ten amendments to the United States Constitution. Proposed following the often bitter 1787–88 debate over ratification — Anti-Federalists demanded protections for individual liberty against the new federal government — and drafted principally by James Madison, the amendments were ratified on 15 December 1791 (ten of the twelve articles proposed; one became the Twenty-seventh Amendment in 1992, the other remains unratified).

Contents

The amendments protect freedom of speech, press, religion and assembly (First); the right to keep and bear arms (Second); freedom from quartering of soldiers (Third); security against unreasonable searches and seizures (Fourth); due process, double jeopardy, self-incrimination and takings rights (Fifth); speedy and public trial, impartial jury and counsel (Sixth); jury trial in civil cases (Seventh); freedom from excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishments (Eighth); retained unenumerated rights (Ninth); and reserved powers to the states and people (Tenth). Originally applied only against the federal government (Barron v. Baltimore, 1833), most protections were "incorporated" against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment during the 20th century — the process that made the Bill of Rights the working charter of American criminal procedure and civil liberties, including in cases such as Gideon and Miranda.
