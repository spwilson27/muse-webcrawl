Calcium–aluminium-rich inclusion

A calcium–aluminium-rich inclusion or Ca–Al-rich inclusion (CAI) is a submillimeter- to centimeter-sized light-colored inclusion found in carbonaceous chondrite meteorites. CAIs consist of minerals rich in calcium and aluminium — among them melilite, spinel, hibonite, perovskite, grossite and anorthite — formed at very high temperatures, and they are the oldest dated solids in the Solar System.

Age and formation

Lead–lead radiometric dating of CAIs, most famously from the Allende meteorite that fell in Mexico in 1969, gives an age of about 4.567 billion years, which is taken as the age of the Solar System and the starting point of planetary timescales (“time zero”). CAIs are thought to have condensed or crystallised from a hot gas of solar composition close to the young Sun in the earliest phase of the solar nebula, before being transported outward and incorporated into the parent bodies of chondritic meteorites. Their mineralogy records some of the highest-temperature processes in the early Solar System.

Isotopic anomalies

CAIs show notable isotopic anomalies, including excesses of 26Mg from the decay of the short-lived radionuclide 26Al, which was an important heat source for early planetesimals, and oxygen isotope compositions distinct from those of Earth and most other Solar System materials. Some rare “FUN” inclusions (Fractionation and Unknown Nuclear effects) show larger mass fractionation and nuclear anomalies, hinting at heterogeneous material in the early nebula.
