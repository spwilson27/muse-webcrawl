Caffenol

Caffenol is a photographic black-and-white film developer made from common household ingredients — instant coffee, washing soda (sodium carbonate), and usually vitamin C (ascorbic acid) — instead of conventional developing agents such as hydroquinone or phenidone.

History and chemistry

The process was introduced in 1995 by Dr. Scott Williams and colleagues in the photographic technology program at the Rochester Institute of Technology, during a class exercise on alternative developers. The developing activity comes largely from caffeic acid and related phenolic compounds in coffee, working in the alkaline environment provided by the washing soda. Popular recipes include the original Caffenol, Caffenol-C (with vitamin C, which increases activity and reduces the heavy staining of the plain version), and the low-contrast stand-development variant Caffenol-CL, often with a small amount of potassium bromide as a restrainer.

Characteristics

Caffenol appeals to hobbyists because its ingredients are cheap, non-toxic, and available in supermarkets, and because it works with nearly all black-and-white films. Negatives typically show a brownish stain that acts as a contrast mask in printing, fine grain, and somewhat reduced effective film speed. Its variability — coffee brands differ in composition — is part of both its charm and its challenge.
