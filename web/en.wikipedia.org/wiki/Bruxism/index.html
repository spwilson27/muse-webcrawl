Bruxism

SpecialtyDentistry, sleep medicine
SymptomsTooth wear, jaw pain, headache, damaged teeth and restorations
TypesSleep bruxism; awake bruxism

Bruxism is excessive teeth grinding or jaw clenching. It is an oral parafunctional activity — that is, it is unrelated to normal function such as eating or talking. Bruxism is a common behaviour: reports of prevalence range from 8% to 31% in the general population. People who grind their teeth can do so during the day (awake bruxism) or during sleep (sleep bruxism), the latter being classified among sleep-related movement disorders.

Causes

The aetiology is multifactorial. Sleep bruxism is associated with sleep arousals and appears influenced by central nervous system factors, genetics and psychosocial stress; awake bruxism is more strongly linked to stress, anxiety and concentration. Certain medications (notably SSRIs), recreational substances, malocclusion, and conditions such as gastro-oesophageal reflux and obstructive sleep apnoea may contribute.

Signs and management

Consequences include attrition and fracture of teeth, failure of dental restorations and implants, hypertrophy and pain of the masseter muscles, temporomandibular joint symptoms, and morning headache. Bruxism is often a diagnosis of exclusion based on history (including partner reports of grinding sounds) and tooth wear; polysomnography is the reference standard for sleep bruxism. There is no cure; management is protective and symptomatic, typically an occlusal splint (mouthguard) to prevent dental damage, behavioural and stress-management approaches, and in selected cases botulinum toxin injections into the masticatory muscles.
