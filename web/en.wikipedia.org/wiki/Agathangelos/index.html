Agathangelos (Armenian: Ագաթանգեղոս, romanized Agatʻangełos) is the traditional name given to the author of a history of the Christianization of Armenia in the early fourth century. The work, conventionally called the History of the Armenians, describes the conversion of King Tiridates III by Gregory the Illuminator and the subsequent spread of Christianity in the Armenian kingdom.

Agathangelos presents himself in the text as a secretary of King Tiridates III who witnessed the events he describes. Modern scholars, however, generally consider the work to be a later composition, dating in its surviving form to the fifth century or later, incorporating older oral and written traditions.

The History

The History attributed to Agathangelos survives in several recensions, including Armenian, Greek, Arabic, and Syriac versions, which differ substantially in detail and extent. The narrative opens with the assassination of King Khosrov of Armenia by the Persian agent Anak, the father of Gregory. Gregory is raised in exile in Caesarea in Cappadocia, where he becomes a Christian, and later returns to Armenia to atone for his father's crime.

Gregory enters the service of Tiridates, the son of Khosrov, but is persecuted when he refuses to worship the goddess Anahit. He is imprisoned in a deep pit at Khor Virap, where he miraculously survives for some thirteen or fourteen years. Meanwhile Tiridates is struck with madness and transformed, in the legend, into the form of a boar. Gregory is released, heals the king, and converts him to Christianity; Tiridates then proclaims Christianity the religion of the realm, traditionally dated to 301 AD, which would make Armenia the first state to adopt Christianity officially.

Historical value

While the miraculous elements of the narrative belong to hagiography, the History preserves valuable information about the religious practices of pre-Christian Armenia, the Zoroastrian cults of the Arsacid period, and the institutions of the early Armenian church. It also contains the account of the martyrdoms of the virgin saints Hripsime and Gayane, whose churches remain major pilgrimage sites.
