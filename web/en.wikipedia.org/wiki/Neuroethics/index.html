Neuroethics

Neuroethics is a field of study concerning the ethical, legal, and social implications of neuroscience. It examines the benefits and dangers of modern brain research, the treatment of neurological and psychiatric disease, and the ways in which knowledge of the brain affects ideas of selfhood, moral responsibility, and personal identity. The term was popularised in 2002, notably through a conference sponsored by the Dana Foundation and through the work of scholars such as William Safire, Adina Roskies, and Martha Farah.

Two branches

Adina Roskies distinguished two complementary areas:

  • The ethics of neuroscience — the ethical conduct of neuroscientific research and the ethical evaluation of neuroscience's applications, including brain imaging privacy, cognitive enhancement, and neurotechnology.
  • The neuroscience of ethics — the study of how the brain gives rise to moral judgement, decision making, and related social behaviour.

Key issues

Brain reading and mental privacy

Functional neuroimaging and brain–computer interfaces raise the prospect of decoding mental states. Neuroethicists debate whether people have a right to cognitive liberty and mental privacy, and several jurisdictions have considered "neurorights" legislation; Chile became the first country to enact such a law in 2021.

Cognitive enhancement

The non-medical use of prescription stimulants, transcranial stimulation, and other interventions to improve memory or attention raises questions about fairness, coercion, authenticity, and safety.

Responsibility and the law

Findings that behaviour has neural causes are sometimes offered in court to mitigate blame. Neuroethics examines whether such evidence should change judgements of criminal responsibility, and warns against both overclaiming ("neurorealism") and dismissing genuine neurological impairment.

Disorders of consciousness and personhood

Brain-based criteria for death, the diagnosis of vegetative and minimally conscious states, and interventions such as deep brain stimulation all bear on concepts of personhood, identity, and informed consent for patients who cannot speak for themselves.

History

Although ethical questions about the brain predate the term — lobotomy and psychosurgery were debated in the mid-20th century — neuroethics crystallised as a named discipline around 2002, with dedicated journals (Neuroethics, founded 2008), societies such as the International Neuroethics Society, and neuroethics components in large projects including the US BRAIN Initiative and the European Human Brain Project.
