No-arbitrage pricing
FieldFinancial economics

No-arbitrage pricing is the principle that, in a market free of arbitrage opportunities, the price of a financial claim must equal the cost of a portfolio that replicates its payoffs. It is the foundation of modern derivative pricing: rather than forecasting returns, one prices a contract by constructing (or imagining) a self-financing hedge whose cost arbitrageurs will force the market price to match.

The law of one price

The underlying intuition is the law of one price: two portfolios that deliver identical payoffs in every future state must have the same price today. If they did not, traders could buy the cheaper, sell the dearer, and lock in a riskless profit — arbitrage — until prices converged.

Risk-neutral valuation

Under no-arbitrage, and given a traded numéraire, derivative prices can be computed as discounted expected payoffs under a risk-neutral (martingale) probability measure. The fundamental theorem of asset pricing makes this precise: a market admits no arbitrage if and only if an equivalent martingale measure exists, and the market is complete if and only if that measure is unique.

Examples

  • Put–call parity: European call and put prices on the same underlying are linked exactly by no-arbitrage.
  • Forward prices: the forward price of an asset equals its spot price carried at the risk-free rate, adjusted for dividends or storage costs.
  • Black–Scholes model: option prices are derived by continuously rehedging a stock–bond portfolio that replicates the option.
  • Covered interest parity: forward exchange rates are pinned down by interest-rate differentials between currencies.

Limitations

Real markets have transaction costs, funding constraints, margin requirements and limits to arbitrage, so prices can deviate from no-arbitrage values, sometimes persistently. Crises — when arbitrage capital withdraws — show some of the largest violations, as in the 2008 dislocation of covered interest parity.
