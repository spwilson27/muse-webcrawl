United States Postal Savings System

The United States Postal Savings System was a postal savings system established by the United States Congress in 1910 and discontinued in 1966. It allowed members of the public to deposit savings at post offices, where the funds were guaranteed by the federal government, providing a safe alternative to banks at a time when bank failures were common and deposit insurance did not exist.

History

Postal savings had long been used in Europe, beginning with the United Kingdom in 1861. In the United States, support grew after the Panic of 1907 shook confidence in banks. The Postal Savings Act was signed on June 25, 1910, and the first postal savings depositories opened in 1911.

Accounts paid 2 percent interest, and deposits were capped (initially at $500, later raised to $2,500). Funds were largely redeposited in local banks or invested in government securities. The system was especially popular with immigrants, who were familiar with postal savings in their home countries, and with rural savers far from banks.

Decline and abolition

Deposits peaked at about $3.4 billion in 1947. After the creation of federal deposit insurance in 1933 and as commercial banks offered higher interest rates, usage declined steadily. Congress abolished the system in 1966, and remaining accounts were closed or transferred by 1967.
