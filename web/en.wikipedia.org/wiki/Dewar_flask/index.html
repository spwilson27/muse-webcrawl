Dewar flask

A Dewar flask (or Dewar bottle, Dewar vessel, or simply Dewar) is a double-walled vessel with a vacuum between its walls, used to thermally insulate the substance stored inside. It is named after its inventor, the Scottish physicist Sir James Dewar, who developed it in 1892 in the course of his research in cryogenics. The domestic vacuum flask — sold under names such as Thermos — is a direct descendant of Dewar's laboratory vessel.

Design

A Dewar flask consists of two vessels, one placed inside the other and joined at the neck. The gap between them is evacuated of almost all air, and the near-vacuum drastically reduces heat transfer by conduction and convection. Radiative transfer is reduced by silvering the facing surfaces of the walls, as Dewar himself did. Modern Dewars are made of borosilicate glass or stainless steel, and large industrial versions — Dewar vessels for bulk cryogenic storage and transport — may hold thousands of litres of liquid nitrogen, oxygen or helium.

History

Dewar invented the flask in 1892 while working on the liquefaction of gases at the Royal Institution; he used it to keep liquefied gases cold long enough to study their properties, and with it achieved the liquefaction of hydrogen in 1898. Dewar never patented the design for domestic use; two German glassblowers, Reinhold Burger and Albert Aschenbrenner, commercialised it in 1904 under the name "Thermos", and Dewar lost a court case over the rights. The vessel remains standard equipment in low-temperature physics and chemistry, and its name is used generically for laboratory cryogen storage vessels.

Safety

Dewars must never be sealed airtight when containing cryogenic liquids, since warming liquid expands enormously as it vaporises and can burst the vessel; laboratory Dewars use loose-fitting stoppers or pressure-relief valves, and glass Dewars are often taped or shielded against implosion.
