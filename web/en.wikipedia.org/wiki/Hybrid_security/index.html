Hybrid security

A hybrid security is a single financial security that combines two or more different financial instruments. Hybrid securities generally combine both debt and equity characteristics, and are often designed to share risk between issuers and investors in ways that plain bonds or shares cannot. The most basic hybrid security is a convertible bond, which combines a straight bond with an embedded option to convert it into shares of the issuer.

Characteristics

Hybrids sit between senior debt and common equity in a company's capital structure. They typically pay a regular coupon or dividend like a bond, but may defer payments, have very long or perpetual maturities, or convert into equity under certain conditions. Because of their subordination and deferral features, rating agencies may grant issuers partial "equity credit" for hybrids.

Types

Common forms include convertible bonds, exchangeable bonds, preference shares, capital notes, and contingent convertible bonds ("CoCos"), which convert into equity or are written down when a bank's capital falls below a trigger level. Payment-in-kind (PIK) instruments and convertible notes issued by start-ups are also hybrids.

Use by banks

Under the Basel III framework, banks issue additional Tier 1 instruments, usually structured as contingent convertible or write-down bonds, to satisfy regulatory capital requirements. These instruments absorb losses while the bank remains a going concern.
