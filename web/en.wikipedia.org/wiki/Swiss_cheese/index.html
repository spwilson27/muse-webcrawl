Swiss cheese

Swiss cheese is any variety of cheese that resembles Emmental, a yellow, medium-hard cheese that originated in the Emme valley of Switzerland. Outside Switzerland, particularly in North America, "Swiss cheese" is a generic name for a family of pale cheeses with large holes ("eyes") and a mild, nutty flavour, produced in the United States and elsewhere in imitation of Emmental. Within Switzerland the term is not used for domestic cheeses, which are known by their specific names and appellations.

Production and the holes

Swiss-type cheeses are made from cow's milk with three bacterial cultures, including Propionibacterium freudenreichii subsp. shermanii. During warm-room ripening these propionibacteria consume lactic acid and release carbon dioxide gas, which collects into bubbles that become the cheese's characteristic eyes; the same metabolism produces much of the nutty, sweet flavour. Agroscope researchers reported in 2015 that microscopically fine particles of hay dust in milk act as nucleation sites for the bubbles — explaining why eyes diminished as milking became more hygienic and sealed. Wheel sizes are large (Emmental wheels traditionally 75–120 kg), and ageing ranges from two months to over a year.

Varieties

True Swiss cheeses with protected designations include Emmentaler AOP, Gruyère AOP (a cooked, pressed cheese without large eyes), Appenzeller, Tête de Moine, Vacherin Fribourgeois and Sbrinz. The American "Swiss cheese" is typically a milder, smaller-eyed factory product sold sliced for sandwiches; related imitations include Jarlsberg (Norway) and Maasdam (Netherlands).

Cultural notes

The holey wedge is a cartoon icon of cheese generally, and "Swiss cheese" serves metaphorically for anything full of gaps — as in the "Swiss cheese model" of accident causation in safety engineering, where slices represent defensive layers whose holes must align for a failure to occur.
