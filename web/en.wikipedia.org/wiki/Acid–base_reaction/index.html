Acid–base reaction

An acid–base reaction is a chemical reaction involving the transfer of a proton (H+) or of electron pairs between an acid and a base. Such reactions underlie neutralisation, titration, and much of the chemistry of aqueous solutions, soils, oceans, and living organisms.

Definitions

Arrhenius

Svante Arrhenius (1884) defined an acid as a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions in water, and a base as one that increases the concentration of hydroxide ions. The classic neutralisation of hydrochloric acid by sodium hydroxide, giving sodium chloride and water, is the archetype.

Brønsted–Lowry

In 1923 Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry independently defined acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors. Every acid–base reaction then pairs an acid with its conjugate base and a base with its conjugate acid. This definition covers reactions in non-aqueous solvents and the behaviour of species such as ammonia and the hydrogen carbonate ion, which can act as both acid and base (amphoterism).

Lewis

G. N. Lewis proposed a broader definition the same year: an acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a base an electron-pair donor. The Lewis definition encompasses metal-ion complexation and reactions such as that of boron trifluoride with ammonia, in which no proton is transferred.

Strength and equilibria

Acid strength is quantified by the acid dissociation constant Ka and its negative logarithm pKa. Strong acids dissociate almost completely in water, while weak acids establish an equilibrium, described by buffer systems such as the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. The pH scale measures the acidity of a solution.

Applications

Acid–base reactions are exploited in volumetric analysis (acid–base titration), antacid medicines, baking (bicarbonate plus acid releasing carbon dioxide), soil treatment, and industrial processes from fertiliser manufacture to petroleum refining.
