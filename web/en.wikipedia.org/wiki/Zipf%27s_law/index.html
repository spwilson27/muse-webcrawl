Zipf's law

Zipf's law
TypeEmpirical statistical law (power law)
Named afterGeorge Kingsley Zipf (popularised, 1930s–40s)
StatementFrequency ∝ 1 / rank
ApplicationsLinguistics, city sizes, firm sizes, web traffic

Zipf's law is an empirical law stating that when a list of measured values is sorted in decreasing order, the value of the n-th entry is often approximately inversely proportional to n. It is most famous in linguistics: in a large body of natural language text, the frequency of a word is roughly inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table. Thus the most frequent word occurs about twice as often as the second most frequent, three times as often as the third, and so on. In English, "the" accounts for roughly 7% of all words, "of" about 3.5%, "and" about 2.8%, and the pattern continues down the ranks.

History

The pattern was noted by the French stenographer Jean-Baptiste Estoup in 1916 and studied by Edward Condon in 1928, but it was popularised by the Harvard linguist George Kingsley Zipf in his books of the 1930s and 1940s, notably Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (1949), where he explained it as the outcome of speakers and hearers balancing effort.

Occurrences

Zipf-like distributions appear far beyond language: city populations within many countries roughly follow a rank-size rule (the largest city about twice the second, and so on), as do firm sizes, income extremes (related to the Pareto distribution), website visits, library book usage, and the frequencies of notes in musical corpora. On a log–log plot of frequency against rank, a pure Zipf distribution is a straight line of slope −1; real data often give slopes near, but not exactly, −1, leading to the generalisation known as the Zipf–Mandelbrot law.

Explanations

Many mechanisms have been proposed. Benoit Mandelbrot derived the law from optimising information transmission; others show that random typing with spaces can generate Zipf-like word distributions, and preferential-attachment ("rich get richer") growth processes produce similar power laws. Statisticians caution that apparent Zipf behaviour can be an artefact of mixing different distributions, and whether a single deep principle unifies all cases remains debated.
