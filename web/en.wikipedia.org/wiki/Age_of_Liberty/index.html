Age of Liberty

The Age of Liberty (Swedish: frihetstiden) was a period in Swedish history spanning roughly 1719 to 1772, following the death of Charles XII in the Great Northern War. During this era Sweden was governed as a parliamentary constitutional monarchy: royal power was sharply limited and the Riksdag of the Estates dominated politics, making Sweden one of the earliest examples of parliamentary rule in Europe. The period ended when Gustav III seized power in the coup of 1772.

Background

The absolute monarchy of Charles XI and Charles XII had left Sweden exhausted after more than two decades of war and the loss of its Baltic empire. When Charles XII was killed at Fredriksten in 1718, his sister Ulrica Eleonora accepted the throne only after renouncing absolutism. New constitutions in 1719, 1720, and 1723 vested most power in the Riksdag and the Council of the Realm.

Politics: Hats and Caps

Two parties dominated the Age of Liberty: the Hats (hattarna), favouring an active foreign policy, revanchism against Russia, and mercantilist support for manufactures; and the Caps (mössorna), advocating peace, economy, and closer ties with Russia and Britain. Both parties were subsidised by foreign powers—France backed the Hats, while Russia and Britain funded the Caps. Chancellor Arvid Horn led a cautious Caps government until the Hats took power in 1738–39.

The Hat regime launched the disastrous Russo-Swedish War of 1741–1743 and later a costly intervention in the Seven Years' War. Financial mismanagement, inflation, and corruption fueled opposition; the Caps returned to power in 1765 and introduced austerity and reforms.

Science, culture, and reform

The era was a golden age of Swedish science: Carl Linnaeus revolutionised taxonomy, Anders Celsius devised his temperature scale, and the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences was founded in 1739. In 1766 the Riksdag passed the world's first freedom of the press act, championed by the Caps and figures such as Anders Chydenius, who also advocateded economic liberalism. Population statistics (Tabellverket, 1749) made Sweden a pioneer of official demography.

End of the era

Growing disorder, factionalism, and foreign interference discredited the system. Crown Prince Gustav, becoming king in 1771, carried out a bloodless coup on 19 August 1772, imposing the Instrument of Government of 1772 and restoring strong royal authority—ending the Age of Liberty.
