Semi-presidential system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Élysée Palace in Paris, seat of the president of France — the archetypal semi-presidential republic
The Élysée Palace in Paris, seat of the president of France — the archetypal semi-presidential republic

A semi-presidential system (or dual executive system) is a system of government in which a president exists alongside a Prime minister and a cabinet, both responsible to the legislature. Unlike a purely parliamentary system, the president is directly elected with a fixed term and significant powers; unlike a presidential system, the government depends on parliamentary confidence. The term was coined by Maurice Duverger in 1980, with the French Fifth Republic as the prototype.

Definition and variants

Duverger's criteria: a president elected by universal suffrage, possessing considerable powers, facing a prime minister and cabinet who hold executive power but can be dismissed by parliament. Scholars divide the family into premier-presidential systems (president cannot dismiss the government alone; e.g. France, Poland, Lithuania) and president-parliamentary systems (government dually accountable; e.g. Russia, Ukraine in some periods), the latter judged more prone to conflict and democratic breakdown.

France and cohabitation

France's Fifth Republic (1958) gives the president dominance in foreign and defence policy and, normally, control through a loyal parliamentary majority. When elections produce a hostile majority, 'cohabitation' results — the president retreats while the prime minister governs domestically — as in 1986–88, 1993–95 and 1997–2002. A 2000 reform shortening the presidential term to five years, synchronised with legislative elections, was designed to make cohabitation unlikely.

Spread and debate

Semi-presidentialism spread after 1989 to much of post-communist Europe, francophone Africa and elsewhere (Portugal, Finland, Taiwan, Sri Lanka in variants). Supporters value a strong arbiter-president plus flexible government; critics warn of dual legitimacy conflicts between president and parliament of the kind Juan Linz diagnosed for presidentialism.
