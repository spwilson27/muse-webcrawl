Michał Kalecki

Born: 22 June 1899, Łódź, Congress Poland
Died: 18 April 1970, Warsaw, Poland
Field: Macroeconomics
Known for: Theory of effective demand; profit equation; political business cycle

Michał Kalecki (Polish: [ˈmixaw kaˈlɛt͡skʲi]; 22 June 1899 – 18 April 1970) was a Polish Marxian economist. He is recognised as having anticipated many of the central ideas of John Maynard Keynes's General Theory — in particular the principle of effective demand — in papers published from 1933, three years before Keynes's book, though writing in Polish he remained little known in the English-speaking world for decades.

Theory

Kalecki built a macroeconomic theory in which output and employment are determined by investment spending, and distribution by the "degree of monopoly" in pricing. His famous profit equation expresses aggregate profits as the sum of capitalists' investment and consumption minus workers' saving: in his aphorism, "workers spend what they get, and capitalists get what they spend". His model generated an endogenous theory of the business cycle driven by investment decisions and lags.

Career

Largely self-taught in economics, he worked at the Institute for the Study of Business Cycles and Prices in Warsaw, then on research fellowships in Sweden, London and Cambridge, where he moved in the circle of Joan Robinson. During the Second World War he worked at the Oxford Institute of Statistics. After the war he served briefly in the Polish government, spent a decade at the United Nations Secretariat in New York (resigning during the McCarthy era), and returned to Poland in 1955 as an economic adviser and professor, co-founding the Central School of Planning and Statistics' research community. His 1943 essay "Political Aspects of Full Employment" — predicting political resistance to sustained full-employment policies — became a classic of political economy.

Legacy

Kalecki's work is foundational for post-Keynesian economics. Joan Robinson and others championed the claim that his version of the theory was in some respects more complete than Keynes's own.
