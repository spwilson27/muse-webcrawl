Balanced budget amendment

A balanced budget amendment is a constitutional rule requiring that a government cannot spend more than its income. Most versions allow deficits during declared wars or national emergencies and permit override by a supermajority vote. Proposals have been debated in many countries; in the United States such an amendment to the federal Constitution has been repeatedly proposed but never adopted, while 49 U.S. states have some form of balanced-budget requirement in their constitutions or statutes.

United States proposals

Amendments were proposed from the 1930s onward. The drive peaked in the 1980s and 1990s: in 1995 a balanced budget amendment passed the House of Representatives and failed the Senate by a single vote. The Contract with America had made it a central Republican pledge. Later votes, including in 2011 during the debt-ceiling crisis and 2018, also failed to secure the required two-thirds majorities. Critics note that statutory measures such as the Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Act (1985) attempted similar discipline without constitutional change.

Arguments

Supporters argue that politicians face incentives to borrow for current benefits while deferring costs, that a rule would restrain the growth of public debt, and that households and businesses must live within their means. Opponents argue that mandatory annual balance forces pro-cyclical policy—cutting spending and raising taxes in recessions, deepening downturns—and that it would constrain responses to war and disaster, transfer fiscal decisions to courts, and encourage accounting gimmicks. Many economists favour rules targeting structural or cyclically adjusted balances, such as Switzerland's debt brake (adopted 2001) and Germany's constitutional Schuldenbremse (2009).

Other countries

Spain amended its constitution in 2011 to limit structural deficits, and the European Union's Fiscal Compact (2012) requires signatories to adopt balanced-budget rules in national law.
