Empathy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A caregiver holding an elderly patient's hand in both of hers
A caregiver holding an elderly patient's hand in both of hers

Empathy is the capacity to understand or feel what another being is experiencing from within their frame of reference — 'putting oneself in another's shoes'. Psychologists distinguish cognitive empathy (perspective-taking, theory of mind), affective empathy (feeling with another, emotional contagion) and compassionate empathy (being moved to help). The concept (from German Einfühlung) is central to moral psychology, psychotherapy, and debates about human nature and artificial intelligence.

Mechanisms

Developmentally, empathy builds from infant emotional contagion through joint attention to explicit perspective-taking in childhood; neuroscience associates it with the mirror-neuron system, the anterior insula and cingulate (for pain empathy) and ventromedial prefrontal networks. Psychopathy and narcissistic disorders show reduced affective empathy with (often) intact cognitive empathy; autism was long misdescribed as an empathy deficit — the 'double empathy problem' reframes it as mutual misunderstanding between differently-wired people.

Debates

Paul Bloom's Against Empathy (2016) argues empathy is biased, narrow and innumerate — a spotlight favouring the near and cute — and that rational compassion should guide policy instead; defenders reply that empathy motivates the very concern Bloom wants, and that its failures are failures of scope, solvable by extending the circle. Empathy training is meanwhile a growth industry in medicine, policing and management.
