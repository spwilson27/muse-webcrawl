Potichomania

Potichomania
TypeDecorative craft
Peak popularityMid-19th century (Victorian era)
MaterialsGlass vessel, printed images, gum, paint

Potichomania (from the French potiche, a large porcelain vase, and manie, craze) is a decorative craft in which the inside of a glass vessel is decorated with pasted prints, pictures or paintings so that the object appears, when viewed from outside, to be made of painted porcelain. The technique was a popular Victorian pastime, particularly in the 1850s and 1860s, when kits with pre-printed images were sold for home use.

A potichomania vase imitating an Oriental porcelain potiche.

Technique

The craftsperson selects a plain glass vase, jar or bowl. Coloured prints — often imitations of Chinese or Japanese porcelain decoration, flowers, or picturesque scenes — are cut out, coated with gum or varnish, and applied face-down to the interior surface of the glass so that the image shows through. The remaining interior is then painted with an opaque ground colour, commonly white, cream or a colour matching porcelain glazes, and the vessel may be finished with painted bands or gilding on the rim. Seen from outside, the glass disappears visually and the object resembles a genuine porcelain potiche.

History

Potichomania arose during the nineteenth-century European fascination with Oriental porcelain and with home crafts generally. It belongs to a family of Victorian "fancy work" techniques alongside decoupage — to which it is closely related, except that the images are applied under glass rather than on a surface. Manuals and suppliers of printed sheets catered to the craze, and the finished vases were used as drawing-room ornaments and flower vases.
