Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus (c. AD 69 – after AD 122), commonly referred to in English as Suetonius, was a Roman historian who wrote during the early Imperial era of the Roman Empire. His most important surviving work is a set of biographies of twelve successive Roman rulers, from Julius Caesar to Domitian, known as De vita Caesarum (The Lives of the Caesars, or The Twelve Caesars).

Life

Suetonius was of equestrian rank. His father served as a tribune in the Thirteenth Legion during the civil war of AD 69. Suetonius studied literature and law, and rose through imperial administration, holding posts in charge of the imperial libraries and archives. As chief secretary (ab epistulis) to the emperor Hadrian, he had access to imperial correspondence and records. He was dismissed by Hadrian around AD 122, reportedly for indiscreet conduct toward the empress Sabina, together with the praetorian prefect Septicius Clarus, to whom The Twelve Caesars is dedicated.

Works

The Twelve Caesars organises each life by rubrics — ancestry, public career, private habits, appearance, death — rather than strict chronology, and is famous for its vivid anecdotes and scandals, which have shaped the popular image of emperors such as Caligula and Nero. Other surviving works include parts of De vita Caesarum's companion collection De viris illustribus (On Illustrious Men), covering poets, orators, historians, and grammarians, including the lives of Terence and Virgil. Many of his writings are lost.

Influence

Suetonius' biographical model strongly influenced later writers, from the Historia Augusta to Einhard's Life of Charlemagne, and through them the biographical writing of the Middle Ages and Renaissance.
