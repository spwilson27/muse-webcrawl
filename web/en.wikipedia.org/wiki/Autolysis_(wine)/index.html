Autolysis (wine)

Autolysis in winemaking relates to the complex chemical reactions that take place when a wine spends time in contact with the lees, or dead yeast cells, after fermentation. While the lees are the source of many enzymes, autolysis specifically refers to the interactions of these enzymes while the yeast cells are breaking down and releasing components into the wine.

Process

After alcoholic fermentation is complete, the yeast cells die and settle to the bottom of the vessel as lees. Over time, enzymes within the dead cells begin to break down the cell walls, a self-digestion process that releases amino acids, polysaccharides, mannoproteins, fatty acids and other compounds into the wine. This process is encouraged in the production of Champagne and other bottle-fermented sparkling wines, where the wine may age on its lees for several years before disgorgement.

Influence on wine

The compounds released during autolysis contribute characteristic aromas and flavours described as biscuity, bready, toasty or yeasty, and they add texture, body and a creamy mouthfeel to the wine. Mannoproteins released from the yeast cell walls can also improve the stability of the wine and soften its perception of acidity. Extended lees ageing is a hallmark of quality sparkling wine production, as well as of some still white wines matured sur lie, such as Muscadet from the Loire Valley.
