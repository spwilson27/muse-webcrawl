Martin Edward Hellman (born October 2, 1945) is an American cryptologist and professor emeritus of electrical engineering at Stanford University, best known as co-inventor, with Whitfield Diffie, of public-key cryptography and the Diffie–Hellman key exchange protocol. His work in the 1970s laid the foundations for modern secure communications, and he has also been active on issues of nuclear risk and global security.

Public-key cryptography

At Stanford in the mid-1970s, Hellman and his graduate student Whitfield Diffie began questioning the assumption that encryption keys had to be distributed in secret. Their 1976 paper "New Directions in Cryptography" introduced the concept of public-key cryptography and described the Diffie–Hellman key exchange, which lets two parties agree a shared secret over an insecure channel. Hellman's student Ralph Merkle independently developed related ideas ("Merkle's puzzles"), and the key-exchange protocol is sometimes called Diffie–Hellman–Merkle.

Hellman also studied the security of the Data Encryption Standard (DES), arguing with Diffie that its 56-bit key was too short and vulnerable to brute-force attack — a position vindicated when DES was later cracked by dedicated hardware. Their advocacy of public research on cryptography, in the face of official pressure to restrict it, helped establish academic cryptology as an open field.

Honours

Hellman shared the 2015 ACM Turing Award with Diffie "for inventing and promulgating both asymmetric public-key cryptography, including its application to digital signatures, and a practical cryptographic key-exchange method." He is a member of the National Academy of Engineering and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame and the National Cybersecurity Hall of Fame.

Other work

Beyond cryptography, Hellman has worked on reducing nuclear risk, co-founding efforts promoting a rethinking of deterrence, and on the ethics of technology. With his wife Dorothie Hellman he wrote about applying lessons from personal relationships to international relations in A New Map for Relationships (2016).
