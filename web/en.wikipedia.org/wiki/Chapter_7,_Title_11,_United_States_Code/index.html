Chapter 7 of the United States Bankruptcy Code (Title 11 of the United States Code) governs the process of liquidation bankruptcy in the United States. An entity or individual that files under Chapter 7 turns over its non-exempt assets to a bankruptcy trustee, who sells the assets and distributes the proceeds to creditors. In exchange, an individual debtor receives a discharge of most unsecured debts.

Process

Upon filing, an automatic stay halts most collection efforts. The United States Trustee appoints a case trustee, who reviews the debtor's petition and schedules, convenes a meeting of creditors under section 341, and investigates the debtor's financial affairs. Most individual cases are "no-asset" cases in which all property is exempt or subject to liens, so unsecured creditors receive nothing.

Means test

The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 introduced a means test: individual debtors whose income exceeds their state's median and who can repay a portion of their debts may have their Chapter 7 case dismissed or converted to Chapter 13, which requires a repayment plan.

Exemptions

Debtors may claim exemptions under federal law or the law of their state (most states have opted out of the federal exemption scheme). Common exemptions protect a portion of home equity (homestead), a motor vehicle, household goods, tools of the trade, and retirement accounts.

Non-dischargeable debts

Certain obligations survive Chapter 7, including most taxes, child support and alimony, most student loans, debts incurred by fraud, and fines owed to government units.
