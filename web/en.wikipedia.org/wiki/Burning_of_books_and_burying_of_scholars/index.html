Burning of books and burying of scholars

[Image: Woodblock-style illustration of the Qin book burning, from a later Chinese edition]

The burning of books and burying of scholars (Chinese: 焚書坑儒, fénshū kēngrú) was a pair of episodes in 213–212 BC under Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of unified China, which became the defining symbol of Qin tyranny in later Chinese historiography. In 213 BC, on the proposal of chancellor Li Si, privately owned copies of historical records, poetry and philosophical writings were ordered burned; in 212 BC, according to the Shiji, more than 460 scholars were buried alive at the capital Xianyang.

The book burning

The immediate occasion was a court debate in which the scholar Chunyu Yue criticised the abolition of feudalism and urged a return to antiquity. Li Si responded that scholars used the past to slander the present and proposed that all histories other than Qin's own records, all copies of the Classic of Poetry and Classic of Documents, and all writings of the Hundred Schools held privately be surrendered and burned within thirty days, on pain of tattooing and hard labour. Works on medicine, divination and agriculture were exempt, and the imperial library kept official copies for court scholars. Those archive copies were themselves largely lost when Xiang Yu burned Xianyang in 206 BC — arguably the greater blow to the old texts.

The burying of scholars

The following year, two court masters of methods (fangshi) who had failed to produce an elixir of immortality slandered the emperor and fled. Enraged, Qin Shi Huang ordered an investigation of scholars in the capital; over 460 were found in violation and "buried" (keng — the word may mean executed and mass-buried, or in some interpretations buried alive). His heir Fusu protested and was exiled to the northern frontier. Modern scholars debate the scale and details — noting that the victims may have been largely fangshi rather than Confucians, and that Han-dynasty historians had reason to blacken the Qin — but the phrase "burning the books and burying the Confucians" has remained a byword for intellectual persecution in Chinese political discourse to the present day.
