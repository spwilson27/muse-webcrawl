Thirty pieces of silver

Thirty pieces of silver was the price for which Judas Iscariot betrayed Jesus, according to an account in the Gospel of Matthew in the New Testament. Before the Last Supper, Judas is said to have gone to the chief priests and agreed to hand over Jesus in exchange for 30 silver coins, later identifying him to the arresting party with a kiss in the Garden of Gethsemane.

Biblical account

Matthew 26:15 records Judas asking, "What are you willing to give me if I deliver him over to you?" and the priests counting out thirty pieces of silver. After Jesus' condemnation, Matthew 27 relates that Judas, overcome with remorse, returned the money to the priests and hanged himself. The priests, deeming the coins "blood money" that could not be returned to the temple treasury, used them to buy the potter's field as a burial place for strangers — a field thereafter called Akeldama, the "Field of Blood." Matthew presents these events as fulfilling prophecy, citing Zechariah and Jeremiah.

Value and identity of the coins

Scholars have debated which coins were meant: candidates include Tyrian shekels, Athenian tetradrachms, and Roman denarii equivalents. Thirty shekels was the price of a slave in Exodus 21:32, and the amount appears in Zechariah 11, where it is described with bitter irony as a "handsome price." In modern purchasing-power terms the sum has often been estimated as roughly several months' wages for a common labourer.

Cultural usage

The phrase "thirty pieces of silver" has become a byword for betrayal for personal gain, applied in politics, literature, and journalism to those seen as selling out a person, cause, or principle. The imagery recurs throughout Western art and literature, from medieval Passion plays to modern novels and song lyrics.
