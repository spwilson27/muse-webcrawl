Landsknecht

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Landsknechte (singular Landsknecht, "servant of the land") were German mercenary soldiers who formed the bulk of the Imperial army from the late fifteenth to the late sixteenth century. Raised first by Emperor Maximilian I from 1486 onward, they were specialists in the use of the pike and the halberd, modelled on the Swiss mercenaries they were intended to rival, and they fought in nearly every European war of the period, above all the Italian Wars between France and the Habsburgs.

Organisation and tactics

Landsknechte were recruited by enterprising colonels under contract (Bestallung) from the emperor or another employer, and organised into regiments of several thousand men subdivided into companies (Fähnlein) of about 400. In battle they formed deep pike squares (Gevierthaufen), stiffened by halberdiers, two-handed swordsmen (Doppelsöldner, who received double pay), and increasing numbers of arquebusiers. Their famous clashes with Swiss squares — the "push of pike" — were among the bloodiest infantry combats of the age.

Appearance and culture

The Landsknechte are famous for their extravagant dress: slashed doublets and hose revealing bright linings, huge berets and feathered hats, and flat cow-mouth shoes. Exempt by imperial privilege from sumptuary laws, they made flamboyance a badge of their trade. Their camps were followed by trains of women and servants (Tross), and their internal affairs were governed by an elaborate code administered by their own officers and provosts. They are also remembered for their marching songs, a body of Landsknechtlieder that survives to this day.

History

Landsknecht units fought at Bicocca (1522) and Pavia (1525), where Georg von Frundsberg's veterans helped destroy the French army. In May 1527, largely unpaid and partly Lutheran Landsknechte formed the mass of the Imperial army that mutinied and perpetrated the brutal Sack of Rome. As the sixteenth century closed, long wars in the Netherlands and the rise of newer infantry systems — Spanish tercios, Dutch and Swedish reforms — gradually displaced them, and the term faded from use during the seventeenth century.
