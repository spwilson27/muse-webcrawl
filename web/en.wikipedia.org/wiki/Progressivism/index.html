Progressivism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Progressivism is a political philosophy that holds that it is possible to improve human societies through political action, reform and the application of knowledge — science, technology, economic development and social organisation. As a movement, progressivism arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries (the Progressive Era in the United States) against the corruption and inequality of industrial capitalism, and the label has been revived in contemporary politics, especially in the US, for the centre-left to left of the Democratic Party and analogous currents elsewhere.

History

Progressive-Era reforms included antitrust enforcement, food and drug regulation, direct primaries and initiatives, women's suffrage, prohibition (a cautionary tale within the tradition) and the beginnings of conservation policy. The New Deal and Great Society extended the programme; late-20th-century progressivism absorbed civil rights, feminism, environmentalism and consumer protection, and its modern agenda typically includes universal healthcare, climate action, labour rights, campaign-finance reform and regulation of concentrated corporate power. The term's history also includes entanglements historians criticise — eugenics sympathies among some early progressives and imperial projects abroad — examined in the scholarly literature.
