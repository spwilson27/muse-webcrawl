A disciplinary institution is an organisation that trains and controls the bodies and conduct of its members through continuous surveillance, regimented schedules and systems of reward and punishment. The term is associated above all with Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (1975), in which prisons, schools, military barracks, hospitals, factories and monasteries are analysed as sharing a common technology of power.

Foucault's analysis

Foucault argued that between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, European societies shifted from sovereign power — exercised through public spectacle and punishment of the body — toward disciplinary power, which works quietly and continuously to produce self-regulating subjects. Disciplinary institutions partition space (cells, desks, wards), control activity through timetables, organise training in graded stages, and combine these in tactics that maximise useful force.

Mechanisms

Three instruments recur: hierarchical observation, architecture and organisation arranged so that inmates are perpetually visible, epitomised by Jeremy Bentham's panopticon; normalising judgement, small penalties and rewards that rank individuals against a norm; and the examination, which combines observation with judgement and turns each individual into a documented "case" in files and records.

Relation to other concepts

The notion overlaps with Erving Goffman's "total institution", though Foucault emphasised that disciplinary techniques spread far beyond closed establishments into society at large — the "carceral archipelago". Later theorists, including Gilles Deleuze, have argued that disciplinary institutions have partly given way to "societies of control" based on codes, modulation and continuous data monitoring.
