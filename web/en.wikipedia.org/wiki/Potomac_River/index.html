The Potomac River is a river in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States flowing from the Potomac Highlands of West Virginia to the Chesapeake Bay. It forms much of the border between Maryland and Washington, D.C., on one side and West Virginia and Virginia on the other, and flows past the capital city, giving it the nickname "the Nation's River".

Course

The river's North and South Branches rise in West Virginia and join near Cumberland, Maryland. From there the Potomac flows southeast through the Appalachian Mountains, descends the rapids and falls at Great Falls, and becomes a tidal estuary downstream of Washington, D.C., widening steadily until it meets Chesapeake Bay between Point Lookout, Maryland, and Smith Point, Virginia.

History

Indigenous peoples lived along the Potomac for thousands of years; the river's name derives from an Algonquian term recorded by early colonists. John Smith explored the river in 1608. The Potomac was a strategic artery during the American Civil War, and George Washington chose the site of the federal capital on its banks. The Chesapeake and Ohio Canal ran along its Maryland shore in the 19th century.

Ecology and water supply

The Potomac supplies drinking water to roughly five million people in the Washington metropolitan area. After severe pollution in the mid-20th century, water quality has improved markedly, and the river now supports recovering populations of American shad, bald eagles and other wildlife.
