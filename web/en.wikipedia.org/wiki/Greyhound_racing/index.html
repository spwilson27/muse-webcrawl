Greyhound racing

Greyhound racing is an organized, competitive sport in which greyhounds are raced around a track. There are two forms: track racing (normally around an oval track) and coursing, the latter of which is now banned in most countries. Track racing, in which greyhounds chase a mechanical lure, was invented in 1919 in the United States by Owen Patrick Smith and first introduced to the United Kingdom in 1926, where it quickly became immensely popular.

The race

Dogs are released from starting traps and chase an inanimate mechanical lure around an oval sand or grass track, typically over distances of 250 to 900 metres. Races are graded by the dogs' form, and betting — both at the track and off-course — has historically been central to the sport's economics. Dogs wear numbered jackets corresponding to their trap positions and are identified by muzzles and racing blankets.

History

Following its introduction at Belle Vue Stadium in Manchester in 1926, greyhound racing drew huge crowds in inter-war Britain; London's White City Stadium hosted the English Greyhound Derby, the sport's most prestigious race. The industry declined from the 1960s onward as betting shops were legalized and other leisure options expanded.

Animal welfare and decline

Greyhound racing has been controversial because of injuries, the euthanasia of dogs not suitable for racing, and the fate of dogs after retirement. Most U.S. states have banned the sport; Florida, once its center, prohibited it by constitutional amendment effective in 2021, leaving only a handful of active tracks in the country. The sport continues in the UK, Ireland, Australia, and New Zealand, though several governments have announced phase-outs. Adoption programs for retired racers are widespread.
