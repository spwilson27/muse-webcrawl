Solar power tower

A solar power tower, also known as a central tower power plant or heliostat power plant, is a type of solar furnace and concentrated solar power system using a tower to receive focused sunlight. An array of flat, movable mirrors called heliostats tracks the sun and concentrates its light onto a receiver at the top of the tower, where a heat-transfer fluid — water/steam, molten salt, or particles — is heated to drive a conventional turbine generator.

Operation

Heliostats focus sunlight with concentration ratios of several hundred, heating the receiver to 500–1,000 °C or more. Molten-salt designs (such as Gemasolar in Spain) can store hot salt in insulated tanks, enabling electricity generation hours after sunset — a key advantage over photovoltaics. Notable plants include Solar One and Solar Two (California demonstration projects), PS10 and PS20 in Spain, the Ivanpah Solar Power Facility in California (392 MW, using direct steam), and Crescent Dunes in Nevada.

Advantages and challenges

Power towers achieve higher temperatures and efficiencies than trough systems and integrate thermal storage relatively cheaply. Challenges include high capital cost, the complexity of thousands of tracking mirrors, water use for cooling in desert sites, and hazards to birds and aircraft glare reported at some facilities.
