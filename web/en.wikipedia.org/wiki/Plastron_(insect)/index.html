Plastron (insect)

An aquatic bug carrying a silvery plastron air film

A plastron is a type of physical gill found in some aquatic insects: a dense layer of fine, water-repellent hairs on the body surface that traps a permanent thin film of air. The film acts as a gas-exchange surface, allowing the insect to breathe underwater without surfacing.

Mechanism

The hairs of a plastron are extremely numerous – in the river bug Aphelocheirus there may be several million per square millimetre of surface – and are bent at their tips so that they resist wetting even under pressure. Oxygen dissolved in the surrounding water diffuses into the air film as the insect consumes the oxygen within it, while carbon dioxide diffuses out; because the film's total volume stays nearly constant, the "gill" does not collapse the way a simple bubble would. Insects with a true plastron can therefore remain submerged indefinitely, provided the water is well oxygenated.

Occurrence

Plastrons occur in several unrelated groups of aquatic insects, including certain bugs (such as Aphelocheirus), riffle beetles (Elmidae), and the eggs of some flies and other insects, where a plastron on the eggshell permits respiration when eggs are submerged. Analogous structures are found on some aquatic spiders.
