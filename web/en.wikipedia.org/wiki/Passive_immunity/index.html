Passive immunity

Passive immunity is the transfer of ready-made antibodies from one individual to another, providing immediate but temporary protection against a pathogen or toxin. Unlike active immunity, in which the recipient's own immune system is stimulated to produce antibodies (as after infection or vaccination), passive immunity confers no immunological memory and lasts only as long as the transferred antibodies persist, typically weeks to months.

Natural passive immunity

The best-known natural example is the transfer of maternal antibodies to a child. During pregnancy, IgG antibodies cross the placenta, protecting the newborn during the first months of life; after birth, the infant receives secretory IgA antibodies in breast milk, especially colostrum, which protect the gut and respiratory tract.

Artificial passive immunity

Artificial passive immunization is used medically when immediate protection is needed or when a patient cannot mount an adequate response. Examples include antivenoms and antitoxins (such as those for diphtheria, tetanus, and botulism), rabies immunoglobulin given after exposure, hepatitis B immunoglobulin, and monoclonal antibodies such as palivizumab against respiratory syncytial virus. Historically, convalescent serum from recovered patients was used against several diseases, and serum therapy earned Emil von Behring the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.
