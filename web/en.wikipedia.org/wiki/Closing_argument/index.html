Closing argument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A closing argument (summation) is the final opportunity for each party's counsel to address the fact-finder in a trial, after all evidence has been presented. Unlike the opening, closing is argument: counsel reviews the evidence, draws inferences, attacks the other side's inferences and credibility, explains the applicable standard of proof (beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases, often instructed again by the judge after argument), and asks for a verdict.

Order, rebuttal and misconduct

Order varies: commonly the prosecution argues first and may rebut after the defence, the plaintiff bearing the burden going first in civil cases — with the rebuttal's "last word" advantage a debated procedural gift. Appellate doctrine polices closing excess: misstating evidence, expressing personal belief ("vouching"), inflaming prejudice (appeals to community vengeance or racial and religious bias), and shifting the burden ("why didn't the defendant testify" — barred since Griffin v. California, 1965) all risk reversal, measured against the trial's overall fairness (Darden standard). Famous closings are literature as well as advocacy, anthologised from Erskine and Darrow to modern televised trials, and jury research suggests summations matter most where the evidence is close — which is where law most needs them to be fair.
