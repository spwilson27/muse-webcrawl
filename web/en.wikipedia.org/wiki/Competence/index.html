Competence

Competence is the set of demonstrable characteristics and skills that enable, and improve the efficiency of, performance of a job or task. The term first appeared in management literature in the early 1970s, notably in David McClelland's 1973 paper "Testing for competence rather than for intelligence", which argued that traditional aptitude tests poorly predicted job success compared with assessing job-relevant competencies directly.

Components

A competence is generally understood to combine knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours. Models of skill acquisition, such as the "four stages of competence" attributed to management training literature, describe progression from unconscious incompetence (not knowing that one lacks a skill), through conscious incompetence and conscious competence, to unconscious competence, in which a skill becomes automatic through practice.

In organisations

Competency frameworks map the behaviours required for effective performance in particular roles, and are used in recruitment, training needs analysis, appraisal and succession planning. Competency-based education similarly defines learning outcomes in terms of what learners can demonstrably do. Critics argue that competency models can become bureaucratic checklists that reward measurable behaviours over judgement and expertise.

Related senses

The word is used in several specialised fields: in law, competence refers to the authority of a court or the mental capacity of a person to stand trial or manage their affairs; in linguistics, following Noam Chomsky, linguistic competence is a speaker's internalised knowledge of their language, contrasted with performance; in biology, competence is the ability of a bacterial cell to take up extracellular DNA.
