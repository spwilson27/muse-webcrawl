Thunderstone (folklore)

A Neolithic stone axe of the kind once kept as a thunderbolt.

A thunderstone is a prehistoric stone artefact — most often a polished Neolithic axe head, a flint arrowhead or a fossil such as a belemnite — that in European and other folklore was believed to be the material remains of a thunderbolt, fallen from the sky during a storm. Such stones were preserved in houses, churches and byres as protection against lightning, fire and illness, and were credited with healing powers. The belief is one of the clearest cases of prehistoric objects being absorbed into the folklore of much later peoples.

Background

Ground stone axes often came to light when fields were ploughed or storms exposed them. Having no knowledge of their manufacture, people explained the smooth, heavy stones as objects produced by lightning striking the earth, reasoning that they were found where bolts had fallen. Classical authors already discuss "thunder stones": Pliny the Elder describes ceraunia, stones believed to fall from the sky, and Roman writers connected axe-shaped stones with the weapons of sky gods. In Norse and Germanic tradition the thunder god's weapon — Thor's hammer, or the thunderbolt of Donar — left such stones behind, and Scandinavian words for them (tordenkile, "thunder wedge") record the idea.

Protective and medicinal uses

Because lightning was said never to strike the same place twice, a thunderstone was thought to guard a building against storms: stones were built into chimneys, kept on window sills or carried in pockets. Scrapings of the stone, or water in which it had stood, were given as medicine for fevers, bites and diseases of cattle. Flint arrowheads — called "elf-shot" in Britain — were similarly believed to be the missiles of fairies or storms, blamed for sudden pains in people and livestock yet also used as charms to cure the very ills they caused.

Distribution

Beliefs of the same type occur far beyond Europe: polished axes are called thunderbolts in parts of Africa, South and East Asia and the Americas, and thunderstones feature in Japanese, Chinese and West African folklore. The near-universal connection made between these artefacts, storms and sky gods is often cited by folklorists as evidence that striking natural objects attract the same explanatory stories wherever they are found.
