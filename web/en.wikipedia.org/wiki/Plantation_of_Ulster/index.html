Plantation of Ulster

The Plantation of Ulster (Irish: Plandáil Uladh) was the organised colonisation (plantation) of Ulster — a province of Ireland — by people from Great Britain during the reign of King James I. Most of the settlers came from Scotland and England and were Protestant, while the native population was largely Irish Catholic. The plantation was established in the aftermath of the Nine Years' War and the Flight of the Earls in 1607, when the leading Gaelic lords of Ulster left for continental Europe and their lands were confiscated by the Crown.

Background

Ulster had been the most Gaelic and least anglicised of Ireland's provinces. The Nine Years' War (1594–1603), led by Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, ended with the Treaty of Mellifont, but in September 1607 O'Neill and Rory O'Donnell, Earl of Tyrconnell, sailed from Rathmullan with their families and followers — the Flight of the Earls. The government treated their departure as forfeiture, and the earls' extensive lands, together with other confiscated estates, amounted to some 500,000 acres across six counties: Armagh, Cavan, Coleraine (later Londonderry), Donegal, Fermanagh, and Tyrone.

The scheme

Under the scheme published in 1609, land was granted in lots of 1,000, 1,500, or 2,000 acres to three classes of grantee: English and Scottish "undertakers", who undertook to import Protestant tenants and build defences; "servitors", Crown officials and army veterans, who could take Irish tenants; and selected "deserving" native Irish, who received smaller grants. The City of London's guilds, through the Irish Society, took the county of Coleraine, rebuilt Derry as Londonderry, and developed Coleraine. Market towns were founded, Protestant churches built, and the settlers introduced new agricultural practices and the Scots and English languages.

Consequences

Although the official scheme envisaged removing most native inhabitants from undertaker estates, in practice many remained as labourers and tenants, and the plantation produced a patchwork of settlement concentrated in the valleys and towns. Resentment at dispossession fuelled the Irish Rebellion of 1641, which began in Ulster with attacks on settler communities. In the long term, the plantation created the Protestant, unionist population of Ulster whose descendants formed the majority in what became Northern Ireland in 1921; its legacy remains central to the contested history of Ireland.
