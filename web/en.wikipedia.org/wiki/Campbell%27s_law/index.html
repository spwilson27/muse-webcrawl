Campbell's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Campbell's law is an adage developed by social scientist Donald T. Campbell in 1976: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."

Explanation

Campbell developed the idea while studying programme evaluation. When an indicator — such as a test score, crime statistic, or waiting-time figure — becomes a target with stakes attached, people subject to it have strong incentives to manipulate the indicator rather than improve the underlying process: teachers teach narrowly to the test or even cheat; police reclassify crimes; hospitals reclassify patients to meet waiting-time targets. The indicator then ceases to measure what it was designed to measure, and the process it governs is distorted.

Related concepts

Campbell's law is closely related to Goodhart's law in economics ("When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), formulated by Charles Goodhart in 1975 in the context of monetary policy, and to the Cobra effect, in which a bounty intended to reduce a problem instead incentivises producing more of it.
