Moshav

A moshav (Hebrew: מוֹשָׁב, plural moshavim, lit. "village" or "settlement") is a type of Israeli town or settlement, in particular a type of cooperative agricultural community of individual farms pioneered by the Labour Zionist movement during the second wave of Jewish immigration (Aliyah) to Ottoman Palestine in the early 20th century. A resident or member of a moshav is called a moshavnik.

Structure

In a moshav, each family farms its own plot of land, which is typically owned by the state or the Jewish National Fund and leased to the family. Unlike the fully communal kibbutz, the moshav combines private enterprise with cooperation: purchasing, marketing, and heavy machinery are handled collectively, and members share mutual-aid obligations. The first moshav, Nahalal in the Jezreel Valley, was founded in 1921 and laid out in a distinctive circular plan by architect Richard Kauffmann, with farmsteads arranged around a central core of public buildings.

History

The moshav model was devised by members of the Second Aliyah who admired the collectivism of the kibbutz but wished to preserve family farming and private households. After the founding of Israel in 1948, hundreds of moshavim were established to settle new immigrants, especially from Middle Eastern and North African countries, in the moshavim of the development regions. A variant, the moshav shitufi (cooperative moshav), pools production while keeping households private.

Modern developments

From the 1980s onward, many moshavim suffered debt crises and underwent privatisation; agricultural land leasing rules were reformed, and many residents now work outside agriculture, with some moshavim becoming largely suburban communities. Today there are over 400 moshavim in Israel.
