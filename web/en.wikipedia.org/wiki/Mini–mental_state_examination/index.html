Mini–mental state examination

The mini–mental state examination (MMSE) or Folstein test is a 30-point questionnaire used extensively in clinical and research settings to measure cognitive impairment. It is commonly used in medicine and allied health to screen for dementia. It was introduced by Marshal Folstein and colleagues in 1975 as a practical, abbreviated form of the mental status examination. (This lowercase title is a redirect-style variant of the main article title Mini–Mental State Examination.)

Test content

The MMSE samples orientation to time and place, immediate registration and short-term recall of three words, attention and calculation (such as serial sevens), language (naming, repetition, following commands, reading and writing), and visuospatial ability (copying intersecting pentagons). Administration takes about five to ten minutes.

Interpretation

Scores range from 0 to 30. Common cut-offs treat 24–30 as normal cognition, 18–23 as mild cognitive impairment and 0–17 as severe impairment, though scores are influenced by age, education, language and sensory deficits. The test is useful for screening and tracking change over time but is not by itself diagnostic of dementia.

Alternatives

Alternatives and complements include the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination and the Mini-Cog. The MMSE is a copyrighted instrument, which has affected its use in some settings in favour of freely available tests.
