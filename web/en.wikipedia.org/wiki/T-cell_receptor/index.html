T-cell receptor

The T-cell receptor (TCR) is a protein complex found on the surface of T cells (T lymphocytes) that is responsible for recognizing fragments of antigen as peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. The binding between TCR and antigen peptides is of relatively low affinity and is degenerate: many TCRs recognize the same antigen peptide, and many antigen peptides are recognized by the same TCR.

Structure

The TCR is a disulfide-linked membrane-anchored heterodimer, consisting of two variable chains. In most T cells the receptor is composed of an alpha (α) chain and a beta (β) chain; a smaller population of T cells bears an alternative receptor formed by gamma (γ) and delta (δ) chains. Each chain consists of two extracellular domains (a variable and a constant region), a transmembrane segment, and a short cytoplasmic tail. The variable domains form the antigen-binding site, with hypervariable complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) that contact the peptide–MHC complex.

The TCR is associated with the invariable CD3 complex (CD3γ, CD3δ, CD3ε and the ζ chain), which transduces activation signals into the cell through immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs).

Generation of diversity

TCR diversity is generated during T-cell development in the thymus by V(D)J recombination of the TCR gene loci, together with junctional diversity from imprecise joining and nucleotide additions. This process can generate an enormous repertoire of distinct receptors, allowing the immune system to recognize a vast range of pathogens. Unlike B-cell receptors, TCRs do not undergo somatic hypermutation or class switching, and they are never secreted.

Signaling

When a TCR engages its cognate peptide–MHC, co-receptors CD4 or CD8 stabilize the interaction and recruit the kinase Lck, which phosphorylates CD3 ITAMs. This recruits ZAP-70 and triggers downstream pathways — including calcium flux, MAP kinase cascades, and activation of NFAT, AP-1 and NF-κB — leading to T-cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation. Co-stimulation (for example via CD28) is normally required for full activation.

Clinical relevance

Engineered TCRs are used in adoptive cell therapies for cancer, and TCR-like antibodies and soluble TCR constructs are in development. Abnormal TCR signaling contributes to autoimmune disease and T-cell leukemias and lymphomas.
