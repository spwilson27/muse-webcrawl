The Second International, also called the Socialist International, was an organization of socialist and labour parties formed in Paris on 14 July 1889, the centenary of the storming of the Bastille. It united socialist parties from some twenty countries, becoming the principal international body of the workers' movement until its collapse during the First World War. Its founding congresses established May Day (1 May) as an international workers' holiday and campaigned for the eight-hour working day.

History

Succeeding the First International of Karl Marx, the Second International excluded anarchists after its 1896 London congress, consolidating its identity around Marxist and social-democratic parties such as Germany's SPD — then the largest socialist party in the world — the French SFIO, and the British Labour Party. Leading figures included Karl Kautsky, Rosa Luxemburg, Jean Jaurès, Eduard Bernstein (whose "revisionism" provoked major debates), and Vladimir Lenin.

The International's resolutions against militarism, notably at Stuttgart (1907) and Basel (1912), pledged workers to resist an imperialist war. However, when war broke out in August 1914, most member parties voted for war credits and supported their national governments, shattering the organization. Its anti-war minority later formed the nucleus of the communist parties gathered in the Comintern (1919), while moderate parties regrouped in the Labour and Socialist International (1923).

Legacy

The Second International standardized much of the symbolism and ritual of the socialist movement — the red flag, "The Internationale" anthem, May Day, and International Women's Day (first proposed by Clara Zetkin in 1910). Its reformist wing laid the foundations of modern social democracy, while its collapse in 1914 remains a landmark case study in the tension between internationalism and nationalism.
