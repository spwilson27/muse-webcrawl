Aura (symptom)

An aura is a perceptual disturbance experienced by some people before or during a migraine headache or an epileptic seizure. Auras most commonly involve vision — flashing lights, zigzag patterns, shimmering or blind spots — but can also affect sensation, speech, smell or movement. Migraine aura typically develops gradually over five minutes or more and lasts under an hour; epileptic auras are usually brief and may serve as a warning that a larger seizure is coming.

Migraine aura

About a quarter to a third of people with migraine experience aura. The classic visual aura is a scintillating scotoma: a flickering, expanding arc or zigzag ("fortification spectrum") that drifts across the visual field, leaving temporary loss of vision in its wake. Sensory auras produce pins-and-needles that march slowly up a limb or one side of the face; speech auras cause transient difficulty finding words. Aura is thought to be caused by cortical spreading depression, a slow wave of neuronal excitation followed by suppression that moves across the cerebral cortex — at a speed matching the gradual march of symptoms. Migraine may occur with aura but without headache ("silent migraine" or acephalgic migraine).

Epileptic aura

In epilepsy, an aura is itself a small focal aware seizure arising from the area of brain where the seizure begins. Depending on the region involved, auras may consist of a rising sensation in the stomach, déjà vu, fear, unusual smells or tastes, or visual phenomena, and can precede a focal impaired-awareness or generalised tonic-clonic seizure by seconds.

Differential diagnosis

Because a first aura — especially in older people or with atypical features — can mimic transient ischaemic attack or stroke, new or changed aura symptoms warrant medical evaluation. Migraine with aura is associated with a modestly increased risk of ischaemic stroke, particularly in women who smoke or use oestrogen-containing contraception.
