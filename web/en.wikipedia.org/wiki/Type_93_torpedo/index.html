The Type 93 is a 610 mm (24 in) diameter torpedo developed and used by the Imperial Japanese Navy during the Second World War. It was the most advanced naval torpedo of its era. Allied naval historians gave it the nickname "Long Lance" after the war. In Japanese, it was sometimes called the sanso gyorai ("oxygen torpedo").

Development

The Type 93 was developed under Captain Shizuo Oyagi and Lieutenant Commander Hideo Shizuo in the early 1930s. Its revolutionary feature was the use of pure oxygen instead of compressed air as the oxidizer for its kerosene fuel. This eliminated most of the nitrogen that produced the tell-tale bubble wake of conventional torpedoes, granting the weapon a nearly wakeless run, a much larger warhead, far greater speed, and extraordinary range.

Performance

At its fastest setting the Type 93 could run at about 48–52 knots for 20,000–22,000 metres; at lower speeds its range extended to roughly 40,000 metres — far beyond that of any Allied torpedo. Its 490 kg warhead was also the heaviest carried by any surface-launched torpedo of the war. Japanese cruisers and destroyers were fitted with reloads and purpose-built launchers, and Japanese night-fighting doctrine was built around massed long-range torpedo attacks.

Combat history

Type 93s sank or crippled numerous Allied warships, particularly during the Solomon Islands campaign of 1942–43, including at the battles of Tassafaronga and Kolombangara. Allied officers initially refused to believe a torpedo could have such range and speed, often blaming submarines or mines instead. The oxygen system made the weapon dangerous to its own ships, however, and several Japanese cruisers were lost when their torpedo batteries detonated under fire.
