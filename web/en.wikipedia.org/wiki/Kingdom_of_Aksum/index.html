Kingdom of Aksum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The great carved stelae field at Aksum, granite towers carved like multi-storey buildings]

The great carved stelae field at Aksum, granite towers carved like multi-storey buildings

The Kingdom of Aksum (also Axum) was an ancient kingdom in the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands, flourishing from about the 1st to the 7th century AD as one of the great trading powers of the ancient world. From its capital at Aksum and its Red Sea port of Adulis, it traded ivory, gold, tortoiseshell and frankincense with Rome, Egypt, Arabia, Persia and India; it minted its own gold coinage, raised colossal carved stelae, and under King Ezana (c. 330s–360s) became one of the first states in the world to adopt Christianity. Aksumite tradition stands at the root of the Ethiopian Empire and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.

Trade empire and stelae

Aksum's wealth rested on highland agriculture and control of trade between the African interior and the Red Sea. Its kings — known from inscriptions and coins in Ge'ez, Greek and Sabaean — campaigned in Yemen and Nubia. The capital's royal tombs were marked by the largest single stone monuments of the ancient world: carved granite stelae imitating multi-storey palaces, the tallest standing some 21 metres, with a still larger one (about 33 metres) fallen and broken.

Christianity and decline

Ezana's conversion, guided by the Syrian Christian Frumentius — first bishop of Ethiopia — made Aksum a Christian state around the 330s–340s, commemorated by crosses replacing pagan symbols on its coins. In the 520s King Kaleb intervened in Yemen to protect Christians there. After the 7th century, the rise of Islam cut Aksum off from the Red Sea trade, the capital declined, and Ethiopian power moved south; the kingdom's memory survived in church tradition and the Kebra Nagast, which traces Ethiopian kingship to Solomon and the Queen of Sheba.
