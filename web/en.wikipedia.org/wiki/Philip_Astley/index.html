Philip Astley (8 January 1742 – 20 January 1814) was an English equestrian, circus owner and inventor, widely regarded as the father of the modern circus. A former cavalry sergeant-major, Astley established the format that circuses still follow: a circular performing ring combined with acrobats, jugglers, clowns and trained animals interspersed with equestrian displays.

Life and career

Astley was born in Newcastle-under-Lyme, the son of a cabinetmaker, and at seventeen joined Eliott's Light Dragoons, serving in Germany during the Seven Years' War. He proved an exceptional horseman and trick rider, and on leaving the army in 1766 set up a riding school at Lambeth, London, near Westminster Bridge, where he also performed feats of horsemanship.

In 1768–70 Astley formalised his performances at what became Astley's Amphitheatre. He discovered that galloping in a circle let centrifugal force help him balance on a moving horse, and standardised the ring at 42 feet (about 13 metres) in diameter — the size still used by equestrian circuses today. To fill intervals between riding acts he hired acrobats, tightrope walkers, jugglers and a clown, creating the variety structure of the modern circus.

Expansion and legacy

Astley took his company on tour across Britain and Europe, and in 1782 opened the first purpose-built circus building in Paris, the Cirque Olympique. He ultimately established some 18 circuses in cities across Europe and licensed imitators, while repeatedly rebuilding his London amphitheatre after fires. He died in Paris in 1814 and was buried at Père Lachaise Cemetery.

Though the word "circus" was popularised by his rival Charles Hughes, Astley's ring, format and business model defined the institution that spread worldwide in the nineteenth century.
