A soliloquy (from Latin solo "to oneself" + loquor "I talk", plural soliloquies) is a monologue addressed to oneself, delivered by a character alone on stage, in which the character's thoughts are spoken aloud so that the audience — but not other characters — hears them. Popular in drama from the Renaissance to the 18th century, the soliloquy gives direct access to a character's inner life, motives, and conflicts, unfiltered by dialogue with others.

Distinctions

A soliloquy differs from a monologue, which is any long speech by one character, often addressed to others, and from an aside, a brief remark to the audience made in the presence of other characters. In fiction, the interior monologue and stream-of-consciousness narration perform a comparable function.

History

Precursors appear in classical and medieval drama, but the soliloquy flourished in Elizabethan and Jacobean theatre. Shakespeare made it a signature device: Hamlet's seven soliloquies, including "To be, or not to be", Macbeth's "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow", and Richard III's opening "Now is the winter of our discontent" are among the most analysed speeches in literature. Restoration and 18th-century drama retained the convention, but realist theatre of the 19th and 20th centuries largely abandoned it in favour of subtext, though playwrights such as Eugene O'Neill revived extended interior speech, and the device persists in film, television ("breaking the fourth wall"), and modern drama.

Function

Soliloquies reveal motivation, advance the audience's understanding of plot, create irony when viewers know more than other characters, and invite intimacy between actor and audience. Because the convention suspends naturalism, modern productions often stage them with direct address or isolation lighting.
