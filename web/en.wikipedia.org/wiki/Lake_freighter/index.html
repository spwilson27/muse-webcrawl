Lake freighters, or lakers, are bulk carrier ships operating on the Great Lakes of North America. Over 200 metres in average length, the largest are over 300 metres, they are built to the maximum dimensions permitted by the locks of the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence Seaway system and spend their entire working lives in fresh water, which gives them unusually long service lives — many lakers sail for 50 years or more, and a few vessels from the early 20th century remain in service.

Design

The classic laker layout places the pilothouse and crew accommodation forward, engines aft, and a long unobstructed hold between, served by deck hatches. Most modern lakers are self-unloaders: cargo is dropped onto conveyor belts in the hold and discharged through a long pivoting boom at rates exceeding 10,000 tons per hour, allowing them to unload at ports with no shore equipment. Thirteen American "thousand-footers", built in the 1970s and 1980s for the Lake Superior iron ore trade, are too large to leave the upper lakes through the Welland Canal.

Trade

The principal cargo is taconite iron ore from Minnesota and Michigan mines to steel mills at the southern ends of Lakes Michigan and Erie; return and other cargoes include coal, limestone, grain, salt and cement. Ships lay up during the winter closure of the Soo Locks (roughly mid-January to late March).

History and losses

The type descends from the wooden steam barge R. J. Hackett (1869), the first vessel built expressly as a Great Lakes bulk freighter, and Alexander McDougall's "whalebacks" of the 1890s. The lakes' storms have claimed famous victims, including the SS Carl D. Bradley (1958) and the SS Edmund Fitzgerald, lost with all 29 crew on Lake Superior on 10 November 1975 — the largest ship ever lost on the lakes and the subject of Gordon Lightfoot's ballad "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald".
