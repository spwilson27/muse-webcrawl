Northern Cheyenne Exodus

The Nebraska Sandhills, on the route of the exodus

The Northern Cheyenne Exodus was an attempt by the Northern Cheyenne people to return to their northern homeland in Montana after being forcibly removed to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma). In September 1878, about 353 Cheyenne under chiefs Dull Knife and Little Wolf fled the reservation near Fort Reno and travelled more than 1,500 miles north, pursued by some 13,000 United States soldiers.

Background

After the Great Sioux War of 1876, in which the Northern Cheyenne had fought alongside the Lakota at the Battle of the Little Bighorn, the tribe surrendered in 1877 and was deported south. In Oklahoma they found inadequate food, malaria and starvation rations; many died, and their appeals to be returned north were refused.

The journey

The band slipped away on the night of 9–10 September 1878 and fought a series of running engagements through Kansas, including the Battle of Turkey Springs and fighting at Punished Woman's Fork, the last battle of the Indian Wars in Kansas. In Nebraska the band split: Little Wolf's group wintered in the Sandhills and reached Montana in spring 1879. Dull Knife's group, mostly old people, women and children, was captured and imprisoned at Fort Robinson, Nebraska.

Fort Robinson outbreak

When ordered back to Oklahoma, Dull Knife's people refused; confined without food, water or heat, they broke out on the night of 9 January 1879. Many were shot down in the snow; around 65 were killed. The survivors were eventually allowed to join Little Wolf in Montana, where the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation was established in 1884. The exodus became one of the best-known episodes of Native American resistance, retold in Mari Sandoz's Cheyenne Autumn and the 1964 film of the same name.
