Investment trust

An investment trust is a form of investment fund found mostly in the United Kingdom and Japan. Investment trusts are constituted as public limited companies and are therefore closed-ended on the stock exchange. The term is somewhat misleading, because investment trusts are not "trusts" in the legal sense but investment companies.

Structure

An investment trust raises a fixed amount of capital through an initial public offering, and its shares are then traded like any other listed company. Because the capital is fixed, the manager is not forced to buy or sell investments in response to investor inflows and outflows, and the trust may borrow money (gearing) to enhance returns. The trust is governed by a board of directors, independent of the external management company it appoints.

Discount and premium

Since the shares trade on an exchange, their price is set by supply and demand and may stand above (premium) or below (discount) the net asset value (NAV) per share. Most investment trusts trade at a discount most of the time; boards may buy back shares or issue new ones to manage the gap.

History

The first investment trust, the Foreign & Colonial Investment Trust, was founded in London in 1868 to invest in overseas government bonds and railways, giving middle-class investors access to diversified portfolios. The sector grew rapidly in Britain and was exported to the United States in the 1920s, where closed-ended "investment trusts" played a notorious role in the Wall Street Crash of 1929.

Split capital trusts

Some trusts are "split capital", issuing different classes of shares — such as zero dividend preference shares, income shares, and capital shares — which divide the trust's income and capital growth between different types of investor with different risk profiles.
