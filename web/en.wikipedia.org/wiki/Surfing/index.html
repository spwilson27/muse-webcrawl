Surfing

Surfing is a surface water sport in which an individual, a surfer, uses a board to ride on the forward section, or face, of a moving wave of water, which usually carries the surfer towards the shore. Waves suitable for surfing are primarily found on ocean shores, but can also be found as standing waves in the open ocean, in lakes, in rivers in the form of a tidal bore, or in wave pools.

History

Surfing originated with the pre-modern Polynesian cultures of the Pacific; it was central to ancient Hawaiian culture, where it was known as heʻe nalu and practised by chiefs and commoners alike on wooden boards. Captain James Cook's crews observed it in the late 18th century. In the early 20th century, Hawaiian surfers including George Freeth and Duke Kahanamoku — an Olympic swimming champion — popularised the sport in California and Australia. The foam-and-fibreglass boards of the post-war era, the shortboard revolution of the late 1960s, and professional tours from the 1970s made surfing a global industry. Surfing made its Olympic debut at the Tokyo 2020 Games.

Equipment and technique

Surfers ride boards of many shapes — shortboards, longboards, fish, and guns for big waves — typically with fins and a leash. The rider paddles to catch a wave, performs a "pop-up" to stand, and steers along the unbroken face, performing manoeuvres such as turns, cutbacks, aerials and tube rides. Competitive surfing is judged on the difficulty and execution of manoeuvres on the best waves ridden in a heat.

Culture

Surfing has spawned a distinctive subculture influencing music (surf rock), film, fashion and slang. Famous breaks include Pipeline and Waimea Bay in Hawaii, Jeffreys Bay in South Africa, Uluwatu in Bali, and Teahupoʻo in Tahiti. Risks include drowning, collisions, and marine animals; big-wave surfing on boards or jet-ski tow-ins pushes the sport's extremes.
