Empathogen

Empathogen

Drug class: Serotonin releasers / entactogens

Examples: MDMA, MDA, MBDB, methylone

Effects: Empathy, euphoria, prosocial feelings, mild stimulation

Related term: Entactogen

Empathogens, also known as entactogens, are a class of psychoactive drugs that produce distinctive emotional and prosocial effects, most notably feelings of empathy, emotional openness, and a sense of closeness with others. The prototype of the class is MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine), commonly known as ecstasy or molly.

Terminology

The term "empathogen" was coined in 1983–84 by Ralph Metzner from the Greek roots empathos ("feeling into") and -gen ("to produce"), describing drugs that "generate a state of empathy". The related term "entactogen" was proposed by David E. Nichols in 1986, from roots meaning "touching within", as an alternative intended to avoid negative associations and any implication of pathological states.

Effects

Empathogens characteristically increase feelings of empathy, love, and emotional communion with others, alongside euphoria, heightened sensory perception, and mild stimulant effects. Unlike classic psychedelics such as LSD, they typically produce comparatively little distortion of visual perception, and users generally remain lucid and oriented.

Mechanism of action

Most empathogens act primarily by promoting the release of the monoamine neurotransmitters serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, largely through reversal of their membrane transporters — especially the serotonin transporter (SERT). The strong serotonergic component is thought to underlie the distinctive prosocial and empathic effects.

Examples

Well-known empathogens include MDMA, MDA, MDEA, MBDB, and various substituted cathinones such as methylone. Many were first synthesised by Alexander Shulgin and documented in his book PiHKAL.

Therapeutic use

MDMA-assisted psychotherapy has been investigated for the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with late-stage clinical trials reporting significant symptom reductions. Risks of use include hyperthermia, dehydration, hyponatremia, and serotonin syndrome, particularly in uncontrolled settings.
