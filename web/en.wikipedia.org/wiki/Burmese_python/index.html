Burmese python

The Burmese python (Python bivittatus, formerly Python molurus bivittatus) is one of the largest species of snake in the world, native to a wide area of Southeast Asia including Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, southern China and Indonesia. Adults commonly reach 3–5 metres (10–16 ft), and exceptional specimens exceed 5.7 m. It is a non-venomous constrictor listed as Vulnerable in its native range, where it is hunted for its skin and for food and traditional medicine.

Description and biology

The body is heavy and patterned with brown blotches bordered in black on a lighter background; an arrow-shaped marking crowns the head. Burmese pythons are strong swimmers and semi-aquatic, favouring marshes, swamps, grasslands and river valleys. They kill prey — mammals and birds up to the size of deer and pigs — by constriction, and can survive for months between meals. Females lay clutches of up to 100 eggs and coil around them, generating heat by muscular shivering, a rare form of facultative endothermy among snakes.

Invasive species

The species is notorious as an invasive predator in Florida, where escaped and released pets established a breeding population in the Everglades, probably augmented by animals freed when Hurricane Andrew destroyed a breeding facility in 1992. Surveys have documented severe declines — in some studies over 90% — in populations of raccoons, opossums, marsh rabbits and other mammals in Everglades National Park, as well as predation on wading birds and even alligators. Florida now bans their import and personal possession, funds python-removal programmes and runs the annual Florida Python Challenge.
