The Sabaeans were an ancient South Arabian people who established the kingdom of Saba — the biblical Sheba — in the southwestern corner of the Arabian Peninsula, in what is now Yemen. From roughly the 8th century BC until AD 275 their kingdom was one of the great powers of ancient Arabia, grown rich on the trade in frankincense and myrrh carried by camel caravan to the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia.

The kingdom of Saba

Sabaean rulers initially bore the title mukarrib ("federator"), reflecting a federation of tribes, and later the title of king (malik). The capital was Marib, an oasis city made prosperous by the great Marib Dam, one of the engineering wonders of the ancient world, whose irrigation system watered an estimated 9,600 hectares and supported a large population. Sabaean inscriptions — more than ten thousand survive, written in the monumental musnad script — record building works, wars, laws and dedications, and include famous campaign reports such as that of the mukarrib Karib'il Watar (c. 685 BC), whom Assyrian records also mention. The Sabaeans founded colonies and trading posts, notably in northern Arabia and across the Red Sea in Ethiopia, where their influence contributed to the later kingdom of Aksum.

The Queen of Sheba

Saba is best known to later tradition through the story of the Queen of Sheba, who travelled to Jerusalem to test King Solomon with hard questions and presented him with gold, spices and precious stones (1 Kings 10). Ethiopian tradition, in the Kebra Nagast, makes her (as Makeda) the mother of Menelik I by Solomon and the ancestress of the Ethiopian imperial line. No contemporary Sabaean inscription names such a queen, and the visit, if historical at all, is generally placed in the 10th century BC, before the earliest firm evidence for the kingdom.

Decline

Saba came under pressure from rival South Arabian kingdoms — Qataban, Hadramawt and especially Himyar — and from changes in trade routes as maritime shipping through the Red Sea bypassed the overland incense caravans. The kingdom was finally absorbed by Himyar around AD 275. The breach of the Marib Dam, recorded in legend and in the Quran (Surah Saba), became a symbol of the civilisation's collapse and the dispersal of its tribes.
