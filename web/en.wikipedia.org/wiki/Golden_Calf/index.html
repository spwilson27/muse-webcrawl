Golden calf

According to the Bible, the golden calf (Hebrew: ʿēgel hazzāhāḇ) was a cult image made by the Israelites when Moses went up to Mount Sinai. In Hebrew, the episode is known as the sin of the calf (ḥēṭʾ haʿēgel). It is first mentioned in Exodus 32.

Biblical narrative

While Moses was on Mount Sinai receiving the Law, the people, believing he would not return, asked his brother Aaron to make them a god. Aaron collected their gold earrings, melted them down, and fashioned a molten calf, declaring a feast to the Lord. When Moses descended and saw the idolatry and revelry, he smashed the tablets of the Law, ground the calf to powder, scattered it on water, and made the Israelites drink it. The Levites, rallying to Moses, executed about three thousand of the offenders.

Other biblical calves

After the split of the united monarchy, Jeroboam I set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan as shrines for the northern kingdom of Israel (1 Kings 12), an act condemned by later biblical writers as the "sin of Jeroboam." Bull imagery was common in ancient Near Eastern religion, where the bull symbolised strength and fertility and was associated with gods such as El and Baal.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether the calf was intended to represent a foreign god, the God of Israel himself, or a pedestal for an invisible deity. In Jewish, Christian, and Islamic tradition the episode became a paradigmatic warning against idolatry; the Qur'an (Surah Ta-Ha) tells a similar story in which the calf is made by al-Samiri. The phrase "golden calf" is now used metaphorically for wealth or materialism worshipped as an idol.
