Andrea Mantegna (c. 1431 – 13 September 1506) was an Italian Renaissance painter, trained in Padua in the workshop of Francesco Squarcione, whose passion for classical antiquity shaped Mantegna's sculptural, archaeologically exact style. A master of linear perspective and dramatic foreshortening, he became court artist to the Gonzaga family of Mantua in 1460 and served them for the rest of his life. His brother-in-law was the Venetian painter Giovanni Bellini.

Early career

A prodigy, Mantegna joined the painters' guild at eleven and by seventeen had left Squarcione after litigation over his earnings. His early masterpieces include the frescoes of the Ovetari Chapel in Padua's Eremitani church (largely destroyed in 1944), the San Zeno Altarpiece in Verona, and the Agony in the Garden. His figures have the hard, carved quality of classical statuary, set in landscapes of rocky outcrops and Roman ruins.

Mantua and major works

As court painter to Ludovico III Gonzaga and his successors, Mantegna decorated the Camera dei Sposi (1465–1474) in the Palazzo Ducale, whose illusionistic ceiling — figures and putti seen from below around an open oculus — is one of the first true examples of di sotto in sù perspective. His nine-canvas Triumphs of Caesar (c. 1484–1506), a monumental Roman procession based on ancient sources, now hangs at Hampton Court. The Lamentation over the Dead Christ, viewed from the feet with uncompromising foreshortening, is among the most celebrated images of the Renaissance. Mantegna was also among the first Italian artists to make engravings a major medium.

Legacy

Mantegna's rigorously classical art influenced northern Italian painting and printmaking; Dürer copied his engravings, and his Mantuan court set a model for Renaissance patronage later continued by Isabella d'Este.
