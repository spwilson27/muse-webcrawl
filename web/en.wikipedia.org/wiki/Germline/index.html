Germline

In biology and genetics, the germline is the population of a multicellular organism's cells that pass their genetic material to the progeny. In animals, germline cells are the gamete-producing lineage — the cells that undergo meiosis to form eggs and sperm — as distinct from somatic cells, which make up the body and whose mutations are not inherited.

Segregation

In many animals the germline is set aside early in development. In mammals, primordial germ cells are specified in the early embryo and migrate to the developing gonads, where they become oogonia or spermatogonia. In other animals, such as Caenorhabditis elegans and Drosophila, germ plasm — specialised cytoplasm containing RNA and protein determinants — is inherited by the cells that will form the germline. Plants do not sequester a germline early; gametes arise late from somatic cells in flowers, so mutations accumulated in a branch can be passed to seeds.

Mutations and heredity

Only mutations occurring in the germline can be transmitted to offspring; these are called germline mutations, in contrast to somatic mutations, which affect only the individual (as in most cancers). Germline mutations are a major source of inherited genetic disease and of evolutionary variation. Because the germline is effectively immortal across generations, August Weismann's germ-plasm theory emphasised its continuity and the barrier between germline and soma.

Applications

The distinction is central to gene therapy: somatic gene therapy alters body cells of a patient, whereas germline gene editing modifies embryos or gametes and is heritable — and is prohibited or tightly restricted in most countries. In oncology, comparing a tumour's genome with the patient's germline genome distinguishes inherited variants from acquired ones.
