Royal Standard of Scotland

The Royal Standard of Scotland, also known as the Lion Rampant or the Royal Flag of Scotland, is the personal banner of arms of the monarch of Scotland. Its blazon is: Or, a lion rampant Gules armed and langued Azure within a double tressure flory-counter-flory of the second — a red lion rampant with blue claws and tongue on a gold field, within a red double border decorated with fleurs-de-lis. By law and custom the standard is the personal flag of the sovereign and its use by others is restricted; it is flown at the royal residences in Scotland — the Palace of Holyroodhouse, Balmoral Castle and others — when the King is in residence.

History

The lion rampant is traditionally said to have been adopted by William I ("the Lion") in the 12th century, though the earliest surviving evidence is the seal of his son Alexander II in 1222. The double tressure flory-counter-flory is traditionally associated with the "Auld Alliance" with France, whose royal arms bore fleurs-de-lis; it appears consistently on Scottish royal arms from the 13th century. The arms formed the basis of the Royal coat of arms of Scotland and, after the Union of the Crowns (1603) and the Acts of Union 1707, of the Scottish version of the royal arms of the United Kingdom, in which the Scottish quarter takes precedence.

Legal status and popular use

Unlike the Saltire (the national flag of Scotland, which anyone may fly), the Lion Rampant is legally restricted: an Act of the Parliament of Scotland of 1672 reserves it to the sovereign, and unauthorised use is an offence overseen by the Lord Lyon King of Arms. In practice the banner is nonetheless widely reproduced and sold as a patriotic emblem, notably by football and rugby supporters, a popular use the Lyon Court has generally tolerated while maintaining the formal restriction. A variant with the design of the royal banner is used by the heir apparent and by certain royal representatives in Scotland under warrant.
