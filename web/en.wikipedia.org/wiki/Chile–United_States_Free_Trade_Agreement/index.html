Chile–United States Free Trade Agreement

The agreement links the economies of Chile and the United States

The Chile–United States Free Trade Agreement is a trade agreement between the United States and Chile signed on June 6, 2003, in Miami, Florida, and entered into force on January 1, 2004. It was the first free trade agreement between the United States and a South American country, and the third U.S. free trade agreement overall after NAFTA and the U.S.–Jordan agreement.

History

Negotiations began in the 1990s; Chile had hoped to join NAFTA but negotiations with the United States proceeded bilaterally instead. Talks formally began in 2000 and concluded in December 2002. The agreement was approved by the U.S. House of Representatives on July 24, 2003, by a vote of 270–156, and by the Senate on July 31, 2003, by a vote of 66–31. President George W. Bush signed the implementing legislation on September 3, 2003.

Provisions

The agreement eliminated tariffs on most goods over a phase-in period of up to 12 years. Upon entry into force, more than 85 percent of bilateral trade in consumer and industrial products became duty-free. Agricultural tariffs were phased out over time, with all tariffs on U.S. agricultural exports to Chile eliminated by 2016. The agreement also covers services, investment, government procurement, intellectual property, labor, and the environment.

Effects

Two-way trade between the United States and Chile grew substantially after the agreement took effect, and Chile became a significant supplier of copper, fruit, wine, and salmon to the U.S. market, while U.S. exports of machinery, refined petroleum, and agricultural goods to Chile increased.
