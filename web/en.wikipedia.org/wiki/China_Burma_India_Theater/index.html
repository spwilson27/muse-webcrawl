China Burma India Theater (CBI) was the United States military designation for the China and Southeast Asian theatre of World War II, covering operations in China, Burma (Myanmar), and India from 1942 to 1945. Allied strategy in the theatre aimed to keep China in the war against Japan, supply Chinese forces, and reopen land communications to China after Japan conquered Burma and cut the Burma Road.

Command and strategy

Major General (later General) Joseph W. "Vinegar Joe" Stilwell, chief of staff to Generalissimo Chiang Kai-shek and commander of U.S. forces in the theatre, clashed repeatedly with Chiang over strategy and reform of the Chinese army. Stilwell was recalled in October 1944 after a crisis with Chiang and replaced by Albert C. Wedemeyer; the theatre was then split into the China Theater and the India-Burma Theater. Claire Chennault commanded the Fourteenth Air Force in China, successor to the American Volunteer Group (the "Flying Tigers").

The Hump and the Ledo Road

After the fall of Burma in 1942, the only supply route to China was the airlift over the eastern Himalaya — "the Hump" — flown by Air Transport Command from Assam to Kunming. The hazardous route cost hundreds of aircraft and over a thousand airmen, but tonnage grew to more than 70,000 tons per month by 1945. Simultaneously, American engineers and Chinese and Indian labourers built the Ledo Road from Assam into northern Burma to link with the old Burma Road; it was renamed the Stilwell Road in 1945.

Operations in Burma

Under British Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten's South East Asia Command, the British Fourteenth Army under William Slim fought the longest land campaign of the war against Japan, culminating in the victories of Imphal and Kohima (1944) and the reconquest of Burma in 1945. Special operations included Orde Wingate's Chindits, the American 5307th Composite Unit (Merrill's Marauders), and the OSS Detachment 101 working with Kachin guerrillas.

Legacy

Often called the "forgotten theatre," the CBI consumed enormous logistical effort for comparatively limited strategic effect, though it tied down substantial Japanese forces. Its shoulder patch — a blue shield with a white sun and star — became an enduring emblem for veterans' associations.
