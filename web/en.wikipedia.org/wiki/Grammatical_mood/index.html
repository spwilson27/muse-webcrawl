Grammatical mood

In linguistics, grammatical mood is a grammatical feature of verbs, used for signalling modality — the speaker's attitude toward what is being said, such as whether it is asserted as fact, commanded, wished for, or presented as hypothetical.

Grammatical mood
Realism moodsIndicative, declarative
Irrealis moodsSubjunctive, conditional, optative, imperative, jussive
Related toModality, tense, aspect

Realis and irrealis

Moods are commonly divided into realis moods, above all the indicative, which present situations as actual, and irrealis moods, which present them as not yet realised. The imperative expresses commands; the subjunctive appears in wishes, hypotheses and subordinate clauses in many languages; the optative expresses hopes and wishes and survives in languages such as Ancient Greek and Sanskrit; the conditional marks the consequence of a hypothetical condition.

Across languages

Languages vary greatly in how many moods they mark morphologically. English retains only remnants of the subjunctive (as in "if I were"), while Romance languages maintain rich subjunctive paradigms, and some languages grammaticalise evidentiality — the source of one's information — as part of their mood systems.
