A mudang (Korean: 무당; Hanja: 巫堂) is a shaman in Korean shamanism, or Musok. Most mudang are women; male shamans are traditionally called baksu. The mudang acts as an intermediary between human beings and the world of gods, ancestors and spirits, conducting rituals called gut (굿) to heal illness, expel misfortune, ensure prosperity, guide the souls of the dead, and bless homes, boats and businesses.

Becoming a mudang

Traditionally, a mudang is called to the profession through sinbyeong ("spirit sickness") — a period of illness, dreams and misfortune interpreted as possession by a spirit that resists ordinary cure until the sufferer accepts the calling and undergoes the initiation rite, the naerim-gut, in which the spirit descends into the novice. Such "charismatic" mudang (gangsin-mu) are distinguished from hereditary ritualists (seseup-mu), historically found in Korea's southern regions, who perform without trance or possession.

Role and rituals

In a gut, the mudang sings, dances, plays bells and drums, changes into the costumes of successive deities, and speaks in their voices, offering food, drink, money and entertainment to the spirits in return for their favour. Clients consult mudang about illness, exam results, marriages, business ventures and the fate of the dead. Despite centuries of suppression under the Confucian Joseon dynasty and stigmatisation in the modern era, the tradition persists in contemporary South Korea, and prominent mudang and their rituals — such as the great gut traditions of Seoul, Hwanghae and Jeju Island — are now recognised as Important Intangible Cultural Properties.
