Cheap fake

Slowing down real footage is a classic cheap-fake technique

A cheap fake is a piece of manipulated media — usually audio or video — created with inexpensive, conventional editing techniques rather than advanced artificial intelligence. The term contrasts with deepfakes, which use machine learning to synthesise realistic but fabricated content. Cheap fakes rely instead on methods such as slowing down or speeding up footage, re-contextualising genuine images, splicing clips, or presenting old material as if it were new.

The term was popularised by researchers Britt Paris and Joan Donovan in their 2019 Data & Society report Deepfakes and Cheap Fakes: The Manipulation of Audio and Visual Evidence, which argued that low-tech manipulation is at least as widespread and damaging as AI-generated media.

Techniques

  • Speed manipulation — slowing video or audio to make a speaker appear impaired, or speeding it up to make them appear agitated or dishonest.
  • Re-contextualisation — using a real photograph or clip but presenting it as depicting a different event, place or date.
  • Selective editing — splicing or trimming genuine footage so that statements appear to say something different from the original.
  • Mislabelling — attaching a false caption or headline to unaltered media.

Notable examples

In May 2019 a video of US House Speaker Nancy Pelosi was slowed to about 75% of its original speed, making her speech sound slurred; versions of the clip were viewed millions of times and shared by prominent political figures before platforms labelled or removed them. Researchers have also documented cheap fakes in elections and conflicts around the world, including miscaptioned photographs circulated during the COVID-19 pandemic and footage from video games presented as real combat video.

Detection and response

Because cheap fakes use real media, they can be harder to debunk than synthetic content: verification typically requires tracing the original source, reverse image search, and checking the context rather than analysing pixels for signs of fabrication. Social media platforms have adopted manipulated-media policies that label or remove misleading edits, and media-literacy advocates argue that provenance standards and rapid fact-checking are the most effective defences.
