Aquatic ecology is the branch of ecology concerned with the interactions of organisms and their environment in water, encompassing both freshwater systems (lakes, rivers, wetlands and groundwater) and marine systems (estuaries, coastal waters and the open ocean). It studies how energy and nutrients flow through aquatic food webs, how physical and chemical properties of water shape communities, and how human activity affects aquatic ecosystems.

Overview

Aquatic ecosystems differ fundamentally from terrestrial ones: water supports organisms buoyantly, limits light with depth, carries dissolved gases and nutrients, and connects habitats through currents and flow. Aquatic ecologists examine primary production by phytoplankton and macrophytes, grazing and predation, decomposition, and the cycling of carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus.

Freshwater and marine realms

Freshwater ecology overlaps with limnology, the study of inland waters, while marine ecology addresses salinity gradients, tides, upwelling and depth zonation from the sunlit epipelagic zone to the deep sea. Organisms are grouped by lifestyle: plankton drift in the water column, nekton swim actively, and benthos live on or in the substrate.

Human impacts

Eutrophication from nutrient runoff, habitat fragmentation by dams, overfishing, pollution and climate-driven warming and acidification are major pressures studied by aquatic ecologists, whose work informs conservation and water-resource management.
