Planned economy

[Image: a Soviet-era poster-style image of factories and five-year plan production charts]

A planned economy (command economy) is an economic system in which investment, production and the allocation of resources — and usually prices — are determined by a government plan rather than by markets. It was the system of the Soviet Union (from the First Five-Year Plan, 1928), of its post-1945 satellites, of Mao-era China, and survives in attenuated form in Cuba and North Korea.

Operation and critique

Planners (Gosplan in the USSR) set output targets for enterprises in physical and value terms, allocated inputs, and fixed prices and wages; consumer goods were residual claimants on resources after heavy industry, defence and investment. The system achieved rapid industrialisation and full employment at the cost of chronic shortages, poor quality and innovation, distorted information (managers hoarding and bargaining over targets) and vast waste. The theoretical critique — Mises's calculation argument (1920) and Friedrich Hayek's knowledge problem — held that without market prices planners cannot rationally allocate resources; the socialist calculation debate with Lange and Taylor proposed market-socialist remedies. After 1989–91 most planned economies transitioned (with varying shock) to market systems; modern echoes include indicative planning, industrial policy and speculation that big data might revive planning's feasibility.
