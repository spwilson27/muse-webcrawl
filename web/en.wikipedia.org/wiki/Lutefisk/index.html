Lutefisk

Lutefisk
Type: Dried fish dish
Place of origin: Scandinavia (Norway, Sweden)
Main ingredients: Stockfish or klipfisk, lye (potash), water

Lutefisk (Norwegian: lutefisk; Swedish: lutfisk) is dried whitefish, typically cod, treated with lye and then rehydrated to produce a gelatinous dish traditional in Norway, parts of Sweden, and among Scandinavian communities in North America. It is especially associated with Christmas meals.

Preparation

Dried stockfish is soaked in cold water for several days, then in a lye solution made from birch ash or caustic soda, and finally in fresh water again to remove the alkali. The treated fish swells and takes on a jelly-like consistency; it is then steamed or baked and served with melted butter, bacon, peas, or white sauce.

History

The dish's origins are obscure, with legends connecting it to Viking-age preservation methods. Lutefisk became a festive food in Scandinavia and was carried by emigrants to the Upper Midwest of the United States, where church suppers featuring lutefisk remain a cultural institution.

In popular culture

Lutefisk is often treated humorously as an acquired taste, featuring in jokes and songs about Scandinavian-American identity, while remaining a genuinely cherished holiday tradition for many families.
