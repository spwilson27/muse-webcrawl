An Allen charge is a supplemental instruction given by a trial judge to a deadlocked jury—often called a "hung jury"—urging the jurors to continue deliberating and to try to reach a unanimous verdict. Named for Allen v. United States (1896), in which the Supreme Court approved such an instruction, it is nicknamed the "dynamite charge" for its supposed ability to blast loose a logjam of disagreement.

Content

The traditional charge instructs jurors in the minority to reconsider their views in light of the majority's position, reminding them that the case must at some time be decided, that no better jury is likely to be selected, and that jurors should not surrender conscientiously held convictions merely to return a verdict. Modern, softened versions—sometimes called "modified Allen charges"—direct the same admonition to all jurors, majority and minority alike, emphasize that no juror should give up an honest belief, and stress that the burden of proof remains unchanged.

Criticism and restrictions

Critics argue the classic charge is inherently coercive, pressuring minority jurors—often holdouts for acquittal—to capitulate, and several states, including Arizona, California, and Montana, have banned or replaced the traditional form with neutral model instructions. Federal courts generally retain modified versions, and appellate courts review the circumstances under which a charge was given, including how long the jury had deliberated and how soon a verdict followed. The instruction presupposes a properly selected jury, chosen through voir dire and able to withstand challenges for cause.
