Bog body

A bog body is a human cadaver that has been naturally mummified in a peat bog. The acidic, cold, oxygen-poor water of sphagnum bogs tans the skin and hair and preserves soft tissue — including skin, internal organs and sometimes stomach contents — while often dissolving bone. Bog bodies have been found across northern Europe, especially in Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Ireland and Britain, and many date to the Iron Age.

Famous examples

The best-known bog bodies include Tollund Man and Grauballe Man in Denmark, Lindow Man in England, Old Croghan Man and Clonycavan Man in Ireland, and the Yde Girl in the Netherlands. Many show signs of violent death — hanging, throat-cutting, bludgeoning — leading scholars such as P. V. Glob to interpret them as human sacrifices, possibly connected with fertility rites described by Tacitus in his Germania.

Preservation and study

Peat's sphagnan compounds block bacterial growth and tan collagen like leather, while calcium leaches from bones, leaving them soft and rubbery. Modern CT scanning, isotope analysis and ancient DNA work have reconstructed the diets, health, hairstyles and last meals of the bog dead; several had eaten gruel containing weed seeds hours before death. Many bog bodies were damaged or lost after discovery by peat cutters before their significance was understood.
