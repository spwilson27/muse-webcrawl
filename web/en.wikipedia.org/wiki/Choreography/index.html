Choreography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Choreography is the art or practice of designing sequences of movements of the body, usually in dance, in which motion, form, or both are specified. A choreographer is one who creates dances by practising this art; choreography is also used more broadly for staged movement in theatre, film, musicals, ice skating, gymnastics, and even synchronised swimming and marching arts.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Greek khoreia (dance) and graphein (to write) — literally, "dance-writing". It originally referred to systems of dance notation, and treatises such as Thoinot Arbeau's Orchésographie (1589) recorded court dances for posterity. In the 20th century the term shifted to mean the composition of dance itself.

Techniques

Choreographers work with elements including space, time, energy, shape, and relationship between dancers. Methods range from setting fixed steps to structured improvisation and task-based processes in which dancers generate material. Influential approaches include the classical ballet vocabulary shaped by Marius Petipa; the modern-dance innovations of Martha Graham, Merce Cunningham (who used chance procedures), and Pina Bausch's Tanztheater; and contemporary practices drawing on release technique, contact improvisation, and hip-hop.

Notation and technology

Because choreography is ephemeral, systems such as Labanotation and Benesh Movement Notation record dances in symbols for preservation and copyright. Video is now the most common record. Motion-capture and digital tools are also used in creation and archiving.

Copyright

In many jurisdictions choreographic works are protected by copyright once fixed in a tangible medium, though short routines and individual steps generally are not. Disputes over dance moves in video games have tested the boundaries of this protection.
