Occupation of France

The Occupation of France refers principally to the military occupation of French territory by Nazi Germany during the Second World War, from the armistice of June 1940 until the liberation in 1944. Under the armistice, Germany directly occupied northern and western France, including Paris and the Atlantic coast, while the south — the zone libre — was administered by the collaborationist Vichy government of Marshal Philippe Pétain until Germany occupied it too in November 1942. The article also serves as an overview page for earlier occupations of France, including the Allied occupation after the Napoleonic Wars (1815–1818) and the German occupation of north-eastern France during the First World War.

Background

German forces invaded France in May 1940; Paris fell on 14 June, and the armistice was signed at Compiègne on 22 June. Some 1.6 million French soldiers were taken prisoner, and millions of civilians fled south in the exode. Alsace and Moselle were effectively annexed to the Reich; the Nord and Pas-de-Calais were attached to the military administration in Brussels; an Italian occupation zone was created in the south-east.

Life under occupation

France paid crippling occupation costs and supplied forced labour to Germany under the Service du travail obligatoire (1943), which drove many young men to join the Resistance. Food rationing, curfews and requisitioning shaped daily life. The Vichy regime collaborated in the persecution of Jews: of roughly 76,000 Jews deported from France, few survived; the Vel' d'Hiv roundup of July 1942, carried out by French police in Paris, became a symbol of that complicity. Resistance networks grew from 1942 and unified under Jean Moulin's National Council of the Resistance in 1943.

Liberation

After the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944, Allied forces and the French Forces of the Interior liberated most of France by the end of the year; Paris was freed on 25 August 1944. The occupation years — remembered in France as les années noires, "the dark years" — left deep marks on French politics and memory, from the épuration (purge of collaborators) to the founding myths of the post-war Republic.
