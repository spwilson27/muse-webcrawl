Great Flood (China)

The Great Flood of Gun-Yu mythology is a central flood narrative of ancient China, set in the era of the legendary sage-king Yao. In the myth, a catastrophic inundation of the Yellow River and other waters threatened civilization for decades, until it was finally controlled through the efforts of Yu the Great, who succeeded where his father Gun had failed.

Gun's failure and Yu's success

According to the tradition preserved in the Book of Documents and later texts, Yao appointed Gun to control the flood; Gun tried to dam the waters using Xirang, a miraculous self-expanding soil stolen from the gods, but after nine years of failure he was executed by Emperor Shun. His son Yu took up the task, spending thirteen years dredging channels and guiding the waters to the sea — famously passing his own home three times without entering. Yu's success legitimized him as founder of the semi-legendary Xia dynasty. Geological studies published in 2016 proposed that a real outburst flood from a landslide-dammed lake at Jishi Gorge around 1920 BCE may underlie the legend.
