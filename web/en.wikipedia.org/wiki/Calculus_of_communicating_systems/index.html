Calculus of communicating systems

The calculus of communicating systems (CCS) is a process calculus introduced by Robin Milner around 1980 in his book A Calculus of Communicating Systems. It provides a mathematical framework for describing and reasoning about concurrent systems whose components communicate by message passing.

Overview

In CCS, primitive expressions are actions — inputs, outputs and the silent action τ — from which processes are built using operators for prefixing, choice, parallel composition, restriction and relabelling. Two processes run in parallel can synchronize on complementary input and output actions, producing a silent internal step.

Semantics and equivalence

CCS is given an operational semantics via labelled transition systems. Milner's notion of bisimulation — processes that can match each other's behaviour step for step — became one of the most influential equivalence concepts in concurrency theory, with variants such as weak bisimulation that abstract from silent actions.

Influence

CCS laid the groundwork for later process calculi, including the π-calculus (which adds mobility of channel names), the ambient calculus, and applied formalisms used to verify communication protocols and concurrent software.
