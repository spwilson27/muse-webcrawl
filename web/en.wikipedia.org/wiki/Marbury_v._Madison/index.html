Marbury v. Madison

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that established the principle of judicial review — the power of federal courts to declare legislative and executive acts unconstitutional. The unanimous opinion, written by Chief Justice John Marshall, arose from the Federalist–Republican struggle over "midnight judges" appointed in the last days of John Adams's presidency; William Marbury sued Secretary of State James Madison for his undelivered commission as a justice of the peace.

Reasoning and legacy

Marshall held that Marbury was entitled to his commission and that a remedy should exist — but that the statute (Judiciary Act of 1789) authorising the Court to issue the writ directly exceeded Congress's power under Article III, and so must be disregarded: "a law repugnant to the Constitution is void", and it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is". The decision at once avoided a confrontation the Court would have lost and claimed the power that made it a co-equal constitutional interpreter. Judicial review existed in embryo before (state precedents, Federalist No. 78); Marbury constitutionalised it, and it was not used again against a federal statute until Dred Scott (1857). Every subsequent debate about judicial supremacy versus departmentalism starts here.
