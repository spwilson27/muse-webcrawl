Jean Dubuffet

Sculpture by Jean Dubuffet in his characteristic black-and-white style

Jean Philippe Arthur Dubuffet (31 July 1901 – 12 May 1985) was a French painter and sculptor, founder of the art brut ("raw art") movement, which championed art made outside official culture — by prisoners, psychiatric patients, children, and self-taught creators.

Life

Born in Le Havre to a wine-merchant family, Dubuffet studied painting in Paris and briefly attended the Académie Julian before abandoning art for the family wine business. He returned to painting full-time only in 1942. After the war he exhibited deliberately crude, thickly impastoed portraits and landscapes that rejected academic beauty, declaring a preference for "art that does not know its name."

Art brut and later work

In 1945 Dubuffet began collecting outsider art; he founded the Compagnie de l'Art Brut in 1948 with André Breton and others, and his collection — eventually over 5,000 works — was donated to the city of Lausanne, opening as the Collection de l'Art Brut in 1976. His own painting moved through phases: earthy hautes pâtes, the black-and-white cellular Hourloupe style (from 1962), which he expanded into monumental painted sculptures and buildings such as the Closerie Falbala at Périgny, and the graffiti-like Théâtres de mémoire.

Legacy

Dubuffet's assault on "asphyxiating culture" influenced the CoBrA group, Art Informel, and later generations of artists, while his advocacy permanently established outsider art as a recognized field. His works are held by major museums worldwide, and the Fondation Dubuffet in Paris preserves his studio and archives.
