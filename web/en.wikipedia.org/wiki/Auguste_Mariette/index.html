Auguste Mariette

Auguste Mariette (11 February 1821 – 18 January 1881), born François Auguste Ferdinand Mariette, was a French archaeologist and a founder of modern Egyptology. Working in Egypt for most of his career, he discovered the Serapeum of Saqqara, directed excavations at dozens of sites, founded the Egyptian Antiquities Service and the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, and worked to keep Egypt's antiquities in Egypt rather than seeing them removed to European collections.

Early life

Mariette was born in Boulogne-sur-Mer and largely taught himself ancient Egyptian after being inspired by a cousin's involvement with the papers of Jean-François Champollion, the decipherer of hieroglyphs. He obtained a post at the Louvre's Egyptian department and in 1850 was sent to Egypt to acquire Coptic manuscripts for the museum.

Discoveries in Egypt

Distracted from his manuscript mission, Mariette noticed a sphinx at Saqqara recalling the avenue described by the ancient geographer Strabo, and excavated to reveal the Serapeum, the burial place of the Apis bulls — one of the great archaeological discoveries of the century. His subsequent excavations ranged over more than thirty sites, including Giza, Abydos, Thebes and Elephantine, uncovering thousands of objects.

Antiquities Service and museum

In 1858 the Egyptian ruler Sa'id Pasha appointed Mariette director of a new Antiquities Service, giving him a monopoly on excavation and control over exports of antiquities. He founded the museum at Bulaq in Cairo, the ancestor of the Egyptian Museum, and remained in Egypt for the rest of his life, also writing the scenario for Verdi's opera Aida. He died in Cairo in 1881 and was buried in a sarcophagus in the museum garden. Not to be confused with the architect Auguste de Montferrand.
