Shortening is any fat that is solid at room temperature and used to make crumbly pastry and other foods. The term comes from the fat's effect of "shortening" gluten strands in dough, inhibiting the formation of long, elastic protein networks and thereby producing a tender, flaky or mealy texture — as in shortbread and shortcrust pastry. In modern usage, "shortening" usually refers to hydrogenated vegetable oils, of which the American brand Crisco, introduced in 1911, is the best-known example.

History

Lard was the principal shortening in Western cooking for centuries, alongside butter. In 1901 the German chemist Wilhelm Normann patented the hydrogenation of liquid oils; Procter & Gamble applied the process to cottonseed oil and launched Crisco in 1911, marketing it as a cleaner, cheaper, purely vegetable alternative to lard and butter. Vegetable shortening became a pantry staple in North America, especially for pie crusts, biscuits and deep frying.

Production

Traditional shortenings were made by partially hydrogenating vegetable oil, which creates a semi-solid fat with good plasticity and shelf life — but also generates trans fatty acids. Following evidence linking trans fats to cardiovascular disease, regulators restricted them (the US FDA revoked the "generally recognized as safe" status of partially hydrogenated oils in 2015, with compliance by 2018–2021). Modern shortenings are instead made from fully hydrogenated oils blended or interesterified with liquid oils, or from palm oil.

Use in baking

Shortening is 100% fat (butter is about 80–85% fat with water), so it produces especially tender, flaky results and tolerates handling well; it also has a neutral flavour, higher smoke point for frying, and gives frostings greater stability than butter. Bakers often combine butter (for flavour) with shortening (for texture). Solid shortenings are measured by weight or by packing into a cup, and are usually cut or rubbed into flour.

Related fats

The category overlaps with lard, butter, margarine, suet and ghee; in British usage "shortening" sometimes denotes baking fats generally, sold under brands such as Trex or Cookeen.
