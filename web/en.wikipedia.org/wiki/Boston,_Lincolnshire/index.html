Boston is a market town and inland port in Lincolnshire, England, on the River Witham near its mouth in the Wash. In the Middle Ages it was one of England's most important ports, trading wool with the Hanseatic League. It is the namesake of Boston, Massachusetts.

History

Boston grew around the trade of the 12th and 13th centuries, ranking among the richest ports in the kingdom from its wool exports. Its decline followed the silting of the river and the shift of trade to other ports. In 1607 a group of Puritan separatists from the area — later known as the Pilgrim Fathers — attempted to flee religious persecution by sailing to the Netherlands; they were betrayed and briefly imprisoned in Boston's Guildhall. Emigrants from the town later helped found Boston in New England in 1630.

Landmarks

The parish church of St Botolph is famed for its 272-foot tower, the "Boston Stump," one of the tallest parish church towers in England and a landmark visible across the flat Fens. The Maud Foster Windmill is among the tallest working windmills in the country. The Guildhall, dating from the 1390s, houses a museum.

Economy and culture

The modern port handles steel, timber, and containers. The surrounding Fens are among the UK's most productive agricultural areas. The town's 2016 referendum vote to leave the European Union, at 75.6%, was the highest in the country, reflecting the significance of migration in local debate.
