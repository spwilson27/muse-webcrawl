Magnetoresistance

Discovered1856 (William Thomson, Lord Kelvin)
VariantsAMR, GMR, TMR, CMR, EMR
Key applicationHard-disk read heads, magnetic sensors

Magnetoresistance is the tendency of a material — especially a metal or semiconductor — to change its electrical resistance in response to an externally applied magnetic field. It was first observed by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) in 1856, who found that the resistance of iron changed by a few percent depending on whether current flowed along or across the magnetisation — the effect now called anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR).

Giant and tunnelling magnetoresistance

In 1988 Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg independently discovered giant magnetoresistance (GMR) in multilayers of ferromagnetic and non-magnetic metals a few nanometres thick: resistance depends strongly on whether the magnetisations of adjacent layers are parallel or antiparallel, because electrons of one spin orientation scatter far more than the other. GMR earned the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics and was rapidly commercialised in the read heads of hard disk drives, enabling the dramatic growth in storage densities of the 1990s–2000s. In tunnelling magnetoresistance (TMR) the layers are separated by a thin insulator across which electrons tunnel; it yields still larger effects at room temperature and is used in MRAM memory and modern sensors.

Other forms

Colossal magnetoresistance (CMR), found in certain manganese perovskite oxides, involves resistance changes of many orders of magnitude near a metal–insulator transition, though typically at low temperature and high field. Extraordinary magnetoresistance (EMR) arises from the geometry of a semiconductor–metal hybrid. Ordinary Lorentz-force magnetoresistance in semiconductors is used in compass and position sensors, while magnetoresistive sensors broadly serve in automotive systems, industrial automation and current measurement.
