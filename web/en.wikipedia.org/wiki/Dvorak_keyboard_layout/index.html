The Dvorak keyboard layout is a keyboard layout for English patented in 1936 by August Dvorak, an educational psychologist and professor at the University of Washington in Seattle, and his brother-in-law William Dealey. It was designed to increase typing speed and reduce errors and fatigue compared with the dominant QWERTY layout.

Design

Dvorak's principles placed the most common letters and letter combinations on the home row, where the fingers rest: about 70% of English keystrokes fall on the home row in Dvorak, versus roughly a third in QWERTY. All vowels sit under the left hand's home-row fingers and the most common consonants under the right, encouraging hand alternation, and the least common letters are placed on the hardest-to-reach rows.

Adoption and debate

Despite advocacy by Dvorak and some impressive typing-contest results, the layout never displaced QWERTY, whose installed base and training infrastructure created powerful lock-in. The Dvorak–QWERTY contest became a famous case study in economics and the history of technology: a widely cited 1990 article by Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis questioned the evidence for Dvorak's superiority and the story that QWERTY was designed to slow typists down, sparking a long-running debate about path dependence.

Dvorak is included as an optional layout in all major operating systems, and variants exist for one-handed typing and for programmers. Barbara Blackburn, once recognised as the world's fastest typist, used Dvorak.
