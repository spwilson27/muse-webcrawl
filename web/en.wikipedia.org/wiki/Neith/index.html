Neith (Egyptian: Nt) was an ancient Egyptian deity, one of the earliest attested goddesses, whose cult centred on the city of Sais in the western Nile Delta. She was a goddess of war and hunting, of weaving, and in later theology a creator goddess and mother of the sun god Ra.

Iconography and roles

Neith's emblem, known from the Predynastic period, is a shield with crossed arrows, also interpreted as a weaving shuttle; she wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt. As a funerary goddess she was one of the four goddesses — with Isis, Nephthys and Serqet — who guarded the canopic shrine and coffin. Herodotus compared her with the Greek Athena, who likewise combined war and weaving. At Esna, in Greco-Roman theology, Neith was a primordial creator who emerged from the waters of Nun and fashioned the world, sometimes described with androgynous traits.

Festival

Herodotus also describes the great Festival of Lamps at Sais, when lamps were burned all night around the temple and throughout Egypt in her honour. Her temple at Sais, largely destroyed, bore the famous inscription reported by Plutarch: "I am all that has been, and is, and shall be, and my veil no mortal has yet uncovered."
