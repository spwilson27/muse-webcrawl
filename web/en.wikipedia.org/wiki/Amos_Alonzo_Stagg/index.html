Amos Alonzo Stagg

Amos Alonzo Stagg (August 16, 1862 – March 17, 1965) was an American athlete and pioneering college coach in multiple sports, most famously American football. One of the founding figures of the game, Stagg played end on Walter Camp's unbeaten Yale teams, was named to the first All-America team in 1889, and then coached for an extraordinary seven decades, remaining an innovator into his nineties. He is credited with — among many innovations — elements of the modern huddle, the forward-pass-friendly T-formation with the man-in-motion, the onside kick, numbered jerseys, and the tackling dummy.

Career

Stagg entered Yale as a divinity student and starred in both football and baseball, declining a professional baseball contract. He helped found the YMCA Training School programme at Springfield, Massachusetts, where in 1891 he played in the first public basketball game, under James Naismith, whom he had recruited to Springfield. In 1892 the new University of Chicago hired him as its first athletic director and football coach; his Maroons won seven Big Ten titles and were national champions in 1905 and 1913. Forced to retire by Chicago at age 70 in 1932, he simply moved west to the College of the Pacific in Stockton, California, coaching there until 1946 and later assisting his son at Susquehanna and serving as a kicking coach at Stockton College until he was 98.

Legacy

Stagg died in 1965 at the age of 102. Uniquely, he was inducted into the College Football Hall of Fame both as a player and as a coach, and into the Basketball Hall of Fame as a contributor. The Amos Alonzo Stagg Award is given annually for service to college football, the NCAA Division III football championship game is the Stagg Bowl, and stadiums and a Chicago high school bear his name.
