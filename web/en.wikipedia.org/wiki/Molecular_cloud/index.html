A molecular cloud, sometimes called a stellar nursery, is a type of interstellar cloud dense and cold enough to allow molecules to form, principally molecular hydrogen (H2). Molecular clouds are the birthplaces of stars. In contrast to the diffuse atomic hydrogen filling most of the interstellar medium, molecular clouds have densities of hundreds to millions of particles per cubic centimetre and temperatures of only about 10–30 kelvin.

Properties

The most massive examples, giant molecular clouds (GMCs), span tens of parsecs, contain 104–106 solar masses, and dominate the molecular mass of galaxies like the Milky Way, which hosts several thousand of them. Smaller examples include dark nebulae and compact, dense Bok globules. Because H2 emits almost no light at cloud temperatures, astronomers trace molecular gas mainly through carbon monoxide (CO), the second most abundant molecule, whose millimetre-wave rotational lines (first detected in 1970) map the clouds, supplemented by dust extinction and far-infrared emission.

The "CO-to-H2 conversion factor" (XCO) translates observed CO luminosity into molecular mass. In the Milky Way, molecular gas totals roughly a billion solar masses, concentrated in the spiral arms and the galactic centre.

Star formation

Within molecular clouds, turbulent, magnetised gas forms filaments and dense cores. Where a core's gravity overcomes thermal, turbulent and magnetic support — roughly, where it exceeds the Jeans mass — it collapses to form a protostar. Only a few percent of a cloud's mass is converted into stars before feedback from massive stars (radiation, winds and supernovae) disperses the cloud, typically within 10–30 million years. Nearby star-forming clouds include the Taurus, ρ Ophiuchi, Chamaeleon and Orion complexes; the Orion Molecular Cloud, about 1,300 light-years away, hosts the Orion Nebula, the nearest region of massive star formation to Earth.

Chemistry

Despite their low temperatures, molecular clouds are chemically rich: radio and infrared astronomy have identified well over 200 interstellar and circumstellar molecules, including water, ammonia, methanol, complex organic molecules and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — raising interest in clouds as a source of prebiotic material delivered to young planetary systems.
