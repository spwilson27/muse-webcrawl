Flak

Flak originally referred to anti-aircraft artillery fire, derived from the German Flugabwehrkanone ("aircraft defence cannon"), in turn derived from Fliegerabwehrkanone. In English, "flak" became a generic term for ground anti-aircraft fire during the Second World War, and the bursts of exploding shells seen around bomber formations.

History

Anti-aircraft artillery developed rapidly during the First World War as aircraft became weapons of war. In the Second World War, Germany fielded the famous 8.8 cm FlaK gun — designed as an anti-aircraft weapon but equally famous in the anti-tank role — while the Allies developed proximity-fuzed shells, radar-directed fire and guns such as the Swedish-designed Bofors 40 mm, used by both sides of the conflict.

Usage

Flak defences combined heavy guns firing time-fuzed high-explosive shells to high altitude with light automatic cannon for low-flying attackers. The black puffs of shell bursts were iconic in aircrew memoirs; by extension, "flak" entered English as a metaphor for strong criticism or opposition, and "flak jacket" became the name for protective vests first issued to aircrews against shell fragments.
