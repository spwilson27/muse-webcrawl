Clipper chip

The Clipper chip was a chipset developed and promoted by the United States National Security Agency (NSA) in the 1990s as an encryption device to be embedded in consumer telecommunications equipment. Announced in 1993, it implemented the classified Skipjack cipher and a controversial key escrow scheme: each chip held a unique serial number and encryption key, copies of which were to be held by government agencies so that, with legal authorization, officials could decrypt any communication made with the device.

How it worked

Each Clipper chip, manufactured initially by VLSI Technology and programmed by Mykotronx, contained the Skipjack algorithm, an 80-bit block cipher kept secret by the NSA. When a device was powered up, it generated a session key and transmitted it encrypted under the chip's unique unit key, together with the chip's serial number, in a data structure called the Law Enforcement Access Field (LEAF). Two federal agencies — the National Institute of Standards and Technology and the Department of the Treasury — each held one half of the escrowed key material, which could be combined under a court order to recover the unit key and then the session keys of recorded traffic.

Opposition and failure

The proposal provoked fierce opposition from the technology industry and civil liberties groups, who objected to government-held backdoor access, warned of security risks, and doubted that criminals would use escrowed devices anyway. In 1994, researcher Matt Blaze published a paper showing that the LEAF integrity check could be forged, allowing Clipper devices to interoperate while defeating the escrow feature. By 1996 the Clipper initiative was effectively dead as manufacturers declined to adopt it and the administration shifted strategy. The Skipjack algorithm was declassified in 1998.

The episode became the defining battle of the "Crypto Wars" of the 1990s and is still cited in modern debates over exceptional access to encrypted communications.
