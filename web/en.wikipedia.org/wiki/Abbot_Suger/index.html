Suger (c. 1081 – 1151) was a French abbot and statesman who served as abbot of the royal Abbey of Saint-Denis from 1122 until his death. A close adviser to Kings Louis VI and Louis VII, he acted as regent of France during the Second Crusade. He is most famous as the patron and guiding mind behind the rebuilding of his abbey church, widely credited as the first building in the Gothic style.

The new Saint-Denis

Between 1135 and 1144 Suger rebuilt the west front and the chevet of Saint-Denis, combining pointed arches, ribbed vaults and large stained-glass windows into a radiant architecture of light, which he justified theologically as an aid to contemplating the divine. His writings on the project, including the Book on the Consecration of the Church of Saint-Denis, are key sources for early Gothic architecture and medieval aesthetics.

Statesman

Of humble birth, Suger was educated alongside the future Louis VI at Saint-Denis. As abbot he reformed the abbey's administration and finances, and as royal counsellor he helped strengthen the Capetian monarchy against rebellious barons. His memoirs of Louis VI remain an important source for the period.
