Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.

Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), was a United States Supreme Court copyright law case that established that a commercial parody can qualify as fair use. The case involved the rap group 2 Live Crew and their parody of Roy Orbison's song "Oh, Pretty Woman," and is widely regarded as one of the most important fair use decisions in American copyright law.

Background

In 1989, 2 Live Crew released the album As Clean As They Wanna Be, which included the song "Pretty Woman," a parody of the 1964 Roy Orbison hit "Oh, Pretty Woman." The group's manager had written to Acuff-Rose Music, the copyright holder, offering a licensing fee and explaining the parodic nature of the song, but Acuff-Rose declined. 2 Live Crew released the parody anyway, and Acuff-Rose sued for copyright infringement.

The District Court granted summary judgment to 2 Live Crew, finding the song a fair use, but the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the commercial nature of the parody rendered it presumptively unfair.

Decision

The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice David Souter, unanimously reversed the Sixth Circuit. The Court held that the appellate court had erred in giving near-dispositive weight to the song's commercial character. Applying the four fair use factors of section 107 of the Copyright Act, the Court emphasized the transformative character of parody: a parody comments on or criticizes an original work and therefore needs to "conjure up" enough of the original to make its target recognizable.

The Court held that the commercial nature of a work does not create a presumption against fair use, and that a parody's taking of the "heart" of the original does not weigh against fair use when that heart is precisely what the parody targets. The case was remanded for further proceedings on the market-harm factor; the parties subsequently settled, with 2 Live Crew agreeing to pay a licensing fee.

Significance

Campbell is credited with introducing the concept of "transformative use" into the center of fair use analysis. The decision has influenced litigation involving parody, satire, search engines, mass digitization projects, and other transformative technologies, and it remains a foundational precedent in United States copyright doctrine.
