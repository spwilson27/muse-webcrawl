Naqshbandi

The Naqshbandi (Persian: نقشبندیه, Naqshbandiyya) is a major Sunni Sufi order (tariqa) of Islam, named after its eponymous founder Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari (1318–1389). It is one of the most widespread Sufi orders in the world, with followers across Central and South Asia, Turkey, the Balkans, the Caucasus, China and the Middle East.

Origins and chain

The order traces its spiritual lineage (silsila) through Abu Bakr, the first caliph, rather than through Ali as most other Sufi orders do — a distinctive feature shared with few other tariqas. Its practices were shaped in Central Asia by masters such as Yusuf Hamadani and Abd al-Khaliq Ghijduwani, who formulated principles including "solitude in society" and awareness in breathing. Baha al-Din Naqshband, buried near Bukhara, gave the order its name and organisational form.

Practices

The Naqshbandi order is noted for its silent dhikr (remembrance of God), in contrast to the vocal dhikr of many other orders, and for strict adherence to the sharia and the sunna. Key principles include hosh dar dam (awareness in the breath), nazar bar qadam (watching one's steps), and khalwat dar anjuman (outward engagement with society, inward engagement with God).

Spread and branches

The order spread widely through figures such as Khwaja Ubayd Allah Ahrar in Timurid Central Asia and, in India, Ahmad Sirhindi (the Mujaddid Alf-i Sani), whose Mujaddidi branch carried Naqshbandi teaching throughout the Mughal, Ottoman and Southeast Asian worlds. Other major branches include the Khalidi branch of Mawlana Khalid al-Baghdadi, influential in Kurdistan and the late Ottoman Empire, and the Haqqani branch in the modern era. Naqshbandi shaykhs played notable political roles, from resistance movements in the Caucasus to religious revival in Turkey and Central Asia.
