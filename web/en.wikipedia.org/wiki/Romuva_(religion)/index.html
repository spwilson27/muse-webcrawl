Romuva is a modern revival of the traditional ethnic religion of the Lithuanians, reconstructing the pre-Christian Baltic faith that survived in Lithuania — the last European country to adopt Christianity (officially in 1387, with Samogitia baptized in 1413) — in folk customs and songs (dainos) long afterwards.

Beliefs and practice

Romuva worship centres on reverence for nature (in the concept of darna, harmony), sacred groves and fire, and a pantheon headed by Dievas (God), the thunder god Perkūnas, the earth goddess Žemyna and the fate goddess Laima. Ceremonies mark the seasonal festivals of the agricultural year — Rasos (summer solstice), Ilgės (All Souls) and others — and life-cycle events such as weddings and funerals. The name derives from a legendary Prussian pagan sanctuary, Romuva.

History

The revival was inspired by 19th-century romantic nationalism and the scholarship of figures such as Vydūnas. Suppressed under Soviet rule, the movement re-emerged in 1967 and legally re-registered in 1992; its leader Jonas Trinkūnas was inaugurated as high priest (krivis) in 2002. Romuva has sought state recognition as a traditional religion in Lithuania, a status debated in the Seimas.
