Zippo Manufacturing Company is an American manufacturer of refillable metal lighters, best known for the Zippo windproof lighter, a rectangular metal lighter with a hinged lid whose distinctive opening "click" and wind-resistant chimney design have made it a cultural icon. The company was founded by George G. Blaisdell in Bradford, Pennsylvania, in 1932, and produced its first lighter in early 1933. Zippo lighters have been manufactured in Bradford continuously ever since, and the company remains privately held by descendants of the founder.

Zippo lighters are famously backed by a lifetime guarantee: the company promises that "it works or we fix it free," repairing any genuine Zippo lighter returned to it regardless of age or condition at no charge. More than 600 million Zippo lighters have been produced since 1933.

History

George G. Blaisdell founded the Zippo Manufacturing Company in 1932. According to company tradition, Blaisdell watched an acquaintance struggle to use an Austrian-made lighter that worked well in wind but was awkward to operate, requiring two hands. Blaisdell obtained the American rights to the design, refined it into a one-handed, rectangular lighter with a hinged top, and named it "Zippo" because he liked the sound of the word "zipper," a recent invention he admired.

The first Zippo lighters were made in early 1933 and sold for $1.95 each. Early production was modest, but the lighter's reliability and the company's unconditional lifetime guarantee helped sales grow. During the Great Depression the lighters were marketed to outdoorsmen and workers, and the company survived on steady demand for a durable, repairable product.

During the Second World War, Zippo ceased production for the consumer market and devoted its entire output to the United States military. The wartime lighters were made of steel rather than brass, because brass was reserved for munitions, and were coated with a black crackle paint finish. The Zippo became a standard item in the kits of American servicemen, who used it not only to light cigarettes but to heat rations, signal in the dark, and light fuses.

After the war, Zippo returned to the civilian market and expanded its range of finishes, engravings, and advertising designs. Lighters were produced with company logos, sports insignia, and commemorative artwork, making them popular advertising items and collectibles. Zippo acquired the W.R. Case & Sons Cutlery Company, the Bradford pocketknife maker, in 1993.

Design and operation

A Zippo lighter consists of a rectangular metal case with a hinged lid, inside which sits a removable insert. The insert contains a windscreen chimney surrounding a cotton wick, a reservoir packed with rayon balls saturated with lighter fluid (a petroleum distillate similar to naphtha), a flint held under spring pressure against a serrated steel wheel, and a felt pad sealing the base of the fuel chamber.

Opening the lid produces the famous "click," and closing it produces a lower "clink"; the sounds are so distinctive that Zippo has sought trademark protection for them. Striking the flint wheel with the thumb throws sparks onto the fluid-soaked wick, igniting it. The perforated chimney shields the flame from wind, drawing air through its holes so the flame bends rather than extinguishes. The flame is put out by closing the lid, which starves it of oxygen.

Zippo lighters are not, despite folklore, perfectly windproof or waterproof, and the fuel evaporates over one to two weeks even when the lighter is unused. Their appeal lies in their ruggedness, simplicity, and repairability: flints, wicks, and felt pads are user-replaceable, and everything else is covered by the lifetime guarantee.

Dating Zippo lighters

Collectors can date most Zippo lighters from the stamp on the bottom of the case. From the 1950s onward the company used a series of dots, slashes, Roman numerals, and finally letter-and-number codes to indicate the month and year of manufacture. Since 1986, bottom stamps have included a letter (A through L for the month) and Roman numerals for the year; from 2001 the year has been printed in Arabic numerals. These codes make Zippos unusually easy to date among mass-produced consumer objects and have supported a large collecting community, with clubs, price guides, and annual limited editions.

Military use

Zippos saw heavy use in the Korean War and the Vietnam War. Vietnam-era lighters are especially sought after by collectors because soldiers frequently had them engraved locally with slogans, unit insignia, maps, and personal messages ranging from the patriotic to the bitterly ironic. These engraved lighters are regarded as artifacts of soldier folk art. The company maintains the Zippo/Case Museum in Bradford, which displays historic lighters including military examples.

In popular culture

The Zippo has appeared in thousands of films, where the snap of the lid and the flare of the flame became a stock way to light a cigarette, a fuse, or a pool of gasoline. Concert audiences in the 1970s popularized raising lit lighters during ballads, a practice that made the Zippo a symbol of rock music, later largely displaced by mobile phone flashlights. Zippo has issued licensed designs for bands, films, sports teams, and brands, and its lighters are featured in the collections of design museums as examples of durable twentieth-century industrial design.

The company today

Zippo remains headquartered in Bradford, Pennsylvania, where it manufactures its lighters and operates the Zippo/Case Museum and flagship store. Although most lighters are still made in the United States, some accessories and butane inserts are produced elsewhere. The modern product line includes windproof and butane lighters, hand warmers, fire-starting tools, candles, and outdoor equipment, as well as products from its Case knives subsidiary. The company is led by descendants of George Blaisdell and continues to honor the lifetime guarantee on every windproof lighter it has ever made.
