Katsuobushi (Japanese: 鰹節) is dried, fermented, and smoked skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis), known in English as bonito flakes when shaved. It is a cornerstone seasoning of Japanese cuisine, prized for its intense umami flavor.

Production

Traditional production is laborious and takes months. The tuna is filleted, simmered, deboned, and repeatedly smoked and dried until it resembles a hard block of wood; in the finest grade, honkarebushi, the blocks are then fermented with the mold Aspergillus glaucus and sun-dried in repeated cycles, reducing moisture and concentrating flavor. The finished block is so hard it is shaved with a blade mounted in a wooden box called a katsuobushi kezuriki, producing fine pinkish-brown flakes.

Culinary uses

Combined with kombu (kelp), katsuobushi makes dashi, the fundamental stock of Japanese cooking: the inosinic acid of the fish and the glutamic acid of the kelp act synergistically to produce umami. As a topping, the flakes garnish dishes such as okonomiyaki and takoyaki, where rising heat makes them appear to wave or "dance," and they top cold tofu (hiyayakko), rice, and ohitashi. Katsuobushi is also a classic filling for onigiri rice balls and, mixed with soy sauce, forms a seasoning known as okaka.

History

Preserved bonito has been produced in Japan since at least the Edo period, when smoking and fermentation techniques were refined in regions such as Tosa (present-day Kōchi) and Suruga. The name of the finished product was famously considered auspicious — suggesting "victorious fish" — and dried bonito was a traditional betrothal gift symbolizing durability.
