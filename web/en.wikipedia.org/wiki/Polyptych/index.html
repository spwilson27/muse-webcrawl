Polyptych

A polyptych (from the Greek poly- meaning "many" and ptychē meaning "fold") is a painting (usually panel painting) which is divided into sections, or panels. Specifically, a "diptych" is a two-part work of art; a "triptych" is a three-part work; a "quadriptych" or "tetraptych" has four parts; and "polyptych" covers the works with more parts, usually five or more.

History

Polyptychs were most commonly created by early Renaissance painters, the majority of whom designed their works to be altarpieces in churches and cathedrals. The form was also popular in Byzantine art (often as small ivory or mosaic devotional works), in Japanese art, and in manuscript illumination. Famous examples include the Ghent Altarpiece by Jan van Eyck (completed 1432), with twelve panels, and the Isenheim Altarpiece by Matthias Grünewald.

Structure

A polyptych typically has a large central panel with the principal figures, flanked by side panels (wings) that may be hinged to close over the centre, with smaller predella panels below showing related scenes. Many polyptychs have been dismantled over the centuries and their panels dispersed among museums, from which art historians reconstruct their original arrangement.
