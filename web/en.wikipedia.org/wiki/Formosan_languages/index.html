Formosan languages

The Formosan languages are the languages of the indigenous peoples of Taiwan, all of them Austronesian. Taiwan's Austronesian languages were historically spoken throughout the island; today most surviving languages are spoken mainly in mountainous and east-coast areas, as centuries of Chinese immigration and language shift have pushed many groups toward Mandarin and Hokkien. The government of Taiwan officially recognises several of these languages, and they are taught as part of the national curriculum's local-language classes.

The Formosan languages are of extraordinary importance to Austronesian linguistics. Because nine of the ten primary branches of the Austronesian family are found only in Taiwan (the tenth, Malayo-Polynesian, comprises all Austronesian languages outside Taiwan), most linguists conclude that Taiwan is the homeland from which Austronesian speakers spread across the Pacific and Southeast Asia beginning around 5,000–6,000 years ago (the "out of Taiwan" model).

Classification

Scholars differ on the internal classification, but the surviving languages are generally grouped into branches including Atayalic (Atayal, Seediq), East Formosan (Amis, Kavalan, Siraya), Paiwan, Puyuma, Rukai, Tsouic (Tsou, Kanakanavu, Saaroa), Bunun, and Thao. Many languages are extinct, including those of the plains indigenous peoples such as Siraya, Taivoan and Makatao, though revival movements are under way; Siraya had been considered dormant since the late nineteenth century before a documented revival effort. The seventeenth-century Dutch colonial period left written records — including the Siraya-language Sinkan manuscripts and a land-lease corpus — that aid reconstruction.

Endangerment and revitalisation

Almost all Formosan languages are endangered, with younger generations often dominant in Mandarin. The Indigenous Languages Development Act (2017) made Taiwan's indigenous languages national languages and funded teaching materials, teacher training and broadcasting. Certification tests, master–apprentice programmes and language nests (immersion preschools modelled on Māori and Hawaiian movements) support revitalisation.

Characteristics

Many Formosan languages display the classic Austronesian voice ("focus") system, in which the verb marks which participant — actor, patient, location or instrument — is the syntactic pivot, and verb-initial word order is common. Their phoneme inventories preserve distinctions (such as those reconstructed for Proto-Austronesian) lost in many Malayo-Polynesian languages, making them central to comparative reconstruction.
