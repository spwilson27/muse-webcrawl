Nordic cross

A Nordic cross, also known as a Scandinavian cross, is a cross design in heraldry and vexillology in which the vertical arm of the cross is shifted toward the hoist side of the flag or shield, and the horizontal arm extends to the edges. The design is characteristic of the national flags of the Nordic countries: Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Iceland, as well as autonomous territories and regions such as Åland, the Faroe Islands, Orkney and Shetland.

History

The oldest example is the Dannebrog, the flag of Denmark, whose white cross on a red field is legendarily said to have fallen from the sky during the Battle of Lindanise in 1219 and is attested in sources from the 14th century. The design spread with Danish influence through Scandinavia; Sweden adopted its yellow cross on blue (associated with the coat of arms of King Eric IX) in the 16th century, Norway combined the design with its own colours during the union period, and Finland and Iceland adopted Nordic cross flags after independence in the early 20th century.

Symbolism

The cross represents Christianity, historically the dominant religion of the region. In the 20th century the shared design also came to symbolise Nordic unity, common heritage and cooperation, embodied institutionally by the Nordic Council.

Usage beyond the Nordic countries

Related offset crosses appear in flags elsewhere through historical connections, including the flags of Orkney and Shetland in Scotland, the proposed and former flags of various regions, and the flag of the German war cemetery commission's era designs. In England, the flag of Saint Boniface and some county designs use upright crosses by contrast.
