A piano sonata is a sonata written for a solo piano. Piano sonatas are usually written in three or four movements, although some are in one or two movements. The genre is one of the central forms of Western classical music, serving as a vehicle for composers' most ambitious keyboard writing from the 18th century onward.

Form

The classical piano sonata typically opens with a fast movement in sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation), followed by a slow movement, sometimes a minuet or scherzo, and a lively finale, often a rondo. Composers frequently experimented with this scheme — merging movements, reordering them, or adding programmatic content.

History

Precursors include the keyboard sonatas of Domenico Scarlatti and C. P. E. Bach. The form matured with Haydn, Mozart and Clementi, and reached a first summit in Beethoven's 32 piano sonatas, which expanded the form's scale and expressive range. Schubert, Chopin, Schumann, Liszt and Brahms carried the genre through the Romantic era; in the 20th century Prokofiev, Scriabin, Bartók and Boulez reshaped it, while many composers turned to freer single-movement designs.

Notable examples

  • Beethoven: Pathétique, Moonlight, Waldstein, Appassionata, Hammerklavier
  • Mozart: Sonata in C major, K. 545 ("Sonata facile")
  • Chopin: Sonatas No. 2 ("Funeral March") and No. 3
  • Liszt: Sonata in B minor
  • Schubert: the late sonatas, D. 958–960
