Luminol (C8H7N3O2) is a chemical that exhibits chemiluminescence, with a blue glow, when mixed with an appropriate oxidising agent. It is used by forensic investigators to detect trace amounts of blood at crime scenes, since luminol reacts with the iron in haemoglobin.

Chemistry

Luminol is synthesised from 3-nitrophthalic acid via hydrazine to form 3-nitrophthalhydrazide, followed by reduction of the nitro group (for example with sodium dithionite) to the amine. In the detection reaction, luminol is oxidised in alkaline solution by hydrogen peroxide (or another peroxide) in the presence of a catalyst — typically iron(II)/iron(III) from haemoglobin — forming an excited intermediate dianion of 3-aminophthalate, which emits a photon at about 425 nm as it relaxes, releasing nitrogen gas.

Forensic use

Crime scene investigators spray a solution of luminol, an oxidant, and an alkaline activator over an area; where blood (even diluted or cleaned residue) is present, the surface glows blue in darkness, visible for roughly 30 seconds and recordable by long-exposure photography. The test is presumptive: certain other substances — bleach, some metals, and plant peroxidases — can also trigger the reaction, and luminol can partially degrade DNA samples, so confirmatory testing follows. Walter Specht is credited with introducing luminol to forensic blood detection in 1937.
