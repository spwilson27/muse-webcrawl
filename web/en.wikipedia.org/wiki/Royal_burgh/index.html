Royal burgh

A royal burgh was a type of Scottish burgh which had been founded by, or subsequently granted, a royal charter. Although abolished by law in 1975, the term is still used by many former royal burghs for ceremonial and marketing purposes. Royal burghs enjoyed distinctive privileges, most importantly a monopoly of foreign trade within their bounds and the right to elect their own town councils and send representatives to the Parliament of Scotland.

History

The first royal burghs were created by King David I in the 12th century, as part of his programme of feudal and economic modernisation of Scotland. Berwick-upon-Tweed, Roxburgh, Edinburgh, Stirling and Perth were among the earliest. Burgesses were granted plots of land (tofts), market rights and freedom from certain feudal dues, in return for rents and loyalty to the Crown. Royal burghs were distinguished from burghs of barony and burghs of regality, which were created by landowners or churchmen and lacked the trading monopoly and parliamentary representation of the royal foundations.

The Convention of Royal Burghs, first recorded in the 16th century, represented the collective interests of the burghs and survived as an influential lobbying body until the 20th century. By the time of the Union with England in 1707 there were some 66 royal burghs.

Abolition and legacy

The Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 abolished burghs as administrative units, effective 16 May 1975, replacing them with district councils. Many former royal burghs retain their arms, provosts and titles for ceremonial use, and community councils may use the designation. Towns such as St Andrews, Dumfries, Kirkcaldy, Montrose and Elgin continue to style themselves royal burghs in local usage.
