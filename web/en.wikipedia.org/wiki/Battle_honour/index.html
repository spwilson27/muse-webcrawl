Battle honour

Battle honours emblazoned on a regimental colour

A battle honour is an award of a right by a government or sovereign to a military unit to emblazon the name of a battle or operation on its flags ("colours"), uniforms, or other appointments. The practice is especially associated with the British Army and the armies of the Commonwealth, in which honours are borne on regimental colours and guidons and form a central part of regimental identity and esprit de corps.

History

The first battle honour awarded in the British system is generally considered to be that for the Battle of Tangier in 1680; honours were granted retroactively for earlier engagements, most famously "Minden" and "Gibraltar". During the great wars of the 19th and 20th centuries, the number of honours grew enormously, and after the First World War each regiment was limited in the number it could emblazon on its colours — typically ten from that war — even if it had been awarded many more.

Selection and display

Honours are recommended by official committees that examine unit war diaries and eligibility rules. Those selected for emblazoning are displayed in bold on the colour, while the full list appears in army lists and regimental histories. Theatre honours, such as "North-West Europe 1944–45", recognize participation in a campaign rather than a single battle.

Other systems

Other armed forces use related distinctions: the United States Army awards campaign streamers to unit flags, and the French Army inscribes battle names on regimental flags in gold letters. Naval vessels may inherit the battle honours of earlier ships of the same name.
