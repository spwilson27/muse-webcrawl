Special functions

Special functions are particular mathematical functions that have more or less established names and notations because of their importance in mathematical analysis, functional analysis, geometry, physics, or other applications. The term is defined by consensus, and thus lacks a general formal definition, but lists of mathematical functions contain functions generally accepted as special.

Bessel functions Jα(x) for integer orders — canonical special functions arising in wave propagation and heat conduction.

Origin

Many special functions arose as solutions of differential equations or integrals of elementary functions that cannot be expressed in closed form: the error function, the gamma function, Bessel functions (from Bessel's equation in cylindrical problems), Legendre polynomials (in spherical problems), and the hypergeometric functions that unify many of them. Tables of values were essential computational tools in the pre-computer era; the 1964 NBS handbook by Abramowitz and Stegun, and its successor, the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions, are the standard references.

Examples

  • Gamma and beta functions — extensions of the factorial.
  • Bessel functions J, Y, I, K and spherical Bessel functions.
  • Orthogonal polynomials — Legendre, Hermite, Laguerre, Chebyshev, Jacobi.
  • Hypergeometric functions — 2F1 and its generalisations, including elliptic integrals.
  • Error function, exponential integral, sine and cosine integrals, Fresnel integrals.
  • Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet L-functions in number theory.
  • Elliptic functions — Weierstrass and Jacobi forms.

Properties and computation

Special functions are characterised by identities: recurrence relations, differential equations, integral representations, generating functions, and asymptotic expansions. Modern computer algebra systems and numerical libraries evaluate them via series, continued fractions, and uniform asymptotics. In physics they appear wherever separation of variables reduces a partial differential equation — such as the Helmholtz or Schrödinger equation — to a named ordinary differential equation.
