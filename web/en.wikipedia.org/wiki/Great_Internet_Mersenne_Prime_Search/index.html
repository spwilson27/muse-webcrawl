Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search

The Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) is a collaborative project of volunteers who use freely available software to search for Mersenne prime numbers. Founded in January 1996 by computer scientist George Woltman, GIMPS is one of the longest-running distributed computing projects and has discovered the majority of the largest known prime numbers, including every record prime since 1996.

How it works

A Mersenne number is of the form Mp = 2p − 1, where p is prime. Volunteers download the Prime95 software, which receives assignments of candidate exponents, performs preliminary trial factoring and P−1 factoring, and then runs the Lucas–Lehmer primality test (and, in modern practice, the faster and error-checking PRP test) using fast Fourier transform multiplication. Results are coordinated through the PrimeNet server, and every discovery is independently verified on different hardware.

Discoveries

GIMPS participants found M1398269 in November 1996, the project's first prime, and a succession of records followed, including M3021377 (1998), M20996011 (2003), M43112609 (2008, the first prime of more than ten million decimal digits, which won the Electronic Frontier Foundation's US$100,000 cooperative computing award), M74207281 (discovered by Curtis Cooper in 2016), M82589933 (2018, found by Patrick Laroche), and M136279841, a prime of over 41 million digits found in October 2024 by Luke Durant using GPUs in a cloud data centre — the first GIMPS prime discovered primarily with GPU computing.

Significance

Beyond the search itself, Prime95 is widely used as a CPU stress-testing tool by computer enthusiasts. GIMPS is frequently cited as a pioneering example of volunteer computing and citizen science on the internet.
