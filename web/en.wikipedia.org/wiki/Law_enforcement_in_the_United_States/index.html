Law enforcement in the United States

Law enforcement in the United States is one of three major components of the American criminal justice system, alongside courts and corrections. It is highly decentralised: the United States has roughly 18,000 law enforcement agencies at the federal, state, county, and municipal levels, employing more than 800,000 full-time sworn officers. There is no single national police force; most policing is done by local police departments and county sheriffs' offices.

Levels of law enforcement

Federal

Federal agencies enforce federal law within defined jurisdictions. Major agencies include the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF), the United States Marshals Service (the oldest federal law enforcement agency, founded 1789), the Federal Bureau of Prisons, and the components of the Department of Homeland Security — Customs and Border Protection, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), the Transportation Security Administration, and the Secret Service.

State

Each state maintains agencies such as highway patrols or state police, state bureaus of investigation, and specialised agencies for parks, alcohol control, and gaming.

Local

Municipal police departments — from the New York City Police Department, the largest in the country with roughly 36,000 officers, to departments with a single officer — handle most day-to-day policing. County sheriffs, usually elected, run jails, serve process, and police unincorporated areas. Special-purpose agencies police transit systems, ports, campuses, and tribal lands.

History

American policing grew out of the English constable and watch systems and, in the South, slave patrols. The first modern municipal police department was founded in Boston in 1838, followed by New York (1845) and Chicago (1851). The twentieth century brought professionalisation under reformers such as August Vollmer and O. W. Wilson, the FBI's rise under J. Edgar Hoover, and federal court decisions constraining police practice, such as Miranda v. Arizona (1966). In recent decades, debates over use of force, qualified immunity, militarisation, and police reform have been prominent, intensifying after 2020.

Oversight and powers

Officers' powers of search, seizure, and arrest are bounded by the Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendments and by state law. Departments are overseen by elected officials, civilian review boards in some cities, and the Department of Justice's civil-rights pattern-or-practice investigations.
