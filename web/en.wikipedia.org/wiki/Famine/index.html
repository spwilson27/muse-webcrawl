Famine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A famine is a widespread scarcity of food, caused by several possible factors including war, natural disasters, crop failure, widespread poverty, an economic catastrophe, or government policies. This phenomenon is usually accompanied or followed by regional malnutrition, starvation, epidemics, and increased mortality. Under the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), famine is formally declared when at least 20 percent of households face extreme food shortages, acute malnutrition exceeds 30 percent, and the death rate exceeds two people per 10,000 per day.

Causes

Historically, famines were often attributed simply to declines in food availability — drought, floods, pests, volcanic winters such as the one following the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora (the "Year Without a Summer"), or wartime destruction of crops. The economist Amartya Sen argued, in Poverty and Famines (1981), that many famines result not from an absence of food but from failures of entitlement: people starve because they cannot afford or access food that exists, owing to unemployment, price spikes, or the collapse of exchange systems. Modern analyses emphasize armed conflict as the leading driver of acute food crises, often combined with climate shocks and economic instability.

Historical famines

Major famines include the Great Famine of Ireland (1845–1852), which killed about one million people and drove mass emigration after potato blight destroyed the staple crop; the Indian famines under colonial rule, including Bengal in 1943; the Soviet famine of 1932–1933, including the Holodomor in Ukraine; the Great Chinese Famine of 1959–1961 during the Great Leap Forward, among the deadliest in history; and famines in Ethiopia (1983–1985), North Korea in the 1990s, Somalia in 2011, and South Sudan in 2017.

Effects and response

Beyond starvation deaths, famines cause stunting in children, social breakdown, migration, and heightened vulnerability to diseases such as cholera and measles, which often kill more people than hunger itself. Responses include emergency food aid, therapeutic feeding for acute malnutrition, cash transfers, and long-term measures such as drought-resistant crops, early-warning systems like FEWS NET, and social safety nets. Since the mid-twentieth century, the global prevalence of famine has declined sharply, though conflicts have caused localized recurrences.
