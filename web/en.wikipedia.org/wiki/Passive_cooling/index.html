Passive cooling

Passive cooling is a building design approach that focuses on heat gain control and heat dissipation in a building in order to improve indoor thermal comfort with low or no energy consumption. It contrasts with active cooling systems such as air conditioning, which use powered equipment, and forms a central part of passive house and sustainable architecture design.

Techniques

Passive cooling strategies fall into two broad groups: preventing heat from entering the building, and removing heat once it is inside. Prevention techniques include shading windows and walls, reflective roofing materials ("cool roofs"), insulation, and careful orientation of the building relative to the sun and prevailing winds.

Heat removal techniques include natural ventilation (cross-ventilation and the stack effect), night flushing of thermal mass, evaporative cooling — long used in the windcatchers (badgir) of Persian architecture — and radiative cooling to the night sky. Earth tubes and ground coupling exploit the stable temperature of the soil below the surface.

Climate suitability

Passive cooling works best in hot, dry climates, where evaporative and radiative methods are effective and night-time temperatures fall enough to cool thermal mass. In hot, humid climates, air movement through ventilation may be the primary available strategy, and passive measures alone may not achieve comfort during the most extreme conditions.

Significance

Buildings account for a large share of global energy use, and cooling demand is rising with climate change. Passive cooling reduces peak electricity demand, operating costs, and vulnerability to power outages, and is increasingly incorporated into building codes and green building standards.
