The Zapotec civilisation (civilización zapoteca) was an indigenous pre-Columbian civilisation of Mesoamerica that flourished in the Valley of Oaxaca in southern Mexico. Archaeological evidence shows it to be one of the earliest complex societies in Mesoamerica, emerging around 700 BC with the founding of the hilltop capital Monte Albán, and enduring in various forms until the Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century. The Zapotecs developed one of the earliest writing systems in the Americas, a sophisticated calendar, and monumental architecture including ballcourts, palaces, and elaborate tombs.

Origins and early development

The Zapotecs called themselves the Be'ena'a, "the Cloud People." Their ancestors settled the fertile valleys of Oaxaca, where villages such as San José Mogote grew into chiefly centres by the Middle Formative period. Around 500 BC the population coalesced at Monte Albán, a defensively positioned mountain ridge levelled by human labour, which became the first true city of the region and dominated the valley for more than a thousand years.

Society and government

Zapotec society was stratified, ruled by hereditary kings and a priestly hierarchy from palace-temple complexes. The state administered tributary provinces, maintained a professional military, and recorded events in hieroglyphic inscriptions on stone monuments. The famous "Danzantes" slabs at Monte Albán, depicting contorted figures, are now interpreted as slain war captives, evidence of early militaristic expansion.

Writing and calendar

The Zapotec script is among the earliest in Mesoamerica, with glyphs appearing by about 600 BC. Scribes carved calendrical dates, names, and historical events using the 260-day ritual calendar shared across Mesoamerica, as well as a 365-day vague year. Although incompletely deciphered, the inscriptions record dynastic history stretching back centuries.

Religion and burial customs

Zapotec religion centred on a supreme sky and lightning deity, Cocijo, and on ancestor veneration. Elite tombs beneath residences held painted murals and ceramic funerary urns depicting deities and ancestors. At sites such as Mitla, later Zapotec rulers built palaces decorated with intricate stone fretwork mosaics known as grecas.

Decline and conquest

Monte Albán declined after about AD 800, and political power fragmented among competing city-states, some allied with or dominated by the Mixtecs. When the Spanish arrived in the 1520s, the Zapotec kingdoms resisted but were subdued. Zapotec languages and cultural traditions nonetheless survive today among the Zapotec peoples of Oaxaca.
