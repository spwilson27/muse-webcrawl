Two-nation theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The two-nation theory is the ideology underpinning the creation of Pakistan as a separate state for the Muslims of British India. It held that Indian Muslims and Indian Hindus are two separate nations, with their own customs, traditions, religion, ideology and way of life, and therefore Muslims should be able to have their own separate homeland in the areas in which they were a majority.

The theory was articulated most prominently by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, president of the All-India Muslim League, notably in his presidential address at the League's Lahore session of March 1940, which led to the Lahore Resolution demanding separate Muslim states. It was opposed by the Indian National Congress and by several Muslim organisations and leaders, including Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, who advocated a united, secular India.

Background

The theory had roots in the nineteenth century: Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, founder of the Aligarh Movement, described Hindus and Muslims as two nations after the Hindi–Urdu controversy of 1867, and the creation of separate electorates for Muslims in 1909 institutionalised communal representation. The poet-philosopher Muhammad Iqbal's Allahabad Address of 1930 proposed a consolidated Muslim state in north-western India.

Implementation and aftermath

The theory resulted in the Partition of India in August 1947, creating the independent dominions of India and Pakistan (the latter comprising West Pakistan and East Bengal). Partition was accompanied by mass migration — some 10 to 20 million people — and communal violence in which hundreds of thousands, perhaps over a million, died.

Criticism and later developments

Critics argued that religion alone could not define nationhood, pointing to India's large continuing Muslim population and to Pakistan's own linguistic and regional divisions. The secession of East Pakistan to become Bangladesh in 1971, after a war fought along linguistic and political rather than religious lines, is frequently cited as a refutation of the theory's premise. In Pakistan the theory remains the founding national ideology and is taught in schools, while in India it remains politically controversial.
