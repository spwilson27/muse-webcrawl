Royal Parks of London

The Royal Parks of London are green spaces once owned by the monarchs of England for their recreation — mostly hunting — and now open to the public. They are managed by The Royal Parks, an executive agency of the Department for Culture, Media and Sport (established as a charity in 2017), and policed by the Metropolitan Police. Together the eight parks cover almost 2,000 hectares (4,900 acres).

The eight parks

  • Hyde Park (142 ha) — acquired by Henry VIII in 1536; site of Speaker's Corner, the Serpentine and large concerts.
  • Kensington Gardens (107 ha) — once part of Hyde Park; home to the Albert Memorial and Kensington Palace.
  • St James's Park (23 ha) — the oldest, between Buckingham Palace and Whitehall; famous for its pelicans.
  • Green Park (19 ha) — meadow park between Buckingham Palace and Piccadilly.
  • Regent's Park (166 ha) — laid out by John Nash; includes London Zoo and the Open Air Theatre.
  • Greenwich Park (74 ha) — in south-east London, site of the Royal Observatory and the Prime Meridian.
  • Richmond Park (955 ha) — a National Nature Reserve with herds of red and fallow deer, enclosed by Charles I.
  • Bushy Park (445 ha) — near Hampton Court Palace, with the Diana Fountain and roaming deer.

History

Originally royal hunting grounds, the parks were gradually opened to the public from the 17th century onward (Hyde Park was opened by Charles I in 1637). Today they host major events including the London Marathon, state ceremonies along the Mall, and Remembrance commemorations, and are vital urban habitats.
