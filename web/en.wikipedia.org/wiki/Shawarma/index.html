Shawarma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A vertical shawarma spit with layers of marinated meat being shaved]

Shawarma is a Levantine dish of marinated meat — lamb, beef, chicken or turkey — stacked in slices on a vertical spit, roasted as it turns, and shaved off in thin ribbons. The name is the Arabic rendering of Turkish çevirme, "turning", a souvenir of its Ottoman-era ancestry alongside döner kebab. It is eaten in wraps or on plates with garlic sauce (toum), pickles, onions, tomato and, in the Jordanian and Palestinian style, fries tucked inside the sandwich itself. From Beirut and Damascus to Amman and Jerusalem, shawarma stands anchor street corners; in the last two decades they have become global fast food.

Technique and service

The stack is engineering: slices of meat and fat are marinated (vinegar, oil, and warm spices — cumin, cardamom, cinnamon, paprika, turmeric for chicken), impaled in a cone, and rotated before a vertical flame. The shawarma man shaves the crisped outer layer to order with a long knife (or electric slicer), catching the shavings in the bread. Chicken shawarma with toum and pickles is the modern default; beef and lamb take tahini-based tarator instead of garlic paste. A second pass on the grill crisps the wrapped sandwich — the saj or press finish.

Descendants

Shawarma travelled with Levantine emigrants and mutated brilliantly: in Mexico, Lebanese immigrants' spit-roasted pork became tacos al pastor, crowned with pineapple; in Greece, the same spit, pork and pita became gyros; in Germany the döner line became Europe's favourite sandwich. A throwaway post-credits scene in the 2012 film The Avengers — the heroes, silent and exhausted, eating shawarma — is credited by restaurateurs with a measurable bump in American interest, one of pop culture's stranger culinary footnotes.
