Hospitalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hospitalism is a term for the adverse physical and psychological effects suffered by infants and young children raised in institutional settings — hospitals, foundling homes, and orphanages — where they received adequate physical care but little emotional contact, stimulation, or individual attention. In its older medical usage it also referred more broadly to the unhygienic and disease-breeding conditions of 19th-century hospitals.

History

The developmental consequences of institutional care were described in the 1940s by the psychoanalyst René Spitz, who compared infants reared in a foundling home with those raised by their mothers in a prison nursery. Despite good nutrition and hygiene, the foundling-home infants showed severe developmental retardation, apathy, susceptibility to infection, and markedly higher mortality. Spitz used "hospitalism" for the condition of children institutionalised for long periods, and "anaclitic depression" for the deterioration of infants separated from their mothers after about six months.

Related research

Spitz's work, along with John Bowlby's theory of attachment and his studies of maternal deprivation, helped transform child-care practice: hospitals began allowing parents to stay with sick children, and orphanages moved toward foster care and smaller, family-like units. Later studies of children adopted from severely depriving orphanages, such as the Romanian orphanages exposed after 1989, confirmed that early institutional deprivation can have lasting effects, partly mitigated by early adoption.
