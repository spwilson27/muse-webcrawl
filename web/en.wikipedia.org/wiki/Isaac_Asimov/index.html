Isaac Asimov (January 2, 1920 – April 6, 1992) was an American writer and professor of biochemistry at Boston University, known for his works of science fiction and popular science. Regarded as one of the "Big Three" science fiction writers alongside Robert A. Heinlein and Arthur C. Clarke, Asimov was a prolific author who wrote or edited more than 500 books. His best-known works are the Foundation series, the Robot series — which introduced the Three Laws of Robotics — and the short story "Nightfall" (1941), voted the best science fiction short story of all time.

Life

Asimov was born in Petrovichi, Russia, and emigrated with his family to Brooklyn, New York, in 1923. He taught himself to read at five, worked in his father's candy stores, and discovered science fiction in the magazines they sold. He studied chemistry at Columbia University (BS 1939, PhD 1948), served as a civilian chemist at the Philadelphia Navy Yard during the Second World War — where he worked alongside Heinlein and L. Sprague de Camp — and joined Boston University School of Medicine as a biochemistry instructor in 1949. From 1958 he gave up most teaching to write full-time. He married Gertrude Blugerman in 1942 (two children) and, after their divorce, the psychiatrist and writer Janet Jeppson in 1973. Asimov died in 1992 of heart and kidney failure arising from AIDS contracted through a blood transfusion during heart bypass surgery in 1983, a cause disclosed by his widow a decade later.

Fiction

Asimov began selling stories to John W. Campbell's Astounding Science Fiction in 1939. His robot stories, beginning with "Robbie" (1940), replaced the menacing "Frankenstein" robot with engineered machines governed by the Three Laws of Robotics, and coined the word robotics. The Foundation stories (1942–1950), inspired by Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, imagined the mathematician Hari Seldon and the science of "psychohistory" guiding a collapsing Galactic Empire; published as the Foundation Trilogy, they won a special Hugo Award for Best All-Time Series in 1966. In the 1980s Asimov wrote sequels uniting the Robot, Empire and Foundation series into a single future history. He also wrote the Elijah Baley robot detective novels, the Lucky Starr juvenile novels, and admired mystery fiction, including the Black Widowers stories.

Nonfiction and influence

Asimov's enormous nonfiction output — science essays for The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction over 33 years, The Intelligent Man's Guide to Science, annotated editions of Shakespeare, the Bible, Don Juan and Paradise Lost, histories of the ancient world, and books on nearly every Dewey Decimal category — made him America's most famous science explainer. He popularised the word "robotics", testified on science policy, and served as a vice-president of Mensa while being far better known as an engaging public speaker and humanist. Asteriod 5020 Asimov, a crater on Mars and numerous schools bear his name.
