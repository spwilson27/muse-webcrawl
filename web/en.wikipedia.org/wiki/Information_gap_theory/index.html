Information gap theory

Information gap theory, often called info-gap decision theory, is a framework for decision-making under severe uncertainty, developed by Yakov Ben-Haim beginning in the 1980s. Unlike probability-based methods, info-gap theory does not assign probabilities to uncertain quantities; instead it asks how wrong a model or estimate can be before a decision would fail, and seeks decisions that are robust to the largest possible error.

Core concepts

The theory is built around info-gap models: nested families of sets of possible values for an uncertain quantity, expanding around a nominal estimate as an "horizon of uncertainty" grows. Two functions characterise a decision: the robustness function — the greatest horizon of uncertainty up to which the decision guarantees a required outcome — and the opportuneness function — the smallest uncertainty at which a windfall outcome better than expected becomes possible. Decision-makers typically seek to maximise robustness while meeting performance requirements.

Applications and criticism

Info-gap methods have been applied in engineering design, environmental management, conservation biology, medicine, economics, and homeland security. Critics note that robustness in the theory is a local property around a single nominal estimate and argue that under genuinely unbounded uncertainty the analysis may not explore the full space of possibilities; proponents reply that info-gap is intended for practical decisions where severe uncertainty makes probabilistic models unreliable.
