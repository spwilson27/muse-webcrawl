Theodor Eicke
Born: 17 October 1892, Hampont, German Empire
Died: 26 February 1943 (aged 50), near Kharkov, Ukraine
Allegiance: Nazi Germany
Service: SS
Rank: SS-Obergruppenführer
Commands: Dachau concentration camp; Inspector of Concentration Camps; SS Division Totenkopf
Conflicts: World War I, World War II

Theodor Eicke (17 October 1892 – 26 February 1943) was a German SS functionary during the Nazi era. He was one of the key figures in the development of the Nazi concentration camp system, serving as commandant of Dachau concentration camp and later as Inspector of Concentration Camps. During the Second World War he commanded the SS Division Totenkopf on the Eastern Front, where he was killed in action in 1943.

Early life and career

Eicke was born in Hampont in Alsace-Lorraine, then part of the German Empire, the youngest of eleven children of a railway stationmaster. He left school without qualifications and joined the Imperial German Army at seventeen, serving during the First World War as a paymaster clerk and earning the Iron Cross second class. After the war he worked in various jobs and drifted into far-right politics, joining the Nazi Party and the SA before transferring to the SS in 1930.

His early SS career was marked by conflicts with superiors and a period of confinement in a psychiatric clinic at Würzburg in 1933 after a falling-out with a Gauleiter. Heinrich Himmler, who had taken an interest in Eicke, arranged his release and appointed him commandant of Dachau in June 1933.

Commandant of Dachau and Inspector of Concentration Camps

At Dachau, Eicke reorganised the camp into a model of the system that followed: he instituted a regime of systematic terror, drew up detailed disciplinary regulations prescribing punishments including floggings and executions, and established the principle that guards were to show no pity toward prisoners, whom he described as enemies of the state. The "Dachau model" became the template for the expanding camp network, and Eicke's trained personnel went on to staff camps across the Reich.

Eicke played a leading role in the Night of the Long Knives in 1934; together with Michael Lippert he personally shot SA leader Ernst Röhm in his cell at Stadelheim Prison after Röhm refused to commit suicide. In July 1934 Eicke was appointed Inspector of Concentration Camps, overseeing the consolidation of the many small early camps into a small number of large facilities, including Sachsenhausen and Buchenwald. Under his inspectorate, the camp SS were organised into the SS-Totenkopfverbände (Death's Head units).

Second World War

At the outbreak of war, Eicke took command of the SS Division Totenkopf, formed largely from concentration camp guard personnel. He led the division in the invasion of France in 1940, where his men perpetrated the Le Paradis massacre of British prisoners of war, and on the Eastern Front from 1941, including the fighting around Demyansk in 1942.

On 26 February 1943, during the Third Battle of Kharkov, the Fieseler Storch spotter aircraft in which Eicke was flying was shot down by Soviet ground fire between the villages of Michailowka and Artjelnoje, and he was killed. He was initially buried nearby; his remains were later moved.

Legacy

Eicke is remembered as one of the principal architects of the concentration camp system. The personnel he trained and the methods he codified at Dachau shaped the camp network through which the Nazi regime imprisoned, exploited, and murdered millions of people.
