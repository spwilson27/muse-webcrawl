Cosmic microwave background

[Image: the Planck satellite's all-sky map of the cosmic microwave background — a mottled oval of orange and blue speckles]

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is cosmic radiation — the thermal radiation left over from the time of recombination in the early universe, when it first became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. It fills all space, is observable as a faint microwave glow in every direction, and, at a temperature of about 2.725 kelvin, is the oldest light in the universe and a decisive evidence for the Big Bang model.

The CMB was predicted by George Gamow, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman in 1948 and discovered serendipitously by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson in 1965, when an unexplained hiss in their Holmdel antenna proved to be the afterglow of creation; they received the Nobel Prize in 1978.

What it tells us

The background is almost perfectly uniform, with temperature fluctuations of only about one part in 100,000 mapped in detail by the COBE (1992), WMAP and Planck satellites. These fluctuations are the seeds from which galaxies grew by gravity, and their pattern encodes the universe's age (13.8 billion years), geometry (spatially flat), and composition — ordinary matter about 5%, Dark matter about 27%, dark energy about 68%.

A slight excess of brightness in one direction (the dipole) measures our motion through space at about 370 km/s relative to the cosmic rest frame. Study of the CMB's polarisation continues, hunting signs of primordial gravitational waves from cosmic inflation.
