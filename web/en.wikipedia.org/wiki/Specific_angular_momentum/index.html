Specific angular momentum

Specific angular momentum
Symbol: h
Units: m²/s
Definition: angular momentum per unit mass

In celestial mechanics the specific angular momentum (or specific relative angular momentum) is the angular momentum of an orbiting body per unit mass. It plays a pivotal role in the analysis of the two-body problem, since it is a conserved quantity for an isolated system moving under a central force.

Definition

For an orbiting body, the specific angular momentum h is the cross product of the position vector r and the velocity vector v relative to the centre of mass of the system: h = r × v. Its magnitude equals the product of the distance and the transverse component of velocity.

Relation to orbital elements

For a Keplerian orbit with semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, and standard gravitational parameter μ, the magnitude of the specific angular momentum is h = √(μa(1 − e²)) = √(μp), where p is the semi-latus rectum. The direction of the vector is perpendicular to the orbital plane, which fixes the orientation of the orbit in space.

Conservation

Because gravity is a central force, the torque on the orbiting body is zero and the specific angular momentum vector remains constant in both magnitude and direction. This conservation is the physical content of Kepler's second law: the line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
