Home equity loan

A home equity loan is a type of loan in which the borrower uses the equity in their home as collateral. It creates a lien against the property and reduces the owner's actual equity. Home equity loans are often used to finance major expenses such as home repairs and renovations, medical bills, or education. Because the loan is secured by the property, interest rates are typically lower than for unsecured borrowing, but the borrower's home is at risk of foreclosure if they default.

Structure

A traditional home equity loan is disbursed as a single lump sum and repaid in fixed installments over a set term (often 5 to 30 years), usually at a fixed interest rate. It is frequently structured as a second mortgage, subordinate to the borrower's first mortgage, and lenders commonly limit total borrowing to a combined loan-to-value ratio of about 80–90% of the home's appraised value, minus the outstanding first-mortgage balance. A related product, the home equity line of credit (HELOC), instead provides a revolving credit line that the borrower can draw against during a draw period and then repay, usually at a variable rate.

Tax treatment and regulation

In the United States, mortgage interest on home equity borrowing was broadly tax-deductible until the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 suspended the deduction for interest on home equity indebtedness (through 2025) unless the funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan. Home equity loans are subject to the Truth in Lending Act and other consumer-protection rules, and aggressive equity lending featured in concerns about predatory lending during the mid-2000s housing bubble: borrowers who stripped out their equity were left exposed when prices fell after 2007.
