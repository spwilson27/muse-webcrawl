The mandolin is a stringed musical instrument in the lute family, generally plucked with a plectrum (pick). It most commonly has four courses of doubled metal strings tuned in unison, giving it a total of eight strings. The courses are tuned in perfect fifths — G, D, A, E — the same tuning as a violin, though the mandolin's scale length is shorter, typically around 33–36 cm (13–14 inches).

The mandolin is a descendent of the mandore and the gittern-like instruments of the medieval and Renaissance periods. Its modern form, with a bowl-shaped back built up from strips of wood, was developed in Naples, Italy, in the mid-18th century, notably by the Vinaccia family of luthiers. The Neapolitan mandolin, with its bent soundboard and floating bridge, became the standard classical model.

History

Small lute-family instruments appeared in Europe from the 15th century onward. The mandore, popular in 16th- and 17th-century France and Spain, is often cited as the mandolin's direct ancestor. In the 1740s–1760s, Neapolitan makers standardized the bowlback mandolin with four double courses of steel strings.

The instrument enjoyed enormous popularity in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Mandolin orchestras and fretted-instrument clubs flourished across Europe and North America, and makers such as Lyon & Healy in Chicago produced mandolins in vast numbers. The Gibson company, under designer Lloyd Loar, introduced carved-top "A-style" and "F-style" mandolins in the 1920s, whose design dominates bluegrass music today.

Construction and types

Mandolins vary in body shape: the Neapolitan bowlback (or "tater bug"), the flatback, and the carved archtop with f-holes or an oval sound hole. Modern families include the mandola (tuned a fifth below), the octave mandolin, the mandocello, and the rare mandobass. Electric mandolins, usually with four single strings, are used in some rock and jazz contexts.

Repertoire and use

Classical composers who wrote for the mandolin include Vivaldi (concertos in C major and G major), Beethoven (four sonatinas), and Hummel. In folk traditions the mandolin is central to Italian music, Irish traditional music, and American bluegrass, where Bill Monroe, the "Father of Bluegrass," defined its percussive chop rhythm and virtuosic lead style. It is also prominent in Brazilian choro and Japanese mandolin orchestras.
