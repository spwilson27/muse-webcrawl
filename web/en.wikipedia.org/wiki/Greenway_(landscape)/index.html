Greenway (landscape)

A greenway is a long, narrow strip of land, often following a natural corridor such as a river, valley or ridgeline, or a disused railway line or canal, that is managed as a park-like route for recreation, walking, cycling and wildlife movement. Greenways link parks, nature reserves, cultural features and communities, forming networks of open space at local, regional and national scales.

Characteristics

Greenways typically combine several functions: they provide paths for non-motorised transport, protect habitats and connect fragmented ecosystems so that plants and animals can move between them, manage floodwater along river corridors, and preserve scenic or historic landscapes. Some are paved urban trails; others are rough footpaths through countryside. Famous examples include the East Coast Greenway in the United States, the greenways (voies vertes) of France, and long-distance routes built on abandoned railways, sometimes called rail trails.

History

The idea descends from the parkway and garden-city movements of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when planners such as Frederick Law Olmsted proposed linked systems of parks. The modern greenway movement grew from the 1960s onwards, driven by recreation planning, environmental protection and urban regeneration, and greenways are now a standard tool of landscape architecture and regional planning.
