Scaffolding (education)

Instructional scaffolding: temporary support that is withdrawn as competence grows

Instructional scaffolding is the support given to a student by an instructor throughout the learning process, tailored to the student's needs and gradually removed as the student develops independent competence. The metaphor likens the teacher's support to the scaffolding used in construction: it enables work that would otherwise be impossible and is dismantled once the structure can stand alone.

Origins

The term was introduced by David Wood, Jerome Bruner, and Gail Ross in their 1976 paper "The Role of Tutoring in Problem Solving", describing how adults helped young children complete a block-building task beyond their unaided ability. The concept is closely related to Lev Vygotsky's zone of proximal development (ZPD) — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other.

Key characteristics

  • Contingency — support is calibrated to the learner's current performance
  • Fading — support is progressively withdrawn
  • Transfer of responsibility — the learner gradually takes over the task

Strategies

Common scaffolding strategies include modelling a task while thinking aloud, breaking complex tasks into manageable steps, using graphic organisers and worked examples, asking prompting and clarifying questions, providing sentence starters in writing tasks, giving timely feedback, and structuring peer collaboration. In digital learning environments, scaffolds may be embedded in software as hints, prompts, and adaptive feedback.

Types

Researchers distinguish between hard scaffolds — static supports planned in advance, such as worksheets and worked examples — and soft scaffolds — dynamic, situational support provided by a teacher in the moment, based on ongoing diagnosis of student understanding.

Criticism and research

Scholars have noted that the term is sometimes applied loosely to any educational support, diluting its meaning. Effective scaffolding requires accurate diagnosis of the learner's level; support that is not faded may foster dependency, while premature withdrawal can overwhelm the learner. Meta-analyses generally find scaffolding effective, particularly in science and mathematics inquiry learning, when it is contingent and gradually removed.
