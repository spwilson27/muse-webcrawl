Captaincy General of the Philippines

The Captaincy General of the Philippines (Spanish: Capitanía General de Filipinas) was the administrative district of the Spanish Empire that governed the Spanish East Indies from 1565 to 1898. It was ruled by a Governor-General, who served simultaneously as captain general (military commander-in-chief) and, for most of the period, as president of the Royal Audiencia of Manila. Its capital was Manila — first at Cebu briefly, then at Intramuros, Manila from 1571. Although formally subordinate to the Viceroyalty of New Spain until Mexican independence in 1821, the Captaincy General exercised wide autonomy; after 1821 it reported directly to Madrid.

History

The Captaincy General was established with Miguel López de Legazpi's expedition of 1565, which founded Spanish settlements at Cebu and, in 1571, Manila. For 250 years the lifeline of the colony was the Manila galleon trade, exchanging Chinese silk and porcelain for American silver between Manila and Acapulco. Administration combined the Governor-General's civil and military rule with the judicial Royal Audiencia (established 1583) and the powerful influence of the religious orders in the provinces. Spain also claimed and administered Guam and the Marianas, and later the Carolines, through the Captaincy General.

The 19th century brought reforms, the opening of Manila to world trade (1834), the end of the galleon trade (1815), and rising Filipino nationalism. The Philippine Revolution began in 1896 under the Katipunan; following the Spanish–American War, Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States in the Treaty of Paris of 10 December 1898, ending the Captaincy General after 333 years.

Government

The Governor-General was appointed by the Spanish Crown and combined the roles of civil governor, captain general of the armed forces, president of the Audiencia, and vice-patron of the Church. Provinces were governed by alcaldes mayores and later civil governors; towns by gobernadorcillos drawn from the local elite (the principalía). The system of encomienda, tribute, and forced labour drafts (polo y servicio) structured the colonial economy in its early centuries.
