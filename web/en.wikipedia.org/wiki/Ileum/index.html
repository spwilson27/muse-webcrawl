Ileum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ileum is the final section of the small intestine in most higher vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles and birds. In humans it follows the duodenum and jejunum and is separated from the caecum of the large intestine by the ileocaecal valve. The ileum is about 3.5 metres long in adult humans — roughly the last three-fifths of the small intestine — and has a slightly smaller diameter and thinner wall than the jejunum.

Function

The ileum's principal role is to absorb vitamin B12 (bound to intrinsic factor), bile salts, and any nutrients not already absorbed by the jejunum. Bile salts absorbed here enter the enterohepatic circulation and are recycled to the liver. The ileal mucosa has smaller villi than the jejunum but abundant lymphoid tissue in the form of Peyer's patches, which provide immune surveillance of intestinal contents. Peristalsis moves the remaining liquid chyme through the ileocaecal valve into the colon.

Clinical significance

The ileum is the site most commonly affected by Crohn's disease, whose transmural inflammation can cause strictures and fistulae. Other conditions include Meckel's diverticulum — a congenital outpouching of the ileum present in about 2% of people — tumours such as carcinoid, and infections including ileitis from Yersinia or tuberculosis, which can mimic Crohn's. Surgical removal of long segments of ileum causes malabsorption of vitamin B12 and bile salts.
