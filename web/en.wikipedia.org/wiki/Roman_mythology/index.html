Roman mythology is the body of traditional stories of ancient Rome, combining native Italic and Etruscan religious traditions with an extensive adaptation of Greek mythology. The Romans identified their gods with Greek counterparts — Jupiter with Zeus, Juno with Hera, Neptune with Poseidon, Minerva with Athena, Venus with Aphrodite, Mars with Ares, Diana with Artemis, Apollo keeping his Greek name — while giving them distinctly Roman emphases on state, law, agriculture and war.

Founding myths

Rome's self-understanding rested on foundation legends: the Trojan hero Aeneas, son of Venus, fleeing Troy to found the Roman race in Italy — the subject of Virgil's epic Aeneid (1st century BCE) — and the later-born twins Romulus and Remus, suckled by the she-wolf, of whom Romulus founded the city in 753 BCE and became its first king.

Character and sources

Early Roman religion was notably ritualistic and civic, centred on correct performance (cultus) and on household and agricultural spirits (lares, penates, Janus, Vesta) more than on narrative myth; much of the storytelling was supplied later by poets — Virgil, Ovid (whose Metamorphoses is the single richest source of classical myth in later European art), and Livy, whose history embeds legends such as the rape of the Sabine women and Horatius at the bridge. State religion culminated in the imperial cult. Roman mythology, transmitted through Latin literature, dominated European education and art from the Renaissance onward, and its names survive in the planets and the calendar (January for Janus, March for Mars).
