Plasmodium falciparum

Plasmodium falciparum is a unicellular protozoan parasite and the deadliest of the Plasmodium species that cause malaria in humans. Transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, it is responsible for the great majority of malaria deaths — the disease killed roughly 600,000 people in recent years, most of them young children in sub-Saharan Africa. Unlike P. vivax and P. ovale, it forms no dormant liver stage (hypnozoites), but its blood-stage infection can be rapidly fatal, notably as cerebral malaria.

Life cycle

Sporozoites injected with mosquito saliva travel to the liver, where each invades a hepatocyte and multiplies asexually into tens of thousands of merozoites over about a week. Released into the blood, merozoites invade red blood cells and progress through ring, trophozoite and schizont stages over a 48-hour cycle; schizont rupture releases new merozoites and the pyrogenic debris that causes malaria's fevers. Infected red cells display parasite proteins (notably PfEMP1, encoded by the var gene family) that make them adhere to blood-vessel walls — sequestration — evading the spleen but causing microvascular blockage in severe disease. Some parasites develop into gametocytes, which a mosquito takes up to complete the sexual cycle in its gut.

Disease

Uncomplicated falciparum malaria causes cyclical fever, headache, chills and anaemia; severe malaria may progress to cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, metabolic acidosis and multi-organ failure, and is a medical emergency. P. falciparum can invade red cells of all ages, enabling very high parasite burdens. Human genetic traits such as sickle-cell trait, thalassaemias and G6PD deficiency persist partly because they protect against it.

Treatment and control

Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) are the standard treatment worldwide, though partial artemisinin resistance has emerged in Southeast Asia and, more recently, East Africa; resistance to earlier drugs (chloroquine, sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine) is widespread. Control rests on insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying, rapid diagnosis and treatment; the RTS,S and R21 malaria vaccines — the first vaccines against a human parasite — are now being rolled out for children in endemic regions. The P. falciparum genome (about 23 Mb, extremely AT-rich) was sequenced in 2002.
