A science fiction magazine is a publication that offers primarily science fiction, either in a monthly or quarterly format, in digest size or, formerly, in pulp format. The first magazine devoted wholly to the genre, Hugo Gernsback's Amazing Stories, appeared in April 1926, and for decades the magazines were the centre of the science fiction field — the market where most major authors first published and where fandom coalesced.

Pulp era and the Golden Age

Gernsback coined "scientifiction" and later popularised the term "science fiction"; the Hugo Awards are named for him. Under John W. Campbell, Astounding Science-Fiction (from 1937) launched the genre's Golden Age, publishing early work by Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, A. E. van Vogt and others, and emphasising rigorous extrapolation. Dozens of pulp titles flourished in the 1930s–40s, most vanishing as paperbacks and post-war paper shortages reshaped publishing.

Digest era

Two influential magazines founded in 1949–50, The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction and Galaxy Science Fiction (edited by H. L. Gold and then Frederik Pohl), raised literary standards and broadened subject matter to social satire. In Britain, Michael Moorcock's New Worlds became the banner of the New Wave in the 1960s. Later arrivals included Asimov's Science Fiction (1977), which under Gardner Dozois dominated the Hugo and Nebula awards in the 1980s–90s.

Decline and digital revival

Newsstand circulation fell steadily from the late 20th century; most surviving print magazines converted to subscription and digital models. Online magazines — among them Clarkesworld, Lightspeed, Strange Horizons and Uncanny Magazine — revived the short-fiction market in the 21st century and became major forces in award voting, while translations expanded the field's international reach.
