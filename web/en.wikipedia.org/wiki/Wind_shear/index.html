Wind shear

Wind shear
DefinitionDifference in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance
TypesVertical, horizontal
Notable hazardsMicrobursts, aviation accidents, tornado formation

Wind shear (also written windshear), sometimes referred to as a wind gradient, is a difference in wind speed and/or direction over a relatively short distance in the atmosphere. Atmospheric wind shear is normally described as either vertical or horizontal. Vertical wind shear is a change in wind speed or direction with a change in altitude. Horizontal wind shear is a change in wind speed with a change in lateral position for a given altitude.

Causes

Wind shear arises near weather fronts, where wind changes across a short horizontal distance; near jet streams; around mountains and buildings, which disrupt the flow near the surface; under temperature inversions, where calm air near the ground is capped by strong winds aloft; and in and around thunderstorms, where powerful updraughts and downdraughts sit side by side. A particularly dangerous form is the microburst, a small-scale intense downdraught producing damaging divergent winds at the surface.

Aviation hazard

Low-level wind shear is a serious hazard to aircraft during takeoff and landing, because a sudden change in headwind alters airspeed and lift faster than pilots can respond. Wind-shear-related accidents, including Delta Air Lines Flight 191 at Dallas/Fort Worth in 1985, led to the widespread installation of low-level wind shear alert systems, terminal Doppler weather radar at major airports, and onboard predictive wind-shear warning systems in airliners.

Role in severe weather

Vertical wind shear is a key ingredient in the organization of thunderstorms. Strong shear separates a storm's updraught from its downdraught, allowing long-lived supercells capable of producing large hail and tornadoes; directional shear with height also imparts rotation to the updraught. Conversely, strong vertical shear can tear apart developing tropical cyclones, so hurricane formation and intensification are favoured by low shear environments.

Other effects

Wind shear affects sound propagation, the dispersion of pollutants, sailing and ballistics, and the loads experienced by wind turbines, whose rotors sample different wind speeds across their diameter in sheared flow. Clear-air turbulence at cruising altitude is frequently associated with shear near jet streams.
