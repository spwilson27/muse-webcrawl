Al-Kindi

Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (Arabic: أبو يوسف يعقوب بن إسحاق الكندي; c. 801 – 873), known in Latin as Alkindus, was an Arab Muslim polymath of the Abbasid era, often called "the philosopher of the Arabs". Working in Baghdad's House of Wisdom, he was a leading figure in the introduction of Greek and Hellenistic philosophy into the Islamic world, and made original contributions to mathematics, cryptanalysis, music theory, medicine and optics.

Life and works

Al-Kindi descended from the Arab tribe of Kinda; his father was governor of Kufa. Educated in Baghdad, he was patronised by the caliphs al-Ma'mun, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, and supervised the translation of Greek scientific and philosophical texts. Under al-Mutawakkil his position deteriorated amid court intrigue and his library was confiscated, though later restored. Medieval bibliographers credit him with some 260 treatises, of which only a fraction survive.

Contributions

In philosophy, al-Kindi sought to harmonise Greek thought — Aristotle, Plato and the Neoplatonists — with Islamic theology, defending doctrines such as creation ex nihilo, and his On First Philosophy became foundational for later falasifa such as al-Farabi and Avicenna. In mathematics he wrote on Indian numerals and geometry. His Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages contains the earliest known description of cryptanalysis by frequency analysis, a method that revolutionised codebreaking. He also wrote influential works on music theory, on optics (in Latin as De aspectibus), on pharmacology — including a method for quantifying drug dosage — and on the determination of the qibla.

Legacy

Al-Kindi's translations of vocabulary and concepts created much of the Arabic philosophical lexicon. The lunar crater Al-Kindi is named in his honour.
