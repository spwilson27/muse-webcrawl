Kaifeng Jews

The Kaifeng Jews are members of a small Jewish community that has existed in Kaifeng, in the Henan province of China, for at least eight centuries. They are the best documented of the historical Jewish communities of China, having maintained a synagogue, inscriptions, and communal records from the Song dynasty until the community's effective dissolution in the 19th century.

History

According to the community's own stone inscriptions (stelae), Jewish merchants — probably from Persia or India — arrived in China during the Song dynasty, traditionally dated to the 10th or 11th century, travelling along the Silk Road. In 1163 the community built its synagogue in Kaifeng, then a capital of the Song dynasty. The emperor is said to have welcomed the merchants and permitted them to practice their religion and to adopt Chinese surnames; the community eventually comprised seven principal clans, including the Ai, Gao, Jin, Li, Shi, Zhang, and Zhao families.

The community maintained contact with the wider Jewish world only intermittently. In 1605 the Kaifeng Jew Ai Tian met the Jesuit missionary Matteo Ricci in Beijing, an encounter that first alerted Europeans to the community's existence. Jesuit reports of the 17th and early 18th centuries described the synagogue, its Torah scrolls, and its observances, which included circumcision, abstention from pork, and the celebration of Passover and other festivals.

Decline

Over the centuries the Kaifeng Jews gradually assimilated into Chinese society: they took Chinese names, entered the civil service examination system, intermarried, and adopted Confucian ancestor customs alongside Jewish practice. The last rabbi died in the early 19th century, knowledge of Hebrew was lost, and the synagogue, damaged by Yellow River floods, was finally demolished in the 1860s. Its Torah scrolls were sold to Western collectors.

Present day

Today several hundred people in Kaifeng identify as descendants of the Jewish community. Some have sought to reconnect with Judaism, studying Hebrew and Jewish practice, though their status is a matter of ongoing religious and political debate. The site of the old synagogue and a small exhibition hall commemorate the community's history.
