Ensign (flag)

An ensign is a flag flown at the stern of a vessel to indicate its nationality, or a national flag variant used for a particular purpose. The term comes from the French enseigne, a sign or badge. In British tradition, which many other countries have followed, three ensigns emerged: the Red Ensign for merchant ships, the White Ensign for the Royal Navy, and the Blue Ensign for government vessels and certain organisations.

British ensigns

The modern system dates from 1864, when an Order in Council rationalised the coloured squadron ensigns of the Royal Navy. The Red Ensign (the "Red Duster") became the civil ensign of the United Kingdom and remains the flag of British merchant shipping. The White Ensign, bearing the red cross of St George with the Union Flag in the canton, is reserved to the Royal Navy and the Royal Yacht Squadron. The Blue Ensign is flown by government departments and, when defaced with a badge, by many yacht clubs and public bodies.

Use in the Commonwealth

British colonies and dominions adopted ensigns defaced with their local arms, giving rise to flags such as the Canadian Red Ensign (used until 1965) and the colonial ensigns from which the national flags of Australia and New Zealand developed — both remain defaced Blue Ensigns. Many British overseas territories still use defaced ensigns as their civil flags.

Other countries

Several other states distinguish civil, state and naval ensigns. Some countries, such as the United States, use the national flag for all purposes, while others fly distinct naval ensigns with different proportions or additional emblems, like the naval ensigns of Japan, India and the Nordic states (whose naval ensigns are swallow-tailed).
