Milton Friedman (31 July 1912 – 16 November 2006) was an American economist and statistician at the University of Chicago, the leading figure of the Chicago school and of Monetarism. He received the 1976 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work on consumption analysis and monetary history and theory.

Ideas and career

In A Theory of the Consumption Function (1957) Friedman proposed the permanent-income hypothesis. With Anna Schwartz, A Monetary History of the United States (1963) argued the Great Depression was caused by the Federal Reserve's failure to prevent a collapse in the money supply — transforming understanding of central banking. He advocated a steady money-growth rule instead of discretionary policy, argued Inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon", and recast the Phillips curve with an expectations-augmented natural rate of unemployment.

As a public intellectual (Capitalism and Freedom, 1962; the television series Free to Choose, 1980) Friedman championed school vouchers, a negative income tax, floating exchange rates, deregulation and an all-volunteer army. His ideas influenced the policies of the Reagan and Thatcher governments and central banks worldwide, though the money-targeting regime he inspired was largely abandoned in practice by the 1990s.
