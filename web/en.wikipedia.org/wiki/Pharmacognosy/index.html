Pharmacognosy

Pharmacognosy is the study of drugs obtained from natural sources, principally plants, but also animals, fungi, marine organisms, and minerals. The term, from the Greek pharmakon (drug) and gnosis (knowledge), was coined by Johann Adam Schmidt in 1811 and popularised by C. A. Seydler's 1815 dissertation Analecta Pharmacognostica. The American Society of Pharmacognosy defines the field as "the study of the physical, chemical, biochemical, and biological properties of drugs, drug substances, or potential drugs of natural origin as well as the search for new drugs from natural sources."

Scope and methods

Classical pharmacognosy dealt with crude drugs — dried plant parts such as foxglove leaf, cinchona bark, and opium — their identification (macroscopic, microscopic, and chemical), cultivation, collection, and standardisation. Modern pharmacognosy adds phytochemistry, bioassay-guided fractionation, genomics of producing organisms, and marine and microbial natural products.[1] A large share of modern medicines derive from natural products: morphine from the opium poppy, quinine from cinchona, digoxin from foxglove, artemisinin from Artemisia annua (work recognised by Tu Youyou's 2015 Nobel Prize), the anticancer drug paclitaxel from the Pacific yew, and the statins from fungi.

Relationship to pharmacopoeias

Pharmacognostic monographs underpin the quality standards published in pharmacopoeias, which specify identity tests and assays for herbal drugs and their preparations. The discipline remains a core subject in pharmacy curricula and in the regulation of herbal medicines and dietary supplements.
