The 1929 Grand Banks earthquake (also called the Laurentian Slope earthquake) occurred on 18 November 1929 at 20:32 UTC in the Atlantic Ocean off the south coast of Newfoundland. The magnitude 7.2 earthquake triggered a large submarine landslide that severed a series of transatlantic telegraph cables and generated a tsunami that devastated communities on the Burin Peninsula of Newfoundland, killing 27 or 28 people — the deadliest tsunami in Canadian history.

Earthquake and landslide

The epicentre was on the continental slope south of the Grand Banks, near the Laurentian Channel. The shock was felt as far away as Montreal and New York City. The earthquake dislodged an estimated 200 cubic kilometres of sediment, which raced down the continental slope as a turbidity current. Twelve transatlantic telegraph cables were cut in 28 places; the sequence and timing of the breaks allowed scientists to estimate the speed of the turbidity current, providing the first direct evidence for these powerful underwater flows and a landmark in the development of marine geology.

Tsunami

The tsunami struck the Burin Peninsula in three main waves, arriving with heights locally exceeding 13 metres in narrow inlets such as Taylor's Bay and Port au Bras. Twenty-seven people died on the peninsula (a 28th victim died of injuries some years later), dozens of communities were damaged, and many homes, fishing stages and boats were swept away. Damage was also recorded in Nova Scotia. The disaster occurred in the same month as the Wall Street Crash, hampering relief fundraising; the Newfoundland government and public donations in Canada supported reconstruction.

Legacy

The event led to lasting improvements in understanding submarine landslides and tsunami risk on Canada's Atlantic coast, and the cable-break data remain a classic case study in oceanography. It remains the strongest recorded earthquake in the region and the benchmark for tsunami hazard assessment in Atlantic Canada.
