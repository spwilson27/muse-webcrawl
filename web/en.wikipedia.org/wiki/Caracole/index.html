Caracole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The caracole was a cavalry manoeuvre of the mid-16th to early 17th century in which successive ranks of pistol-armed horsemen rode up to the enemy, discharged their firearms, and wheeled away to reload, allowing a rolling fire without a charge. It is most closely associated with the German Reiter (Schwarze Reiter) mercenary cavalry.

Description

A squadron formed in many ranks — sometimes ten or more deep. The front rank trotted or cantered within pistol range of the enemy (often pike blocks), fired one or both of its wheellock pistols, then split left and right, filing to the rear to reload while the next rank came forward. In theory this maintained continuous fire; in practice, firing from a moving horse at range had limited effect against armoured pikemen, and the manoeuvre could degenerate into disorder if counter-charged.

Rise and decline

The caracole emerged in the mid-1500s as cavalry traded the lance for pairs of pistols. Its effectiveness is debated — against other cavalry and disordered infantry it could work well — but commanders such as Henry IV of France and Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden preferred decisive charges with the sword at the gallop, and the caracole fell from favour in the early 1600s. The word comes from the Spanish caracol, "snail", describing the wheeling movement, and survives in dressage for a half-turn movement.
