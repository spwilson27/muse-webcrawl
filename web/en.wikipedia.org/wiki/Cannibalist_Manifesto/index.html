Cannibalist Manifesto

The Cannibalist Manifesto (Portuguese: Manifesto Antropófago) is a 1928 manifesto by the Brazilian writer Oswald de Andrade, a central text of Brazilian modernism. Published in the first issue of the Revista de Antropofagia (Magazine of Cannibalism), it proposed that Brazilian culture should "cannibalise" European and foreign influences — devouring them and transforming them into something authentically Brazilian.

Background

The manifesto emerged from the modernist ferment that followed São Paulo's Week of Modern Art in 1922. Its immediate inspiration was Abaporu, a painting by Oswald's wife Tarsila do Amaral, whose title — from Tupi words meaning "man who eats people" — suggested the movement's name. Andrade took the metaphor from the ritual cannibalism of the Tupí people, who were believed to eat their enemies to absorb their strength.

Content

Written in aphoristic fragments mixing Portuguese, French and English, the manifesto famously begins "Tupi or not tupi, that is the question" — a pun on Shakespeare. It rejects both slavish imitation of Europe and nostalgic nationalism, urging Brazil to absorb foreign culture critically and produce something new. "Only anthropophagy unites us," Andrade wrote, "socially, economically, philosophically."

Legacy

Antropofagia became a foundational concept in Brazilian art and thought. It was revived in the 1960s by the Tropicália movement in music and by artists such as Hélio Oiticica and Lygia Clark, and remains influential in postcolonial theory as a model of creative cultural appropriation.
