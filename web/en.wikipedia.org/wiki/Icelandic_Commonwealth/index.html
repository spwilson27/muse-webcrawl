Icelandic Commonwealth

The Icelandic Commonwealth, also known as the Icelandic Free State (Icelandic: Þjóðveldið Ísland), was the political structure that existed in Iceland between the establishment of the Althing in 930 and the signing of the Old Covenant (Gamli sáttmáli) in 1262, which brought Iceland under the authority of the Norwegian Crown. The Commonwealth period is remarkable in European history for a society that had a national parliament and a developed legal system but no king, no executive branch, and no standing army.

Establishment

Iceland was settled during the Age of Settlement (c. 870–930), principally by Norse migrants, many of whom were leaving Norway in part to escape the centralising rule of King Harald Fairhair. Around 930 the leading settlers established the Althing, a general assembly held annually for two weeks each summer at Þingvellir in south-western Iceland. A body of law, traditionally associated with Úlfljótr and modelled partly on the Norwegian Gulathing law, was adopted, and the island was divided into quarters, each with its own spring assembly.

Political structure

Power in the Commonwealth rested with chieftains called goðar (singular goði), who held a chieftaincy (goðorð) that was a form of property: it could be inherited, bought, sold, lent, or shared. Free farmers (bændur) were required to attach themselves to a goði as his thingmen (þingmenn), but they were free to change allegiance. At its fullest development there were 39 goðorð, later increased to 48 seats in the legislative court.

The only national official was the law speaker (lögsögumaðr), elected for a three-year term, who presided over the Althing and was required to recite the entire body of law aloud over the course of his term. The legislative and judicial work was done by the Lögrétta, composed of the goðar and their advisers, and by a system of courts: quarter courts at the Althing and, after about 1005, a Fifth Court (fimmtardómr) acting as a court of appeal.

Law and enforcement

The Commonwealth had no public executive: judgments of the courts, including outlawry, had to be enforced by the successful litigant himself, usually with the support of his goði and kin. This reliance on private enforcement, feud, and negotiated settlement is vividly depicted in the Sagas of Icelanders, such as Njáls saga and Egils saga. The laws were eventually written down in the collection known as Grágás ("Grey Goose Laws"), first committed to writing in the winter of 1117–1118.

Christianisation

In 999 or 1000 the Althing faced a crisis between Christian and pagan factions. The law speaker Þorgeir Þorkelsson, himself a pagan, retired under a cloak for a day and a night and then announced a compromise: Icelanders would all be baptised and publicly Christian, while private pagan practice — including infant exposure and the eating of horse meat — would temporarily remain permitted. Iceland thus adopted Christianity by legal decision rather than by war. The Church later became a major landowner, and two bishoprics were established, at Skálholt (1056) and Hólar (1106).

Decline and fall

During the 13th century — the Age of the Sturlungs — the goðorð became concentrated in the hands of a few powerful families, including the Sturlungs, Haukdælir, Ásbirningar, Vatnsfirðingar, Svínfellingar, and Oddaverjar. Their armed rivalries, increasingly entangled with the ambitions of the Norwegian king Haakon IV, produced civil war, with episodes such as the Battle of Örlygsstaðir (1238) and the burning at Flugumýri (1253). Exhausted by the conflict, Icelandic chieftains and assemblies accepted the Old Covenant (Gamli sáttmáli) in 1262–1264, swearing allegiance and taxation to the Norwegian Crown in return for peace, legal continuity, and regular shipping. Jónsbók, a new law code issued in 1281, completed the transition to royal rule.

Legacy

The Commonwealth period is the setting of most of the Icelandic sagas and is often regarded in Iceland as a golden age of independence. Modern Iceland traces the Althing of 930 as the origin of its parliament, making it one of the oldest parliamentary institutions in the world. The period has also attracted attention from political theorists and economists as a historical example of a stateless legal order.
