The Advanced Photo System (APS) is a film format for still photography introduced in 1996 by Kodak, Fujifilm, Canon, Minolta, and Nikon. It was the last major new consumer film format before digital photography displaced film, and its 24 mm film (designated IX240) was marketed under names including Advantix (Kodak), Nexia (Fujifilm), and Nuvis (Minolta).

Features

APS film came in a sealed drop-in cartridge that eliminated manual threading and protected the film from light and dust; processed negatives were returned inside the cartridge rather than as cut strips. The film carried a magnetic (and, in a cheaper variant, optical) data layer on which cameras recorded exposure and print instructions ("information exchange", IX). A defining feature was selectable aspect ratios on the same roll: C (classic, 3:2), H (HDTV, 16:9), and P (panoramic, 3:1), recorded as full-frame exposures with cropping instructions to the printer.

Reception and decline

Consumers welcomed the easy loading and compact cameras, but the smaller negative (roughly 56% of the area of a 35 mm frame) gave inferior enlargement quality, processing cost more, and the format could not be used in existing cameras. The rapid rise of digital cameras after 2000 ended APS growth; the last manufacturers ceased APS film production in 2011. The term "APS-C", used for a common digital sensor size, survives as a legacy of the format's Classic frame dimensions.
