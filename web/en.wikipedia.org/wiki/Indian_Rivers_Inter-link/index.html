Indian Rivers Inter-link

The Indian Rivers Inter-link (also called the National River Linking Project, NRLP) is a large-scale civil engineering proposal to connect the rivers of India through a network of canals and reservoirs, transferring water from basins considered "surplus" — mainly the Himalayan rivers and the flood-prone northeast — to "deficit" regions in western and peninsular India. Proponents argue it would mitigate floods and droughts, expand irrigation and generate hydropower; critics cite enormous cost, ecological damage, displacement and inter-state and international disputes.

History of the idea

Ideas for inter-basin transfer date to the 19th century, when Arthur Cotton proposed linking south Indian rivers for navigation and irrigation, and were revived by K. L. Rao's Ganga–Cauvery link proposal (1972) and Captain Dinshaw Dastur's "Garland Canal" (1977). The National Water Development Agency (NWDA) was set up in 1982 to study the scheme, which eventually crystallised into 30 links: 14 in the Himalayan component and 16 in the Peninsular component, involving some 15,000 km of canals.

Implementation

Successive governments endorsed the project, and the Supreme Court of India in 2012 directed its timely implementation. The Ken–Betwa link, connecting two tributaries of the Yamuna in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, became the first to receive full clearances and construction approval in the 2020s; other links such as Godavari–Cauvery remain in planning amid objections from states and environmentalists. State-level projects, including Andhra Pradesh's Pattiseema lift scheme (2015) linking the Godavari and Krishna, have been presented as precursors.

Criticism

Opponents question the assumption that donor basins have surplus water, note the energy cost of lifting water over the Deccan plateau, and warn of impacts on deltas, forests, wildlife corridors and downstream communities, as well as diplomatic complications with Bangladesh, Nepal and Bhutan.
