The Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) is a benthopelagic fish of the family Gadidae, widely consumed by humans. It lives in the cold waters of the North Atlantic, from the east coast of North America to the Barents Sea, and is one of the most historically important food fish in the world. Recognisable by its chin barbel, mottled greenish-brown colouring and prominent lateral line, it commonly grows to about a metre, though individuals approaching two metres and 100 kg have been recorded.

Biology

Atlantic cod inhabit waters from the shoreline down to 600 m, often near the bottom. They are opportunistic predators, feeding on smaller fish such as capelin and herring, crustaceans and molluscs. Spawning occurs in winter and early spring in particular offshore grounds (such as the Arcto-Norwegian stock's Lofoten fishery); a large female can release several million eggs, which drift in surface currents. Cod can live for more than 20 years, though fishing pressure has greatly reduced average ages.

Fishery and collapse

Cod fisheries sustained communities around the North Atlantic for centuries — the dried and salted product (stockfish and bacalao) was a staple trade good, and the Grand Banks off Newfoundland were legendary for their abundance. Industrial trawling after the Second World War caused catastrophic overfishing: in 1992 Canada declared a moratorium on the northern cod stock, throwing tens of thousands out of work, and the stock has recovered only slowly. North-east Atlantic stocks (Iceland, Barents Sea) have fared better under quota management, and the species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Cod remains a classic fish for fish and chips, and its liver yields cod liver oil.
