The United States Department of the Interior (DOI) is the federal executive department responsible for the conservation and management of most federal land and natural resources, and for the administration of programmes relating to Native Americans, Alaska Natives, Native Hawaiians, and the insular areas of the United States. Because of its wide range of responsibilities it was historically nicknamed the "Department of Everything Else."

History

The department was created on 3 March 1849, the eve of Zachary Taylor's inauguration, consolidating the General Land Office, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the Patent Office, and pension offices from other departments. Over the 19th and 20th centuries it spun off functions such as patents and education while acquiring land management and scientific agencies. Yellowstone, the world's first national park, was placed under its supervision in 1872, and the National Park Service was founded in 1916.

Bureaus and offices

Major components include the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the Bureau of Reclamation, the U.S. Geological Survey, the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement, and the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement. Together its agencies manage roughly 500 million acres of public land, 56 million acres of Indian trust land, and extensive offshore energy resources.

Secretaries

Notable secretaries include Harold Ickes, who served through the New Deal and Second World War; Stewart Udall, associated with the 1960s conservation movement; and Deb Haaland, who in 2021 became the first Native American cabinet secretary in U.S. history. The secretary is eighth in the presidential line of succession.
