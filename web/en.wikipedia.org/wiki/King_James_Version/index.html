The King James Version (KJV), also called the Authorized Version, is an English translation of the Christian Bible commissioned in 1604 by King James I and first published in 1611. Prepared by 47 scholars working in six companies at Westminster, Oxford, and Cambridge, it drew heavily on the Bishops' Bible and William Tyndale's earlier translation. It became the standard Bible of English-speaking Protestantism and one of the most influential books in the English language.

Background

At the Hampton Court Conference of 1604, Puritan leader John Reynolds proposed a new translation, and James—disliking the popular Geneva Bible's marginal notes, which he saw as seditious—approved the project. Translators worked from the Hebrew Masoretic Text and the Greek Textus Receptus, consulting earlier English versions and the Latin Vulgate.

Language and influence

The KJV's deliberate archaism and rhythmic prose shaped English literature for centuries, coining or popularizing phrases such as "salt of the earth," "a thorn in the flesh," "the powers that be," and "let there be light." Although later revisions culminated in the standardized 1769 Oxford text, the 1611 translation remains in print and is the exclusive Bible of the "King James Only" movement.

Reception

Initial reception was mixed, but by the 18th century the KJV had displaced rivals. It was carried throughout the British Empire and became foundational in American religious culture—famously placed in hotel rooms by the Gideons. During the same century that saw the KJV consolidated, England also witnessed the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, an event linked to the anti-Catholic climate in which the translation was produced.
