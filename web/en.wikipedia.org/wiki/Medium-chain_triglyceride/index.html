Medium-chain triglyceride

Medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) are triglycerides with two or three fatty acids having an aliphatic tail of 6–12 carbon atoms, i.e. medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs). Rich food sources for commercial extraction of MCTs include palm kernel oil and coconut oil. MCTs are commonly used in medical nutrition, sports nutrition, and ketogenic diets.

Sources and production

MCT oil is produced by fractionation of coconut or palm kernel oil, isolating the caprylic (C8) and capric (C10) triglycerides (and sometimes caproic (C6) and lauric (C12) acids). The resulting oil is clear, nearly tasteless, and liquid at room temperature. Milk fat and some other foods contain smaller proportions of medium-chain fatty acids.

Digestion and metabolism

Unlike long-chain triglycerides, MCTs are digested rapidly: they require little bile-salt and pancreatic-lipase action, pass from the gastrointestinal tract into the portal vein, and are transported directly to the liver, where they are readily oxidised for energy or converted to ketone bodies. They are therefore less likely to be stored as body fat and are more ketogenic than long-chain fats.

Medical uses

MCTs are used in enteral and parenteral nutrition for patients with fat malabsorption, pancreatic insufficiency, short bowel syndrome, and certain fat-oxidation disorders. MCT-based ketogenic diets are an established therapy for drug-resistant epilepsy in children, and are also used in the dietary management of disorders such as glucose transporter type 1 deficiency. Excess intake may cause gastrointestinal symptoms such as cramping and diarrhoea.
