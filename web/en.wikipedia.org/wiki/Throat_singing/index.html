Throat singing

Labelled placeholder: a throat singer performing in Tuva.

Throat singing refers to a set of singing practices in which a single vocalist produces two or more distinct pitches simultaneously by manipulating the resonances of the vocal tract. The best-known traditions are found among the peoples of Central Asia — including Tuvan khoomei, Mongolian khöömii, and related styles in Altai, Khakassia and Buryatia — but analogous techniques exist elsewhere, including Inuit katajjaq and Sardinian cantu a tenore.

Technique

By tightening the throat, shaping the tongue and lips, and selecting a single harmonic of a low drone fundamental, the singer brings out a flute-like overtone melody above the drone. Main Tuvan styles include khoomei (a soft, wind-like tone), sygyt (a piercing whistle-like overtone) and kargyraa (a deep, growling style engaging the ventricular folds).

Cultural context

In Tuva and Mongolia throat singing is traditionally linked to herding life and the imitation of natural sounds — wind, water and animals. In 2009, Mongolian and Chinese throat singing traditions were inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Inuit throat singing

Inuit katajjaq, traditionally performed by pairs of women standing face to face in a rhythmic vocal game, uses breath sounds and voiced and unvoiced timbres rather than overtone melodies, and ends when one performer laughs or runs out of breath.
