ΔT

ΔT (delta T) is, in astronomy and timekeeping, the difference between two time scales: the uniform Terrestrial Time (TT) and Universal Time (UT1), which is based on the actual rotation of the Earth:

ΔT = TT − UT1

Because the Earth's rotation is irregular and is gradually slowing owing to tidal friction, UT1 drifts against uniform time scales, and ΔT slowly increases. In the early 2020s ΔT was approximately 69 seconds.

Why it matters

ΔT is needed to compute where in the sky celestial events — especially solar and lunar eclipses — were or will be visible from a given place on Earth. Retrodicting ancient eclipses recorded in Babylonian, Chinese, Greek, and medieval sources is one of the principal ways ΔT is measured for centuries before telescopic observations.

Related quantities

ΔT must not be confused with the difference TT − TAI (fixed at 32.184 s) or with the quantity DUT1 (UT1 − UTC) used in time-signal broadcasts. The insertion of leap seconds into UTC keeps civil time within 0.9 s of UT1, but does not affect ΔT itself.
