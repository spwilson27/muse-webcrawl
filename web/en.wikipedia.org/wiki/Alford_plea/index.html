An Alford plea is a guilty plea in United States criminal court in which the defendant does not admit to having committed the crime and maintains innocence, but acknowledges that the prosecution's evidence would likely persuade a judge or jury of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant is then treated as though he had pleaded guilty in the ordinary way and is sentenced accordingly.

Origin

The plea takes its name from North Carolina v. Alford (1970). Henry Alford had been charged with first-degree murder, an offense then punishable by death in North Carolina if a jury convicted. He pleaded guilty to second-degree murder while insisting he was innocent, explaining that he pleaded to avoid the risk of a death sentence. The Supreme Court upheld his conviction, holding that a guilty plea may be accepted even when accompanied by protestations of innocence, provided it is voluntary, informed, and supported by a strong factual basis.

Use

Alford pleas are available in most U.S. jurisdictions, though a few states and the federal system restrict or disfavor them; Indiana, for example, does not permit them in death-penalty cases, and military courts do not accept them. Judges are generally required to find a factual basis for the plea and may refuse to accept one. The plea has been used in prominent cases, including that of the West Memphis Three, who were released in 2011 under Alford pleas after nearly two decades in prison.

Comparison with nolo contendere

Unlike a nolo contendere plea, an Alford plea is formally a guilty plea and generally can be used as an admission in related contexts; both allow a defendant to accept punishment while maintaining innocence. See also arraignment.
