Anterior interosseous nerve

From: Median nerve

Innervates: Flexor pollicis longus, flexor digitorum profundus (lateral half), pronator quadratus

The anterior interosseous nerve (also called the volar interosseous nerve) is a branch of the median nerve that supplies the deep muscles of the front of the forearm. It is essentially a purely motor nerve, accompanied along its course by the anterior interosseous artery, a branch of the common interosseous artery.

Course

The nerve arises from the median nerve in the proximal forearm, just distal to the elbow, and descends on the anterior surface of the interosseous membrane between the radius and ulna, deep to the flexor digitorum profundus. It ends in the pronator quadratus near the wrist, sending small articular branches to the wrist joint.

Function

It supplies the flexor pollicis longus, the lateral (radial) half of the flexor digitorum profundus serving the index and middle fingers, and the pronator quadratus. Its function is tested by asking the patient to make an "OK" sign: weakness of the flexor pollicis longus and the index-finger profundus produces a flattened, triangular pinch instead of a round circle.

Clinical significance

Anterior interosseous syndrome (Kiloh–Nevin syndrome) is a compression neuropathy or neuritis of the nerve, producing weakness of pinch without any sensory loss, since the nerve carries no cutaneous branches. Causes include compression by fibrous bands or accessory muscles such as the Gantzer muscle, forearm fractures, and neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage–Turner syndrome). Management may be conservative, with surgical decompression considered for cases that fail to recover.
