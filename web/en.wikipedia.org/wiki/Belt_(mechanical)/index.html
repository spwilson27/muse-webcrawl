Belt (mechanical)

[Image placeholder: An open flat belt drive connecting two pulleys in a historic machine shop]

A belt is a loop of flexible material used to link two or more rotating shafts mechanically, most often in parallel. Belts may be used as a source of motion, to transmit power efficiently between pulleys, or to track relative movement. In most applications the belt is looped over pulleys — also called sheaves — with the pulleys rotating in the same direction for an open belt drive, or in opposite directions for a crossed belt drive.

Types

Flat belts have a rectangular cross-section and run on flat-faced pulleys; they were historically made of leather and were ubiquitous in line-shaft factories. Round belts have a circular cross-section and run in grooved pulleys. V-belts have a trapezoidal cross-section that wedges into a matching pulley groove, greatly increasing friction and power capacity; multiple V-belts are often run side by side. Multi-groove (poly-V) belts combine many small ribs in one flat-backed belt and are used as serpentine belts in automobile engines. Timing belts (synchronous or toothed belts) carry moulded teeth that engage grooves in the pulleys, giving a fixed speed ratio without slip — used in camshaft drives and positioning systems. Spring belts and link belts are used for light and fractional-horsepower drives.

Mechanics

Friction belt drives transmit power through friction between belt and pulley; the tension difference between the tight and slack sides provides the tangential driving force. Belt speed and pulley diameters set the speed ratio. Slip and creep cause small losses of exact ratio in friction drives, while synchronous belts avoid slip entirely. Belt drives offer advantages over gears and chains: they tolerate misalignment, absorb shock and vibration, are quiet, cheap, and require no lubrication; their disadvantages include limited life, sensitivity to heat and oil, and lower maximum power than chain drives of comparable size.

History

Belt drives were known in ancient China for spinning machinery and appear in the work of Hero of Alexandria. The leather flat belt became a defining technology of the Industrial Revolution, transmitting power from central steam engines through overhead line shafts to individual machines. The invention of the V-belt in 1917 by John Gates of the Gates Rubber Company transformed automotive and industrial drives, and synthetic rubber toothed belts followed in the mid-twentieth century.
