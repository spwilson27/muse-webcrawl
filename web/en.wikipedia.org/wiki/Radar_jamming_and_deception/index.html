Radar jamming and deception

Radar jamming and deception is a form of electronic countermeasure (ECM) used in electronic warfare. Radar jamming deliberately radiates radio frequency signals to interfere with the operation of an enemy radar by saturating its receiver with noise or false information; radar deception uses techniques such as chaff, decoys and repeaters to create misleading targets or conceal real ones.

History

Radar countermeasures appeared almost as soon as radar itself. During the Second World War, both sides developed noise jammers and "Window" — clouds of aluminium strips, later called chaff — first used on a large scale in the Hamburg raid of July 1943, which blinded German air-defence radars. The cat-and-mouse contest between radar designers and jammer designers, sometimes called the "Wizard War", continued through the Cold War and into modern networked air defence.

Jamming techniques

Noise jamming floods the radar's frequency band with energy, raising the noise floor so real echoes are hidden; barrage jamming covers a wide band, while spot jamming concentrates power on one frequency. Deception jamming instead returns plausible but false echoes: range gate pull-off gradually shifts the apparent range of a tracked target until the radar's tracking gate loses the real echo, and similar techniques manipulate velocity and angle tracking. Modern jammers use digital radio frequency memory (DRFM) to capture, modify and retransmit the radar's own pulses coherently.

Deception and decoys

Chaff creates a cloud of slowly falling reflectors that masks aircraft or generates false targets. Towed and expendable decoys, such as miniature air-launched decoys, imitate the radar signature of real aircraft to draw missiles away. Ships use offboard decoys like Nulka, which hover while broadcasting a ship-like signature.

Counter-countermeasures

Radars resist jamming with frequency agility, pulse compression, sidelobe blanking, monopulse tracking and burn-through power. The balance between ECM and ECCM remains a central contest in modern air warfare.
