Agricultural economics

Agricultural economics is an applied field of economics concerned with the application of economic theory to optimising the production and distribution of food, fibre, and other agricultural products. Originally focused on land use and farm management, the field has expanded to cover food systems, rural development, agricultural policy, international trade in farm goods, natural resource and environmental economics, and the economics of food consumption and nutrition.

Scope and topics

Major topics include:

  • Production economics and farm management — input use, productivity, technology adoption, and farm size.
  • Agricultural markets and prices — commodity markets, futures, marketing margins, and price volatility.
  • Agricultural policy — subsidies, trade policy, food security, and safety nets.
  • Development economics — agriculture's role in structural transformation and poverty reduction in low-income countries.
  • Resource and environmental economics — land, water, soil, and the environmental impacts of farming, including climate change.
  • Food economics — consumer behaviour, food safety, nutrition, and supply chains.

History

The field's roots lie in nineteenth-century farm management studies and works such as von Thünen's model of agricultural land use (1826). It became institutionalised in the early twentieth century, especially in U.S. land-grant universities, with professional associations such as the Agricultural and Applied Economics Association (founded 1919) and the International Association of Agricultural Economists (1929). Theodore W. Schultz, a leading figure, won the 1979 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for work on human capital and agriculture in development.

A distinctive empirical regularity is the long-run decline in real agricultural prices and the "farm problem": because demand for food is price- and income-inelastic, productivity growth lowers farm incomes, prompting persistent policy intervention in many countries. King’s law describes how bumper harvests can reduce total farm revenue.

Methods and outlets

Agricultural economists use econometrics, optimisation, and increasingly experimental and big-data methods. Major journals include the American Journal of Agricultural Economics, Agricultural Economics, the Journal of Agricultural Economics, and Food Policy.
