Clerestory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In architecture, a clerestory (pronounced KLEER-store-ee) is a high section of wall that contains windows above eye level. Its purpose is to admit light, fresh air, or both to the centre of a large space whose lower walls are obstructed by aisles, roofs, or adjoining structures.

The term derives from the Middle English clerestorie, from the idea of a "clear storey" — a storey of windows that is clear (unobstructed) above the roof of the aisle below.

History

Clerestory lighting was used in ancient Egypt, where the central hall of the temple of Karnak was raised above the surrounding roof slabs so that light entered through grilles between the columns. Roman basilicas and baths made extensive use of raised central roofs with windows, a scheme inherited by early Christian churches such as Old St Peter's in Rome.

In Romanesque and especially Gothic architecture, the nave wall was organised into three levels — arcade, triforium, and clerestory — and the clerestory grew in size as buttressing techniques allowed ever larger windows, culminating in the vast glazed clerestories of cathedrals such as Amiens and Beauvais.

Other uses

The principle survives in industrial "sawtooth" and monitor roofs, in railway carriages and double-decker buses with raised central roof sections, and in modern houses designed for passive ventilation and daylighting, where clerestory windows admit light while preserving privacy and wall space.
