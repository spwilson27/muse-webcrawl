Geotextile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geotextiles are permeable fabrics which, when used in association with soil, have the ability to separate, filter, reinforce, protect or drain. Typically made from polypropylene or polyester, they are among the most widely used geosynthetic products in civil engineering.

Types

Geotextiles come in three basic forms: woven fabrics, made by interlacing yarns on a loom and offering high tensile strength; nonwoven fabrics, made by bonding fibres mechanically (needle-punching), thermally or chemically, and commonly used for filtration and drainage; and knitted fabrics. Natural-fibre geotextiles of jute or coir are used for temporary erosion control where biodegradation is acceptable or desired.

Functions and applications

As separators, geotextiles prevent road aggregate from mixing with soft subgrade soils, extending pavement life. As filters, they let water pass while retaining soil particles, for example behind retaining walls and in French drains. High-strength woven geotextiles provide reinforcement in embankments over soft ground and in steepened slopes, and heavy fabrics give protection to geomembrane liners in landfills. They are also used for coastal erosion control, silt fences on construction sites, railway ballast stabilisation and container drainage in horticulture.

History

The modern geotextile industry grew in the 1950s–70s from filter fabrics used in Dutch Delta Works and American erosion-control projects; the term "geotextile" was popularised at a 1977 Paris conference, and the products are now specified by standard test methods of ASTM and ISO.
