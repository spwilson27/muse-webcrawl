Bioorganometallic chemistry

Bioorganometallic chemistry
FieldOrganometallic chemistry, biochemistry, inorganic chemistry
Key examplesVitamin B12, hydrogenases, nitrogenase, cisplatin

Bioorganometallic chemistry is the study of biologically active molecules that contain carbon directly bonded to metals or metalloids. The importance of metal–carbon bonds in enzymatic and medicinal processes is a relatively recent development in a field that long treated organometallic chemistry and biology as separate domains. Bioorganometallic compounds occur naturally, as in vitamin B12 and several metalloenzymes, and are also developed synthetically as drugs and imaging agents.

Naturally occurring bioorganometallics

The most prominent natural example is cobalamin (vitamin B12), whose cobalt–carbon bond participates in rearrangement and methyl-transfer reactions in enzymes such as methionine synthase. Hydrogenases—enzymes that catalyze the reversible oxidation of molecular hydrogen—contain iron or nickel–iron active sites ligated by carbon monoxide and cyanide, ligands characteristic of organometallic chemistry. The iron–molybdenum cofactor of nitrogenase, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen, contains an interstitial carbide, a carbon atom bound within a cluster of metal atoms. Methylmercury, by contrast, is a toxic bioorganometallic formed by microbial methylation of mercury.

Medicinal applications

Synthetic organometallic compounds are explored as pharmaceuticals. The platinum drug cisplatin—strictly a coordination compound rather than an organometallic—pioneered metal-based chemotherapy, and organometallic successors such as ruthenium arene complexes and ferrocene derivatives (for example ferroquine and ferrocifen) have been investigated as anticancer and antimalarial agents. Metal carbonyl complexes are used in radiopharmacy and explored as carbon-monoxide-releasing molecules.
