Allotropes of phosphorus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Samples of white, red and violet phosphorus

Phosphorus, like several other elements, exists in multiple allotropes — structurally distinct forms with different physical and chemical properties. The most common are white phosphorus, red phosphorus and black phosphorus; violet (Hittorf's) phosphorus and scarlet phosphorus are also well characterised.

White phosphorus

White phosphorus consists of tetrahedral P₄ molecules. It is a waxy, translucent solid that glows in the dark (chemiluminescence), ignites spontaneously in air near 30 °C, and is highly toxic. It is stored under water and was historically used in matches and incendiary weapons.

Red and black phosphorus

Red phosphorus is a polymeric, amorphous form produced by heating white phosphorus; it is far less reactive and is used on matchbox striking surfaces. Black phosphorus, made under high pressure, has a layered graphite-like structure, is the most thermodynamically stable allotrope, and its monolayer form, phosphorene, is studied as a semiconductor. Violet phosphorus has a complex monoclinic structure of cross-linked tubes of P₈ and P₉ units.
