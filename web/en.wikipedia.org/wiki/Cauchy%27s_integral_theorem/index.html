Cauchy's integral theorem

Cauchy's integral theorem (also known as the Cauchy–Goursat theorem) in complex analysis, named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy (and Édouard Goursat), is an important statement in the theory of contour integrals of holomorphic functions in the complex plane. It states that if a function is holomorphic (complex differentiable) throughout a simply connected domain, then the integral of the function along any closed contour lying in that domain is zero.

Statement

Let U be a simply connected open subset of the complex plane ℂ, and let f be a holomorphic function on U. Then for every closed rectifiable curve γ in U,

∮γ f(z) dz = 0.

Equivalently, the integral of f between two points in U is independent of the path chosen between them, provided the path stays within the domain.

History

Cauchy proved a version of the theorem in the 1820s under the assumption that the derivative f′ is continuous. In 1900, Édouard Goursat proved the theorem without that assumption — a crucial refinement, since the existence of a continuous derivative is not known a priori for holomorphic functions — using the technique of subdividing a triangle (or rectangle) into ever smaller pieces. This result is often called the Cauchy–Goursat theorem.

Consequences

The integral theorem is the foundation of much of complex analysis. Together with its local converse, it implies that holomorphic functions are analytic — that is, representable by convergent power series. From it follow the Cauchy integral formula, which expresses the value of a holomorphic function inside a disk in terms of its values on the boundary circle; Cauchy's estimates; Liouville's theorem; the residue theorem; and the fact that holomorphic functions are infinitely differentiable. It also implies that line integrals of holomorphic functions depend only on endpoints in simply connected domains, so that holomorphic functions possess local primitives.

Domain requirements

The assumption that the domain has no "holes" is essential. For example, the function f(z) = 1/z is holomorphic on the punctured plane, but its integral around the unit circle is 2πi, not zero, because the domain is not simply connected and the contour winds around the singularity at the origin. Generalisations of the theorem to multiply connected domains are expressed in terms of winding numbers and the homology of cycles.
