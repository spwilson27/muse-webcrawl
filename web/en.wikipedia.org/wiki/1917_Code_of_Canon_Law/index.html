1917 Code of Canon Law

The 1917 Code of Canon Law (Latin: Codex Iuris Canonici), also referred to as the Pio-Benedictine Code, was the first official comprehensive codification of Latin canon law. It was promulgated by Pope Benedict XV on 27 May 1917, by the apostolic constitution Providentissima Mater Ecclesia, and took effect on 19 May 1918. It remained in force until the revised 1983 Code of Canon Law came into effect on 27 November 1983.

History

For centuries the law of the Latin Church was contained in a sprawling mass of collections, beginning with Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) and the later compilations that formed the Corpus Iuris Canonici. By the nineteenth century this body of law had become vast, partly obsolete and difficult to use. Calls for codification, on the model of the modern civil codes of Europe, were raised at the First Vatican Council (1869–1870).

Pope Pius X took up the task in 1904 with the motu proprio Arduum sane munus, appointing a commission of cardinals under the leadership of Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, the project's principal architect. The work of drafting, consultation with bishops worldwide and revision occupied thirteen years, spanning the pontificates of Pius X and Benedict XV — hence the name "Pio-Benedictine Code".

Structure

The Code contains 2,414 canons arranged in five books, following the scheme of Roman law:

  1. General Norms (Normae generales)
  2. Persons (De personis) — clerics, religious and laity
  3. Things (De rebus) — sacraments, sacred places and times, divine worship, the magisterium, benefices and temporal goods
  4. Processes (De processibus) — trials and procedures
  5. Crimes and Penalties (De delictis et poenis)

Influence and replacement

The 1917 Code applied to the Latin Church only; the Eastern Catholic Churches were later given their own codification in stages, completed by the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches in 1990. Following the Second Vatican Council, Pope John XXIII announced a revision of the Code in 1959 together with the convocation of the council. The resulting 1983 Code, promulgated by Pope John Paul II, abrogated the 1917 Code, though many of its canons passed into the new code in revised form.

The 1917 Code remains of interest to traditionalist Catholics; communities using the pre-conciliar liturgy have at times argued that aspects of the older discipline retain authority, a position addressed by the Holy See in documents such as Summorum Pontificum (2007).
