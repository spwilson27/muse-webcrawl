Student attrition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Student attrition refers to the loss of students who fail to complete their studies at an educational institution, most commonly discussed in the context of higher education. It is the complement of student retention, and is typically measured through dropout rates, stop-out rates, and cohort graduation rates tracked over a fixed period such as four or six years.

Causes

Research on attrition identifies multiple interacting causes. Financial pressures — tuition costs, the need to work while studying, and student debt — are among the most frequently cited reasons for leaving. Academic under-preparation, particularly in mathematics and writing, increases the risk of early failure and withdrawal. Social and cultural factors also matter: first-generation college students and students from underrepresented groups may experience a weaker sense of belonging or face institutional environments perceived as unwelcoming.

Vincent Tinto's influential model of student departure emphasises integration: students persist when they become academically and socially integrated into the institution, forming connections with faculty, peers, and campus life. Later models added financial, psychological, and organisational factors, and distinguished voluntary withdrawal from academic dismissal.

Measurement and consequences

Attrition carries costs for students, who may leave with debt but no credential; for institutions, which lose tuition revenue and face accountability measures tied to completion rates; and for society, since degree holders on average earn more and pay more in taxes. In the United States, only about six in ten students who begin a bachelor's degree at a four-year institution complete it within six years, with markedly lower rates at open-admission and for-profit institutions.

Interventions

Common institutional responses include early-alert systems that flag students showing signs of disengagement, intrusive academic advising, learning communities, supplemental instruction in high-failure courses, emergency financial aid, and reforms to developmental (remedial) course sequences. Predictive analytics are increasingly used to target support, though these raise questions about bias and privacy.
