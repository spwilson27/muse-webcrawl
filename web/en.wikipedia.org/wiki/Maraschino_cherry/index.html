Maraschino cherry

A maraschino cherry is a preserved, sweetened cherry, typically made from light-coloured sweet varieties such as Royal Ann, Rainier or Gold cherries. The name derives from the Marasca cherry of the Dalmatian coast, whose fruit was originally pickled and preserved in maraschino liqueur distilled from the same cherries.

Production

In modern commercial production, the cherries are first preserved in a brine solution containing sulfur dioxide and calcium chloride, which bleaches them and firms their texture. They are then pitted, re-bleached, sweetened with sugar syrup, dyed — traditionally with FD&C Red 40 to a bright red, or left blonde — and flavoured with almond oil or similar flavouring. The result is a shelf-stable garnish with a dense, candied texture quite unlike a fresh cherry.

History and uses

Cherries preserved in maraschino liqueur were a luxury import in nineteenth-century America, served in fine hotels and restaurants. Prohibition ended alcohol-based production, and Oregon State University professor Ernest H. Wiegand developed the brine process in the 1920s that underlies the modern product. Maraschino cherries are now ubiquitous as cocktail garnishes (famously in the Manhattan and the Old Fashioned garnish traditions), on ice-cream sundaes, and in fruitcakes and baked goods. Upscale producers have revived naturally coloured, liqueur-preserved versions closer to the original.
