Pierre Janet, pioneer of the psychology of dissociation.

Pierre Marie Félix Janet (30 May 1859 – 24 February 1947) was a French psychologist and neurologist, a pioneering figure in the study of dissociation and traumatic memory, and a contemporary rival of Sigmund Freud whose work influenced the early development of psychoanalysis and psychotherapy.

Early work

Trained in philosophy, Janet turned to the study of hysteria, working at Le Havre and then at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris under Jean-Martin Charcot. His doctoral thesis, published as L'automatisme psychologique (1889), argued that hysterical symptoms arise from "fixed ideas" split off (dissociated) from consciousness, which act autonomously — a concept he called psychological automatism.

Concepts

Janet developed the notion of dissociation as a failure of integration under reduced "psychological tension," and described how traumatic memories can persist as dissociated subconscious fixed ideas, returning as symptoms until they are narrated and integrated — an early form of trauma therapy by "liquidation" of the memory. He coined terms such as psychasthenia, described obsessive disorders in Les obsessions et la psychasthénie (1903), and built a hierarchical model of behaviour from reflexes to reflective thought.

Career and rivalry

Janet founded the laboratory of psychology at the Salpêtrière, was elected to a chair at the Collège de France in 1902, and co-founded the Journal de psychologie normale et pathologique. At the 1913 London congress he publicly criticised Freud's psychoanalysis, claiming (as Breuer and Freud had built on his and Charcot's ideas) priority disputes that fanned a long Franco-Austrian quarrel. In later life his reputation was eclipsed by psychoanalysis, but from the 1970s his work was rediscovered by trauma researchers and historians, who credit him with founding dissociation theory and an early psychology of trauma.
