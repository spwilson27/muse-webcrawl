Idyll

A peaceful pastoral scene in the tradition of the literary idyll.

An idyll (or idyl) is a short poem, often in the form of a dialogue or soliloquy, describing a picturesque and charming scene of rural or pastoral life. The word derives from the Greek eidyllion, "little picture". The genre was created by the Sicilian Greek poet Theocritus in the 3rd century BC, whose Idylls depict shepherds singing, competing and courting in an idealised Sicily.

Classical and later tradition

Virgil adapted Theocritus in his Eclogues, transferring the pastoral world to Italy and giving it political undertones; the tradition passed through Renaissance poets such as Sannazaro and Spenser into English literature. In later centuries the term loosened: Tennyson's Idylls of the King (1859–1885) are a cycle of narrative poems on the Arthurian legend, grand in scale but retaining the name. "Idyll" has also been applied to prose works and to short musical pieces evoking rural peace.

Modern meaning

Today "idyllic" describes any place or experience that is extremely happy, peaceful or picturesque, especially in the countryside — a sense that preserves the classical genre's mood of harmonious simplicity, set apart from the anxieties of urban life.
