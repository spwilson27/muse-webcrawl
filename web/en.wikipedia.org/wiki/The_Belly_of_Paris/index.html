The Belly of Paris

The Belly of Paris (French: Le Ventre de Paris) is a novel by Émile Zola, published in 1873. It is the third volume of Zola's twenty-novel Les Rougon-Macquart cycle and the first of his works set entirely on the working of a single great subject: Les Halles, the vast central food market of Paris rebuilt under the Second Empire, which Zola called the "belly" of the city — its digestive organ and, in his naturalist scheme, the machine that metabolised modern Paris.

Plot

Florent Quenu, a mild schoolteacher wrongly deported to the penal colony of Cayenne after the coup d'état of 1851, escapes and returns half-starved to Paris. His half-brother Quenu, now prospering as a pork butcher with his formidable wife Lisa Macquart, hides him and finds him a post as inspector in the fish market at Les Halles, where he is surrounded by mountains of food he barely touches. The "battle of the Fat and the Thin" — Zola's governing image — plays out as the plump, satisfied shopkeepers of the market grow suspicious of the gaunt, idealistic Florent, who drifts into a futile revolutionary conspiracy. Lisa betrays him to the police, and he is deported again; the market closes serenely over his absence, as a market inspector concludes that respectable people are fat.

Style and significance

The novel is celebrated for its painterly set-pieces — the celebrated "cheese symphony", in which the ripening cheeses of a stall are described as an overwhelming olfactory orchestra, and its dawn sequences of vegetables arriving by cart. Zola researched Les Halles with a documentarian's appetite, walking the market at night with a notebook. The novel's theme, the crushing of the hungry and the idealistic by the well-fed machinery of the Empire, and its virtuoso descriptive writing, make it one of the most admired of the Rougon-Macquart series.
