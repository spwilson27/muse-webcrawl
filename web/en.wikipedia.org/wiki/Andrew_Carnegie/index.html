Andrew Carnegie (25 November 1835 – 11 August 1919) was a Scottish-American industrialist who led the expansion of the American steel industry in the late 19th century and became one of the richest men in history. He later gave away the great majority of his fortune — about $350 million — to fund libraries, universities, and foundations, a philosophy he set out in his 1889 essay "The Gospel of Wealth".

Carnegie in later life, after the sale of his steel company.

Early life and career

Born in Dunfermline, Scotland, to a weaver's family impoverished by steam-powered looms, Carnegie emigrated with his family to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, in 1848. He worked as a telegraph messenger and operator, then for the Pennsylvania Railroad under Thomas A. Scott, investing shrewdly in railroads, sleeping cars, and oil. After the Civil War he moved into iron and then steel, adopting the Bessemer process and driving down costs through vertical integration — controlling mines, coke works, rail lines, and mills.

Carnegie Steel and sale

His Carnegie Steel Company, centred on Pittsburgh, became the largest steel producer in the world. In 1901 he sold it to J. P. Morgan for $480 million; the sale created the United States Steel Corporation, the first billion-dollar company. Carnegie's reputation among workers was damaged by the violent Homestead Strike of 1892, which occurred under his manager Henry Clay Frick while Carnegie was in Scotland.

Philanthropy

Carnegie funded more than 2,500 public libraries in the United States, Britain, and elsewhere, as well as Carnegie Hall in New York, the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now part of Carnegie Mellon University), the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Peace Palace at The Hague, and pension funds for teachers (the origin of TIAA).
