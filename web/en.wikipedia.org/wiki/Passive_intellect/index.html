Passive intellect

The passive intellect (Greek: nous pathetikos; Latin: intellectus possibilis, "possible intellect") is a concept in Aristotelian and medieval philosophy describing the receptive aspect of the human intellect, which receives intelligible forms, in contrast to the active intellect, which actualises them. The distinction originates in chapter 5 of Book III of Aristotle's De Anima (On the Soul).

Aristotle's De Anima, in which the active/passive intellect distinction is drawn.

Aristotle's account

Aristotle argued that thinking, like perception, requires something that becomes all things and something that makes all things. The passive intellect is "that by which the soul thinks and judges"; it is potentially identical with the intelligible forms it receives, as a blank tablet (tabula rasa) is potentially what is written on it, but it is actually nothing before it thinks. Aristotle described the passive intellect as perishable and unable to think without the active intellect, a claim that generated centuries of commentary.

Interpretations

Alexander of Aphrodisias and Averroes

Alexander of Aphrodisias identified the active intellect with God and treated the passive (material) intellect as a mortal disposition of the soul. Averroes, by contrast, held that both the material intellect and the active intellect are single, separate, eternal substances shared by all human beings — a position known as monopsychism, which provoked fierce controversy in the Latin West.

Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas rejected monopsychism: for him, both the agent (active) intellect and the possible (passive) intellect are powers of each individual human soul. The passive intellect receives the intelligible species abstracted by the agent intellect from sensory images (phantasms), and it is also the storehouse of concepts once formed.

Significance

The active/passive intellect distinction shaped Islamic philosophy (falsafa), Jewish philosophy, and scholastic psychology, and it remains a standard topic in the history of philosophy of mind, where it anticipates later debates about receptivity and spontaneity in cognition, including in Kant.
