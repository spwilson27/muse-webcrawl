The whitebark pine (Pinus albicaulis) is a species of pine native to the mountains of western North America, where it grows at high elevations near treeline in subalpine conditions. It is a five-needle pine with light grey bark, and it is regarded as a keystone and foundation species in high-elevation ecosystems of the Rocky Mountains, Sierra Nevada, Cascades, and Great Basin ranges.

Ecology

Whitebark pine seeds are large, wingless, and rich in fats and protein. The cones do not open on their own; the species depends overwhelmingly on Clark's nutcracker, a bird that extracts the seeds and caches them in the soil, planting new trees in the process. Forgotten caches germinate and regenerate the pine stands. The seeds are also an important food for grizzly bears and other wildlife in the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem.

Threats and decline

Whitebark pine has declined severely across much of its range due to the introduced fungal disease white pine blister rust (Cronartium ribicola), outbreaks of mountain pine beetle, altered fire regimes following decades of fire suppression, and climate change. In 2022 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the species as threatened under the Endangered Species Act; it is also assessed as endangered on the IUCN Red List. Restoration efforts include planting seedlings grown from rust-resistant parent trees.
