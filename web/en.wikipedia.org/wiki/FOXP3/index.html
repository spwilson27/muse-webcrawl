FOXP3 (forkhead box P3) is a gene encoding the transcription factor forkhead box protein P3, also known as scurfin. It is the master regulator of the development and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), the CD4+ T cells that suppress immune responses and maintain self-tolerance. FOXP3 belongs to the forkhead/winged-helix family of transcription factors and binds DNA through its forkhead domain.

Function

Expression of FOXP3 in developing T cells in the thymus commits them to the regulatory lineage. In mature Tregs, FOXP3 controls a large transcriptional programme: it activates genes such as IL2RA (CD25) and CTLA4 and represses effector cytokines including interleukin-2 itself, maintaining the suppressive phenotype. Stable expression depends on demethylation of a conserved region of the gene (the Treg-specific demethylated region, TSDR), which is used as an epigenetic marker of genuine, stable Tregs. Forced expression of FOXP3 can convert conventional T cells toward a regulatory phenotype, a fact exploited in efforts to engineer therapeutic Tregs.

Clinical significance

Mutations in FOXP3 cause IPEX syndrome (immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked) in humans — a severe, usually fatal autoimmune disorder of infancy marked by intractable diarrhoea, eczema, type 1 diabetes and thyroid disease. The equivalent mouse mutation, scurfy, produces a similar fulminant lymphoproliferative autoimmunity, and the identification of scurfin as the scurfy gene product in 2001 established FOXP3's central role in tolerance. Because tumours can recruit FOXP3+ Tregs to suppress anti-cancer immunity, FOXP3 is also studied in oncology; conversely, boosting FOXP3+ Treg function is a goal in transplantation and autoimmune therapy. Its discovery was recognised in part by the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for peripheral immune tolerance, awarded to Shimon Sakaguchi (who identified the Treg lineage), Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell (who identified the FOXP3/scurfy connection).
