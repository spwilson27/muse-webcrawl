Merger

A merger is an agreement that unites two existing companies into one new company. Mergers and acquisitions are commonly done to expand a company's reach, increase its market share, or gain a competitive advantage. Unlike an acquisition, in which one company takes over another and remains the surviving entity, a merger typically results in a brand-new legal entity formed from the combined firms.

Types of mergers

Mergers are commonly classified by the relationship between the merging companies. A horizontal merger joins two companies in the same industry, often direct competitors. A vertical merger joins a company with one of its suppliers or customers. A conglomerate merger joins firms in entirely unrelated businesses, while a congeneric merger joins companies in related but distinct markets. A market-extension merger joins companies that sell the same products in different markets.

Reasons for merging

Companies merge to achieve economies of scale, diversify products or markets, increase market power, acquire technology or talent, and improve financial performance. Mergers may also be motivated by tax considerations or the desire to eliminate a competitor.

Legal and regulatory aspects

Large mergers are subject to review by competition authorities, such as the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice in the United States and the European Commission in the European Union, which may block combinations that would substantially lessen competition. Shareholders of both companies typically must approve the transaction.
