Pronatalism

Pronatalism (or pro-natalism) is an ideology or policy approach that promotes the reproduction of human life and encourages higher birth rates. Pronatalist policies are usually expressed in government programs providing financial and social incentives for families to have more children, and they have historically been motivated by concerns about military manpower, economic growth, ageing populations, and national decline.

History

Ancient Rome under Augustus legislated rewards for marriage and childbearing and penalties for celibacy. In the 20th century, France adopted extensive family allowances and a family code in response to long-running low fertility, while totalitarian regimes such as Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union awarded medals to mothers of large families. Ceaușescu's Romania pursued coercive pronatalism through Decree 770 of 1966, which banned abortion and contraception and produced severe social consequences.

Modern pronatalism

Contemporary pronatalist policies in countries such as Hungary, Poland, Russia, South Korea, and Singapore include baby bonuses, subsidized childcare, housing benefits, and parental leave, typically in response to fertility rates far below replacement level. A cultural pronatalist movement has also emerged in some technological and political circles, arguing that population decline threatens innovation and economic dynamism. Critics raise concerns about women's autonomy, environmental impact, and the limited effectiveness of financial incentives. See also natalism and total fertility rate.
