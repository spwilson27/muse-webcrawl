William of Malmesbury (Latin: Willelmus Malmesbiriensis; c. 1085/1090 – c. 1143) was the foremost English historian of the 12th century. A monk and librarian of Malmesbury Abbey in Wiltshire, he wrote the Gesta Regum Anglorum ("Deeds of the English Kings") and the Gesta Pontificum Anglorum ("Deeds of the English Bishops"), which together form the most important narrative sources for English history between Bede and the later Middle Ages.

Life

William was born in Wiltshire of mixed Norman and English parentage — a fact he mentions with pride. Educated at Malmesbury Abbey, he became the abbey's librarian and built its collection into one of the finest in England, reading widely in classical and patristic literature. He declined offers of advancement, including the abbacy of Malmesbury itself, preferring a life of study. He travelled widely in England gathering material and interviewing witnesses for his histories.

Works

The Gesta Regum Anglorum, completed around 1125 and later revised, covers English history from 449 to 1125 and was dedicated to Robert, Earl of Gloucester. The Gesta Pontificum Anglorum treats the history of England's bishoprics and monasteries. His Historia Novella ("New History") continues the story into the civil war of King Stephen's reign, known as the Anarchy. William also wrote saints' lives, a commentary on the Lamentations of Jeremiah, and works of theology and classical scholarship.

Historical method and reputation

Unlike many medieval chroniclers, William evaluated his sources critically, distinguished legend from evidence, and strove for chronological order and lively characterization. Modern historians regard him as "the most learned and most readable of the twelfth-century English historians," and his works remain indispensable for the study of Anglo-Norman England.
