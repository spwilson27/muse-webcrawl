George Wells Beadle (22 October 1903 – 9 June 1989) was an American geneticist. In 1958 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Edward Tatum and Joshua Lederberg for the discovery that genes act by regulating specific chemical events — the "one gene–one enzyme" hypothesis.

Work

Beadle studied genetics at the University of Nebraska and Cornell University, and worked on maize and on the fruit fly Drosophila. At Stanford, he and Tatum turned to the bread mould Neurospora crassa: by irradiating spores to induce mutations and testing which nutrient each mutant required in order to grow (1941), they showed that a mutation disabling a single gene disabled a single step in a metabolic pathway — each gene directed the making of one specific enzyme. This result founded biochemical genetics and made Neurospora a key model organism.

Later career

Beadle chaired the biology division at Caltech and, from 1961 to 1968, was chancellor (president) of the University of Chicago. He was also known for his late-career study of the origin of maize (corn) from teosinte, championing the view that maize was domesticated from teosinte in Mexico.
