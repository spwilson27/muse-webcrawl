Akathisia

Akathisia is a movement disorder characterised by a subjective feeling of inner restlessness and an objective inability to sit or stand still. Sufferers typically pace, shift their weight from foot to foot, rock, cross and uncross their legs, or repeatedly sit and stand, and describe an intense compulsion to move accompanied by anxiety and dysphoria. The word derives from the Greek a- (without) + káthisis (sitting). The Czech neuropsychiatrist Ladislav Haškovec coined the term in 1901.

Akathisia produces an irresistible urge to be in constant motion.

Causes

The most common cause is medication. Acute akathisia is a well-known side effect of antipsychotic (neuroleptic) drugs, particularly the high-potency first-generation agents such as haloperidol, but it also occurs with second-generation antipsychotics, antidepressants (especially SSRIs), antiemetics such as metoclopramide and prochlorperazine, and calcium-channel blockers. It usually appears within days of starting or increasing the dose. A chronic form, tardive akathisia, can emerge after months of treatment and may persist after the drug is stopped. Akathisia is also seen in Parkinson's disease and, less commonly, in other neurological conditions.

Mechanism

The precise mechanism is incompletely understood, but it is generally attributed to blockade of dopamine D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal and mesocortical pathways, with contributions from noradrenergic and serotonergic systems — which explains why beta-blockers, antiserotonergic drugs, and benzodiazepines can be effective treatments.

Diagnosis and management

Assessment uses rating instruments such as the Barnes Akathisia Scale, which scores both the patient's subjective distress and observable movements. Akathisia is frequently misdiagnosed as worsening psychotic agitation or anxiety, leading to harmful increases in the very medication causing it. Management begins with reducing the dose or switching the offending drug; adjunctive treatments with evidence of benefit include the beta-blocker propranolol, low-dose mirtazapine, benzodiazepines, and anticholinergics when accompanied by parkinsonism. Severe akathisia is intensely distressing and has been associated with aggression and suicide, making prompt recognition important.
