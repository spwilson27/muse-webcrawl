Underwater breathing apparatus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An underwater breathing apparatus is equipment that supplies a diver with breathing gas at ambient pressure underwater. The term covers several families of equipment: self-contained apparatus carried by the diver, and surface-supplied equipment in which gas is delivered through an umbilical hose from a compressor or cylinders at the surface.

Types

Open-circuit scuba, the most common recreational system, stores compressed air (or nitrox) in cylinders and vents exhaled gas into the water; its modern form, the Aqua-Lung, was developed by Jacques Cousteau and Émile Gagnan in 1943. Rebreathers recycle exhaled gas, scrubbing carbon dioxide and adding oxygen, allowing much longer dives and producing few or no bubbles — valued by military and technical divers and underwater photographers. Surface-supplied equipment, descending from the standard diving dress with its copper helmet, remains standard for commercial work because it offers unlimited gas, hard-wired communications, and a physical link to the surface. Snorkels, though sometimes classed with breathing apparatus, only function at the surface.

Considerations

Breathing gas must be supplied at the pressure of the surrounding water, which increases about one atmosphere per 10 metres of depth; this drives gas consumption and the risks of nitrogen narcosis, oxygen toxicity, and decompression sickness. Saturation diving systems, atmospheric diving suits (which keep the occupant at surface pressure), and submarine escape equipment are specialised offshoots meeting particular military and industrial needs.
