Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale

The Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale (SSHWS) classifies hurricanes — Western Hemisphere tropical cyclones that exceed the intensities of tropical depressions and tropical storms — into five categories distinguished by the intensities of their sustained winds. The scale is used officially by the United States National Hurricane Center for cyclones in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific basins.

A Category 5 hurricane at peak intensity, viewed from satellite.

History

The scale was developed in 1971 by civil engineer Herbert Saffir and meteorologist Robert Simpson, then director of the National Hurricane Center. Originally it incorporated central pressure and storm surge as well as wind; these elements were removed in a 2009 revision, and the scale was renamed the "Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale" in 2010, making it a pure wind scale.

Cyclones with maximum sustained winds of at least Category 3 are described as major hurricanes. Wind measurements are based on the one-minute average of maximum sustained surface winds; the scale is open-ended at the top, so Category 5 has no upper bound.

Criticism

Meteorologists have criticised the scale for ignoring factors such as rainfall, storm surge, storm size and forward speed, which can cause devastation even in lower-category storms. Hurricane Katrina (2005), for example, made landfall as a Category 3, yet its surge produced catastrophic flooding. Proposals for supplementary or alternative indices, such as integrated kinetic energy, have been discussed, but the Saffir–Simpson scale remains the standard for public communication in the United States.
