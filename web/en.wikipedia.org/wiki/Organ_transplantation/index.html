Organ transplantation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Organ transplantation is a medical procedure in which an organ is removed from one body and placed in another (or moved within the same body) to replace a damaged or absent organ. Kidneys are by far the most transplanted organs, followed by livers, hearts, lungs, pancreases and intestines; tissues such as corneas, skin, bone and heart valves are also grafted.

History

The modern era began in 1954, when Joseph Murray's team performed the first successful kidney transplant, between identical twins, in Boston. The great obstacle is immune rejection: the recipient's immune system attacks the graft's foreign antigens (see antigen), so donors and recipients are matched by blood group and HLA tissue type, and recipients take immunosuppressive drugs — transformed by ciclosporin from the 1980s — for life. Christiaan Barnard performed the first human heart transplant in 1967.

Shortage and frontiers

Demand vastly exceeds supply everywhere, producing waiting lists, allocation ethics and, in some regions, illicit organ markets. Responses include opt-out donor laws, paired kidney exchange, and xenotransplantation: genetically modified pig hearts and kidneys were transplanted into human patients in a series of landmark cases from 2022. Brain death criteria, and the definition of death itself, remain ethically charged foundations of the field.
