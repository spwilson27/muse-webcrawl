Karl Hermann Amandus Schwarz (25 January 1843 – 30 November 1921) was a German mathematician, known for his work in complex analysis. A student of Karl Weierstrass, he held chairs at Halle, the ETH Zürich, Göttingen, and finally Berlin, where he succeeded to Weierstrass's own chair. Numerous fundamental results in analysis — the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the Schwarz lemma, the Schwarz reflection principle and the Schwarzian derivative — bear his name.

Life and career

Schwarz was born in Hermsdorf in Silesia and initially studied chemistry in Berlin before switching to mathematics under the influence of Kummer and Weierstrass, taking his doctorate in 1864. He taught at Halle from 1867, moved to the Swiss Federal Polytechnic in Zürich in 1869, then to Göttingen in 1875, and in 1892 to Berlin, where he remained until his death. In 1873 he married Marie Kummer, daughter of his teacher Ernst Kummer, herself connected by family to Felix Mendelssohn's circle.

Mathematical work

Schwarz's doctoral dissertation studied surfaces of zero Gaussian curvature. He gave the first rigorous proof that a spherical surface has minimal area among surfaces of given volume, and solved the Dirichlet problem for the disk by means of the alternating method that carries his name. In function theory he proved the reflection principle for extending analytic functions across real-analytic arcs, introduced the Schwarz lemma — a cornerstone estimate for bounded analytic functions in the unit disk — and studied conformal mappings of polygons, using the differential equation involving the Schwarzian derivative (independently treated by Lagrange and later named for Schwarz). His name is most familiar to students through the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for integrals, which he published in 1888 (Bunyakovsky had anticipated the integral form). He also devised the "Schwarz lantern," a famous counterexample showing that inscribed polyhedra need not converge in area to a smooth surface.

Legacy

Schwarz supervised notable doctoral students including Lipót Fejér, Paul Koebe and Ernst Zermelo, and his Berlin seminar helped set the standard of rigour in German analysis. His collected works were published in 1890.
