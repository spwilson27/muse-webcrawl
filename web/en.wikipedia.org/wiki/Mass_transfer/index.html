Mass transfer

Mass transfer is the net movement of mass from one location to another, usually meaning the transport of a chemical species within or between phases. It is one of the three classical transport phenomena, alongside momentum transfer and heat transfer, and it underlies the unit operations of chemical engineering such as distillation, absorption, extraction, adsorption, and drying. Mass transfer occurs by molecular diffusion, driven by a concentration gradient, and by bulk (convective) motion of the fluid.

Fundamentals

Diffusive flux is described by Fick's laws: the molar flux of a species is proportional to the negative of its concentration gradient, with the proportionality constant being the diffusion coefficient. Where a fluid flows, the total flux combines diffusive and bulk-flow contributions. Across a phase interface, transfer is often modelled with mass transfer coefficients and driving forces expressed in terms of the departure from equilibrium, as in the two-film theory of Whitman.

Applications

  • Distillation: separation of liquid mixtures by repeated vaporisation and condensation.
  • Gas absorption: removal of a solute from a gas into a liquid solvent (e.g. CO2 scrubbing).
  • Liquid–liquid extraction: transfer of a solute between immiscible liquids.
  • Membrane processes: dialysis, reverse osmosis, and gas permeation.
  • Biological systems: oxygen transfer in bioreactors and lungs; drug absorption.

Analogies

The mathematical similarity of the governing equations for mass, heat, and momentum transfer gives rise to analogies such as the Chilton–Colburn analogy, which relates the mass transfer coefficient to friction and heat transfer coefficients through dimensionless groups including the Sherwood, Schmidt, and Lewis numbers.
