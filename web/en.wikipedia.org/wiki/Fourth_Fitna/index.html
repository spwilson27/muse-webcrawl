The Fourth Fitna (Arabic: الفتنة الرابعة), also called the Great Abbasid Civil War, was a conflict over the succession to the Abbasid caliphate that erupted in 809 on the death of Harun al-Rashid between his sons al-Amin, caliph in Baghdad, and al-Ma'mun, governor of Khurasan. The war's centerpiece, the year-long siege of Baghdad (812–813), devastated the caliphal capital, destroyed the political settlement Harun had arranged, and permanently diminished the military and fiscal foundations of Abbasid power in Iraq.

Background

At Mecca in 802 Harun al-Rashid had formalized a division of the empire: al-Amin, son of the Abbasid princess Zubayda, would succeed as caliph in Iraq; al-Ma'mun, son of a Persian slave mother, would rule an autonomous Khurasan in the east as his heir presumptive in turn; a third son, al-Qasim, received the Byzantine frontier. Copies of the settlement were hung in the Kaaba. After Harun died at Tus in 809 — on campaign with al-Ma'mun at his side — relations between the brothers' courts collapsed: al-Amin demanded obedience and revenue from Khurasan, altered the succession in favor of his own infant son, removed al-Ma'mun's name from the coinage and the Friday prayer, and in 811 sent an army east under Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan.

The war

Against expectations, al-Ma'mun's outnumbered Khurasani army under Tahir ibn Husayn destroyed Ali ibn Isa's host at Rayy in 811, killing its commander. Tahir then advanced relentlessly through Persia into Iraq. Baghdad's defenders — a fractious coalition of regular troops, the caliph's household, and the urban crowd of abna' and street fighters — held out through a fourteen-month siege of sieges and sorties in which mangonels bombarded the suburbs, the palaces of the elite were looted, and the city's quarters were ruined. Al-Amin, his authority collapsed, attempted to negotiate surrender and escape by boat on the Tigris in September 813; he was captured and beheaded by Tahir's Persian troops, his head sent to al-Ma'mun in Merv.

Aftermath

Victory did not restore unity quickly. Al-Ma'mun remained in Merv until 819, ruling through his vizier al-Fadl ibn Sahl and provoking further revolts in Iraq and Arabia, including a rival Alid caliph proclaimed at Kufa and the upheaval surrounding his designation of the Alid imam Ali al-Rida as heir (817). Baghdad rose again under his brother Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi. Only after 819–820, with al-Fadl's assassination and al-Ma'mun's return to Baghdad, did the civil war wind down, though Kharijite, Alid, and provincial risings flickered into the 820s. The war shifted the empire's center of gravity: Khurasani troops replaced the old Baghdad establishment, the Tahirid family became hereditary governors of the east, and Baghdad never fully recovered its position as the uncontested heart of an integrated empire. A generation later al-Ma'mun's religious policy provoked the Mihna, the inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an.
