Turbocharger

A turbocharger (formerly also called a turbosupercharger) is a forced-induction device for internal combustion engines that uses energy from the engine's exhaust gases to drive a turbine, which in turn drives a compressor that forces additional air into the engine's intake. The denser charge lets the engine burn more fuel per cycle, increasing power output from a given displacement, or allowing a smaller engine to deliver the same power with better efficiency.

Construction and operation

A turbocharger consists of a radial-flow turbine wheel and a centrifugal compressor wheel mounted on opposite ends of a common shaft, housed in snail-shaped casings. Exhaust gas spins the turbine — at speeds that can exceed 150,000 rpm — and the compressor draws in and compresses intake air, which is often cooled by an intercooler before entering the engine. A wastegate limits boost pressure by bypassing exhaust around the turbine, and blow-off or recirculation valves protect the compressor when the throttle closes. Because the turbine is driven by exhaust rather than by the engine's crankshaft, a turbocharger is not a supercharger in the strict mechanical sense; "turbo lag" is the brief delay before boost builds as exhaust flow rises. Variable-geometry turbines, twin-scroll housings and electrically assisted turbos are used to reduce lag and widen the operating range.

History and use

The turbocharger was patented in 1905 by the Swiss engineer Alfred Büchi and developed for aircraft and marine diesels before spreading to cars — mass-market landmarks included the Saab 99 Turbo (1978) and its wide adoption in diesel cars, and later in downsized petrol engines seeking lower CO2 emissions. Turbocharging is now nearly universal in diesel engines and common across petrol passenger cars, trucks, motorsport and aircraft piston engines.
