Cochlea

The cochlea is the part of the inner ear involved in hearing. It is a spiral-shaped cavity in the bony labyrinth, in humans making 2.75 turns around its axis, the modiolus. A core component of the cochlea is the organ of Corti, the sensory organ of hearing, which contains roughly 15,000 hair cells whose movement converts sound vibrations into nerve impulses.

Structure

The cochlea is filled with fluid: perilymph fills the scala vestibuli and scala tympani, while endolymph fills the scala media (cochlear duct) between them. The basilar membrane, which supports the organ of Corti, varies in width and stiffness along the spiral—narrow and stiff at the base, wide and flexible at the apex.

Function

Sound vibrations reach the cochlea through the oval window, moved by the stapes. Pressure waves travel through the perilymph and displace the basilar membrane in a travelling wave whose peak position depends on frequency: high frequencies peak near the base, low frequencies near the apex. This tonotopic arrangement maps pitch onto place. The deflection of hair-cell stereocilia opens ion channels, generating receptor potentials; inner hair cells send signals via the cochlear nerve to the brain, while outer hair cells actively amplify the motion, sharpening frequency tuning and giving the ear its sensitivity.

Clinical significance

Damage to hair cells—from ageing (presbycusis), noise exposure, infection, or ototoxic drugs—causes sensorineural hearing loss, which is usually irreversible in mammals. Cochlear implants bypass damaged hair cells by electrically stimulating the auditory nerve directly, restoring a form of hearing to people with profound deafness. Georg von Békésy received the 1961 Nobel Prize for his discoveries about the physical mechanism of stimulation within the cochlea.
