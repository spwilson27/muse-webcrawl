Stanley Prusiner

Born: 28 May 1942, Des Moines, Iowa, US
Fields: Neurology, biochemistry
Institutions: University of California, San Francisco
Known for: Discovery of prions
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1997)

Stanley Ben Prusiner (born May 28, 1942) is an American neurologist and biochemist. He is the director of the Institute for Neurodegenerative Diseases at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF). He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1997 for his discovery of prions.

Prion research

Prusiner became interested in the transmissible spongiform encephalopathies — scrapie in sheep, kuru, and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease — after encountering a patient with CJD during his residency. In 1982 he published the controversial claim that the infectious agent of scrapie was composed principally of a single protein, which he termed a prion ("proteinaceous infectious particle"), with no nucleic acid genome. The idea initially met intense scepticism because it contradicted the assumption that all infectious agents carry DNA or RNA.

Subsequent work by Prusiner and others identified the prion protein gene (PRNP), showed that the disease-causing form (PrPSc) is a misfolded conformation of a normal host protein (PrPC) that propagates by templating the misfold onto normal copies, and connected prions to bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") and its human counterpart, variant CJD. Protein-only inheritance has since become an established concept, and related templated-misfolding mechanisms are now studied in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases.

Honours

Prusiner received the Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research in 1994 and the Nobel Prize three years later. His memoir Madness and Memory appeared in 2014.
