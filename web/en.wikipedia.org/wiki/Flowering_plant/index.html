Flowering plants are plants that bear flowers and fruits, and form the clade Angiospermae (from Greek words for "vessel" and "seed"), commonly called angiosperms. They include all forbs, grasses and grass-like plants, a vast majority of broad-leaved trees, shrubs and vines, and most aquatic plants. Angiosperms are by far the most diverse group of land plants, with 64 orders, 416 families, approximately 13,000 known genera and 300,000 known species. They are distinguished from the other seed-producing plants, the gymnosperms, by having flowers, xylem consisting of vessel elements instead of tracheids, endosperm within their seeds, and fruits that completely envelop their seeds.

Characteristics

The characteristic feature of angiosperms is the flower, a reproductive structure that typically contains both male (stamens) and female (carpels) parts. After fertilization, the ovary of the flower develops into a fruit enclosing the seeds. Angiosperms also exhibit double fertilization, producing both an embryo and a nutritive tissue called endosperm. Their reduced gametophytes and efficient vascular tissue contribute to their dominance.

Diversity and classification

Modern classification, developed by the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG), divides flowering plants into several major groups: the basal angiosperms (including Amborella, water lilies, and Austrobaileyales), the magnoliids, the monocots (grasses, palms, lilies, orchids), and the eudicots, which account for about three-quarters of all species and include most familiar trees, herbs, and shrubs.

Evolution

Fossil evidence places the earliest flowering plants in the Early Cretaceous, around 134 million years ago, though molecular estimates sometimes suggest an older origin. Angiosperms radiated rapidly during the Cretaceous — Charles Darwin famously called their apparently sudden rise an "abominable mystery" — in part through co-evolution with pollinating insects.

Importance

Flowering plants provide nearly all plant-based food for humans and livestock, directly or indirectly; the most important families include the grasses (Poaceae: rice, wheat, maize), legumes (Fabaceae), nightshades (Solanaceae: potato, tomato), and roses (Rosaceae: apples, strawberries). They also supply timber, fibres, medicines, and ornamental plants, and dominate most terrestrial ecosystems.
