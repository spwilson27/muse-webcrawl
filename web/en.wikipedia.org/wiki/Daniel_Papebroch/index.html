Daniel Papebroch

Daniel Papebroch, also spelled Papenbroeck or Papebrochius (17 March 1628 – 28 June 1714), was a Flemish Jesuit priest and scholar, one of the Bollandists, best known for his critical work on the Acta Sanctorum and for pioneering the science of diplomatics.

Life

Born in Antwerp, Papebroch entered the Society of Jesus in 1646. He joined the small group of scholars gathered around Jean Bolland who were producing a vast critical edition of the lives of the saints, organised by feast day. From 1660 to 1662 he travelled with Godfrey Henschen through Germany, Italy and France collecting manuscripts from monastic libraries. After Bolland's death in 1665, Papebroch became the driving force of the enterprise, editing many volumes of the Acta Sanctorum for March, April, May and June, and serving as its editor-in-chief for over forty years.

Controversies

Papebroch applied rigorous historical criticism to hagiographical traditions. In 1675 he published the Propylaeum, arguing that many early medieval charters — including donations attributed to the Merovingian kings — were forgeries, and questioning documents on which the Benedictines based land claims and the Carmelites their claimed descent from the prophet Elijah. The Maurist Jean Mabillon responded with De re diplomatica (1681), which graciously refuted Papebroch's blanket scepticism while establishing sound criteria for judging documents; Papebroch conceded the point with equal grace, and the exchange is credited with founding diplomatics. Carmelite anger at his doubts over the Elijah tradition led the Spanish Inquisition to condemn volumes of the Acta Sanctorum in 1695, though Rome never confirmed the ban and it was lifted after his death.
