Foie gras

TypeLiver pâté / delicacy
OriginFrance (technique traces to ancient Egypt)
Main ingredientsLiver of a duck or goose fattened by force-feeding (gavage)

Foie gras (French for "fat liver") is a speciality food product made of the liver of a duck or goose that has been fattened. By French law, foie gras is defined as the liver of a duck or goose fattened by gavage — force-feeding corn through a tube into the bird's oesophagus, a process that enlarges the liver many times its natural size.

History

The technique traces back to ancient Egypt, where geese were fattened for the table, and was known to the Romans, who fattened geese with dried figs. Foie gras became a hallmark of French gastronomy, centred on the Périgord and Alsace regions, and remains protected as part of France's cultural and gastronomic heritage.

Production and forms

France is the largest producer and consumer; duck foie gras accounts for the great majority of production, goose being rarer and more delicate. It is sold whole (foie gras entier), as a pâté, mousse or parfait, and served seared, as a terrine, or as an accompaniment to dishes such as tournedos Rossini.

Controversy

Force-feeding is criticised by animal-welfare advocates as cruel, and production or sale has been banned or restricted in several countries, regions and cities, including India, parts of the United States, and (for production) many EU states, though import and sale often remain legal. Producers defend the practice as traditional and argue it exploits birds' natural capacity to store fat before migration.
