Marcus Junius Brutus

Marcus Junius Brutus (c. 85 BC – 23 October 42 BC) was a Roman politician, orator, and philosopher, best known as one of the leaders of the conspiracy that assassinated Julius Caesar on the Ides of March, 44 BC.

Brutus was the son of Servilia, who was Caesar's mistress, leading to speculation—unlikely on chronological grounds—that Caesar was his father. He was raised in part by his uncle Cato the Younger and married Cato's daughter Porcia. In the civil war between Caesar and Pompey, Brutus sided with Pompey, but after Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus (48 BC) Caesar pardoned him and promoted him, making him praetor for 44 BC.

Assassination of Caesar

Despite Caesar's favor, Brutus joined the conspiracy organized by Gaius Cassius Longinus, moved by appeals to the example of his ancestor Lucius Junius Brutus, founder of the Republic, and by anonymous calls to act against tyranny. On 15 March 44 BC the conspirators stabbed Caesar to death in the Theatre of Pompey. Brutus defended the act as tyrannicide, but public opinion turned against the assassins, and he and Cassius withdrew to the East, where they raised armies and seized control of the eastern provinces.

Death

At the Battle of Philippi in Macedonia in October 42 BC, Brutus defeated the forces of Octavian but, after Cassius's death in the first engagement, was defeated by Mark Antony in the second battle on 23 October. Fleeing the field, Brutus took his own life. Shakespeare's Julius Caesar and Dante's Inferno—which places Brutus in the lowest circle of Hell, chewed by Satan alongside Cassius and Judas—have shaped his complex reputation as both patriot and traitor.
