Sea urchins are spiny, globular marine animals of the class Echinoidea, within the echinoderms — the phylum that also includes starfish, sea cucumbers, brittle stars and crinoids. About 950 species live on the sea floor of every ocean, from the intertidal zone to depths of nearly 5,000 metres. Their round, hard shell (the "test") is covered with movable spines used for defence, locomotion and burrowing.

Anatomy

Sea urchins display pentaradial (five-part) symmetry as adults. They move using hundreds of translucent tube feet powered by a water vascular system, together with their spines. On their underside is a sophisticated five-toothed chewing apparatus called Aristotle's lantern (named because Aristotle compared it to a lantern), used to scrape algae from rocks. They have no true eyes or brain, though the whole body is light-sensitive and some species have image-forming vision distributed over the test.

Ecology

As dominant grazers of kelp and algae, urchins shape reef communities. When their predators (such as sea otters, sheephead or triggerfish) decline, urchin populations can explode and overgraze kelp forests into "urchin barrens", a classic example of a trophic cascade. Urchins reproduce by broadcast spawning; their free-swimming pluteus larvae are a standard model organism in developmental biology — much of the science of fertilisation and gene regulatory networks was worked out in sea urchin embryos.

Human uses

The gonads of several species are eaten as uni, a prized delicacy especially in Japanese cuisine, and in Mediterranean and Chilean cooking. Urchin fisheries exist worldwide and have repeatedly over-exploited stocks. Their spines can inflict painful punctures, and a few species (such as flower urchins) are venomous.
