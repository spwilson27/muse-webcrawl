Iranian New Wave

The Iranian New Wave refers to a movement in Iranian cinema that began in the late 1960s and continued, in a distinct second phase, after the 1979 revolution. It is characterised by poetic and philosophical storytelling, realist imagery, dialogue rich in symbolism, and frequent use of child protagonists and non-professional actors.

First wave

Early landmarks include Dariush Mehrjui's The Cow (1969), adapted with playwright Gholam-Hossein Sa'edi and praised despite censorship, Sohrab Shahid Saless's minimalist films, and works by Bahram Beyzai, Parviz Kimiavi, and Nasser Taghvai.

Second wave

After the revolution, directors such as Abbas Kiarostami (Where Is the Friend's Home?, Close-Up, Taste of Cherry, Palme d'Or 1997), Mohsen Makhmalbaf, Jafar Panahi, Majid Majidi, and Asghar Farhadi brought Iranian cinema worldwide acclaim at Cannes, Venice, and Berlin, despite restrictive censorship at home.

Characteristics

Common features include open endings, long takes, rural settings, ambiguous mixes of documentary and fiction, and narratives that circle back on themselves — techniques partly born of aesthetic choice and partly of the need to work around censorship rules.
