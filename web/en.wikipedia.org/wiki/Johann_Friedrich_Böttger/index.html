Johann Friedrich Böttger

Johann Friedrich Böttger (4 February 1682 – 13 March 1719) was a German alchemist credited with the invention of European hard-paste porcelain, the first true porcelain produced in Europe. His discovery laid the foundation for the Meissen porcelain manufactory, the first in Europe.

Early life

Böttger was born in Schleiz, Thuringia. Apprenticed to a pharmacist in Berlin, he gained a reputation as an alchemist after claiming he could transmute base metals into gold. Augustus II the Strong, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, had him brought to Dresden and held under guard to produce gold.

The invention of porcelain

Under the supervision of the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus, Böttger turned his attention to ceramics. Experiments with Saxon clays and kaolin led, in 1708, to the first successful firing of hard-paste porcelain in Europe, and in 1709 the Meissen manufactory was established.

Death

Böttger died in Dresden in 1719, aged 37, having never escaped the Elector's control. Meissen porcelain, marked with the crossed swords, became one of the most celebrated ceramics in the world.
