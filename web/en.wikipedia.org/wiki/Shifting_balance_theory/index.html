Shifting balance theory

The shifting balance theory is a theory of evolution proposed by the American population geneticist Sewall Wright. It proposes that adaptive evolution may proceed most quickly when a population is divided into subpopulations (demes) with restricted gene flow. According to the theory, natural selection, genetic drift, and migration interact such that a population can escape local adaptive peaks and reach higher peaks on the adaptive landscape — a journey that a single large panmictic population, climbing only uphill by selection, could not make.

The three phases

Phase I — drift. In small subpopulations, genetic drift allows allele frequencies to wander randomly, letting a deme explore the adaptive landscape and cross a "valley" of lower fitness between peaks. Wright argued that in a large population, selection holds the population at a local optimum, whereas in very small populations drift is too strong and selection ineffective; intermediate subdivision is therefore optimal.

Phase II — selection. Once drift has carried a deme into the domain of attraction of a higher adaptive peak, natural selection within the deme drives the new gene combination to fixation, moving the subpopulation up the higher peak.

Phase III — interdemic selection. Demes that have reached higher peaks contribute disproportionately more migrants to other demes, either because they are more productive (differential migration) or because their emigrants found successful new colonies. The superior gene combination thereby spreads through the species as a whole.

Reception

The shifting balance theory was one of the most debated ideas in evolutionary biology, standing in contrast to R. A. Fisher's view that large populations and mass selection suffice for adaptive evolution. It influenced the development of founder-effect speciation models and Ernst Mayr's peripatric speciation. Critics, including Jerry Coyne, Nicholas Barton, and Michael Turelli, argued in a widely cited 1997 review that the conditions required for all three phases to operate — especially phase III — are restrictive, and that empirical evidence for the process is weak. Defenders, including Wright's biographers and later theorists, have maintained that the theory remains a coherent and potentially important account of how epistatic gene combinations evolve. The theory is closely connected to Wright's adaptive landscape metaphor and his concept of epistasis.
