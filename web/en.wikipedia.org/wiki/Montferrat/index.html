Montferrat (Italian: Monferrato, IPA: [moɱferˈraːto]; Piedmontese: Monfrà) is a historical and geographical region of Piedmont in northwestern Italy. It consists of the hilly country between the river Po and the Ligurian Apennines, roughly divided into Upper Montferrat (Alto Monferrato) around Acqui Terme and Ovada, and Lower Montferrat (Basso Monferrato) around Casale Monferrato and Asti.

History

Montferrat was originally a march (border county) of the Holy Roman Empire, created in the 10th century under the Aleramici dynasty. The marquises of Montferrat became prominent crusaders: William V took part in the Second Crusade, his son Conrad of Montferrat became king of Jerusalem, and Boniface I led the Fourth Crusade and founded the Kingdom of Thessalonica. The Aleramici male line ended in 1305, and the march passed by marriage to the Byzantine Palaiologos dynasty, and then, in 1536, to the Gonzaga dukes of Mantua.

Fought over in the Italian Wars and the War of the Mantuan Succession, Montferrat was partitioned in the early 18th century: most of it passed to the Duchy of Savoy after the War of the Spanish Succession, and the remainder followed Mantua's fate. With Italian unification it became part of Piedmont.

Wine and landscape

Montferrat is a celebrated wine region, producing Barbera d'Asti and Barbera del Monferrato, Moscato d'Asti and Asti spumante, Grignolino, Freisa, and Cortese-based whites. In 2014 the vineyard landscapes of Langhe-Roero and Monferrato were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, recognising the region's centuries-old wine culture, its hilltop villages, castles, and underground wine cellars known as infernot.
