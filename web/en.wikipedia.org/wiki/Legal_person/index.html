Legal person
Legal persons are bearers of rights and duties in law

In law, a legal person is any person or entity that can do the things a human person is usually able to do in law — such as enter into contracts, sue and be sued, own property, and so on. The concept includes both natural persons (human beings) and juridical persons (also called artificial, juristic or fictitious persons): groups of people or organisations that are treated by the law as if they were a single individual, such as corporations, companies, partnerships, states, municipalities, universities, trade unions, charities and international organisations.

History

The idea that collectivities could hold rights developed in Roman law — where municipalities and collegia could own property — and was elaborated by medieval canon lawyers, who described the church and other bodies as persona ficta. Pope Innocent IV is often credited with the fiction theory of corporate personality. In the common law world, foundational cases such as Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] confirmed that a duly incorporated company is a separate legal person from its shareholders, with the consequence of limited liability. In the United States, cases including Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad (1886) and much later Citizens United v. FEC (2010) progressively extended constitutional rights to corporations.

Capacity and rights

The capacities of a juridical person differ from those of a natural person: a corporation cannot vote or marry, but it can hold property, contract and litigate in its own name, and it continues to exist beyond the lives of its members ("perpetual succession"). Whether and which human or constitutional rights legal persons enjoy varies by jurisdiction and by right — freedom of speech and protection against unreasonable search are recognised for corporations in the United States, while the European Convention on Human Rights can be invoked by companies in respect of fair trial and property rights.

Non-human natural persons and novel cases

Some legal systems have granted legal personhood to natural features and animals: New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua Act 2017 made the Whanganui River a legal person, and Te Urewera (a former national park) received similar status; courts in India have intermittently declared rivers and animals legal persons, with mixed outcomes on review. Ships are treated as legal persons for certain purposes in admiralty law. Debates continue over possible personhood for advanced artificial intelligences, great apes and ecosystems.
