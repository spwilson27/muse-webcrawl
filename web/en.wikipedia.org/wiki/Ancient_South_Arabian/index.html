Ancient South Arabian (also called Epigraphic South Arabian or Old South Arabian) is a group of closely related extinct Semitic languages spoken in the southwestern Arabian Peninsula — the territory of modern Yemen — from roughly the eighth century BC to the sixth century AD. They were the languages of the great caravan kingdoms of Saba, Ma'in, Qataban, and Hadramawt, which grew rich on the incense trade in frankincense and myrrh. Tens of thousands of inscriptions survive, recording laws, irrigation works, wars, dedications to gods, and contracts.

Languages and script

The four principal languages correspond to the four kingdoms: Sabaic (the most widely attested, language of Saba), Minaic, Qatabanic, and Hadramitic. They were written in the Ancient South Arabian script, called Musnad, a consonantal alphabet of 29 signs with a highly geometric, monumental appearance; a cursive form (zabūr) was used on wooden sticks for everyday documents. The script is not the ancestor of the Arabic alphabet but is related to the script family from which the Ethiopic script also descends.

History

Inscriptions from the kingdoms document sophisticated irrigation agriculture, including the famous Marib Dam. From the late fourth century AD, the Himyarite kingdom, which had united South Arabia, moved toward monotheism, and inscriptions begin to invoke a single god, Raḥmānān, in Jewish and later Christian contexts. After the Aksumite and then Sasanian occupations of the sixth century and the coming of Islam, Ancient South Arabian disappeared, replaced by Arabic. Its relationship to the Modern South Arabian languages (such as Mehri and Soqotri) is debated, but they are not direct descendants.
