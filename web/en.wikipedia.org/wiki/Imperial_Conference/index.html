Imperial Conference

The Imperial Conferences were periodic gatherings of the prime ministers (and other senior representatives) of the self-governing colonies and dominions of the British Empire, held in London between 1887 and 1937. Beginning as the Colonial Conference of 1887, organized around Queen Victoria's Golden Jubilee, they became the principal forum in which Britain and the dominions consulted on trade, defense, and constitutional questions, and they charted the Empire's evolution into the Commonwealth of Nations.

The 1926 conference produced the Balfour Declaration on dominion equality.

Development

Colonial Conferences met in 1887, 1894 (in Ottawa), 1897, and 1902; from 1907 they were renamed Imperial Conferences and scheduled regularly every four years. The 1917 and 1918 meetings took the form of an Imperial War Conference, with an Imperial War Cabinet coordinating the war effort. Postwar conferences in 1921, 1923, 1926, 1930, and 1937 grappled with dominion autonomy, foreign policy coordination, and imperial preference in trade — the latter addressed separately at the 1932 Ottawa Economic Conference.

Constitutional landmarks

The 1926 conference adopted the Balfour Declaration, declaring Britain and the dominions "autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another in any aspect of their domestic or external affairs, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown". The 1930 conference prepared the way for the Statute of Westminster 1931, which gave that equality legal effect by removing the British Parliament's authority to legislate for the dominions. The conferences were succeeded after the Second World War by the Commonwealth Prime Ministers' Conferences (from 1944), the ancestors of today's Commonwealth Heads of Government Meetings.
