Missionary

Missionary
Related termsMission, evangelism, proselytism
Major traditionsChristianity, Islam (da'wah), Buddhism, Baháʼí Faith

A missionary is a member of a religious group sent into an area to promote their faith or to provide services such as education, literacy, health care, or social welfare. The word derives from the Latin missio ("sending"), as in Jesus's words in John 20:21, "As the Father has sent me, I am sending you."

History

Buddhist missions under the Mauryan emperor Ashoka (3rd century BC) spread Buddhism across Asia. In Christianity, the apostles, especially Paul, carried the faith around the Mediterranean world; later missions accompanied figures such as Patrick in Ireland, Augustine of Canterbury in England, and Cyril and Methodius among the Slavs. From the 16th century, Catholic orders — Franciscans, Dominicans, and Jesuits such as Francis Xavier and Matteo Ricci — accompanied European colonisation in the Americas, Africa, and Asia. Protestant missions expanded in the 19th century with figures such as William Carey in India, David Livingstone in Africa, and Hudson Taylor in China, often founding schools, hospitals, and Bible translations. Missionary activity also played a major role in Islam (da'wah) and, in the 19th–20th centuries, in the worldwide spread of the Baháʼí Faith.

Criticism and modern practice

Missions have been criticised for their entanglement with colonialism, for coercive practices in institutions such as North American residential schools, and for undermining indigenous cultures; they have also been credited with advancing literacy, printing, medicine, and in some cases opposing abuses such as slavery and widow-burning. Modern missions increasingly emphasise partnership with local churches, development work, and "reverse mission" from the Global South to the historically Christian West. The study of missions is called missiology.
