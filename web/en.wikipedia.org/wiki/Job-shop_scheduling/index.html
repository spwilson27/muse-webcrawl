Job-shop scheduling

Job-shop scheduling is an optimization problem in computer science and operations research in which n jobs are assigned to m machines at specified times to optimize one or more objectives. Each job consists of a sequence of operations, and each operation must be processed on a specific machine for a given duration. No two operations of the same job may be processed simultaneously, and a machine can process at most one operation at a time.

Problem formulation

In the classic formulation, every job follows its own predetermined route through the machines (its operation order), unlike flow-shop scheduling, where all jobs follow the same route, and open-shop scheduling, where the order is free. Common objectives include minimizing the makespan (the total completion time of all jobs), total tardiness, or maximum lateness. In the standard three-field notation of scheduling theory, the basic job-shop problem is written Jm || Cmax.

Complexity

Job-shop scheduling is among the most difficult combinatorial optimization problems: the version with more than two machines is strongly NP-hard, and even modest instances (the famous 10×10 instance posed by Fisher and Thompson in 1963) resisted exact solution for over twenty years. The problem is typically modeled with a disjunctive graph, in which conjunctive arcs represent operation orderings within jobs and disjunctive arcs represent the choice of ordering of operations on each machine.

Solution methods

Exact approaches include branch and bound and constraint programming, with the shifting bottleneck procedure long considered a benchmark heuristic. In practice, dispatching rules (such as shortest processing time first), local search, tabu search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and other metaheuristics are used to obtain good schedules for industrial-size instances. Job-shop scheduling models real production environments in which small batches of varied products are manufactured to order.
