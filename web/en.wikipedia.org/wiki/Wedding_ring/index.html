Wedding ring

A wedding ring or wedding band is a finger ring that indicates that its wearer is married. It is usually forged from metal, traditionally gold or another precious metal, and is worn on the base of the finger adjacent to the palm. Wedding rings are exchanged by many couples during the marriage ceremony and worn thereafter as a public symbol of marital commitment and fidelity.

History

The exchange of rings has ancient roots: Egyptians wove rings of plant material as symbols of eternity, and Romans used iron and later gold rings in betrothal and marriage customs. In medieval Europe the church incorporated ring-giving into the wedding liturgy, and gimmel rings and inscribed "poesy rings" were popular in the early modern period. The custom of men wearing wedding rings became widespread only in the 20th century, spreading during the Second World War as a reminder of wives at home; by the late 20th century double-ring ceremonies were the norm in many countries.

Customs and materials

In many Western countries the wedding ring is worn on the fourth finger of the left hand — the "ring finger" — a custom once explained by the belief in a vena amoris ("vein of love") running directly to the heart. In parts of Europe and Latin America it is worn on the right hand. Bands may be plain or set with stones, and modern materials include platinum, palladium, titanium, tungsten carbide, and silicone. Religious practice varies: some traditions, such as those of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, do not include rings in the temple ceremony itself, while Eastern Orthodox weddings feature a blessing and exchange of rings as a central rite. Historically, a wedding ring differs from an engagement ring, which is typically given at the proposal and, in the diamond form popularized in the 20th century, worn together with the band after marriage — a practice celebrated in many wedding receptions.
