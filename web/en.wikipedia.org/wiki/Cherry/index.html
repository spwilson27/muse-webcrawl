Cherry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bunches of glossy ripe red cherries on the branch]

A cherry is the small round stone fruit of several Prunus species — chiefly the sweet cherry (Prunus avium), eaten fresh, and the sour or tart cherry (P. cerasus), destined for pies, preserves and liqueurs. Cherries ripen early in the orchard year and barely keep, which makes fresh-cherry season a short festival wherever they grow. Turkey is the largest sweet-cherry producer, the United States second, with Washington State its biggest growing region. Birds got there first in evolutionary terms: avium means "of the birds", and wild cherries spread through bird droppings long before orchards.

History

Cherries were domesticated around the Black and Caspian Seas; Roman general Lucullus is credited — too neatly, perhaps — with bringing prized varieties to Italy after campaigns in Asia Minor. In Japan the flowering cherry (P. serrulata and kin), bred for blossom rather than fruit, became the national symbol of transience, celebrated at hanami viewing parties. Europe's sour cherries entered liqueurs like kirschwasser and maraschino — the latter originally made from Dalmatian Marasca cherries, the ancestor of the lurid-red cocktail cherry, now usually a bleached, sweetened, dyed product that would puzzle its namesakes.

Cultivation and hazards

Cherry orchards fight weather and birds: rain at harvest splits the fruit, frost kills the early blossom, and netting is standard. Harvest is by hand for fresh fruit, machines shake processing cherries. Like other stone fruit, cherry pits and leaves contain cyanogenic compounds (the reason traditional cherry-pit pillows are heated). Cherries are rich in anthocyanins, and tart-cherry juice has built a sports-recovery following on modest but real anti-inflammatory evidence.
