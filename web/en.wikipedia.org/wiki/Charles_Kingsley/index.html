Charles Kingsley

Charles Kingsley (12 June 1819 – 23 January 1875) was a broad-church Anglican priest, university professor, social reformer, historian and novelist. He was a prominent leader of Christian socialism in England, a friend and correspondent of Charles Darwin, and an early supporter of the theory of evolution. He is remembered chiefly for his novels Westward Ho! (1855) and the children's classic The Water-Babies (1863).

Life and career

Kingsley was educated at Magdalene College, Cambridge, and became rector of Eversley in Hampshire in 1844, a living he held for the rest of his life. His friendship with F. D. Maurice drew him into the Christian socialist movement; under the pen name "Parson Lot" he wrote pamphlets and contributed to Politics for the People during the Chartist agitation of 1848. His novels Yeast (1848) and Alton Locke (1850) attacked rural and urban poverty respectively.

Works

The historical novel Hypatia (1853) is set in fifth-century Alexandria. Westward Ho!, a stirring tale of Elizabethan seafarers fighting Spain, was a bestseller that popularised the Devon coast and even gave its name to the town of Westward Ho! in Devon — the only place in Britain whose name includes an exclamation mark. The Water-Babies, A Fairy Tale for a Land Baby (1863), written for his son, combined a moral story about a chimney-sweep with Kingsley's enthusiasm for natural history and evolution. His Glaucus (1855) helped launch the Victorian aquarium craze.

Science and controversy

Kingsley corresponded with Darwin, who quoted his remark that it was "just as noble a conception of the Deity" to believe in divinely ordained self-developing forms in the second edition of On the Origin of Species. He championed public health and sanitary reform, and helped found the Chester Society for Natural Science. He is also associated with the phrase "muscular Christianity". His memoir was assembled by his widow, Frances Kingsley (1877). Kingsley's attitudes toward race and his anti-Catholic remarks in a famous 1864 review prompted John Henry Newman's Apologia Pro Vita Sua.
