Sparse approximation

Sparse approximation (also known as sparse representation or sparse coding) is the problem of representing a signal as a linear combination of a small number of elementary waveforms, called atoms, drawn from a (typically overcomplete) collection known as a dictionary. Formally, given a dictionary matrix D and a signal y, one seeks a coefficient vector x with few nonzero entries such that y ≈ Dx.

Formulation

The ideal formulation minimizes the number of nonzero coefficients (the ℓ0 "norm") subject to an error tolerance, an NP-hard combinatorial problem. Practical approaches include greedy algorithms such as matching pursuit and orthogonal matching pursuit, and convex relaxations that replace ℓ0 with the ℓ1 norm, as in basis pursuit and the LASSO. Theoretical guarantees for uniqueness and recovery are studied in terms of dictionary coherence and the restricted isometry property.

Applications

Sparse approximation underlies compressed sensing, in which signals are recovered from far fewer measurements than traditional sampling requires. Other applications include image denoising and inpainting, source separation, face recognition, audio processing, and neuroscience, where sparse coding has been proposed as a model of the primary visual cortex. Dictionary learning methods such as K-SVD learn the atoms themselves from data rather than using fixed transforms like wavelets.
