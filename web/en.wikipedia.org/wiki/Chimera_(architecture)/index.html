Chimera (architecture)

Chimeras on Notre-Dame de Paris

In architecture, a chimera is a grotesque stone figure, often combining parts of different animals or of humans and animals, used as decoration on buildings, particularly Gothic cathedrals. Unlike a gargoyle, a chimera does not serve as a waterspout; it is purely ornamental.

The term derives from the Chimera of Greek mythology, a fire-breathing monster with the head of a lion, the body of a goat, and the tail of a serpent.

History

Grotesque figures appeared in Romanesque architecture, but chimeras became especially prominent in the Gothic Revival of the 19th century. The most famous examples are the chimeras of the Galerie des Chimères of Notre-Dame de Paris, which were added during the restoration led by Eugène Viollet-le-Duc in the 1840s–1860s. The most celebrated of these, the Stryge, leans pensively on the balustrade of the cathedral's gallery, gazing over Paris.

Use and symbolism

Chimeras were thought to ward off evil, to represent the fantastical and the demonic in medieval thought, and to demonstrate the sculptor's imagination. In Gothic Revival buildings in Britain, France, and the United States, they remain a distinctive decorative motif.
