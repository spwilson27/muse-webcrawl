Astrometry

Astrometry is a branch of astronomy that involves precise measurements of the positions and movements of stars and other celestial bodies. It provides the kinematics and physical origin of the Solar System and the Milky Way galaxy. The information obtained by astrometric measurements includes the distances to stars (via parallax), their proper motions across the sky, and the detection of unseen companions such as exoplanets through the wobble they induce in their host stars.

History

Astrometry is one of the oldest branches of astronomy. Hipparchus compiled the first star catalogue in the 2nd century BC, and Tycho Brahe's naked-eye measurements reached an accuracy of about one arcminute. The first successful measurement of stellar parallax, of 61 Cygni by Friedrich Bessel in 1838, gave the first reliable distances to the stars. Photographic astrometry dominated the 20th century before the advent of space-based instruments.

Space astrometry

The European Space Agency's Hipparcos satellite (1989–1993) measured the positions, parallaxes, and proper motions of over 100,000 stars to milliarcsecond accuracy, revolutionising the field. Its successor, Gaia, launched in 2013, is surveying more than a billion stars with microarcsecond precision, producing a three-dimensional map of the Milky Way.

Applications

Astrometric data underpin celestial reference frames used for navigation, telescope pointing, and satellite tracking. Astrometry is also used to detect exoplanets, to measure the masses of binary stars, to determine the orbits of asteroids (including potentially hazardous ones), and to test theories of gravity through the precise motions of bodies in the Solar System.
