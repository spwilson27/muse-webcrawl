Extreme weather events of 535–536

The extreme weather events of 535–536 were the most severe and protracted short-term episodes of cooling in the Northern Hemisphere in the last 2,000 years. The event is thought to have been caused by an extensive atmospheric dust veil, possibly resulting from a large volcanic eruption in the tropics, or debris from space. Its effects were widespread, causing unseasonable weather, crop failures, and famines worldwide. The event initiated the Late Antique Little Ice Age, a period of cooling lasting from 536 to about 660 AD.

Evidence

The Byzantine historian Procopius recorded in 536, in his report on the wars with the Vandals, that "during this year a most dread portent took place. For the sun gave forth its light without brightness... and it seemed exceedingly like the sun in eclipse, for the beams it shed were not clear." In 538, the Roman statesman Cassiodorus described weak sunlight, failed crops, and frost. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica show a major sulfate spike around 533–534 ± 2 years, evidence of an extensive acidic dust veil. Tree-ring analysis shows abnormally low growth in Irish oak in 536 and another sharp drop in 542, and a similar pattern in Swedish, Finnish and Sierra Nevada trees.

Causes

The leading explanation is a major volcanic eruption that injected sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere. Candidate volcanoes include Ilopango in El Salvador (whose Tierra Blanca Joven eruption has been dated near this period) and an unidentified volcano possibly in Iceland or the North Pacific; research published in 2018 and 2020 points to Icelandic eruptions in 536, 540 and 547 sustaining the cooling. An alternative hypothesis, proposed by David Keys and Mike Baillie, involves comet debris, but volcanism is the mainstream view.

Consequences

Temperatures in Europe dropped by up to 2.5 °C (4.5 °F). Snow fell in China in summer, crops failed from Ireland to Mesopotamia, and drought struck Peru. The cooling, famine and social stress preceded the Plague of Justinian (541–542) and have been linked by some historians to the decline of the Sasanian and Byzantine Empires, migrations on the Eurasian steppe, and political upheavals in Scandinavia reflected in Norse myths of the Fimbulwinter.
