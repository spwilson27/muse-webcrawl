Fairy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fairy (also fay, fae, fair folk; from Latin fata, "the Fates") is a type of mythical being or legendary creature found in the folklore of multiple European cultures, including Celtic, Slavic, German, English, and French folklore. Fairies are generally described as supernatural or preternatural beings, often humanlike in appearance, possessed of magical powers, and notoriously capricious in their dealings with mortals.

Origins and characteristics

The word fairy derives from Old French faerie, denoting both the beings and their enchanted realm. In folk belief, fairies inhabit an otherworld — under hollow hills, across the western sea, or in a parallel version of the human landscape — and encounters with them involve taboos about food, names, and time: a night in fairyland might last a hundred years in the mortal world. Explanations offered for the origin of fairy belief include euhemerised memories of conquered peoples, nature spirits, and the spirits of the dead.

Belief in fairies overlapped with fear: children were guarded against being taken as changelings, iron and rowan were used as protections, and fairy gifts were treated as dangerous. Scottish tradition divided fairies into the Seelie and Unseelie courts, the kindly and the malign.

In literature and art

Fairies entered high literature through medieval romance and works such as Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene and Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream and The Tempest. The literary fairy tale, from Charles Perrault to the Brothers Grimm, fixed the fairy godmother in the imagination. Victorian Britain made fairies a mania in painting and theatre, while J. M. Barrie's Tinker Bell and the flower fairies of Cicely Mary Barker shaped the modern, miniature, winged image — quite unlike the often tall and dangerous beings of older folklore. The Cottingley Fairies photographs of 1917, later admitted to be fakes, famously deceived Arthur Conan Doyle.
