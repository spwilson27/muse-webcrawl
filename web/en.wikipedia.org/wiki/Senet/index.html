Senet


A senet board from ancient Egypt (placeholder image)

Senet (Ancient Egyptian: znt, meaning "passing") is a board game from ancient Egypt, among the oldest known board games in the world. It is played on a grid of three rows of ten squares, and boards have been found in burials dating back to the First Dynasty (c. 3100 BC) and throughout pharaonic history.

Gameplay

Two players each control a set of pieces — commonly five or seven pawns, often cones and spools — and move them along an S-shaped path through the thirty squares according to throws of casting sticks or knucklebones. Certain squares carried special markings and significance: the "house of happiness", the "house of water" (which sent a piece backwards), and the final squares associated with gods and judgement. The exact rules are not fully preserved and modern reconstructions differ.

Religious significance

Over time senet acquired religious meaning, coming to symbolise the journey of the soul through the afterlife. A passage in the Book of the Dead depicts the deceased playing senet against an invisible opponent, and later boards bore inscriptions identifying squares with deities and stages of the passage through the Duat.

Archaeology

Notable boards include four found in the tomb of Tutankhamun, some made of ebony and ivory, and the game is depicted in tomb paintings such as that in the tomb of Nefertari at Thebes. Senet remained popular for more than two thousand years, spreading to the Levant and Cyprus, before falling out of use in the Roman period.
