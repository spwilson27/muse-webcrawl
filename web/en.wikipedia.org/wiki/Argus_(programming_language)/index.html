Argus (programming language)

An Argus system: networked "guardians" encapsulating state and handlers.

Argus is an experimental programming language and system developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the late 1970s and 1980s by Barbara Liskov and her colleagues, for constructing distributed programs. Argus extended the data-abstraction language CLU with the notion of the guardian: a network-resident process that encapsulates and protects the integrity of data objects, offering operations ("handlers") invoked remotely by other guardians.

Design

Argus was designed to support programs that must be reliable in the presence of machine crashes and network failures. Its central mechanism was the atomic transaction: operations on data distributed among guardians could be grouped into nested atomic actions that either completed entirely or had no effect, using two-phase commit and stable storage with versioned objects. Guardians recovered their state after crashes so that atomicity was preserved across failures.

Influence

The Argus project demonstrated that programming-language support — rather than libraries alone — could provide distribution, transactions and crash resilience in an integrated way, and it influenced later work on distributed objects, transactional systems and language-based reliability. Liskov's work on Argus and CLU contributed to her receiving the 2008 Turing Award for contributions to practical and theoretical foundations of programming language and system design, especially related to data abstraction, fault tolerance and distributed computing.
