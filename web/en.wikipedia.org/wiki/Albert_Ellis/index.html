Albert Ellis (27 September 1913 – 24 July 2007) was an American psychologist who founded rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT), one of the earliest forms of cognitive-behavioral therapy. In a 1982 survey of American and Canadian psychotherapists, Ellis was ranked the second most influential psychotherapist in history, behind Carl Rogers and ahead of Sigmund Freud.

Development of REBT

Trained originally in psychoanalysis, Ellis became dissatisfied with its passivity and slow pace. In 1955 he began presenting what he first called rational therapy, arguing that emotional disturbance results less from events themselves than from the irrational beliefs people hold about those events. He summarised this in an "ABC" model: an Activating event triggers Beliefs, which produce emotional and behavioural Consequences; therapy works by Disputing irrational beliefs. He famously catalogued core irrational ideas such as the demand to be loved and approved by everyone and the insistence that one must be thoroughly competent at all times.

Career and character

Ellis founded the Institute for Rational Living (later the Albert Ellis Institute) in New York in 1959 and held his famous Friday Night Workshops there for decades. He was known for a direct, humorous and often profane style, for writing therapeutic songs, and for an extraordinary output of books and articles, including A Guide to Rational Living (1961). He also wrote early, liberal works on sexuality, such as Sex Without Guilt (1958), which cost him professionally at the time.

Legacy

REBT became a foundation of the cognitive revolution in psychotherapy and influenced Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy. Ellis continued working into his nineties and died in 2007.
