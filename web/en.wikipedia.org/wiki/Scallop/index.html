Scallop

Scallops are a group of marine bivalve molluscs of the family Pectinidae. They are cosmopolitan in the world's oceans and are recognised by their distinctive fan-shaped shells with radiating ribs and two flaring "ears" (auricles) beside the hinge. Many species are prized as seafood; the word "scallop" in cooking usually refers to the large adductor muscle, sometimes served together with the roe ("coral").

Phylum: Mollusca

Biology

Unlike most bivalves, many scallops can swim: by rapidly clapping their valves together they expel jets of water that propel them away from predators such as starfish. Along the edge of the mantle they bear rows of numerous bright blue eyes — as many as 200 in some species — which use a concave mirror of guanine crystals behind the retina to form images, an unusual optical design. Scallops are filter feeders, straining plankton from the water. Some attach to the seabed by byssal threads or cement one valve in place, while others rest free on the bottom. Most species are sequential or simultaneous hermaphrodites, releasing eggs and sperm into the water.

Fisheries and symbolism

Important commercial species include the Atlantic sea scallop (Placopecten magellanicus), the Japanese yesso scallop, and the great scallop (Pecten maximus), harvested by dredging, diving, and aquaculture. The scallop shell is an ancient symbol: in Christianity it is the emblem of Saint James the Great and of pilgrims on the Camino de Santiago, and it appears in art as the shell of Venus in Botticelli's The Birth of Venus. It is also the logo of the oil company Shell.
