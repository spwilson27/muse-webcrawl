Poisson point process

In probability theory, statistics and related fields, a Poisson point process is a type of random mathematical object that consists of points randomly located on a mathematical space with the essential feature that the points occur independently of one another.

The process is often called simply the Poisson process, and is used to model random points in time and space, such as the times of radioactive decay, telephone calls arriving at an exchange, photons hitting a detector, or the positions of trees in a forest. The number of points in any bounded region follows a Poisson distribution whose mean is proportional to the region's size (in the homogeneous case), and counts in disjoint regions are independent.

Types

A homogeneous Poisson point process has constant intensity; an inhomogeneous (non-homogeneous) process has an intensity function varying over space. Poisson point processes are fundamental in queueing theory, telecommunications, astronomy and spatial statistics.
