The Geʾez script (ግእዝ), also known as Ethiopic, is an abugida used in Ethiopia and Eritrea. It originated as a consonantal alphabet (abjad) of 26 signs derived from the Ancient South Arabian script, and developed by the 4th century AD into a fully vocalised syllabary in which each base character is modified in up to seven "orders" to indicate the following vowel. The script is called fidäl (ፊደል) in Amharic, meaning "script" or "letter".

It was created for the Geʾez language and later adapted for Amharic, Tigrinya and several other Ethiopian and Eritrean languages, some of which use additional characters.

History

The earliest inscriptions from the Dʿmt kingdom were written in the South Arabian musnad script. Over the centuries a distinct cursive-derived local form emerged; early Geʾez inscriptions, such as those on coins and stelae of the Aksumite kings, were still consonantal. Under King Ezana in the 4th century AD the script acquired its characteristic vocalic modifications and the left-to-right direction was standardised, replacing the earlier boustrophedon and right-to-left writings. Around the same period, word division — originally marked by a colon-like sign (:) — eventually gave way to spaces in modern printed text.

Structure

Each consonant appears in seven orders corresponding to the vowels ä, u, i, a, e, ə and o. The first order (the gəʾəz order) carries the inherent vowel /ä/. Additional labialised forms exist for several consonants, and Amharic uses extra characters for sounds such as /v/ and palatalised consonants.

Numerals and punctuation

The traditional Ethiopic numeral system is an alphabetic, non-positional system with distinct signs for 1–9, 10–90, 100 and 10,000; it remains in use in some traditional contexts alongside Western Arabic numerals. Traditional punctuation includes the word separator ፡ and sentence mark ።, and symbols for paragraphs and quotation used in manuscript practice.

Use today

The script is the working writing system of Ethiopia and Eritrea, used for Amharic (the working language of the Ethiopian federal government), Tigrinya, and other languages, and for the liturgy of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches. It is encoded in Unicode in the Ethiopic blocks and is supported by standard keyboards and input methods in the region.
