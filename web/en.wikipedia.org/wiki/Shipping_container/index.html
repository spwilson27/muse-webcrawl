Shipping container

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Stacks of multicoloured shipping containers at a container terminal]
Containers stacked at a port.

An intermodal container (shipping container) is a standardised steel box used to transport goods by ship, rail and truck without unloading the cargo between modes. The common sizes are 20-foot (TEU) and 40-foot (FEU) lengths, 8 feet wide and 8 feet 6 inches high, made of corrugated weathering (Corten) steel with twistlock corner castings. Standardised under ISO rules in the late 1960s, the container is credited with revolutionising world trade.

Design

Containers have doors at one end, a plywood or steel floor, and a unique reporting mark (owner code, serial number and check digit) painted on the sides. Types include dry freight, refrigerated \'reefers\' with integral cooling units, open-top, flat-rack and tank containers for liquids.

Containerisation

Because every box fits every cellular ship, crane, chassis and rail wagon built for it, goods are packed once at the factory and opened at destination. Port labour costs collapsed, older break-bulk docks (London, New York) closed, and new container ports grew — manufacturing shifted globally as freight rates fell to a tiny fraction of goods\' value.

Other uses

Retired containers are reused as site offices, storage and housing in \'container architecture\'. During the COVID-19 pandemic, container shortages and port congestion sent spot freight rates to record highs, underlining the box\'s central role in supply chains.
