Survivorship bias

Survivorship bias or survival bias is the logical error of concentrating on entities that passed a selection process while overlooking those that did not, typically because of their lack of visibility. This can lead to incorrect conclusions because the surviving sample is systematically different from the original population.

The aircraft example

The best-known illustration comes from the Second World War, when the statistician Abraham Wald considered how to armour bomber aircraft. Analysts proposed reinforcing the areas where returning planes showed the most bullet holes. Wald argued the opposite: the holes marked where a plane could be hit and still return, so armour belonged where the returning planes had no holes — because planes hit there had not survived to be counted.

Other examples

Survivorship bias appears in finance, where failed funds quietly disappear from databases and inflate average returns; in business advice drawn only from successful companies; in perceptions of older buildings or music as inherently better, when only the finest examples survive; and in medical and historical studies based only on those who lived long enough to be observed.

Related concepts

It is a form of selection bias and is related to the base rate fallacy and Berkson's paradox. Accounting for it requires reconstructing the full population, including the unobserved failures.
