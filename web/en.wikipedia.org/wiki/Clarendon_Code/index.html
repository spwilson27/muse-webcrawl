Clarendon Code

Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon, the statesman after whom the Code was named.

The Clarendon Code was a series of four legal statutes passed between 1661 and 1665 which effectively re-established the supremacy of the Anglican Church after the Restoration of the English monarchy, and discriminated against Protestant nonconformists (dissenters) and Roman Catholics. It is named after Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon, the chief minister of Charles II at the Restoration — although Clarendon himself was not the driving force behind all of its provisions, and the Cavalier Parliament pressed for a harsher settlement than he sometimes favoured.

The four statutes

  • Corporation Act 1661 — required all municipal officials to take Anglican communion, renounce the Solemn League and Covenant, and swear allegiance to the Crown, effectively excluding dissenters from local government.
  • Act of Uniformity 1662 — required all ministers to assent to the revised Book of Common Prayer and to be episcopally ordained. About 2,000 clergy who refused were ejected from their livings on St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662 — the "Great Ejection".
  • Conventicle Act 1664 — forbade religious assemblies (conventicles) of five or more people outside the Church of England, with escalating penalties including transportation.
  • Five Mile Act 1665 — forbade ejected ministers from coming within five miles of any parish from which they had been expelled, or from teaching in schools, unless they swore an oath of non-resistance.

Effects and repeal

The Code drove nonconformity underground and created a permanent class of Protestant dissenters — Presbyterians, Independents, Baptists and Quakers — excluded from public office and the universities. Charles II attempted to soften it with his Royal Declaration of Indulgence (1672), but Parliament forced its withdrawal and instead passed the Test Act of 1673. The penal provisions were gradually relaxed: the Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to Trinitarian dissenters, though civil disabilities remained until the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828 and Catholic emancipation in 1829.
