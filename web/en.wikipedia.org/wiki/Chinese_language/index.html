Chinese language

Chinese (中文, Zhōngwén; 漢語, Hànyǔ) is a group of languages comprising the most spoken branch of the Sino-Tibetan language family, with some 1.3 billion native speakers — more than any other language family branch. Its major varieties include Mandarin, Wu, Yue (Cantonese), Min, Xiang, Hakka and Gan, many of which are mutually unintelligible in speech but historically share a common written standard.

Varieties

Chinese is traditionally divided into seven or more major dialect groups. Mandarin, centred on the Beijing dialect, is by far the largest, spoken natively by about 70% of Chinese speakers and serving as the basis of the national standard. Yue, including Cantonese, dominates Guangdong and Hong Kong; Wu includes Shanghainese; Min varieties, such as Hokkien, are spoken in Fujian and Taiwan. Whether these groups are "dialects" or separate languages is a long-standing linguistic and political question.

Phonology and grammar

All modern Chinese varieties are tonal: Standard Mandarin has four main tones plus a neutral tone, while Cantonese has six or more. Chinese grammar is highly analytic, with little inflection; word order, particles and classifiers (measure words) carry much grammatical information. Syllable structure is relatively simple, built around an initial consonant, a final, and a tone.

Writing system

Chinese is written with logographic characters, each typically representing a syllable and a morpheme. The script descends from oracle bone inscriptions of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1200 BCE), making it the oldest continuously used writing system in the world. In the 1950s the People's Republic of China promulgated simplified characters, while traditional characters remain standard in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau. Romanisation systems, principally pinyin, are used for teaching and transliteration.

History

Scholars reconstruct stages including Old Chinese (to about the 3rd century CE), Middle Chinese (the language of the Qieyun rhyme dictionary, 601 CE) and the early modern vernaculars. Classical Chinese (wényán) served as the written standard for two millennia until the literary revolution of the 1910s–20s promoted written vernacular Chinese (báihuà).
