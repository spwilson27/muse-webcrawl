History of Kerala

The history of Kerala concerns the region on the Malabar Coast of southwestern India that forms the modern state of Kerala, created on 1 November 1956. For millennia its ports traded spices — above all black pepper — with West Asia, the Mediterranean world, and China; later it became the first point of sustained European contact with India, while in the modern era it has become known for high literacy and social development indicators alongside distinctive traditions of religious coexistence.

Ancient and medieval periods

The early historic Chera kingdom, attested in Sangam literature and in Roman accounts of the port of Muziris, traded pepper, ivory, and teak for Roman gold. Tradition holds that St Thomas the Apostle reached Kerala in AD 52, founding one of the world's oldest Christian communities; Jewish merchants also settled early — the Jewish copper plates of Cochin date to around AD 1000 — and Islam arrived via Arab traders by the early medieval period. The second Chera (Kulasekhara) empire (c. 800–1102) gave way to competing chiefdoms, including the Zamorin of Calicut, whose port dominated the pepper trade.

European contact

Vasco da Gama's landing near Calicut in 1498 opened the European era. The Portuguese, then the Dutch (who defeated Portugal at Kochi in 1663), then the English East India Company successively sought control of the spice trade. In the 18th century Marthanda Varma expanded Travancore, defeating the Dutch at Colachel (1741); Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan of Mysore invaded northern Kerala in the later 1700s. By the 19th century Travancore and Cochin were princely states under British paramountcy and Malabar a district of Madras Presidency.

Modern Kerala

Social reform movements — led by figures such as Sree Narayana Guru and Ayyankali — attacked caste oppression in the late 19th and 20th centuries. After independence, the Malayalam-speaking regions were united as Kerala in 1956. In 1957 Kerala elected one of the world's first democratically chosen communist governments, under E. M. S. Namboodiripad; land reforms, mass literacy campaigns, public health investment, and later Gulf remittances produced the celebrated "Kerala model" of development — high human development at modest income levels.
