Sphere packing

In geometry, a sphere packing is an arrangement of non-overlapping spheres within a containing space. The spheres considered are usually of identical size, and the space is usually three-dimensional Euclidean space. The sphere packing problem asks for the arrangement in which the spheres fill as large a proportion of the space as possible; this proportion is the density of the packing.

Regular packings

In three dimensions, the densest packing is achieved by the familiar arrangements in which spheres are stacked in hexagonal layers, as in piles of oranges or cannonballs. The two simplest optimal arrangements are the face-centred cubic (FCC) packing and the hexagonal close packing (HCP), along with infinitely many variants obtained by choosing the stacking sequence of layers. All have density π/(3√2) ≈ 0.74048, meaning about 74% of space is filled.

The claim that no packing can do better is the Kepler conjecture, stated by Johannes Kepler in 1611. It resisted proof for nearly four centuries until Thomas Hales, with Samuel Ferguson, announced a proof in 1998 relying heavily on computer case-checking. The proof was published in 2005, and the Flyspeck project completed a fully formal, machine-verified version in 2014.

Other dimensions

In one dimension the problem is trivial, and in two dimensions the optimal arrangement is the hexagonal packing of circles, with density π/(2√3) ≈ 0.9069. In higher dimensions the problem is largely open. In 2016 Maryna Viazovska proved that the E8 lattice gives the optimal packing in dimension 8, and shortly afterwards, working with collaborators, that the Leech lattice is optimal in dimension 24. These remain the only dimensions beyond three in which the optimal density is known exactly.

Applications

Sphere packing bears on the arrangement of atoms in crystals, on error-correcting codes (via packings in coding spaces), and on the geometry of lattices. Random ("jammed") packings, by contrast, have a density of about 0.64, a figure relevant to the physics of granular materials.
