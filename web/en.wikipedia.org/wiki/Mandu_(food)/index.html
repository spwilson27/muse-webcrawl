Mandu (food)

Mandu
TypeDumpling
Place of originKorea
Main ingredientsWheat dough; minced meat and/or vegetables, tofu, glass noodles
VariationsJjin-mandu (steamed), gun-mandu (pan-fried), mul-mandu (boiled), teok-mandu-guk

Mandu (Korean: 만두) are dumplings in Korean cuisine, typically consisting of a savoury filling wrapped in a thin circle of wheat dough and then cooked by steaming, boiling, pan-frying, or deep-frying. Mandu can be served on their own, with a dipping sauce of soy sauce and vinegar, or in a soup such as mandu-guk.

History

Dumplings are believed to have reached Korea from Central Asia along the Silk Road, or via the Mongol invasions of the 13th–14th centuries, when Yuan-dynasty customs were introduced to the Goryeo court. An early reference appears in Goryeo-era records of stuffed dishes served at court banquets. During the Joseon dynasty, mandu became a festive food, especially associated with the Lunar New Year (Seollal), when families gathered to fold dumplings together — a tradition that continues today. In North Korea's Kaesong region, large, elaborately shaped mandu remain a noted speciality.

Varieties

By cooking method, mandu are classified as jjin-mandu (steamed), gun-mandu (pan-fried, crisped on one or more sides), mul-mandu (boiled), and twigim-mandu (deep-fried). By filling, common types include gogi-mandu (meat), kimchi-mandu, saeu-mandu (shrimp), and napjak-mandu, the large flat dumplings of Daegu. Oversized steamed buns filled like mandu are called wang-mandu ("king dumplings").

In soup

Teok-mandu-guk, a soup of sliced rice cakes and mandu in beef broth, is a signature New Year's dish; eating it is traditionally said to add a year to one's age and bring good fortune. Plain mandu-guk is served year-round.
