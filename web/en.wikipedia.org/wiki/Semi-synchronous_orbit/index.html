Semi-synchronous orbit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Semi-synchronous orbit: orbital period ≈ 11 h 58 min; altitude ≈ 20,200 km for Earth.

A semi-synchronous orbit is an orbit with a period equal to half the average rotational period of the body being orbited. For Earth, a satellite in a semi-synchronous orbit completes two revolutions in one sidereal day, giving a period of about 11 hours 58 minutes, at an altitude of approximately 20,200 km (12,544 miles) above sea level.

Characteristics

Because the satellite makes exactly two orbits per day, its ground track repeats daily, passing over the same locations at the same local times twice a day. Semi-synchronous orbits are typically near-circular and, for navigation constellations, inclined at about 55 degrees to the equator.

Uses

The best-known users of this orbit are the satellites of the Global Positioning System (GPS), which orbit at about 20,200 km altitude with a period of 11 hours 58 minutes. The Russian GLONASS system uses a slightly lower, near-semi-synchronous orbit with a period of 11 hours 15 minutes. Medium Earth orbit communications and navigation payloads favour this regime because it combines wide coverage footprints with repeating geometry for receivers on the ground.
