Thermocouple

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A thermocouple probe with its two dissimilar metal wires twisted at the tip
A thermocouple probe with its two dissimilar metal wires twisted at the tip

A thermocouple is an electrical device consisting of two dissimilar electrical conductors forming a junction, which produces a temperature-dependent voltage as a result of the Seebeck effect. Cheap, rugged, self-powered and usable from cryogenic temperatures to above 2,300 °C, thermocouples are the most widely used temperature sensors in industry — in furnaces, engines, kilns, power plants and spacecraft — and are standardised in types (K, J, T, E, N and the noble-metal S, R, B) by their metal pairs.

Principle and practice

When two junctions of dissimilar metals sit at different temperatures, a small voltage (microvolts per degree) appears; measuring it (with cold-junction compensation) gives the hot junction's temperature. Type K (chromel–alumel) is the general-purpose standard (−200 to +1,250 °C); noble-metal types serve hotter, oxidising duties at higher cost.

History

Thomas Johann Seebeck discovered the effect in 1821 while seeking electricity–magnetism links; the thermopile (many junctions in series) followed for radiation measurement, and thermocouples industrialised with pyrometry in the steel age. Their simplicity — no excitation, no electronics at the point of sensing — keeps them irreplaceable even in the age of the platinum RTD and the infrared camera.
