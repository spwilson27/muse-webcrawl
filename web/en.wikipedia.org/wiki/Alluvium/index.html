Alluvium

Alluvium (from Latin alluvius, from alluere, "to wash against") is loose clay, silt, sand or gravel that has been deposited by running water in a stream bed, on a floodplain, in an alluvial fan or in related settings. Deposits are typically young, unconsolidated and sorted by the flow that laid them down: coarse gravels near the channel, fine silts and clays on the floodplain.

Importance

Alluvial deposits form some of the world's most fertile agricultural soils — the floodplains of the Nile, Ganges, Yellow River and Mississippi — and are renewed by seasonal flooding. They also hold major aquifers and placer deposits of gold, tin and gemstones washed out of bedrock upstream. Ancient, lithified alluvium is termed alluvialite.

Landforms

Rivers deposit alluvium as point bars on the inside of meanders, as natural levees along their banks, as deltas where they meet standing water, and as broad alluvial fans where confined mountain streams emerge onto plains. Repeated deposition over millennia builds the wide, flat valleys characteristic of mature river systems.
