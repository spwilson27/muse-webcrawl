The PHQ-9 is a multipurpose instrument for screening, diagnosing, monitoring and measuring the severity of depression. It is the nine-item depression module of the larger Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) and was developed by Kurt Kroenke, Robert Spitzer and Janet Williams, with an educational grant from Pfizer, validating the work in a landmark 2001 study. The PHQ-9 is incorporated into many clinical practice guidelines and is one of the most widely used depression measures in primary care and mental-health settings worldwide.

Scoring

Each of the nine items corresponds to one of the DSM criteria symptoms of a major depressive episode. Respondents rate how often they have been bothered by each problem over the previous two weeks: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days) or 3 (nearly every day). The items cover little interest or pleasure, feeling down or hopeless, sleep problems, low energy, appetite changes, feeling bad about oneself, trouble concentrating, psychomotor changes, and thoughts of self-harm.

A score of 10 or greater has been found to have a sensitivity and specificity of about 88% for major depression. A categorical scoring algorithm — endorsing at least five items at "more than half the days", including depressed mood or anhedonia — can also be used to support a provisional diagnosis of major depressive disorder.

Use

Because it is brief, free to use and translated into many languages, the PHQ-9 is routine in primary care screening programs, collaborative-care models, telehealth and clinical trials, where a change of five points is commonly regarded as clinically significant. Its ninth item, concerning thoughts of death or self-harm, prompts further risk assessment when endorsed, although the item alone is a poor predictor of individual suicide risk. Related instruments include the two-item PHQ-2 screener and the PHQ-15 for somatic symptoms.
