Bohdan Zynoviy Mykhailovych Khmelnytsky (c. 1595 – 6 August 1657) was a Ruthenian nobleman and military commander of Zaporozhian Cossacks as Hetman of the Zaporozhian Host, then in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. He led an uprising against the Commonwealth and its magnates in 1648–1654 that resulted in the creation of an independent Cossack state, the Cossack Hetmanate. In 1654, he concluded the Treaty of Pereiaslav with the Russian Tsardom, placing the Hetmanate under the protection of the Russian tsar and beginning a long process that eventually drew the Cossack lands into the Russian Empire.

Early life

Khmelnytsky was born around 1595, probably in Subotiv near Chyhyryn, the son of a Cossack officer in the service of the Polish crown. He was educated at a Jesuit college, fought in the Battle of Cecora (1620) against the Ottoman Empire, where his father was killed and he was captured, spending two years as a prisoner in Constantinople. After his return he became a registered Cossack officer and estate holder.

The uprising

A personal dispute with a Polish official, Daniel Czapliński, who seized Khmelnytsky's estate and, according to tradition, had his young son beaten, drove him to revolt. Elected hetman by the Zaporozhian Cossacks in early 1648, he allied with the Crimean Tatars and won major victories over Commonwealth forces at Zhovti Vody and Korsun in 1648, followed by Pyliavtsi. The uprising spread across Ukraine and was accompanied by massacres of Polish nobles, Roman Catholic and Uniate clergy, and tens of thousands of Jews — events remembered as a catastrophe in Jewish history. The 1649 Treaty of Zboriv granted the Cossacks a measure of autonomy, but fighting resumed; after the Cossack defeat at Berestechko (1651) and setbacks thereafter, Khmelnytsky turned to Moscow.

Pereiaslav and legacy

The 1654 Pereiaslav Agreement brought the Hetmanate under the tsar's protection and led to the Russo-Polish War of 1654–1667, after which Ukraine was partitioned along the Dnieper River. Khmelnytsky died in 1657 and was buried in Subotiv. In Ukrainian national tradition he is honoured as a founding father of the Ukrainian state and a national hero; in Polish memory he is a rebel who devastated the Commonwealth, and in Jewish memory the uprising he led is associated with mass killings. His image appears on the Ukrainian five-hryvnia banknote, and a major monument to him stands in Kyiv.
