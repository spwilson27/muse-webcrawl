Three-Chapter Controversy

Date543/544 – 7th century
Principal figuresEmperor Justinian I; Pope Vigilius; Theodore of Mopsuestia; Theodoret of Cyrrhus; Ibas of Edessa
Key eventSecond Council of Constantinople (553)

The Three-Chapter Controversy, a phase of the Chalcedonian controversy, was a dispute about three persons — Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and Ibas of Edessa — and their writings, known collectively as the Three Chapters (tria kephalaia). In 544 the Eastern Roman emperor Justinian I issued an edict condemning the Three Chapters, hoping thereby to reconcile the miaphysite (non-Chalcedonian) Christians of Egypt and Syria to the imperial church. The condemnation was confirmed by the Second Council of Constantinople in 553, but it provoked fierce resistance in the Latin West and a schism that lasted into the 7th century.

The Three Chapters

The condemned works were: (1) the person and writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428), teacher of Nestorius, whose Christology was suspected of dividing Christ into two persons; (2) the writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus against Cyril of Alexandria and the Council of Ephesus; and (3) the letter of Ibas of Edessa to Maris, which attacked Cyril and praised Theodore. The Council of Chalcedon (451) had restored Theodoret and Ibas without condemning their writings, so Chalcedon's defenders in the West saw Justinian's edict as an attack on the council itself.

Vigilius and the council of 553

Pope Vigilius, brought to Constantinople and held there for years, first condemned the Chapters in his Iudicatum (548), then withdrew it under pressure from outraged Western bishops, and finally, after the council of 553 — which he refused to attend — accepted its decrees in 554. The tumult damaged papal prestige in the West: bishops in Aquileia, Milan and Istria broke communion with Rome in the Schism of the Three Chapters, which in Istria and the Lombard territories was not fully healed until about 698.

Outcome

The condemnation failed in its purpose: miaphysite churches were not reconciled, and the controversy consumed Byzantine church politics for decades, paving the way for later compromises such as monoenergism and monotheletism. Historically it illustrates the depth of the Christological divisions after Chalcedon and the use of imperial power in doctrinal affairs.
