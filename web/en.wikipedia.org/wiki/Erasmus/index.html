Desiderius Erasmus Roterodamus (1466/1469–1536), commonly known simply as Erasmus, was a Dutch Christian humanist, Catholic priest and theologian, regarded as the greatest scholar of the Northern Renaissance. A prolific writer and tireless correspondent, he championed education reform, satirized abuses in the Church, and prepared a landmark Greek–Latin edition of the New Testament.

Life

Born in Rotterdam, Erasmus was educated by the Brethren of the Common Life and at Deventer, entered the Augustinian canons at Steyn, and was ordained a priest in 1492. He travelled widely—Paris, England (where he befriended Thomas More), Italy, Leuven, Freiburg and Basel—living by his pen and patronage.

Works

His satirical In Praise of Folly (1511) mocked corruption and superstition. The Novum Instrumentum (1516), the first published Greek New Testament, influenced Reformation debates and later translations. His Adagia collected classical proverbs, and the Colloquia popularized humanist dialogue.

Relation to the Reformation

Erasmus criticized Luther's determinism in De libero arbitrio (1524), and Luther replied with De servo arbitrio. Erasmus remained Catholic while urging reform from within; all his works were placed on the Church's Index of Prohibited Books in 1559.

Legacy

The EU's Erasmus student-exchange programme is named after him.
