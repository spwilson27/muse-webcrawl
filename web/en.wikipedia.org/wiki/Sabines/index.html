Sabines

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sabines (Latin: Sabini) were an Italic people who lived in the central Apennine Mountains of the Italian Peninsula, in the region northeast of Rome known as Sabina. Speaking an Osco-Umbrian language, they were close neighbours and early rivals of the Romans, and played a foundational role in Roman legendary history.

The Rape of the Sabine Women

According to Livy's account, Romulus, finding his new city short of women, invited the Sabines to a festival and seized their unmarried women. War followed, but the abducted women intervened between the armies, and the two peoples united under Romulus and the Sabine king Titus Tatius, who ruled jointly until Tatius's death. The episode — the "Rape of the Sabine Women" (from Latin rapere, to seize) — became one of the most depicted subjects in Renaissance and Baroque art.

History

Sabine elements in early Rome are reflected in tradition: the second king, Numa Pompilius, was said to be a Sabine from Cures, and the Quirinal Hill was associated with Sabine settlement. The Claudian gens claimed Sabine origin. Despite this, Sabine communities resisted Roman expansion and were defeated in a series of campaigns, being finally subdued in 290 BC by Manius Curius Dentatus and absorbed into the Roman state with citizenship without the vote, later receiving full citizenship.

The Sabines were famed in Roman moral tradition for austerity and frugality, and their rural virtues were contrasted with urban luxury. Their territory, with towns such as Reate (modern Rieti) and Cures, became a prosperous agricultural region of villas and olive groves.
