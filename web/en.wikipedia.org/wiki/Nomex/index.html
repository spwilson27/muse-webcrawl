Nomex

Nomex is a flame-resistant meta-aramid material developed in the early 1960s by DuPont and first marketed in 1967. Chemically it is poly(m-phenylene isophthalamide), related to nylon and to the para-aramid Kevlar, which DuPont developed in the same programme of research led in part by Stephanie Kwolek and Wilfred Sweeny.

Properties

Nomex does not melt, drip or support combustion in air; instead it chars and thickens when exposed to intense heat, forming a protective barrier. It resists most chemicals and retains its mechanical strength at temperatures up to around 370 °C (700 °F) for limited periods. It is produced as fibres, yarns, woven fabrics, and papers and pressboards for electrical insulation.

Applications

The material is best known in protective clothing: firefighters' turnout gear and flash hoods, military flight suits and tank crew clothing, and racing drivers' fire suits, gloves and balaclavas — racing safety standards adopted after fatal fires of the 1960s made flame-resistant suits compulsory. Nomex honeycomb, made from Nomex paper, is a core material in aircraft panels and in the structures of racing cars and boats. Nomex paper is widely used as electrical insulation in transformers and motors, and in loudspeaker cones and other speciality products.
