London Declaration

The London Declaration was a declaration issued by the 1949 Commonwealth Prime Ministers' Conference in London that resolved the question of India's continued membership of the Commonwealth after it became a republic. It is generally regarded as the founding moment of the modern Commonwealth of Nations.

Background

Until 1949, membership of the Commonwealth rested on a common allegiance to the British Crown, as expressed in the Balfour Declaration of 1926 and the Statute of Westminster 1931. India, independent since 1947, was preparing to adopt a republican constitution, and other members feared that requiring allegiance to the Crown would either force India out of the association or exclude any future republic. Ireland's departure from the Commonwealth in April 1949 sharpened the problem.

Terms

The declaration, issued on 28 April 1949, recorded the agreement of the eight member governments that India would remain a full member as a republic, accepting King George VI "as the symbol of the free association of its independent member nations and as such the Head of the Commonwealth". The member countries were declared "freely co-operating ... in the pursuit of peace, liberty and progress", pursuing their independence as "free and equal members of the Commonwealth of Nations".

Significance

By severing membership from allegiance to the Crown, the London Declaration opened the Commonwealth to the republics that would make up most of its later membership as decolonisation proceeded; Pakistan used the precedent to remain after becoming a republic in 1956, as did Ghana in 1960 and many states thereafter. The monarch's role as Head of the Commonwealth became a purely symbolic one, held personally rather than as an automatic attribute of the Crown, a distinction that mattered when the members chose Charles III as successor to Elizabeth II in 2018. The declaration also replaced the older term "British Commonwealth" with "Commonwealth of Nations".
