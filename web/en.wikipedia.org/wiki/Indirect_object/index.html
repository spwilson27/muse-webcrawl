Indirect object

An indirect object is, in grammar, an object that is used with a transitive verb and a direct object, indicating the entity for whose benefit or to whom an action is done. In the English sentence "She gave Maria the book", Maria is the indirect object and the book is the direct object: Maria is the recipient of the giving.

Forms

In English, the indirect object typically precedes the direct object and can often be paraphrased with a prepositional phrase using to or for: "She gave the book to Maria." Verbs that take both objects are called ditransitive verbs. In many other languages, the indirect object is marked by the dative case or by a dedicated preposition, and its position in the sentence may be freer than in English.

Complications

Not every prepositional phrase expressing a recipient corresponds to an indirect object, and linguists distinguish true indirect objects from oblique arguments by tests such as passivisation: in English, an indirect object can often become the subject of a passive ("Maria was given the book"), a property not shared by ordinary prepositional phrases. Some analyses treat the two objects of a ditransitive verb in terms of primary and secondary objects instead.
