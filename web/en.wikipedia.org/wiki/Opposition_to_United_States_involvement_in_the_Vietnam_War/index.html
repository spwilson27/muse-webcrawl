Opposition to United States involvement in the Vietnam War

Anti-Vietnam War movement
Periodc. 1963–1973
LocationUnited States (with solidarity movements worldwide)
Key eventsMarch on the Pentagon (1967); Moratorium to End the War (1969); protests after the Kent State shootings (1970)

Opposition to United States involvement in the Vietnam War began in the early 1960s and grew into one of the largest protest movements in American history. Activists — including students, civil rights campaigners, clergy, veterans and mainstream politicians — argued variously that the war was immoral, unwinnable, an improper intervention in a civil war, or a diversion from domestic needs. The movement helped transform US politics and contributed to the decisions to de-escalate and eventually end American participation in the war.

Early opposition

Small protests by pacifist and left-wing groups in the early 1960s broadened after the Gulf of Tonkin incident and the start of sustained bombing in 1965. "Teach-ins" spread across university campuses in 1965, and Students for a Democratic Society led a march of some 25,000 people on Washington that April. As the draft expanded and casualties rose, opposition widened beyond the campus: Martin Luther King Jr. publicly denounced the war in 1967, and boxer Muhammad Ali's refusal of induction became a landmark of draft resistance.

Mass movement, 1967–1970

In October 1967 about 100,000 people rallied at the Lincoln Memorial and marched on the Pentagon. The Tet Offensive of early 1968 undermined official claims of progress and shifted majority public opinion against the war. Protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago were met with violence that shocked television audiences. The Moratorium of 15 October 1969 mobilised millions nationwide, followed by a huge demonstration in Washington in November. President Nixon's announcement of the invasion of Cambodia in April 1970 triggered campus strikes across the country; at Kent State University, Ohio National Guardsmen killed four student protesters on 4 May 1970.

Later years and legacy

Though street protest declined as the draft wound down and US troops were withdrawn, "Vietnamisation" did not end the movement's political influence: Congress cut funding for combat operations, passed the Case–Church Amendment (1973) and the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto. The movement left a lasting legacy in debates about military intervention, the all-volunteer force and the relationship between protest and policy.
