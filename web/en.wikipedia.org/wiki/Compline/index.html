Compline

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Night prayer sung in a monastic choir

Compline (pronounced KOM-plin), also known as night prayer, is the final prayer service of the day in the Christian tradition of canonical hours. The English word derives from the Latin completorium, "that which completes", because compline completes the liturgical day. The office is prayed after evening prayer (vespers) and before retiring for the night, typically in the quiet and darkness of the evening, and it has a character of peaceful trust and preparation for sleep — traditionally understood also as a preparation for death.

History

Devotions before sleep existed in early Christian practice, and a formal night office took shape in the monastic rules of the fourth to sixth centuries. The Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 530) gives compline a fixed place as the last of the seven daytime and night offices, prescribing psalms, a hymn, a reading, and the Gospel canticle Nunc Dimittis ("Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace", Luke 2:29–32), which remains a hallmark of the office. The Roman Rite's compline came to include the antiphon to the Virgin Mary appropriate to the season, such as the Salve Regina.

Structure

Although the details vary among Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran usages, compline generally includes:

  • An opening versicle and a brief examination of conscience or confession
  • One or more psalms (Psalms 4, 91, and 134 are traditional choices)
  • A hymn, such as Te lucis ante terminum ("Before the ending of the day")
  • A short reading from Scripture
  • A responsory, traditionally In manus tuas ("Into thy hands, O Lord, I commend my spirit")
  • The Nunc Dimittis with its antiphon
  • A concluding prayer and blessing, often followed by a Marian antiphon

In other traditions

In Byzantine (Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic) usage, the corresponding service is called apodeipnon, celebrated in Great and Small forms. In the Church of England, the office of Compline is provided in traditional language in Common Worship and in the Book of Common Prayer tradition through the service of Night Prayer; its quiet, candlelit character has made it popular in parishes, cathedrals, and university chaplaincies. Many religious communities broadcast or stream sung compline, giving the ancient office a wide modern audience.
