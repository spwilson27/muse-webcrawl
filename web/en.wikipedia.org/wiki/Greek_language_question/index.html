The Greek language question (Greek: το γλωσσικό ζήτημα, to glossikó zítima) was a controversy over whether the official language of the Greek state should be Katharevousa, an archaizing, "purified" form of Greek devised in the early 19th century, or Demotic (Δημοτική), the natural vernacular spoken by ordinary Greeks. The dispute dominated Greek cultural and political life for more than 150 years and periodically erupted into violence.

History

At Greek independence in the 1820s, reformers such as Adamantios Korais sought a written language cleansed of foreign (especially Turkish) elements, producing Katharevousa, which became the language of the state, education, law and the press — even though nobody spoke it natively. Demoticists, galvanised by Ioannis Psycharis's 1888 manifesto My Journey, demanded literature and schooling in the people's language. The conflict turned deadly in the Athens "Gospel riots" of 1901, when protests against a Demotic translation of the Gospels left several dead, and again over a Demotic translation of Aeschylus in 1903.

Resolution

Through the 20th century the two varieties coexisted in a state of diglossia: the long-running contest became entangled with broader political divisions, and the military junta of 1967–74 imposed Katharevousa aggressively. After the junta's fall, the question was finally settled by Law 309 of 1976, which made Demotic the language of administration and education; in 1982 the monotonic (single-accent) system of spelling was adopted. The episode is a classic case study of language politics and national identity.
