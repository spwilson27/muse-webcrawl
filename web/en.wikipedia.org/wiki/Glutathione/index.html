Glutathione

Glutathione
AbbreviationGSH
CompositionTripeptide of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine
Chemical formulaC10H17N3O6S
Molar mass307.32 g/mol
FunctionMajor intracellular antioxidant

Glutathione (GSH) is an antioxidant found in plants, animals, fungi, and some bacteria. It is a tripeptide composed of the amino acids glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, linked by an unusual gamma peptide bond between glutamate and cysteine. Glutathione is the most abundant low-molecular-weight thiol in cells and is capable of preventing damage to important cellular components caused by reactive oxygen species such as free radicals, peroxides, and heavy metals.

Biosynthesis

Glutathione is synthesized in two ATP-dependent steps in the cytosol: first, gamma-glutamylcysteine is formed from glutamate and cysteine by the enzyme glutamate–cysteine ligase (the rate-limiting step, subject to feedback inhibition by glutathione); then glycine is added by glutathione synthetase. Because it is made within cells, glutathione does not need to be obtained from the diet, though its precursor cysteine can be limiting.

Functions

Reduced glutathione (GSH) neutralizes reactive oxygen species and is oxidized to glutathione disulfide (GSSG); the enzyme glutathione reductase recycles GSSG back to GSH using NADPH. Glutathione also participates in detoxification through glutathione S-transferases, which conjugate it to xenobiotics for excretion; in the metabolism of formaldehyde and glyoxal; in maintaining other antioxidants such as vitamins C and E in active forms; and in immune function and cellular signaling. The drug N-acetylcysteine, which supplies cysteine, is used to restore glutathione in acetaminophen (paracetamol) poisoning, in which hepatic glutathione depletion causes liver damage.

Clinical relevance

Altered glutathione levels are associated with aging and a range of diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, though oral glutathione supplementation has poor bioavailability and evidence for most supplement claims is limited.
