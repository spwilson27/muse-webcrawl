Adverse childhood experiences

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are potentially traumatic events that occur in childhood (0–17 years), including experiencing or witnessing violence, abuse or neglect; having a family member attempt or die by suicide; and growing up in a household with substance use problems, mental health problems, or instability due to parental separation or incarceration. ACEs are linked to chronic health problems, mental illness, and substance use in adulthood, and are considered a major public health concern.

The ACE study

The term originates in the Adverse Childhood Experiences Study conducted by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Kaiser Permanente in the 1990s, led by Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda. Over 17,000 adult members of a health maintenance organization completed surveys about ten categories of childhood adversity and their current health status. The study found a strong graded ("dose–response") relationship between the number of ACE categories experienced and adult risk behaviours and diseases, including heart disease, cancer, chronic lung disease, depression and alcoholism. About two-thirds of respondents reported at least one ACE, and more than one in five reported three or more.

Mechanisms

Prolonged activation of the stress-response system in childhood — sometimes called "toxic stress" — can disrupt brain development and alter immune, hormonal and even epigenetic functioning, increasing vulnerability to disease decades later. ACEs also increase the likelihood of adopting coping behaviours such as smoking and heavy drinking that themselves damage health.

Prevention and response

Public health strategies include strengthening economic supports to families, promoting social norms that protect against violence, ensuring quality childcare and early education, and providing trauma-informed care in schools, healthcare and justice systems. Researchers caution that an ACE score is a population-level tool rather than a deterministic prediction for any individual, and that protective factors and resilience substantially modify outcomes.
