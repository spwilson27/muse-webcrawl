Auscultation

Auscultation (from the Latin auscultare, "to listen") is the act of listening to sounds within the body, usually with a stethoscope, as part of a physical examination. It is most often applied to the heart, lungs, abdomen (bowel sounds) and blood vessels. The technique was systematised by the French physician René Laennec, who invented the stethoscope in 1816 and published the foundational treatise De l'auscultation médiate in 1819.

History

Physicians had long listened with the ear applied directly to the chest ("immediate auscultation"); Hippocratic writings describe shaking a patient to hear splashing sounds. Laennec, embarrassed to place his ear on a young female patient's chest in 1816, rolled paper into a tube and discovered that it transmitted heart sounds clearly — the origin of the stethoscope and of "mediate" auscultation. His 1819 treatise correlated sounds with diseases verified at autopsy, founding modern chest medicine.

What is assessed

Heart sounds

Normal heart sounds (the "lub-dub" of valve closure, S1 and S2) are assessed for added sounds — murmurs indicating turbulent flow from valve stenosis or regurgitation, gallop rhythms, and pericardial rubs.

Lung sounds

Normal vesicular breath sounds are compared across the chest. Abnormal sounds include crackles (rales) associated with fluid in the airspaces, wheezes from narrowed airways, rhonchi, pleural friction rubs, and areas of absent breath sounds in pneumothorax or effusion.

Other uses

Auscultation is used to hear bowel sounds, to detect bruits over narrowed arteries, to measure blood pressure with a sphygmomanometer (Korotkoff sounds), and in midwifery to monitor the fetal heart.

Modern practice

Auscultation remains a core clinical skill, now supplemented by electronic and digital stethoscopes that amplify, record and analyse sounds, and by point-of-care ultrasound, which some clinicians argue is displacing parts of the traditional examination.
