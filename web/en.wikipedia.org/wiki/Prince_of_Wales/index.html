Prince of Wales

The Prince of Wales's feathers, badge of the Prince of Wales.

The Prince of Wales (Welsh: Tywysog Cymru) is a title traditionally granted to the heir apparent to the English, and later British, throne. It originated with the native Welsh princes of Gwynedd who claimed overlordship of Wales and was adopted by the English crown after the conquest of Wales. The current holder is William, Prince of Wales, created on 9 September 2022 after the accession of his father, Charles III.

First native holderLlywelyn the Great (de facto); Owain Glyndŵr (last native, proclaimed 1400)
First English heirEdward of Caernarfon (later Edward II), 1301
Current holderWilliam, Prince of Wales (since 2022)
Longest-servingCharles III (1958–2022, 64 years)
Associated titlesDuke of Cornwall, Duke of Rothesay, Earl of Chester

History

Welsh rulers such as Llywelyn ap Iorwerth and Llywelyn ap Gruffudd used the title Prince of Wales in the 13th century; Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised by the English crown in the Treaty of Montgomery (1267). After Edward I's conquest (1282–83) and Llywelyn's death, Edward granted the title to his own son at the Parliament of Lincoln in 1301 — the origin of the tradition (the story that Edward promised the Welsh a prince "born in Wales who spoke no English", presenting his infant son born at Caernarfon, is a later legend).

Status

The title is not automatic: it is created by the sovereign for the heir apparent, usually with an investiture. Charles's investiture took place at Caernarfon Castle in 1969; William received no formal investiture ceremony. Unlike the Dukedom of Cornwall, the principality carries no territorial income; the Prince's income derives from the Duchy of Cornwall.

Badge and motto

The heraldic badge is three white ostrich feathers issuing from a gold coronet, with the motto Ich dien ("I serve") on a ribbon — traditionally said to have been adopted by Edward the Black Prince from John of Bohemia, killed at Crécy in 1346.
