Grand Prix motor racing

Grand Prix racing in the 1930s, dominated by the German Silver Arrows and Italian teams.

Grand Prix motor racing, formally known as Formula One Grand Prix racing in its modern form, has its roots in organised automobile racing that began in France in 1894. It evolved over time from primitive races on public roads to purpose-built circuits. A "Grand Prix" (French for "grand prize") was originally the premier race of a motoring weekend.

Origins

The first race to be called a Grand Prix was held at Le Mans in 1906, organised by the Automobile Club de France. In the 1920s and 1930s, national Grands Prix proliferated across Europe, and governing bodies introduced formulae limiting engine size and weight. The late 1930s were dominated by the state-backed German Mercedes-Benz and Auto Union "Silver Arrows", challenged by Alfa Romeo and Maserati.

Formula One

After the Second World War, the FIA established the Formula One World Championship in 1950, uniting selected Grands Prix into a points championship for drivers; a constructors' championship followed in 1958. The term Grand Prix is now synonymous with the rounds of that championship, held in countries around the world over a season of more than twenty races.

Other Grands Prix

The name is also used outside Formula One — in MotoGP motorcycle racing, IndyCar, touring cars, and endurance racing (for example the Grand Prix at Le Mans historically), and by analogy in other sports such as tennis, snooker and athletics.
