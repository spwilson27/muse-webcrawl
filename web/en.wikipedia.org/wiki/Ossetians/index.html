Ossetians

Ossetians
Ossetian: Ирӕттӕ (Irættæ)
Total population: c. 700,000
Regions: North Ossetia–Alania (Russia), South Ossetia, Georgia, Turkey, diaspora
Languages: Ossetian, Russian, Georgian
Religion: Predominantly Eastern Orthodox Christianity; minorities of Islam and the traditional Uatsdin faith

The Ossetians (Ossetian: Ирӕттӕ, Irættæ) are an Iranian ethnic group of the Caucasus Mountains, indigenous to the region known as Ossetia. They speak the Ossetian language, an Eastern Iranian language. Ossetians are generally considered to be the descendants of the Alans, a Sarmatian tribe, and — more distantly — of the Scythians of the Eurasian steppe.

History

The Alans formed a powerful tribal confederation north of the Caucasus in the early centuries CE. During the Mongol invasions of the 13th and 14th centuries, the Alans were driven from the steppe into the mountain valleys of the central Caucasus, where their descendants became the Ossetians. The Jász of Hungary are a related group descended from Alans who moved westward.

In the 18th and 19th centuries Ossetia came under Russian rule. Under the Soviet Union, Ossetian territory was divided: North Ossetia became an autonomous republic within the Russian SFSR, while South Ossetia became an autonomous oblast within the Georgian SSR. After the collapse of the USSR, the status of South Ossetia became the subject of conflict between Georgia and Russia, most sharply in the Russo-Georgian War of August 2008, after which Russia recognised South Ossetia as an independent state, a recognition shared by only a handful of countries.

Culture

Ossetian culture preserves the Nart epic tradition, heroic tales of the mythical Narts that the Ossetians share with their Circassian, Abkhaz and other North Caucasian neighbours. Traditional society was organised around clans and the customary law known as æghdau. Ossetian beer (bægæny) and the ritual baking of three pies, symbolising God, the sun and the earth, remain important in religious festivals.

Religion

Most Ossetians are Orthodox Christians, Christianised in stages between the early medieval period and the 18th–19th centuries. A substantial minority, mainly among the Digor in the west, practise Sunni Islam, adopted from the 17th century onward under Kabardian influence. The traditional faith, Uatsdin, blending pre-Christian beliefs with Orthodox elements, has experienced a revival since the late Soviet period.
