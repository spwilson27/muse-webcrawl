Cipher Bureau (Poland)

The Cipher Bureau (Polish: Biuro Szyfrów) was the Polish interwar agency charged with cryptography and signals intelligence. Formed in 1919, in the aftermath of the Polish–Soviet War — in which Polish codebreakers such as Jan Kowalewski had already achieved notable successes — it is best known for its pioneering work against the German Enigma machine: the Bureau was the first to break Enigma, in 1932, and shared its methods with Britain and France on the eve of the Second World War, laying the foundations of Allied Ultra intelligence.

Breaking Enigma

The Bureau's German section (BS-4), led by Maksymilian Ciężki, recruited young mathematicians from the University of Poznań — an unusual step, since other countries relied on linguists. In late 1932 the mathematician Marian Rejewski, using permutation theory and documents supplied by French intelligence from the spy Hans-Thilo Schmidt, reconstructed the wiring of the military Enigma and read German messages. Working with Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski, he developed a series of cryptanalytic machines and techniques: the cyclometer, the bomba kryptologiczna (cryptologic bomb) of 1938, and Zygalski's perforated sheets.

The 1939 handover

As German operating procedures changed and war approached, the Bureau's leadership decided to share its seven years of work. At a conference in the Kabaty Woods near Pyry, outside Warsaw, on 25–26 July 1939, the Poles presented their British and French counterparts — including Dilly Knox and Gustave Bertrand — with their methods and a replica Enigma machine for each ally. Five weeks later Germany invaded Poland. The gift transformed Allied codebreaking: at Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman built on the Polish foundations to develop the British bombe and large-scale Enigma decryption.

Wartime and legacy

After Poland's defeat, many Bureau personnel escaped to France, where they worked at PC Bruno and later Cadix in Vichy France, breaking German traffic, before scattering as the war turned. Rejewski and Zygalski survived the war (Różycki died in 1942 when his ship sank in the Mediterranean) but the Polish contribution was kept secret for decades; public recognition came only from the 1970s onward, as the Ultra secret was revealed. Monuments in Poznań and Warsaw now honour the three mathematicians.
