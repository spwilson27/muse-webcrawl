Puffing Billy is the world's oldest surviving steam locomotive. It was built in 1813–1814 by William Hedley, Jonathan Forster and Timothy Hackworth for Christopher Blackett, owner of Wylam Colliery near Newcastle upon Tyne, to haul coal wagons on the Wylam waggonway to staithes on the River Tyne at Lemington.

Background

Wylam's owner wanted to replace horses on the colliery waggonway. Hedley, the colliery's viewer (manager), first had to establish that a smooth-wheeled locomotive could haul a useful load on smooth rails by adhesion alone — many contemporaries assumed rack-and-pinion or toothed wheels were necessary. In 1813 Hedley demonstrated the principle with a test carriage worked by men at the cranks. The result persuaded Blackett to commission steam locomotives, of which Puffing Billy was the first successful example; a near-sister engine, Wylam Dilly, followed.

Design and career

The locomotive has a single flue boiler and a vertical cylinder arrangement driving the wheels through a system of beams and gearing, giving four coupled wheels. As originally built it ran on five pairs of wheels after it proved too heavy for the cast-iron plate rails, spreading its roughly 9-ton weight; when stronger edge rails were laid, it was rebuilt with four wheels. It worked at Wylam for nearly fifty years, an exceptionally long career for so early a machine, and was repeatedly rebuilt — its present appearance owes much to an 1830s rebuilding.

Preservation and legacy

Retired around 1862, Puffing Billy was presented to the Patent Office Museum in London (later part of the Science Museum), where it remains on display. Its success at Wylam directly influenced George Stephenson, who was born at Wylam and built his first locomotive, Blücher (1814), for Killingworth colliery. The name "Puffing Billy" became a generic Australian and British term of endearment for steam engines; the Puffing Billy Railway in Victoria, Australia, is named after it.
