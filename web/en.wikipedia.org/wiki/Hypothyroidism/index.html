Hypothyroidism is a disorder of the endocrine system in which the thyroid gland does not produce enough thyroid hormone. It can cause a number of symptoms, such as poor ability to tolerate cold, tiredness, constipation, a slow heart rate, depression, and weight gain. Occasionally there may be swelling of the front of the neck due to a goitre. It is the opposite of hyperthyroidism, in which the gland is overactive.

Causes

Worldwide, the most common cause of hypothyroidism is iodine deficiency. In iodine-sufficient countries the most common cause is the autoimmune condition Hashimoto's thyroiditis, in which the immune system gradually destroys the thyroid. Other causes include surgical removal of the thyroid, radioiodine treatment, certain medicines such as lithium and amiodarone, pituitary or hypothalamic disease (central hypothyroidism), and congenital hypothyroidism in newborns.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop slowly and are often non-specific: fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, muscle aches, a hoarse voice, slowed thinking, and depression. Signs include bradycardia, delayed relaxation of tendon reflexes, puffy face, and non-pitting oedema (myxoedema). Severe, prolonged untreated hypothyroidism can lead to myxoedema coma, a rare but life-threatening state with hypothermia and reduced consciousness.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by blood tests. In primary hypothyroidism, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is raised while free thyroxine (T4) is low. Subclinical hypothyroidism denotes a raised TSH with a normal T4 and few or no symptoms; it is common and its treatment is more debated. Antibodies against thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) support a diagnosis of Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Because untreated hypothyroidism in pregnancy and infancy impairs development, newborn screening and careful management in pregnancy are routine in many countries.

Treatment

Treatment is with levothyroxine, a synthetic form of T4, usually taken once daily for life. The dose is titrated against TSH measurements, and most patients' symptoms resolve over weeks to months. Desiccated thyroid extract and combination T4/T3 therapy exist but are not recommended as standard first-line treatment by major guidelines.

Epidemiology

Overt hypothyroidism affects roughly 0.3–1% of the population in iodine-sufficient regions, and subclinical disease is considerably more common, especially in older women. Women are affected several times more often than men.
