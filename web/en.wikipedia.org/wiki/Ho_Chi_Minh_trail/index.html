Ho Chi Minh trail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: North Vietnamese soldiers and bicycles carrying supplies along a jungle section of the trail]
North Vietnamese soldiers and bicycles carrying supplies along a jungle section of the trail

The Ho Chi Minh Trail (Đường mòn Hồ Chí Minh) was a logistical network of roads, paths and waterways running from North Vietnam through eastern Laos and Cambodia into South Vietnam, used by the People's Army of Vietnam and the Viet Cong to infiltrate personnel and supplies during the Vietnam War. Despite the most intensive interdiction bombing campaign in history, the trail was never closed, and it was decisive in sustaining the war in the South.

Construction and organisation

Authorised in May 1959 (Group 559, named for the month and year), the trail began as porters' paths through the Annamite Range. Over sixteen years it grew into some 20,000 km of roads — much of it engineered, camouflaged and maintained by tens of thousands of troops and youth volunteers (disproportionately young women) — with truck parks, fuel pipelines, hospitals and anti-aircraft defences, supplied by the USSR and China.

American interdiction

The US dropped more tonnage on Laos (over 2 million tons) than on all targets in the Second World War, and seeded the trail with electronic sensors (Operation Igloo White) and defoliants; commando teams (MACV-SOG) raided it on the ground. Bombing killed and maimed — and Laos remains the most bombed country per capita in history, its unexploded ordnance still killing today — but throughput rose year on year, culminating in the conventional offensives of 1972 and 1975 supplied by truck down a paved, multi-lane highway in all but name.

Legacy

The trail is Vietnam's supreme symbol of endurance — commemorated by museums, the Trường Sơn cemetery and a modern expressway bearing its name — and for the US, the classic case of airpower's limits against dispersed, repairable logistics.
