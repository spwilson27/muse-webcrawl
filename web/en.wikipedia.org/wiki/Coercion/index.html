Coercion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coercion is the practice of compelling a person or party to act involuntarily, by use of threats, intimidation, or force. It involves a set of forceful actions that violate the free will of an individual in order to induce a desired response — for example, a bully demanding lunch money, or a state enforcing its laws.

Forms

Coercion may be physical (direct violence or restraint) or psychological (threats, blackmail, extortion, and other pressure that makes compliance the least bad option). Political scientists distinguish the coercive apparatus of the state — police and military power, which Max Weber argued the state claims a legitimate monopoly over — from private coercion such as organised-crime intimidation.

In law and philosophy

In contract law, agreement obtained under duress or undue influence may be voidable; in criminal law, coercion may ground offences such as extortion and, in some circumstances, partially excuse offences committed by the coerced person. Philosophers debate the boundary between coercion and legitimate pressure or persuasion: Robert Nozick's influential analysis asked whether a proposal counts as a threat depends on the baseline against which the recipient's situation is measured.
