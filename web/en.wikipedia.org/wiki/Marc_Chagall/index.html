Marc Chagall (born Moïche Segal; 6 July 1887 – 28 March 1985) was a Russian-born French artist. An early modernist, he worked in painting, drawing, printmaking, stained glass, ceramics, and stage design. His dreamlike compositions drew on Jewish folklore, memories of his childhood in Vitebsk, and the Hasidic culture of Eastern Europe, and he is regarded as one of the most successful artists of the 20th century.

Early life

Chagall was born in Liozna, near Vitebsk, in the Russian Empire (present-day Belarus), the eldest of nine children in a Lithuanian Jewish family. He studied in St Petersburg before moving to Paris in 1910, where he associated with Apollinaire, Delaunay, and the avant-garde of the École de Paris. In Paris he painted some of his most celebrated early works, including I and the Village (1911).

War years and exile

Caught in Russia by the First World War, Chagall married Bella Rosenfeld in 1915; she became the recurring muse of his art. After the Revolution he founded an art academy in Vitebsk but clashed with Kazimir Malevich and left Russia in 1922. In 1941, fleeing occupied France, he escaped to the United States with the help of the Emergency Rescue Committee. Bella died in 1944; Chagall returned to France in 1948.

Later work

In his later decades Chagall produced major public commissions: the ceiling of the Paris Opéra (1964), stained-glass windows for Metz Cathedral, Reims Cathedral, the Hadassah Medical Center in Jerusalem, and the United Nations (1964), as well as mosaics and tapestries. The Musée Marc Chagall in Nice opened in 1973. He died at Saint-Paul-de-Vence in 1985.
