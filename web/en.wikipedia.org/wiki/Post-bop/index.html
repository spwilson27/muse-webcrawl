Post-bop

Stylistic origins: Bebop, hard bop, modal jazz, free jazz
Era: early 1960s onwards
Key artists: Miles Davis, John Coltrane, Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock

Post-bop is a genre of small-combo jazz that evolved in the early 1960s, blending elements of bebop, hard bop, modal jazz and free jazz without belonging wholly to any of them. The term describes music that continued to develop the bebop tradition while absorbing the innovations of the 1960s avant-garde.

Characteristics

Post-bop typically features freer harmonic structures, modal vamps, unusual time signatures and abstract melodies compared with hard bop, while retaining compositional structure and swing rather than moving fully into free improvisation. Rhythm sections interact more democratically with soloists, and compositions by band members replace standards as the main repertoire.

Key recordings

Central figures include Miles Davis and his "Second Great Quintet" (with Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter and Tony Williams), whose albums E.S.P. (1965), Miles Smiles (1967) and Nefertiti (1967) define the style. Other landmark recordings include John Coltrane's A Love Supreme (1965), Shorter's Speak No Evil (1966), Hancock's Maiden Voyage (1965), Charles Mingus's The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady (1963) and works by Eric Dolphy and Andrew Hill, such as Hill's Point of Departure (1964).
