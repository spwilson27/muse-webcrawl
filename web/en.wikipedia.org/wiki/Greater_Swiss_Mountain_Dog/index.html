The Greater Swiss Mountain Dog (German: Grosser Schweizer Sennenhund) is a large breed of dog developed in the Swiss Alps. It is the largest of the four Swiss mountain dog breeds, which also include the Bernese Mountain Dog, the Appenzeller Sennenhund and the Entlebucher Mountain Dog. The breed is a draught and droving dog, historically used by farmers and merchants for pulling carts, herding cattle and guarding property.

History

The breed descends from mastiff-type dogs brought to the Alpine region in antiquity, from which the Swiss developed several distinct regional working types. By the late nineteenth century the large short-coated tricolour type had become rare, and the breed was at one point considered extinct. It was revived through the efforts of Professor Albert Heim, who promoted it at dog shows beginning in 1908, and it was recognised by the Swiss Kennel Club in 1909. Two further world wars disrupted breeding, but the breed slowly spread abroad; the first specimens reached the United States in 1968.

Characteristics

The Greater Swiss Mountain Dog is a strong, muscular and heavily boned dog, slightly longer than it is tall. The double coat is short and dense, with a distinctive tricolour pattern: a black main colour with rich rust markings on the cheeks, eyebrows, legs and chest, and white markings on the face, chest, paws and tail tip. The head is large and flat-skulled, with a gentle expression and triangular drop ears.

Temperament

The breed is known for being confident, alert and devoted to its family, with a natural watchdog instinct and a willingness to work. Despite its size it is generally good-natured and stable, though it requires early socialisation and consistent training. Like many large working breeds, it matures slowly, both physically and mentally.

Health

Greater Swiss Mountain Dogs are generally healthy but, as a large deep-chested breed, are predisposed to gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat). Other reported concerns include hip and elbow dysplasia, osteochondrosis of the shoulder, entropion and other eyelid disorders, and splenic torsion. Typical life expectancy is about eight to eleven years.
