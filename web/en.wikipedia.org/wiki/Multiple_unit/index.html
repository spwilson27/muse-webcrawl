Multiple unit

A multiple unit (MU) is a self-propelled train composed of one or more carriages joined together, in which the traction motors are distributed along the train rather than concentrated in a separate locomotive. Multiple units can be coupled together and controlled by a single driver from the leading cab — the feature, developed by Frank J. Sprague for electric traction in the 1890s, from which the name derives.

Types

Electric multiple units (EMUs) draw power from an external supply — third rail or overhead catenary — and dominate suburban, metro, and increasingly intercity services worldwide, including Japan's Shinkansen (technically an EMU). Diesel multiple units (DMUs) carry diesel engines under the floor or in power cars and are common on non-electrified regional routes. Variants include diesel–electric multiple units (DEMUs), battery electric multiple units (BEMUs), and electro-diesel units able to run on both external power and onboard engines.

Advantages and disadvantages

Distributing traction gives multiple units rapid acceleration, good adhesion, and no need to run a locomotive around at termini, making them ideal for frequent-stop services. They are flexible — trains can be lengthened or shortened by adding or removing cars — and regenerative braking can be spread across many axles. Disadvantages include higher maintenance costs, since many cars carry complex equipment; more noise and vibration for passengers in powered cars; and less flexibility than locomotive-hauled stock if a single car fails.

History

Sprague's multiple-unit train control was first applied in Chicago in 1897 and quickly became standard for subways and elevated railways. Steam railcars and early petrol and diesel railcars preceded the true DMU, which spread widely after the Second World War; British Rail, Deutsche Bundesbahn, and Japanese National Railways all built large fleets. Today nearly all new passenger trains for urban and regional service, and most high-speed trains, are multiple units.
