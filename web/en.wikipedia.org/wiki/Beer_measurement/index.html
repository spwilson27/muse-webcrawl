Beer measurement

When drinking beer, there are many factors to be considered, including bitterness, the variety of flavours present, and their intensity. Brewers quantify these properties using standardised beer measurements: colour, gravity (extract content), bitterness in International Bitterness Units (IBU), and alcohol content. This article summarises the principal scales used worldwide.

Colour

Beer colour is measured on the Standard Reference Method (SRM) scale in the United States and the European Brewery Convention (EBC) scale in Europe; EBC values are roughly twice SRM values. The older Lovibond scale is approximately equivalent to SRM. Pale lagers measure around 2–4 SRM, amber ales 10–17, and stouts 30–40 or more.

Gravity and strength

The sugar content of wort is expressed as specific gravity, or on the Plato (°P) or Balling scales used in central Europe. The difference between original gravity (before fermentation) and final gravity gives alcohol by volume (ABV), the standard measure of beer strength, typically 4–6% for regular beer. Scotland historically taxed beer by "shilling" categories derived from price per hogshead.

Bitterness

International Bitterness Units measure the concentration of iso-alpha acids from hops: one IBU is roughly one milligram of iso-alpha acid per litre. Mass-market lagers measure 8–20 IBU, pale ales 30–50, and American IPAs 40–100 or more, though perceived bitterness also depends on malt sweetness and other factors.

Mouthfeel and carbonation

Carbonation is expressed in volumes of CO₂, typically 2.2–2.7 volumes for most styles, lower for British cask ale and higher for German wheat beers. Serving temperature, glassware and foam (head) retention are also assessed by tasters.
