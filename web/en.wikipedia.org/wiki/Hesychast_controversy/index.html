Hesychast controversy

The Hesychast controversy was a theological dispute in the Byzantine Empire during the 14th century concerning the doctrine of Hesychasm, the contemplative prayer tradition practised especially by the monks of Mount Athos. The controversy centred on whether the light experienced by hesychast monks in prayer — identified with the uncreated light of the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor — was truly divine.

Background

Hesychasm (from the Greek hesychia, "stillness" or "quiet") involved the disciplined repetition of the Jesus Prayer, controlled breathing and a bowed posture, aiming at union with God and the vision of the divine light. Around 1337 the practice was attacked by Barlaam of Seminara, a Calabrian scholar and monk, who ridiculed the monks' claims and denounced the supposed vision of uncreated light as heretical.

Gregory Palamas and the defence

The Athonite monk Gregory Palamas defended the hesychasts, most systematically in his Triads. Palamas argued for a distinction between the essence of God, which is unknowable and imparticipable, and the energies of God, which are uncreated yet truly God and can be experienced by humans. The light of Tabor, he held, is one of these uncreated energies, not a created symbol or a merely intellectual radiance.

Resolution

A series of councils at Constantinople — in 1341, 1347 and 1351 — progressively vindicated Palamas and condemned his opponents, including Barlaam and later Gregory Akindynos and Nikephoros Gregoras. The council of 1351, sometimes called the Fifth Council of Constantinople in the Orthodox tradition, solemnly affirmed the essence–energies distinction. Palamas was canonised in 1368, and his teaching became a defining doctrine of Eastern Orthodox theology.

Legacy

The controversy coincided with the Byzantine civil wars of the 1340s and became entangled with the politics of the regency for John V Palaiologos and the usurpation of John VI Kantakouzenos, who supported the Palamites. In modern times the essence–energies distinction remains a point of divergence between Orthodox and Roman Catholic theology.
