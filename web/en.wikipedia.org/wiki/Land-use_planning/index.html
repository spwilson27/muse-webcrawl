Land-use planning is the process of regulating and managing the use of land in an effort to promote more desirable social and environmental outcomes as well as more efficient use of resources. It is a branch of urban and regional planning concerned with how land is allocated among competing uses — residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, recreational, and conservation — and with the rules governing development.

Methods

The principal instruments of land-use planning include the comprehensive (or master) plan, which sets long-term policy goals; zoning ordinances, which divide territory into districts and specify permitted uses, densities, and building forms; subdivision regulations controlling how land is parcelled; and development review procedures such as permits, environmental impact assessments, and public hearings. Modern practice relies heavily on geographic information systems (GIS) for mapping and analysis.

History

While ancient cities regulated building lines and nuisances, modern land-use planning arose in response to industrialization and urban crowding in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Landmark steps include Britain's Housing and Town Planning Act 1909 and Town and Country Planning Act 1947, and New York City's 1916 Zoning Resolution, the first comprehensive zoning ordinance in the United States. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld zoning as a valid exercise of police power in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty (1926).

Approaches and criticism

Planning approaches range from Euclidean zoning (strict separation of single uses) to form-based codes, mixed-use and transit-oriented development, smart growth, and urban growth boundaries such as Oregon's statewide system. Critics argue that conventional zoning contributes to urban sprawl, housing unaffordability, racial and economic segregation, and car dependence; others defend planning as essential for protecting public health, infrastructure efficiency, farmland, and ecosystems. Contemporary practice increasingly integrates climate adaptation, hazard mitigation, and environmental justice.
