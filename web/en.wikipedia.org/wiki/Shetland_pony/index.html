Shetland pony

Country of originShetland Islands, Scotland
HeightUp to 107 cm (42 in) for registered ponies in the UK standard
TraitsSmall, hardy, heavy-boned pony with a thick double coat, long mane and tail

The Shetland pony is a Scottish breed of pony originating in the Shetland Islands in the north of Scotland. It may stand up to 107 cm (42 in) at the withers. It has a heavy coat and short legs, is strong for its size, and is used for riding, driving, and pack purposes. It is one of the smallest and hardiest pony breeds, adapted over centuries to the harsh island climate and sparse grazing, and is now popular worldwide as a child's mount, a driving pony, and a companion animal.

History

Ponies have lived on Shetland since the Bronze Age; small horses from Scandinavia and possibly tundra stock contributed to the breed, which developed in isolation and was shaped by crofters who used ponies for carrying peat and seaweed, ploughing, and transport. From the mid-19th century thousands of Shetlands were exported to mainland Britain as pit ponies in coal mines, where their size and strength suited narrow seams. The Shetland Pony Stud-Book Society was founded in 1890 to maintain breed purity. Exports to the United States from 1885 led to the development of the taller, finer American Shetland Pony.

Characteristics

The traditional Shetland is small, strong and short-legged, with a broad forehead, small ears, a dense double winter coat, and abundant mane and tail that shed rain. All colours occur except spotted. The breed is noted for intelligence, sometimes stubborn temperament, longevity, and remarkable strength relative to size — it is among the strongest equines weight-for-weight. Registered ponies are not measured in hands in the British stud book but by height in centimetres.

Uses

Today Shetlands serve chiefly as children's riding ponies, driving ponies (including four-in-hand teams and scurry driving), therapy and guide animals, and participants in "Shetland Grand National" charity races at shows in Britain.
