War crimes of the Wehrmacht

War crimes of the Wehrmacht
Period: 1939–1945
Theatres: Eastern Front, Balkans, Italy, Western Europe
Perpetrators: Members of the German armed forces (Heer, Kriegsmarine, Luftwaffe), often in cooperation with the SS and police
Key criminal orders: Commissar Order, Barbarossa decree, Commando Order, Severity Order

War crimes of the Wehrmacht were a series of criminal acts committed by the regular armed forces of Nazi Germany during the Second World War. While the largest share of Nazi crimes — including the Holocaust — were carried out by the SS and police formations, the Wehrmacht planned and participated in numerous war crimes, particularly during the war against the Soviet Union and in occupied territories in the Balkans and Italy.

Background

The Wehrmacht was created in 1935 from the Reichswehr and swore a personal oath of loyalty to Adolf Hitler. Senior commanders were integrated into the regime's plans for a war of annihilation (Vernichtungskrieg) in Eastern Europe. Before the invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, the high command issued a series of orders that removed the legal protections of Soviet prisoners of war and civilians.

Criminal orders

The Commissar Order (Kommissarbefehl) of June 1941 directed that captured Soviet political commissars be shot immediately. The Barbarossa decree exempted German soldiers from prosecution for crimes against Soviet civilians. The Commando Order of October 1942 required that Allied commandos be executed even if captured in uniform. Generals such as Walter von Reichenau issued their own "Severity Order" calling on troops to act ruthlessly against Jews and partisans.

War in the East

The Wehrmacht cooperated closely with the Einsatzgruppen in the mass murder of Jews; army units provided logistics, cordons and intelligence during massacres such as Babi Yar. Of roughly 5.7 million Soviet prisoners of war, about 3.3 million died in German custody from starvation, exposure and shooting — a mortality rate unmatched among Western Allied prisoners. Anti-partisan warfare (Bandenbekämpfung) in Belarus and elsewhere killed hundreds of thousands of civilians, with "dead zones" created by burning villages and murdering their inhabitants. The siege of Leningrad deliberately starved the city's population.

Crimes in the Balkans

In occupied Serbia and Greece, the Wehrmacht applied reprisal ratios of 100 hostages shot for every German soldier killed, as in the Kragujevac massacre of October 1941, in which thousands of men and schoolboys were executed. Greek villages such as Kalavryta and Distomo were destroyed and their populations murdered.

Crimes in Italy and the West

After Italy's armistice in 1943, German forces disarmed Italian troops and massacred thousands of Italian prisoners, notably the Acqui Division on Cephalonia. In France, the Wehrmacht took part in reprisals including the Tulle and Oradour-sur-Glane massacres (the latter principally by the Waffen-SS). During the Ardennes offensive, Wehrmacht and SS units murdered American prisoners, most infamously at Malmedy.

Post-war trials and the "clean Wehrmacht" myth

The High Command Trial at Nuremberg convicted several senior officers, but the Cold War encouraged a myth of a "clean" Wehrmacht unsullied by Nazi crimes. Historians from the 1970s onward, and the controversial Wehrmacht exhibition of 1995, demonstrated the breadth of ordinary soldiers' involvement. Modern scholarship estimates the Wehrmacht's responsibility for the deaths of millions of Soviet POWs and civilians.
