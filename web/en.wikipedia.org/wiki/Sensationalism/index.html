Sensationalism

Sensationalism is a type of editorial tactic used in mass media in which events and topics in news stories are selected and phrased to excite the greatest number of readers and viewers, often at the expense of accuracy, context, or public importance. It typically involves exaggerated headlines, emotionally charged language, and prominent coverage of crime, scandal, sex, and celebrity.

History

Sensational reporting has roots in the penny press of the 1830s, which competed for mass readership with human-interest stories and crime news. It reached a famous peak in the 1890s with the circulation war between Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal — the era of "yellow journalism" — which is often blamed for inflaming public opinion before the Spanish–American War. Twentieth-century tabloids, and later 24-hour television news and online media competing for clicks, extended the practice.

Techniques and criticism

Sensationalist coverage commonly relies on alarming or misleading headlines, selective facts, moral panic framing, and disproportionate attention to rare but frightening events. Critics argue that it distorts public perception of risk, trivializes civic affairs, and creates incentives to manufacture controversy. Media scholars also note that the pursuit of audience reaction in digital media — sometimes called clickbait — is a modern form of sensationalism driven by advertising metrics.
