The Song of Roland (French: La Chanson de Roland) is an Old French epic poem and the oldest surviving major work of French literature. Composed in the late eleventh century, it is the most famous of the chansons de geste and is based on the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, during Charlemagne's campaign in Spain — though it transforms the historical ambush by Basques into a heroic stand against a vast Saracen army.

Plot

Charlemagne has campaigned in Spain for seven years; only Saragossa, held by King Marsile, resists. The traitor Ganelon, Roland's stepfather, betrays the Frankish rearguard — commanded by Roland and the twelve peers — to a Saracen ambush at Roncevaux. Refusing out of pride to blow his horn, the olifant, to summon help until too late, Roland fights magnificently and dies, bursting his temples as he finally sounds the horn and trying to break his sword Durendal so it will not fall into enemy hands. The Archbishop Turpin and Oliver, Roland's wise companion — "Roland is bold, and Oliver is wise" — also die. Charlemagne avenges them, defeats the Emir Baligant, and has Ganelon torn apart by horses at his trial in Aachen.

Significance

The poem, preserved in the Anglo-Norman Oxford manuscript (Digby 23) and ascribed at its end to one "Turoldus," exemplifies the oral-formulaic style later analysed by Milman Parry: its story circulated in oral tradition for three centuries before being written down. It shaped medieval ideals of chivalry, loyalty, and holy war in the age of the Crusades, and left its hero's marks across Europe — the Orlando of Italian romance, the Breach of Roland in the Pyrenees, and the statues of Roland in German cities.
