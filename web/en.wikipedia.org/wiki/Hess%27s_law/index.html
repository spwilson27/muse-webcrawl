Hess's law

Hess's law, also known as Hess's law of constant heat summation, states that the total enthalpy change during the complete course of a chemical reaction is independent of the sequence of steps taken. It is a consequence of the conservation of energy and the fact that enthalpy is a state function.

Statement and use

Because enthalpy depends only on initial and final states, the enthalpy change of a reaction can be computed by adding the enthalpy changes of any series of reactions into which it can be decomposed. Hess's law allows calculation of enthalpy changes that are hard to measure directly, such as the enthalpy of formation of carbon monoxide from its elements, by using measured enthalpies of related combustions. Named after Swiss-born Russian chemist Germain Hess, who published the law in 1840, it is a precursor of the first law of thermodynamics.

Applications

The law underlies standard enthalpy-of-formation tables and Born–Haber cycles used to determine lattice enthalpies of ionic compounds.
