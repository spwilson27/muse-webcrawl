Oligomer

In chemistry and biochemistry, an oligomer (from Greek oligo-, "few", and meros, "part") is a molecule consisting of a few repeating units (monomers), typically between two and about twenty. Oligomers are intermediate in size and complexity between monomers and long-chain polymers: their physical properties change appreciably as units are added or removed, unlike polymers, whose properties are roughly constant with chain length.

Terminology

Oligomers are named by the number of units they contain: a dimer has two units, a trimer three, a tetramer four, a pentamer five, a hexamer six, and so on. If the units are identical, the result is a homo-oligomer; if different, a hetero-oligomer. In proteins, "oligomer" describes a functional assembly of several polypeptide subunits, such as dimeric enzymes or the tetrameric haemoglobin molecule.

Examples

Common oligomers include oligosaccharides (short carbohydrate chains such as those in human milk and in dietary fibre), oligonucleotides (short DNA or RNA strands used as primers, probes, and aptamers), and short oligomers in industrial chemistry, such as oligomeric plasticisers, lubricants, and the intermediates formed in the early stages of polymerisation. Cyclic oligomers, in which the units form a ring, arise in several polymerisation processes.

Applications

Synthetic oligonucleotides underpin much of molecular biology and modern genomics. In materials science, oligomers are used as reactive diluents in UV-curable coatings, adhesives, and 3D-printing resins, where their low viscosity and defined chain length are advantageous.
