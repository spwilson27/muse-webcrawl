Economists distinguish several types of unemployment, each arising from different causes. The main categories are frictional, structural, cyclical, and seasonal unemployment, together with related concepts such as technological, classical (real-wage), and hidden unemployment.

Frictional unemployment

Frictional unemployment is the time spent between jobs when a worker is searching for, or transitioning from one job to another. It exists even in a healthy economy, because matching workers to suitable vacancies takes time, and it is partly reduced by better information about job openings. It is considered largely voluntary and unavoidable.

Structural unemployment

Structural unemployment occurs when there is a mismatch between the skills workers have and the skills employers need, or between where workers live and where jobs are located. Causes include technological change, automation, outsourcing, and the decline of particular industries or regions. It tends to be longer-lasting than frictional unemployment and is addressed by retraining and education.

Cyclical unemployment

Cyclical unemployment (also called demand-deficient or Keynesian unemployment) rises during recessions, when aggregate demand falls and firms lay off workers, and falls during expansions. It is the type targeted by fiscal and monetary stimulus policy. (See also structural unemployment for contrast.)

Seasonal unemployment

Seasonal unemployment results from predictable seasonal variation in demand for labour, as in agriculture, tourism, construction, and retail around holidays. Statistical agencies often adjust unemployment figures to remove seasonal effects.

Other types

Technological unemployment is job loss caused by labour-saving technology. Classical unemployment (real-wage unemployment) is attributed to wages being kept above the market-clearing level, for example by minimum-wage laws or union bargaining. Hidden unemployment covers people not counted in official statistics, such as discouraged workers who have stopped looking. The level of unemployment that persists when cyclical unemployment is zero is sometimes called the natural rate of unemployment.
