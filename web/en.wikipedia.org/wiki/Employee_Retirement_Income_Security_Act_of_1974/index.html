The Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA) is a United States federal law that sets minimum standards for most voluntarily established retirement and health plans in private industry, in order to protect individuals enrolled in these plans. Enacted on September 2, 1974, and codified principally at Title 29 of the United States Code, ERISA requires plans to provide participants with information about plan features and funding, establishes fiduciary duties for those who manage and control plan assets, requires a grievance and appeals process for participants, and gives participants the right to sue for benefits and breaches of fiduciary duty.

Background

Public concern over pension security grew through the 1960s as several prominent plans failed, most notoriously the 1963 closure of the Studebaker-Packard plant in South Bend, Indiana, whose underfunded pension plan left thousands of workers with sharply reduced or no benefits. Congressional hearings, investigative journalism and a 1972 NBC documentary, Pensions: The Broken Promise, built momentum for reform. After years of competing bills, ERISA passed with broad bipartisan support and was signed by President Gerald Ford on Labor Day, September 2, 1974.

Key provisions

Pension plan standards

ERISA establishes rules for participation, vesting (the point at which an employee's accrued benefit becomes non-forfeitable), benefit accrual and minimum funding of defined-benefit pension plans. It generally does not require an employer to establish a plan, nor does it set minimum benefit levels, but it regulates plans that employers choose to offer.

Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation

The Act created the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC), a federal corporation that insures benefits under covered defined-benefit plans, financed primarily by premiums paid by plan sponsors. If an underfunded plan terminates, the PBGC pays guaranteed benefits up to statutory limits.

Fiduciary duties

ERISA imposes duties of loyalty and prudence on plan fiduciaries, requiring them to act solely in the interest of participants and beneficiaries, to diversify plan investments, and to follow plan documents. Fiduciaries who breach these duties may be personally liable.

Reporting, disclosure and claims

Plans must furnish participants with summary plan descriptions, annual reports (Form 5500), and individual benefit statements, and must maintain a reasonable claims procedure with the right to appeal denials and to sue in federal court after exhausting administrative remedies.

Individual retirement accounts

ERISA also created the individual retirement account (IRA), allowing workers not covered by an employer plan to make tax-deductible retirement contributions — a provision that later expanded into a central pillar of American retirement saving.

Health plans and preemption

Title I of ERISA covers employee welfare benefit plans, including employer-sponsored health insurance, imposing reporting, fiduciary and claims rules but — unlike later amendments such as COBRA (1985) and HIPAA (1996) — few substantive benefit mandates. ERISA's broad preemption clause, which supersedes state laws that "relate to" an employee benefit plan, has generated decades of litigation; it generally shields self-insured employer health plans from state insurance regulation, while a "savings clause" preserves state regulation of insurance itself. In Gobeille v. Liberty Mutual (2016) and other cases the Supreme Court has continued to define the reach of preemption.

Criticism and reform

ERISA has been criticised for its complex compliance burden, which some argue accelerated the decline of defined-benefit pensions, and for limiting remedies available to health-plan participants denied care. Major later amendments include the Retirement Equity Act of 1984, the Pension Protection Act of 2006, and the SECURE Acts of 2019 and 2022, which adjusted funding, participation and required-distribution rules.
