Digital poetry

Image: Kinetic digital poem

Digital poetry is a form of electronic literature in which poetic texts are created for, and experienced on, digital devices. It exploits the capabilities of computing — animation, interactivity, hyperlinks, sound, and programmed generation — so that the work could not be fully transferred to print. Digital poetry is a major genre within electronic literature.

Forms

Recognised forms include hypertext poetry, which uses links to create non-linear reading paths; kinetic poetry, in which words move and change over time; computer-generated poetry, including works produced by algorithms and, more recently, language models; interactive and game-like poems; and code poetry, where program code itself functions as text. Early experiments include Christopher Strachey's love-letter generator of 1952 and the stochastic texts of Theo Lutz (1959).

History and communities

The genre grew with the web in the 1990s, supported by organisations such as the Electronic Literature Organization (founded 1999) and journals including Beehive and Poems That Go. Noted practitioners include Stephanie Strickland, Talan Memmott, Mez Breeze, and Young-hae Chang Heavy Industries, whose Flash-based works combined synchronised text with jazz soundtracks. The obsolescence of platforms such as Flash has made preservation a central concern for archivists of digital poetry.
