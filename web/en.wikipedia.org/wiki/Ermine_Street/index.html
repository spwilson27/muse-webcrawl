Ermine Street

Ermine Street
Route: Londinium (London) – Eboracum (York)
Length: about 200 miles (320 km)
Modern equivalents: A10, A1/A1(M), A15, A1034 in part
Built: Roman Britain, 1st century AD

Ermine Street is a major Roman road in England that ran from Bishopsgate in London (Londinium) north to York (Eboracum), a distance of about 200 miles (320 km). It is one of the great trunk roads of Roman Britain, and much of its line is still followed by modern roads, principally the A10 out of London and the A1 further north.

Name

The name derives not from the fur but from the Earningas, an Anglo-Saxon tribal group whose territory the road crossed near Royston; it was earlier called Earninga Stræt, "the street of the Earningas". The Roman name of the road is unknown.

Route

From Bishopsgate in Londinium the road ran north through Godmanchester (Duroliponte area settlements), Water Newton (Durobrivae) on the River Nene, and Ancaster, to Lincoln (Lindum Colonia), where it met an important junction. North of Lincoln the original route crossed the Humber by a ferry from Winteringham to Brough (Petuaria), shortening the journey; an alternative, longer land route ran west through Doncaster (Danum). The road then continued through Castleford to York (Eboracum), the military capital of northern Britain.

History and legacy

Built in the decades after the Roman invasion of AD 43 to move legions and supplies north, Ermine Street remained a principal artery in the Anglo-Saxon and medieval periods and became part of the Old North Road coaching route. Long straight stretches of the modern road network, parish boundaries, and place names (such as the various Strattons and Strettons) still trace its alignment, and surviving agger (raised bank) sections are visible in places, notably near Lincoln and in the Ancaster area.
