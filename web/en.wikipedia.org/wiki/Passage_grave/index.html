Passage grave

A passage grave or passage tomb is a type of Neolithic megalithic tomb consisting of one or more burial chambers covered by a cairn or earthen mound and reached by a narrow stone passage, which typically allows the chamber to be re-entered over successive generations. Passage graves are found across Atlantic Europe — Ireland, Britain, Brittany, Iberia, Scandinavia and northern Germany — mostly dated to between about 4000 and 2500 BCE.

Architecture and alignments

The passage is built of upright megaliths (orthostats) roofed with lintels or corbelled vaulting, often with a cruciform or circular chamber. Many are decorated with megalithic art — spirals, lozenges and zigzags pecked into the stones. Famous examples include Newgrange and Knowth in Ireland's Brú na Bóinne complex, Maeshowe in Orkney, La Hougue Bie in Jersey, and the Gavrinis tomb in Brittany. Several are astronomically aligned: at Newgrange the rising sun at the winter solstice shines through a roof-box above the entrance down the passage into the chamber.

Interpretation

Excavated chambers contain cremated and unburned human remains, pottery, stone tools and ornaments, suggesting collective ancestral burial and ritual use over long periods. Passage graves belong to the wider megalithic cultures of Neolithic Europe, distinct from the simpler portal tombs and gallery graves of the same era.
