Congestion pricing

Electronic charging gantries at a zone boundary.

Congestion pricing or congestion charging is a system of surcharging users of public goods that are subject to congestion—most prominently road space in city centers—by charging higher prices at peak times or for entry into a defined zone. The economic rationale, rooted in Pigouvian analysis and developed by transport economist William Vickrey, is that drivers impose delays on others for which they do not pay; a charge internalizes this externality, reducing traffic to more efficient levels and generating revenue often earmarked for public transport.

Implementations

Singapore introduced the first scheme in 1975 (later electronic road pricing). London's congestion charge began in 2003; Stockholm's charge was made permanent after a 2006 trial and referendum; Milan, Gothenburg, and Durham operate smaller schemes. In the United States, congestion pricing has long applied to managed express toll lanes, and New York City launched the first central-business-district tolling program in the U.S. (Central Business District Tolling Program) in January 2025.

Effects and debate

Evaluations generally find reduced traffic volumes within zones, faster and more reliable journeys, lower emissions, and increased transit use, with charges typically falling as a smaller burden than feared once alternatives improve. Critics raise concerns about equity for lower-income drivers, effects on businesses, privacy of enforcement cameras, and whether charges become general revenue tools. Public acceptability often rises after implementation, as in Stockholm's referendum reversal.
