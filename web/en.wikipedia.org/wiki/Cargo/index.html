Cargo

In transportation, cargo refers to goods or produce transported — generally for commercial gain — by water, air or land. Freight is a closely related term, sometimes used for the cargo itself and sometimes for the charge for transporting it. Cargo was originally a shipload, but now covers all types of freight, including that carried by rail, van, truck or intermodal container. Goods carried for personal use, such as household effects, are distinguished from commercial cargo in customs and insurance practice.

Containerised cargo aboard a container ship — the dominant form of general cargo today.

Categories

  • Bulk cargo — unpackaged commodities poured or pumped in quantity: dry bulk (grain, coal, ore) and liquid bulk (crude oil, chemicals, LNG).
  • Break bulk — goods carried in bags, crates, drums or bales and handled individually.
  • Neo-bulk — uniform unpackaged loads such as vehicles, steel and timber.
  • Containerised cargo — goods packed in standard ISO containers (TEU/FEU), which revolutionised handling after the 1950s and now dominate general-cargo shipping.
  • Project and heavy-lift cargo — oversized industrial equipment requiring specialised transport.
  • Perishable and reefer cargo — temperature-controlled goods; dangerous goods are regulated under codes such as the IMDG and IATA DGR.

Handling and documentation

Cargo moves under contracts of carriage evidenced by a bill of lading (sea), air waybill (air) or consignment note (road/rail), documents that record the shipper, consignee, description, weight and terms. The harmonised system of Incoterms allocates costs and risks between buyer and seller. Handling is performed by stevedores and terminal operators using cranes, forklifts and conveyors; freight forwarders and customs brokers intermediate between shippers and carriers. Cargo insurance covers loss or damage in transit, separate from the carrier's limited liability.

Scale

Seaborne trade carries the great majority of world cargo by weight — around 11 billion tonnes loaded annually in recent years — while air cargo moves a small share by weight but roughly a third of world trade by value. Road and rail dominate inland distribution. The efficiency of cargo logistics underpins global supply chains, and disruptions — from port congestion to the 2021 Suez Canal blockage — can propagate worldwide.
