Muzzle brake

A muzzle brake or recoil compensator is a device connected to, or a feature integral to the construction of, the muzzle or barrel of a firearm or cannon that is intended to redirect a portion of propellant gases to counter recoil and unwanted muzzle rise. The concept was first used for artillery and anti-tank guns. Muzzle brakes are used on large-calibre guns as well as on rifles, in order to limit recoil or "kick", reduce the time to re-aim between shots, and reduce the dust signature when firing.

Design

As the projectile exits the barrel, the high-pressure propellant gases behind it would normally expand forward, contributing to recoil. A muzzle brake intercepts these gases with baffles or ports and deflects them sideways, backwards or upwards. Deflecting gas to the sides and rear creates a forward force that counteracts part of the recoil; ports angled upward counteract muzzle rise (a device optimised for this is often called a compensator). Brakes range from simple slotted tubes to multi-chamber designs; the large "pepper-pot" and double-baffle brakes of tank guns and artillery are prominent examples, as on the M109 howitzer or the .50 BMG Barrett M82 rifle.

Advantages and disadvantages

Effective brakes can reduce felt recoil by 30–50% or more, allowing faster follow-up shots and making powerful cartridges more manageable. The disadvantages are substantial: redirected gas greatly increases the muzzle blast and noise experienced by the shooter and especially by bystanders to the sides, and can raise a cloud of dust or debris that reveals the shooter's position. For these reasons brakes are restricted at some ranges and in some competitions, and are generally disliked in team tactical settings. Linear compensators attempt a compromise by directing blast forward.

Related devices

Muzzle brakes should be distinguished from flash suppressors, which reduce visible muzzle flash but do little for recoil; from recoil boosters, used on some recoil-operated machine guns; and from suppressors (silencers), which slow and cool the escaping gases to reduce the sound signature. Some modern devices combine braking and flash-hiding functions.
