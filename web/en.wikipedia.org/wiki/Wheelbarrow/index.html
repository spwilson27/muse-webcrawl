Wheelbarrow

Wheelbarrow
TypeHand-propelled cart
ComponentsWheel, tray (bucket), handles, legs
UsesConstruction, gardening, agriculture
OriginChina, Han dynasty (by 2nd century CE)

A wheelbarrow is a small hand-propelled vehicle, usually with just one wheel, designed to be pushed and guided by a single person using two handles at the rear, or by a sail to push it in ancient China. It is used to carry small loads in construction, gardening, and agriculture, distributing the weight of the load between the wheel and the operator so that much heavier loads can be moved than could be carried by hand alone.

History

The wheelbarrow first appeared in China during the Han dynasty. A mural in a tomb near Chengdu, dated to about 118 CE, depicts a man using a wheelbarrow, and the device is traditionally associated with the chancellor Zhuge Liang (181–234 CE), who is credited with popularising the "wooden ox" for military supply transport. Chinese wheelbarrows typically placed a large single wheel in the centre of the frame, with the load balanced on either side.

The wheelbarrow appeared in Europe much later, in the medieval period, with the earliest depictions dating to around 1170–1250 CE in northwestern Europe. Unlike the Chinese design, the European wheelbarrow placed the wheel at or near the front, beneath the load. By the 13th century it had become common on building sites, in mines, and on farms.

Design

A typical modern wheelbarrow consists of a tray or bucket (of steel, plastic, or wood), a single pneumatic-tyred wheel at the front, two support legs, and two handles. Variants include two-wheeled garden carts, motorised barrows, and folding designs. Capacities commonly range from about 60 to 120 litres.
