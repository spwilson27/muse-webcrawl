Ralph Steinman

Ralph Marvin Steinman (January 14, 1943 – September 30, 2011) was a Canadian-American immunologist at Rockefeller University. He discovered the dendritic cell in 1973 and spent his career demonstrating its central role in the immune system. He was awarded a share of the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery, but died three days before the prize was announced, without knowing he had won it.

Discovery of dendritic cells

Working in Zanvil Cohn's laboratory at Rockefeller University in the early 1970s, Steinman identified a previously unknown cell type in mouse spleen with long branching processes, which he named the "dendritic cell" (from the Greek dendron, tree). Initially sceptical colleagues doubted the cells' importance, but Steinman's group showed that dendritic cells are the most potent antigen-presenting cells: they capture antigens in tissues, migrate to lymph nodes and activate T cells, thereby initiating adaptive immune responses and shaping immunological tolerance.

Career and recognition

Steinman joined Rockefeller in 1970 and remained there for his entire career, becoming Henry G. Kunkel Professor and director of the Center for Immunology and Immune Diseases. His work underlies modern approaches to vaccines, cancer immunotherapy and treatments for autoimmune disease and transplantation. He received many awards, including the Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research (2007) and the Canada Gairdner International Award (2003).

Death and Nobel Prize

Diagnosed with pancreatic cancer in 2007, Steinman extended his own life with experimental dendritic-cell-based immunotherapies of his own design. He died on September 30, 2011. On October 3, the Nobel Assembly awarded him half of the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (shared with Bruce Beutler and Jules Hoffmann), unaware that he had died. Because the prize is normally not awarded posthumously, the Nobel Foundation reviewed the case and decided the award would stand, since the decision had been made in good faith while he was believed alive.
