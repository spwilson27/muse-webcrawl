An alligator is a large crocodilian reptile of the genus Alligator in the family Alligatoridae. The two living species are the American alligator of the southeastern United States and the smaller, critically endangered Chinese alligator of the lower Yangtze. The name derives from Spanish el lagarto, "the lizard".

Description

Alligators are distinguished from true crocodiles by their broader, U-shaped snouts and by a dental arrangement in which the enlarged fourth lower tooth fits into a socket inside the upper jaw, invisible when the mouth is closed. Adult American males average 3.4–4.6 m (11–15 ft) and can exceed 450 kg. They are apex predators feeding on fish, birds, turtles, snakes and mammals, and possess one of the strongest measured bite forces of any living animal.

Behaviour and ecology

Females build mound nests of vegetation, guard their eggs, and carry hatchlings to water; nest temperature determines the sex of the young. Alligators engineer their habitat by excavating "gator holes" that retain water in dry seasons and shelter other wildlife. Hunting drove the American alligator to endangered status by the 1960s, but protection under the Endangered Species Act produced a celebrated recovery; the species was delisted in 1987 and now numbers in the millions. The Chinese alligator, by contrast, survives with only a few hundred wild individuals.
