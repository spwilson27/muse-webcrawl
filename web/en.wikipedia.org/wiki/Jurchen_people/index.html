The Jurchen (Chinese: 女真, Nǚzhēn) were a Tungusic-speaking people who inhabited the region of Manchuria in Northeast Asia. During the tenth and eleventh centuries they were divided between independent tribes and groups subject to the Khitan Liao dynasty, but in the early twelfth century the Wanyan clan united them; under Aguda they destroyed the Liao and founded the Jin dynasty, which conquered northern China from the Song dynasty.

Origins and early history

The Jurchen were the successors of the Mohe tribes of the region, themselves descendants of the Sushen-Yilou peoples of earlier Chinese records. They lived by agriculture, hunting, fishing and stock-raising in the valleys of the Sungari (Songhua) and Amur rivers, and traded in horses, furs and ginseng. Chinese and Korean sources divided them into "raw" (independent) and "cooked" (tributary and partially Sinicized) Jurchen. Their language, Jurchen, was written from 1119 in a Jurchen script derived from Khitan and Chinese models.

The Jin dynasty

In 1114 Wanyan Aguda revolted against the Liao; the following year he proclaimed the Jin ("Golden") dynasty. By 1125 Jin had destroyed Liao, and in 1127 its armies captured the Northern Song capital Kaifeng in the Jingkang Incident, taking the emperors Huizong and Qinzong captive and driving the Song court south of the Yangtze. The Jurchen ruled northern China for a century, increasingly adopting Chinese administrative practices and Confucian institutions while preserving their own banner-style military organisation of meng-an and mou-ke units. Their empire was destroyed by the Mongols, in alliance with the Southern Song, in 1234.

Ming period and the Manchu

After the fall of Jin, Jurchen groups again lived under Mongol Yuan and then Ming rule in the northeast, organised by the Ming in guard units. In the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries the Jianzhou Jurchen leader Nurhaci united the tribes, created the Eight Banners, and founded the Later Jin (from 1636, Qing) state; his son Huang Taiji renamed the people "Manchu" in 1635. The Manchu-led Qing dynasty conquered China in 1644 and ruled until 1912, and the modern Manchu ethnic group descends largely from the Jurchen.
