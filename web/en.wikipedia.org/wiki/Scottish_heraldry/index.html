Scottish heraldry

Scottish heraldry is the form of coats of arms and armorial bearings used in Scotland, regulated — uniquely rigorously — by the Lord Lyon King of Arms and recorded in the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in Scotland, established by Act of Parliament in 1672. Unlike in England, arms in Scotland are treated as heritable property belonging to one person at a time, and unauthorised use is an offence enforceable in the Lyon Court.

Distinctive features

Scottish heraldry has several hallmarks. The crest and motto are integral parts of a grant: the motto usually appears in a scroll above the crest (contrasting with English practice below the shield). Clan members may wear the chief's crest within a strap-and-buckle badge, signifying allegiance rather than ownership. Cadets of a family must difference their arms — traditionally through the Scottish system of bordures and small charges, governed by the "Stodart system" — because undifferenced arms may be borne by only one person. Common features of Scottish shields include the tressure (notably the royal tressure flory-counter-flory), simple, bold designs reflecting medieval origins, and charges such as stags, boars' heads, lymphads (galleys), and mullets.

History and law

Heraldry reached Scotland in the 12th–13th centuries; early rolls and seals centre on great families such as the Stewarts and Comyns. The Lyon King of Arms, an office of great antiquity, heads the heraldic authority, supported by heralds and pursuivants (e.g., Unicorn Pursuivant, Carrick Pursuivant). The 1672 Register remains a working legal record; new grants and matriculations are made regularly, including to corporate bodies and to members of the Scottish diaspora in the Commonwealth and United States.

Royal arms

The royal arms of Scotland feature the red lion rampant of Scotland within a double tressure on gold; in the United Kingdom's arms, the Scottish version gives the Scottish lion precedence in the first and fourth quarters, and pairs it with the unicorn — Scotland's national animal — as a supporter, crowned and chained.
