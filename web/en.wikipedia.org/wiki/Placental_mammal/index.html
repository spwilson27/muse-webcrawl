Placental mammal

Placental mammals (infraclass Placentalia) are the most diverse and widespread group of mammals, comprising more than 5,000 living species — from shrews and bats to whales and humans — united by giving birth to relatively well-developed young nourished before birth through a placenta. They are one of the three living groups of mammals, alongside the egg-laying monotremes and the marsupials.

Characteristics

Placentals carry their young in the uterus for a long gestation relative to marsupials, the fetus being fed through an allantoic placenta. Adults lack the epipubic bones that stiffen the trunk in monotremes and marsupials, the corpus callosum connects the cerebral hemispheres, and the young are born at an advanced stage — though the balance differs: rodents and carnivores have altricial (helpless) young, while horses and whales have precocial young able to move soon after birth.

Evolution

Molecular evidence places the last common ancestor of living placentals in the Late Cretaceous, around 90–100 million years ago, while the oldest widely accepted placental fossils (such as the shrew-like Juramaia, sometimes argued to be older still) come from around the Cretaceous–Palaeogene transition. The group radiated explosively after the end-Cretaceous extinction 66 million years ago into the niches vacated by the non-avian dinosaurs.

Classification

Living placentals fall into four great superorders: Xenarthra (armadillos, sloths, anteaters), Afrotheria (elephants, aardvarks, hyraxes, dugongs), Euarchontoglires (primates, rodents, lagomorphs, treeshrews, colugos) and Laurasiatheria (bats, carnivores, hoofed mammals, whales, insectivores). Rodents alone account for about 40 percent of species, bats about 20 percent.
