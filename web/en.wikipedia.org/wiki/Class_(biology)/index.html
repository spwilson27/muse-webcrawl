Class (biology)

In biological classification, class (Latin: classis) is a taxonomic rank, as well as a taxonomic unit in that rank. It sits below phylum (division in botany) and above order. Other well-known ranks in descending order of size are life, domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species, with class fitting between phylum and order.

The hierarchy of biological classification's eight major taxonomic ranks; class lies between phylum and order.

History and usage

The concept of class Genel was introduced by the French botanist Joseph Pitton de Tournefort in the late 17th century and was adopted by Carl Linnaeus in his Systema Naturae (1735), which divided each kingdom into a small number of classes — for animals, Mammalia, Aves, Amphibia, Pisces, Insecta, and Vermes. As knowledge of diversity grew, the number of recognized classes expanded dramatically; for example, the phylum Arthropoda alone contains more than a dozen classes, including Insecta, Arachnida, and Malacostraca.

Subdivisions

Where finer resolution is needed, ranks such as subclass, infraclass, and superclass may be inserted around the class level. In zoology there is no mandatory ending for class names, unlike families; familiar examples include Mammalia (mammals), Aves (birds), Reptilia (reptiles), Amphibia (amphibians), and Insecta (insects). The composition of a class is determined by taxonomists and, under modern phylogenetic practice, classes are expected to be monophyletic — comprising an ancestor and all its descendants.
