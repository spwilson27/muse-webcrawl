Macroscopic quantum phenomena

Macroscopic quantum phenomena are phenomena in which the quantum mechanical behaviour of a system is evident on a macroscopic, directly observable scale, rather than being confined to atoms and molecules. They arise when a macroscopic number of particles occupy a single coherent quantum state described by one wavefunction with a well-defined phase.

Examples

The canonical examples are Superconductivity — in which Cooper pairs condense into a coherent state that carries current without resistance and expels magnetic fields via the Meissner effect — and superfluidity in liquid helium, in which the fluid flows without viscosity and exhibits quantised vortices. Bose–Einstein condensates of ultracold atoms are a third, laboratory-created example. Quantised flux through a superconducting ring, in units of h/2e, and the Josephson effects are direct measurements of macroscopic phase coherence.

Significance

These phenomena show that there is no sharp boundary between quantum and classical behaviour: with sufficient isolation from thermal decoherence, quantum coherence can span centimetres and involve Avogadro-scale numbers of particles. They are the physical basis of SQUIDs, superconducting qubits and the most precise measurement devices in physics.
