Five and dime

TypeVariety store
PioneerF. W. Woolworth Company (1879)

A five and dime (also five-and-ten-cent store or dimestore) is a variety store selling inexpensive merchandise, originally priced at five or ten cents. The format was pioneered by Frank Winfield Woolworth, whose first successful store opened in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in 1879, and became a fixture of American main streets for a century.

History

Woolworth's insight was to buy goods directly from manufacturers and sell at fixed low price points, displaying merchandise openly on counters for self-service browsing. Competing chains — S. S. Kresge (later Kmart), McCrory, Ben Franklin and others — spread across North America and abroad. Many stores featured lunch counters, some of which became sites of the 1960 civil rights sit-ins, beginning at a Woolworth in Greensboro, North Carolina.

Decline

Inflation eroded the fixed price points, and competition from discount department stores, malls and later dollar stores hollowed out the format. Woolworth closed its remaining U.S. five-and-dime stores in 1997. The modern dollar store is regarded as the format's successor.
