The Second Schleswig War (Danish: Krigen i 1864; German: Deutsch-Dänischer Krieg) was a conflict fought in 1864 between Denmark on one side and Prussia and the Austrian Empire on the other, over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein. It was the second of the Schleswig Wars, after the First Schleswig War (1848–1851), and ended in a decisive Danish defeat: Denmark lost some 40% of its territory and about a third of its population.

Background

The duchies' status was the notorious "Schleswig-Holstein Question": Schleswig was a Danish fief with a mixed Danish-German population; Holstein was German-speaking and a member of the German Confederation, though ruled by the Danish king. The London Protocol of 1852 had tried to settle the matter, but Denmark's November Constitution of 1863, incorporating Schleswig more closely into Denmark, breached it. Prussian minister-president Otto von Bismarck exploited the dispute, allied with Austria, and issued an ultimatum.

Course of the war

Prussian and Austrian forces crossed the Eider on 1 February 1864. The Danes abandoned the ancient Danevirke fortifications in a difficult winter retreat — a national trauma in Denmark — and made their stand at Dybbøl, which was stormed after a heavy bombardment on 18 April 1864. The Prussian crossing to the island of Als on 29 June destroyed the last Danish field position; with Jutland overrun and no great-power help forthcoming, Denmark sought peace.

Treaty of Vienna

By the Treaty of Vienna (30 October 1864), Denmark ceded Schleswig, Holstein and Saxe-Lauenburg to joint Austro-Prussian administration. The division of the spoils led directly to friction between the allies and thus to the Austro-Prussian War of 1866. The northern part of Schleswig was returned to Denmark only after the plebiscites of 1920, following the First World War — a reunion still celebrated in Denmark.
