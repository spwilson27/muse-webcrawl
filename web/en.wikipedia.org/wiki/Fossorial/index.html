Fossorial

A fossorial animal (from Latin fossor, meaning "digger") is one adapted to digging which lives primarily, but not solely, underground. Examples of fossorial vertebrates include moles, mole-rats, ground squirrels, badgers, armadillos and aardvarks among mammals; burrowing owls and some other birds; many lizards, amphisbaenians ("worm lizards") and blind snakes among reptiles; and burrowing frogs among amphibians. Numerous invertebrates — notably earthworms, moles crickets and many beetle and ant species — are also fossorial.

Fossoriality has evolved independently many times, and the lifestyle grades into that of semi-fossorial animals (which dig burrows for shelter but forage mostly above ground, such as rabbits and prairie dogs) and subterranean animals that spend essentially their entire lives below the surface (such as marsupial moles, golden moles and naked mole-rats).

Adaptations

Convergent adaptations to digging include powerful forelimbs with enlarged claws (as in moles and armadillos) or robust heads and snouts used as shovels (as in amphisbaenians and some mole-rats, which dig with their incisor teeth); short, dense fur that sheds soil and can lie in either direction; reduced or skin-covered eyes; small external ears that do not fill with soil; and physiological tolerance of the low-oxygen, high-carbon-dioxide atmosphere of sealed burrows. Fossorial species dig by scratch-digging, chisel-tooth digging, head-lift digging or humeral rotation (the specialised stroke of true moles).

Ecology

Burrowing animals are major ecosystem engineers: their digging aerates and mixes soil, redistributes nutrients, and their burrows shelter many other species — gopher tortoise burrows, for example, host hundreds of commensal species. Subterranean life offers protection from predators and climatic extremes but imposes costs in energy, navigation and communication, met through seismic signalling and acute senses of touch and smell.
