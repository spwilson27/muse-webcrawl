Seed crystal

A seed crystal is a small piece of single crystal or polycrystalline material from which a large crystal of the same material is to be grown. Introducing a seed into a supersaturated solution or a supercooled melt initiates crystallization on its surfaces, because growing onto an existing crystal lattice is energetically easier than forming a new nucleus spontaneously. The resulting large crystal inherits the seed's crystallographic orientation.

A seed crystal determines the orientation of the much larger crystal grown from it.

Applications

Seed crystals are central to industrial crystal growth. In the Czochralski process, a seed is dipped into molten silicon, germanium, or another material and slowly withdrawn while rotating, pulling a large single-crystal ingot (boule) from which semiconductor wafers are cut. Similar seeding is used to grow optical and laser crystals, synthetic gemstones, and large crystals for research.

In solution growth and hobbyist crystal growing — for example alum or copper sulfate crystals grown in science classes — a small crystal formed by evaporation is tied to a thread and suspended in fresh saturated solution, growing larger and better formed than crystals nucleated randomly. Seeding also matters in protein crystallography, where microseeds can induce crystals of proteins that resist spontaneous nucleation, and in cloud seeding analogies and pharmaceutical crystallization, where seed loading controls crystal size distribution.

Theory

Nucleation of a new phase requires surmounting a free-energy barrier: tiny crystal embryos tend to redissolve unless they exceed a critical size. A seed crystal is already far above that size, so material deposits on it directly (heterogeneous growth with essentially no barrier), at supersaturations too low for homogeneous nucleation. Defects in the seed, such as dislocations, can propagate into the grown crystal, so seed quality is important.
