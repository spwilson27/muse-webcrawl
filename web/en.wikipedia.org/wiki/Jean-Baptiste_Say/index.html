Jean-Baptiste Say

Portrait of Jean-Baptiste Say

Jean-Baptiste Say (5 January 1767 – 15 November 1832) was a French economist and businessman, a leading classical liberal thinker best known for formulating Say's law, the proposition that production creates its own demand ("supply creates its own demand"), a cornerstone of classical economics later contested by Keynes.

Life

Born in Lyon to a Protestant textile family, Say worked in insurance and banking, edited a journal during the French Revolution, and served on the Tribunat under Napoleon until his criticism of fiscal policy led to his removal in 1804. He then ran a cotton-spinning factory at Auchy-lès-Hesdin, gaining practical business experience unusual among economists. After the Restoration he lectured publicly in Paris; in 1819 he became the first chair of industrial economics at the Conservatoire des Arts et Métiers, and in 1830 a professor at the Collège de France.

Treatise on Political Economy

His Traité d'économie politique (1803, repeatedly revised) presented economics as the study of production, distribution, and consumption of wealth, emphasizing utility, entrepreneurship, and free markets against mercantilism. Say introduced the entrepreneur as a distinct economic agent who combines factors of production and bears risk. His law of markets held that general gluts are impossible because producing goods generates the income to buy them — a doctrine John Maynard Keynes attacked in The General Theory (1936), making Say a lasting reference point in macroeconomic debate.

Influence

Through translations, the Treatise shaped liberal economics in Europe and America throughout the nineteenth century. Say also influenced thought on money — he held that money is a neutral veil over real exchange — and corresponded with David Ricardo and Thomas Malthus.
