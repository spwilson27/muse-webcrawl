Yukawa in 1949.

Hideki Yukawa (23 January 1907 – 8 September 1981) was a Japanese theoretical physicist and the first Japanese Nobel laureate in Physics (1949). In 1935 he proposed that the nuclear force between protons and neutrons is mediated by a new particle — the meson — whose mass, inferred from the force's short range, should be about 200 times the electron's. The pion's discovery in cosmic rays (1947, Cecil Powell's group, after the muon had been found and initially confused with Yukawa's particle) confirmed the prediction and founded meson physics.

Yukawa's potential, V(r) ∝ e^(−μr)/r, describes forces carried by massive particles and remains a standard concept (screened Coulomb interactions bear his name). He built Japan's modern physics school at Kyoto and Osaka, directed the Research Institute for Fundamental Physics (now the Yukawa Institute), and in later life wrote widely on science, creativity and peace — he was among the eleven signatories of the Russell–Einstein Manifesto.

The 1935 paper

Yukawa's reasoning inverted Rutherford's problem: the nuclear force's range (~2 fm) implies, by the uncertainty principle, a mediator mass of ~100 MeV/c². Written while he was an obscure lecturer, the paper was ignored until the muon (1936) seemed to be the particle and wasn't — its weak interaction with nuclei disqualified it — and the true pion's discovery made Yukawa's the canonical example of predicting a particle from a force's range, the template later used for the W and Z bosons.
