Public housing

Public housing is housing owned and usually managed by a government authority and let at below-market rents to households who cannot afford adequate housing in the private market. It is one form of social housing, distinguished by direct public ownership; other models rely on non-profit housing associations or rent subsidies in private tenancies.

Models around the world

In the United States, public housing began with the Housing Act of 1937 and is administered by local housing authorities with federal funding from HUD; about a million units remain, housing roughly two million people, alongside the much larger Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8) programme. Large post-war estates acquired a reputation for concentrated poverty and crime — Chicago's Cabrini–Green and St Louis's Pruitt–Igoe were demolished — and policy shifted toward mixed-income redevelopment under HOPE VI.

In the United Kingdom, council housing built by local authorities housed about a third of the population by the late 1970s; the Right to Buy (1980) sold over a million and a half homes to tenants and sharply reduced the stock. In Singapore, the Housing and Development Board houses about 80% of the resident population, mostly in owner-occupied flats on 99-year leases — a near-universal model unlike the residual welfare model elsewhere. Vienna's municipal and limited-profit housing shelters some 60% of residents and is widely cited for quality and social mix. In Hong Kong, roughly a third of the population lives in public rental estates.

Debates

Arguments concern residualisation (housing only the poorest, with stigma and segregation), maintenance backlogs, allocation rules and waiting lists, and whether governments should build directly, subsidise non-profits, or subsidise rents. Recent policy in many countries has revived public building after decades of decline.
