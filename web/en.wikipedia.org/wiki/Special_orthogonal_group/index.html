Special orthogonal group

In mathematics, the special orthogonal group in dimension n, denoted SO(n), is the group of all rotations about the origin of n-dimensional Euclidean space Rn under the operation of composition. Equivalently, it is the subgroup of the orthogonal group O(n) consisting of all orthogonal matrices with determinant +1:

SO(n) = { A ∈ O(n) : det A = +1 }.

Because rotations preserve orientation while general orthogonal transformations may also include reflections (determinant −1), SO(n) is the identity component of O(n) and has index two in it.

Low-dimensional examples

  • SO(1) is the trivial group containing only the identity.
  • SO(2) is the group of rotations of the plane; it is isomorphic to the circle group of complex numbers of modulus 1, and is abelian.
  • SO(3) is the rotation group of three-dimensional space, fundamental to classical mechanics, robotics, and computer graphics. Its elements can be parameterised by an axis and an angle, or by Euler angles. SO(3) is not simply connected: its universal covering group is the special unitary group SU(2), and its fundamental group is Z/2.
  • SO(4) is closely related to unit quaternions and double-covers via SU(2) × SU(2).

Structure

SO(n) is a compact, connected Lie group of dimension n(n − 1)/2. Its Lie algebra, denoted so(n), consists of all skew-symmetric real n × n matrices. For n ≥ 3 the group is not simply connected; its universal cover is the spin group Spin(n), which plays a central role in quantum mechanics and the theory of spinors, since fermionic states transform under spin rather than rotation representations.

Generalizations

For an indefinite quadratic form of signature (p, q), one defines the indefinite special orthogonal group SO(p, q). The most famous example is the Lorentz group SO(1, 3) (more precisely its identity component SO+(1, 3)) of special relativity, which describes transformations between inertial frames in Minkowski spacetime.
