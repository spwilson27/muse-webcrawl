Venus of Dolní Věstonice

The Venus of Dolní Věstonice is a ceramic figurine of a nude female figure dated to roughly 29,000–25,000 years ago, from the Gravettian culture. It was discovered in 1925 at the Palaeolithic site of Dolní Věstonice in Moravia, in the present-day Czech Republic. Standing about 11 cm tall, it is among the oldest known ceramic artefacts in the world, made millennia before the pottery of the Neolithic.

Discovery and description

The figurine was found in an ash layer, in two pieces, by the team of archaeologist Karel Absolon. It is modelled from local loess clay mixed with bone ash and fired at low temperature. The figure has a heavy body with large breasts, a head without facial detail except for two slanted slits for eyes, and four holes on top of the head that may once have held decoration. A fragmentary fingerprint preserved in the clay has been attributed to a young person, perhaps the maker.

Significance

Together with the many other figurines and fired-clay animal figures from the site, the Venus shows that Upper Palaeolithic people in Moravia had mastered ceramic technology long before farming societies. The figurine is kept at the Moravian Museum in Brno and is rarely exhibited publicly because of its fragility and value.
