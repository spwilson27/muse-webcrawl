Art criticism

Art criticism is the discussion or evaluation of visual art. Art critics usually criticise art in the context of aesthetics or the theory of beauty. A goal of art criticism is the pursuit of a rational basis for art appreciation. The variety of artistic movements has resulted in a division of art criticism into different disciplines, each using different criteria for their judgements.

Criticism mediates between artists, institutions, and audiences.

History

Writing about art goes back to antiquity (Pliny's accounts of artists; Chinese connoisseur literature), but criticism as a public genre emerged in eighteenth-century France with the Salon exhibitions and writers such as Denis Diderot, whose Salon reviews (1759–1781) are classics of the form. In nineteenth-century Britain, John Ruskin championed Turner and the Pre-Raphaelites and made criticism a major literary career; in France, Charles Baudelaire and later the poet-critics supported modern painting. The twentieth century brought formalist criticism (Roger Fry, Clive Bell, and in America Clement Greenberg, the influential advocate of Abstract Expressionism and of "medium specificity"), followed by challenges from social art history, feminism, and postmodern theory, which questioned formalist criteria and the canon itself.

Methods

Critical writing typically combines description, formal analysis, interpretation, and judgement, though critics differ in emphasis: formalists attend to medium and composition; contextualists to biography, politics, patronage, and reception; theorists to representation, identity, and institutions. Criticism appears in newspapers, magazines (e.g., Artforum, Frieze), catalogues, broadcast media, and online platforms, and it influences reputations, museum programming, and the art market — a power that is itself a frequent subject of debate.
