Field: Philosophy of language

Key figures: Saul Kripke, Keith Donnellan, Michael Devitt

Causal theory of reference

A causal theory of reference is a philosophical theory about how terms acquire specific referents: it proposes that a term refers to whatever it is connected to by an appropriate causal chain, rather than by any description speakers associate with it. The theory is contrasted with descriptivist theories of reference associated with Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell.

Origins

The theory emerged in the 1970s from lectures by Saul Kripke (published as Naming and Necessity, 1980) and papers by Keith Donnellan. Kripke argued that a proper name is a rigid designator, picking out the same object in every possible world, and that its reference is fixed by an initial "baptism" and then transmitted through a historical chain of communication.

The theory

On this view, a speaker who uses a name successfully refers to its bearer so long as their use traces back, through a chain of earlier uses, to the event at which the object was named — even if most of what the speaker believes about the bearer is false. Michael Devitt developed the account further and extended it to natural kind terms such as "water" and "tiger", whose reference is grounded in causal-perceptual contact with samples of the kind.

Criticism

Objections include the qua-problem (a causal contact is always contact with an object under some category), cases of reference change such as "Madagascar", and John Searle's and Gareth Evans's arguments that descriptions must play some role. Evans proposed a hybrid causal-descriptivist account.
