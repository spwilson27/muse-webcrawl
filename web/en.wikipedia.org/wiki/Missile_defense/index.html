Missile defense

Missile defense is a system, weapon, or technology involved in the detection, tracking, interception, and also the destruction of attacking missiles. Conceived as a defense against nuclear-armed intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), its application has broadened to include shorter-ranged non-nuclear tactical and theater missiles. The United States, Russia, China, India, Israel, the United Kingdom, and France have all developed missile defense systems.

Phases of interception

  • Boost phase — intercepting the missile while its motors burn (seconds after launch); attractive because the target is bright and slow-moving relative to later phases, but demanding in basing and reaction time.
  • Midcourse phase — intercepting in space, the longest phase for ICBMs; exoatmospheric systems such as the U.S. Ground-Based Midcourse Defense (GMD) with Ground-Based Interceptors in Alaska and California operate here, but decoys complicate discrimination.
  • Terminal phase — intercepting during re-entry over a defended area; systems include THAAD (high-altitude) and Patriot PAC-3 (lower altitude, point defense).

Major systems

Naval systems center on the Aegis Ballistic Missile Defense System with SM-3 and SM-6 interceptors. Israel fields the multi-tiered Arrow 2/3, David's Sling, and Iron Dome systems. Russia deploys the A-135/A-235 anti-ballistic missile system around Moscow and the dual-purpose S-400 and S-500. India has developed its Prithvi Defence Vehicle and Advanced Air Defence interceptors; China has tested HQ-19 and midcourse interceptors.

History

Efforts began with postwar studies and the U.S. Nike Zeus program, followed by the Sentinel and Safeguard ABM systems; the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limited U.S. and Soviet deployments. In 1983 President Ronald Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars"). The U.S. withdrew from the ABM Treaty in 2002 and deployed GMD beginning in 2004. Missile defense remains strategically controversial: critics cite cost-exchange disadvantages and imperfect test records; supporters argue it deters and blunts limited attacks by states such as North Korea and Iran.
