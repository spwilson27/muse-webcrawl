Paul Bert

Paul Bert (17 October 1833 – 11 November 1886) was a French physiologist and politician. A student of Claude Bernard, he is remembered as the founder of modern aerospace and diving medicine through his research on the effects of air pressure on the body.

Scientific work

Bert's masterwork, La Pression barométrique (1878), reported experiments — including work in pressure chambers and on animals — establishing that decompression sickness is caused by nitrogen bubbles forming in blood and tissues when pressure falls too quickly, and that oxygen under high partial pressure is toxic to the central nervous system, an effect still called the Paul Bert effect. His findings laid the groundwork for safe diving practice, altitude physiology and aviation medicine.

Political career

Bert was active in the republican opposition to the Second Empire, served as prefect of the Nord in 1871, and was elected to the Chamber of Deputies. As Minister of Public Instruction in 1881–82 under Léon Gambetta, he advanced free, compulsory and secular primary education and the Ferry laws against clerical influence in schools. He was also a prominent advocate of French colonial expansion. In 1886 he became resident-general in Annam and Tonkin, where he died of dysentery in Hanoi that November.
