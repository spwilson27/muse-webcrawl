The beagle is a breed of small scent hound, similar in appearance to the much larger foxhound. The beagle was developed primarily for hunting hare — a pursuit known as beagling — in which packs of hounds follow quarry on foot rather than on horseback. Possessing a great sense of smell and a loud, baying voice, the beagle is one of the most popular hound breeds as a pet, and it is also widely used as a detection dog for contraband foodstuffs, narcotics and explosives. The breed's name is also famously borne by HMS Beagle, the ship that carried Charles Darwin on his voyage of discovery.

History

Dogs of beagle type have existed in England for centuries; small hounds for hunting on foot were described in medieval sources, and Elizabeth I kept "pocket beagles" small enough to fit in a saddlebag. The modern breed was standardised in the 19th century, largely through the work of the Reverend Phillip Honeywood, whose pack in Essex in the 1830s is regarded as foundational. The Beagle Club was founded in 1890 and the first breed standard drawn up shortly afterwards. Beagles were exported to the United States, where the breed was recognised by the American Kennel Club in 1885 and where two size varieties (13-inch and 15-inch) are shown.

Appearance

The beagle is a sturdy, compact hound with a slightly domed skull, long ears that reach nearly to the end of the nose when drawn out, a square muzzle, and a tail carried gaily with a white tip — the "flag" that helps hunters follow the dog in cover. The short double coat is dense and weatherproof. Any hound colour is acceptable in the UK standard, but the classic pattern is tricolour (black, tan and white); red-and-white, lemon-and-white and other combinations are also common.

Temperament and senses

Beagles are merry, even-tempered and gentle, bred to work cooperatively in packs; they are generally good with children and other dogs but can be wilful, and their powerful nose often overrides training when a scent catches their attention. The breed has around 220 million scent receptors (compared with about 5 million in humans) and, together with the bloodhound and the basset hound, one of the keenest senses of smell of any dog. Their distinctive baying bark historically allowed hunters to follow the pack by sound.

Roles

Beyond beagling and companionship, beagles are extensively used by customs and quarantine services — famously the United States Department of Agriculture's "Beagle Brigade", which inspects luggage for prohibited agricultural products — because of their nose, food motivation, small size and non-threatening appearance. The breed is also common in laboratory research, a use that has long attracted animal-welfare controversy and campaigning.

In popular culture

Snoopy, the dog in Charles M. Schulz's comic strip Peanuts, is the world's most famous fictional beagle. Other notable beagles include Gromit's companion archetype figures in film and television, the dog in Underdog (Shoeshine Boy), and Uno, who in 2008 became the first beagle to win Best in Show at the Westminster Kennel Club Dog Show.
