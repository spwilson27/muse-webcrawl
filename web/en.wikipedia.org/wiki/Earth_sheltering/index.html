Earth sheltering

Earth sheltering is the architectural practice of using earth (soil) against building walls for external thermal mass, to reduce heat loss, and to maintain a steady indoor air temperature. Earth-sheltered buildings may be partially or completely covered with earth or integrated into terrain. Types include earth-bermed houses, in-hill houses, and fully underground (subterranean) structures.

Benefits and drawbacks

The surrounding earth acts as insulation and thermal mass, moderating temperature extremes and reducing heating and cooling costs. Earth sheltered buildings are also protected from wind, noise, and fire, and blend into the landscape. Challenges include waterproofing, drainage, condensation, higher construction costs for structural reinforcement (the roof must support the weight of soil), and limited natural light in deeper designs.

Earth sheltering has ancient precedents, from cave dwellings to the sod houses of the North American prairies and the yaodong cave houses of China's Loess Plateau. It saw renewed interest during the energy crises of the 1970s as part of the passive solar and sustainable architecture movements.
