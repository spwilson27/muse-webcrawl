Far from the Madding Crowd

Far from the Madding Crowd (1874) is Thomas Hardy's fourth novel and his first major literary success. It originally appeared anonymously as a monthly serial in the Cornhill Magazine, where it gained a wide following. The title comes from Thomas Gray's poem "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" (1751): "Far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife". The novel is the first of Hardy's works set in his fictional Wessex.

Plot summary

Gabriel Oak, a young sheep farmer, loses his flock when his sheepdog drives them over a cliff and is reduced to working as a hired shepherd. He is employed by Bathsheba Everdene, a spirited young woman who has inherited her uncle's farm at Weatherbury and who once rejected Oak's proposal of marriage. Bathsheba impulsively sends a valentine to the respectable middle-aged farmer William Boldwood, who falls desperately in love with her; she is then swept off her feet by the dashing Sergeant Francis Troy, whom she marries. Troy proves a gambler and a flirt; his former lover Fanny Robin dies in childbirth with Troy's child, and Troy, presumed drowned after disappearing, returns on the night Boldwood's Christmas party, where the unhinged Boldwood shoots him dead. Boldwood is imprisoned, and Bathsheba at last marries the faithful Gabriel Oak.

Themes

The novel contrasts Oak's patient, skilful stewardship of the land with the vanity and impulsiveness that bring suffering to the other characters. Hardy depicts rural labour — sheep-shearing, the hiring fair, the storm in which Oak saves the harvest — with detailed knowledge, while the pastoral setting is shadowed by chance, desire and social rigidity. Bathsheba is an unusually independent heroine for Victorian fiction, managing her own farm and market dealings in a male world.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted many times, notably John Schlesinger's 1967 film with Julie Christie as Bathsheba and Terence Stamp as Troy, and Thomas Vinterberg's 2015 film starring Carey Mulligan and Matthias Schoenaerts. It has also been adapted for radio, television and the stage.
