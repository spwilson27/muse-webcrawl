In cosmology, galaxy filaments are the largest known structures in the universe, consisting of walls of gravitationally bound galactic superclusters. These massive, thread-like formations can commonly reach 50 to 80 megaparsecs — and in exceptional cases far more — and form the boundaries between large voids in the universe. Together, filaments, walls, clusters, and voids make up the foamy cosmic web.

Formation

Filaments form under the influence of gravity acting on small density fluctuations in the early universe, as described in the ΛCDM model. Collapse proceeds fastest along the shortest axis of a collapsing region, first producing flattened "pancakes" (walls), then filaments where walls intersect, and finally clusters at the densest nodes. Most of the universe's dark matter and a large share of its ordinary matter reside in filaments; gas falling along them shocks and heats, feeding galaxy clusters and fuelling star formation in the galaxies strung along the threads.

Discovery and notable structures

Redshift surveys beginning in the 1980s — notably the CfA surveys of Margaret Geller and John Huchra — first revealed the web-like arrangement, including the CfA2 Great Wall (1989), roughly 750 million light-years long. Later surveys (2dF, Sloan Digital Sky Survey) found the Sloan Great Wall (2003), about 1.4 billion light-years in extent, and still larger claimed structures such as the Huge-LQG (2012) and the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, whose sizes strain the expectation that the universe should appear homogeneous on the largest scales. Our own Milky Way belongs to the Laniakea Supercluster, which lies within the Pisces–Cetus Supercluster Complex, a filament about a billion light-years long.
