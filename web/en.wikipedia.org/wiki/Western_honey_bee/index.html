Western honey bee
Scientific name: Apis mellifera
Order: Hymenoptera
Family: Apidae
Origin: Europe, Africa and western Asia

The western honey bee (Apis mellifera) is the most common of the honey bee species worldwide and the species principally kept in beekeeping. Native to Europe, Africa and western Asia, it has been introduced to every continent except Antarctica and is the world's most important managed pollinator.

Biology and society

Honey bees are eusocial insects living in colonies of tens of thousands, each centred on a single fertile queen, with female workers performing all colony tasks and male drones produced seasonally. Workers communicate the location of forage through the waggle dance, decoded by Karl von Frisch. Foragers collect nectar, which is converted into honey, and pollen, a protein source; bees pollinate roughly one-third of human food crops as they forage.

Subspecies and threats

More than 30 subspecies are recognised, including the Italian bee (A. m. ligustica), the Carniolan (A. m. carnica) and the Africanised "killer bee", a hybrid with A. m. scutellata that spread through the Americas after 1957. Colonies face threats from the parasitic mite Varroa destructor, viruses, pesticides and habitat loss, and beekeepers report elevated losses labelled colony collapse disorder since the mid-2000s.
