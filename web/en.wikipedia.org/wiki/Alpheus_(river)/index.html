The Alpheus or Alfeios (Greek: Αλφειός) is the longest river of the Peloponnese peninsula in Greece, at about 110 kilometres (68 mi) long. It rises in the mountains of Arcadia, in the central Peloponnese, flows westward through the plain of Elis past the ancient sanctuary of Olympia, and empties into the Ionian Sea south of Pyrgos.

Course

The river begins near the Arcadian town of Megalopoli, fed by springs and tributaries from the surrounding uplands, and flows northwest before turning west. In its middle course it passes close to ancient Olympia, where the sanctuary of Zeus stood between the Alpheus and its tributary the Kladeos. At the coast, near the modern village of Epitalio, the river reaches the Ionian Sea, forming a small delta and wetland system; the artificial Lake Ladon lies on a major tributary of the same name.

History and mythology

In Greek mythology the river was personified as the god Alpheus, famed for his pursuit of the nymph Arethusa to Sicily. Ancient writers noted stretches where the river disappears underground — karstic sinkholes in Arcadia — which fed the legend that it flowed beneath the sea to rise again in the spring of Arethusa at Syracuse. The river's waters were also said by Pausanias to be the only ones acceptable for a paste applied to the statue of Zeus at Olympia. Heracles was believed to have diverted the Alpheus to cleanse the stables of Augeas.

In the modern era the river supports irrigation in Elis and Arcadia and hydroelectric generation along the Ladon tributary, and its lower basin is an important habitat for migratory birds.
