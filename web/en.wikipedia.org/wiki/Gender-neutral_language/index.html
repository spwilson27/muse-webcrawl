Gender-neutral language

Gender-neutral language or gender-inclusive language is language that avoids bias towards a particular sex or social gender. In English, gender-neutral language use involves avoiding words that may be considered to have a masculine bias, such as the generic use of "man" and "he", in favour of words that are not explicitly marked for gender, such as "people", "they", and "chairperson" rather than "chairman".

History

Efforts to reform gendered language in English gathered momentum in the 1970s alongside the feminist movement. Writers and style guides began recommending alternatives to generic masculine terms, arguing that language both reflects and shapes social attitudes towards women and other groups. Many major publishers, universities, and government bodies subsequently adopted guidelines promoting gender-inclusive usage.

Strategies

Common strategies for gender-neutral writing include:

  • Replacing gendered occupational titles, such as "fireman" with "firefighter" and "policeman" with "police officer".
  • Using the singular they as a gender-neutral pronoun.
  • Pluralising sentences so that plural pronouns can be used.
  • Rewriting sentences to avoid pronouns altogether.
  • Using paired forms such as "he or she" where a pronoun is unavoidable.

In other languages

In languages with grammatical gender, such as Spanish, French, and German, gender-neutral reforms are more complex because nouns, adjectives, and articles are themselves gendered. Proposals include new endings, such as the Spanish "-e" or the use of the @ symbol and "x" (as in "Latinx"), though these remain contested. In German, forms such as the gender star (Genderstern) have been proposed to include non-binary people.

Criticism

Critics of gender-neutral language reform argue that language change should occur naturally rather than by prescription, that some proposed forms are awkward or unpronounceable, and that generic masculine terms were traditionally understood as gender-inclusive. Supporters respond that language conventions do change over time and that inclusive forms are already common in formal writing.
