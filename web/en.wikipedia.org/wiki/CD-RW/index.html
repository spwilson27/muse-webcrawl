CD-RW

Media type: Optical disc
Capacity: Typically 650–700 MB
Standard: Orange Book Part III
Introduced: 1997

CD-RW (Compact Disc-ReWritable) is a digital optical disc storage format introduced in 1997. A CD-RW disc can be written, read many times, erased and re-written repeatedly, unlike the write-once CD-R. The format was defined in Part III of the "Orange Book" standards for recordable compact discs.

Technology

CD-RW media use a phase-change alloy recording layer, typically a silver–indium–antimony–tellurium alloy, sandwiched between dielectric layers. A laser heats the layer to change it between crystalline and amorphous states, which have different reflectivities; heating to a lower temperature restores the crystalline state and erases the data. Because the reflectivity is lower than that of pressed CDs or CD-Rs, CD-RW discs require drives with automatic gain control ("MultiRead" capability) to be read reliably, and many older audio CD players cannot play them. Discs are generally rated for about 1,000 rewrite cycles.

Speeds

CD-RW media are sold in speed classes: the original 1×–4× "normal speed", "High Speed" (4×–12×), "Ultra Speed" (16×–24×) and "Ultra Speed+" (up to 32×) discs, each requiring a compatible drive. Packet writing software allowed discs to be used in a floppy-like manner with formats such as the Universal Disk Format (UDF).

Legacy

CD-RW was widely used for data backup, file transfer and audio compilation in the late 1990s and 2000s, before being largely displaced by USB flash drives, recordable DVDs and cloud storage.
