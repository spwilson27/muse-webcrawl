Carpetbagger

In post–Civil War United States history, a carpetbagger was a Northerner who moved to the Southern United States after the American Civil War, during the Reconstruction era. The term originated from the perception that these arrivals carried their belongings in carpet bags (travel bags made from carpeting material) and came with the intent of profiting from the economic and political disorder in the South.

Southern Democrats widely used the term to describe white Northern newcomers, whom they depicted as low-grade opportunists. In use since the 1860s, the word is still an epithet for outsiders perceived as exploiting local communities, although it has been adopted in some fields, including the modern carpet industry, whose executives displaced by corporate takeovers were ironically dubbed "carpetbaggers."

Background

The Republican Party in the South after the Civil War was a coalition of three groups: freedmen (formerly enslaved African Americans), who were the main target of white Southern hostility; "scalawags," Southern whites who supported Reconstruction; and carpetbaggers, of whom about 20 percent were Black.

Motives and demography

Despite negative stereotyping, carpetbaggers included teachers, ministers, Union Army veterans, Freedmen's Bureau agents, businessmen, and investors. Many were motivated by idealism and a desire to help rebuild the South and protect freedmen's rights, while others did seek economic or political gain. They became a major voting bloc in Southern Republican governments, holding offices at local, state, and congressional levels.

Decline

As Reconstruction governments fell to Democratic "Redeemer" regimes in the 1870s, carpetbaggers lost influence; many returned North or adapted to the new order. The stereotype of the carpetbagger persisted in Southern folklore and textbooks for generations.
