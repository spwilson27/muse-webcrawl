Orbital mechanics

Orbital mechanics or astrodynamics is the application of ballistics and celestial mechanics to the practical problems concerning the motion of rockets and other spacecraft. The motion of these objects is usually calculated from Newton's laws of motion and universal gravitation. Orbital mechanics is a core discipline within aerospace engineering and space mission design.

Principles

In the two-body problem, a spacecraft orbiting a much more massive body follows a conic section — ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola — with the central body at one focus, as described by Kepler's laws. The total specific orbital energy is conserved and equals:

ε = v²/2 − μ/r = −μ/(2a)

where v is speed, r is distance from the central body, μ is the standard gravitational parameter, and a is the semi-major axis. From this follows the vis-viva equation, which gives the speed at any point of the orbit.

Orbital maneuvers

Changes of orbit require changes in velocity (delta-v). The most fuel-efficient transfer between circular coplanar orbits is generally the Hohmann transfer, an elliptical path tangent to both orbits. Bi-elliptic transfers can be more efficient for very large changes in radius. Plane changes are among the most expensive maneuvers because the required delta-v grows with the spacecraft's speed. The Oberth effect states that a burn is most effective when performed at the highest speed, typically at periapsis.

Patched conics and perturbations

Interplanetary trajectories are often planned with the patched-conic approximation, treating the spacecraft as subject to one body's gravity at a time within its sphere of influence. Real orbits are perturbed by atmospheric drag, the non-spherical gravity of the central body (such as Earth's J2 oblateness, exploited deliberately in sun-synchronous orbits), third-body gravity, and solar radiation pressure, all of which astrodynamicists model for precise navigation.
