Seppuku (切腹, "cutting the belly"), sometimes referred to as harakiri (腹切り, a more colloquial term using the same characters in reverse order), is a form of ritual suicide that originated with Japan's samurai class. It consists of a short blade, usually a tantō, being plunged into the abdomen and drawn across from left to right, so that the intestines spill out. Under the samurai code of bushidō, seppuku was used voluntarily to die with honour rather than fall into the hands of enemies, to protest a decision, or to atone for failure; it was also imposed by feudal lords as a capital punishment for samurai, who were thereby allowed to die a death considered honourable rather than face execution.

Ritual

The full ceremony was highly formalised. The samurai bathed, dressed in a white kimono, and ate a final meal. He was seated on tatami mats before witnesses, with his blade placed on a small tray (sanbo) in front of him. A trusted attendant, the kaishakunin ("second"), stood behind him with a drawn katana; the second's role was to decapitate the samurai at the moment of the cut, sparing him prolonged agony and the dishonour of crying out. The blade was wrapped in paper so that the hands would not slip, driven into the left side of the abdomen, drawn to the right, and sometimes turned upward in a second cut (jūmonji giri). A variant performed without a second, or by cutting the throat, existed for battlefield deaths.

Female members of the samurai class practised a related rite called jigai, cutting the throat with a tantō after tying the knees together so the body would be found in a seemly posture.

History

The first recorded act of seppuku is attributed to the warrior Minamoto no Yorimasa in 1180, during the Genpei War; as his forces were defeated at the Battle of Uji, Yorimasa is said to have withdrawn to the Byōdō-in temple, written a poem on his war fan, and disembowelled himself rather than be captured. The practice spread among the warrior class during the Kamakura and Muromachi periods and was codified under the Tokugawa shogunate, which prescribed it as the standard capital penalty for samurai and regulated its ceremonial form.

Among the most famous historical cases are the forty-seven rōnin, who were ordered to commit seppuku in 1703 after avenging their lord Asano Naganori, and the generals of the Boshin War era. Following the Meiji Restoration, seppuku was abolished as a judicial punishment in 1873, though voluntary acts continued: the novelist Yukio Mishima performed seppuku in 1970 after a failed coup attempt, an event that shocked modern Japan.

Motives

Under bushidō, seppuku served several purposes: kanshi, suicide to protest a lord's decision; junshi, following one's lord in death (later banned by the Tokugawa); funshi, in grief or indignation; and tsumebara, the judicial form imposed as punishment. During the Second World War the ethic of death before surrender contributed to the kamikaze attacks and mass suicides of soldiers and civilians, though these were distinct from the formal ritual.
