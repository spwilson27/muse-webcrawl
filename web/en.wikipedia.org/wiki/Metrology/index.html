Metrology is the scientific study of measurement. It establishes a common understanding of units, crucial in linking human activities. Modern metrology has its roots in the French Revolution's political motivation to standardise units in France, when a length standard taken from a natural source was proposed. This led to the creation of the decimal-based metric system in 1795, establishing a set of standards for other types of measurements. Several other countries adopted the metric system between 1795 and 1875; to ensure conformity between the countries, the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures (BIPM) was established by the Metre Convention.

Definition and branches

The BIPM defines metrology as "the science of measurement, embracing both experimental and theoretical determinations at any level of uncertainty in any field of science and technology." Metrology is commonly divided into three overlapping subfields:

  • Scientific (or fundamental) metrology — the establishment of units of measurement, unit systems, and measurement standards, including the realisation of the SI base units from their definitions.
  • Applied, technical or industrial metrology — the application of measurement to manufacturing and other processes, ensuring the suitability and calibration of instruments.
  • Legal metrology — the regulatory requirements for measuring instruments and methods used in trade, health, safety, and environmental protection.

Measurement standards and traceability

A central concept is metrological traceability: a measurement result must be relatable to a reference — ultimately the SI — through a documented, unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the measurement uncertainty. National metrology institutes (such as NIST in the United States, PTB in Germany, and the NPL in the United Kingdom) maintain national standards and disseminate them to accredited calibration laboratories.

The SI and the 2019 redefinition

The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units: the second, metre, kilogram, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela. On 20 May 2019, the SI was redefined so that all units are based on fixed numerical values of fundamental constants — the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the Boltzmann constant, the Avogadro constant, the speed of light, the caesium hyperfine frequency, and the luminous efficacy of monochromatic radiation. The kilogram, formerly defined by the mass of a platinum–iridium artefact, is now realised through experiments such as the Kibble balance.

Uncertainty

Every measurement is accompanied by an estimate of its measurement uncertainty, evaluated following the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM). Uncertainty budgets account for instrument resolution, calibration, environmental conditions, and repeatability.
