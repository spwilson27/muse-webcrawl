The arcuate fasciculus is a bundle of association fibres (axons) in the brain that forms part of the superior longitudinal fasciculus. It arches around the Sylvian fissure, connecting the caudal temporal lobe and inferior parietal lobule with the frontal lobe. Classically it was described as linking Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus) with Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus), and was therefore central to early models of language.

Anatomy

Modern tractography divides the tract into a long (direct) segment connecting temporal cortex directly to Broca's region, and anterior and posterior indirect segments relayed through the inferior parietal lobule (Geschwind's territory). The left arcuate fasciculus is typically larger and more strongly lateralised than the right, in line with left-hemisphere language dominance.

Function

The arcuate fasciculus is the core of the dorsal language stream, supporting phonological processing, repetition, and the mapping of sound onto articulation. Damage was classically said to cause conduction aphasia, in which comprehension and spontaneous speech are relatively preserved but repetition of heard speech is severely impaired. Development of the tract in childhood parallels the maturation of language abilities, and its organisation has been compared across primates: the tract is greatly expanded in humans relative to chimpanzees and macaques, particularly in its temporal projections.
