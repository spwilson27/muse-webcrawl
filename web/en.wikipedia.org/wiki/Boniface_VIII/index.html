Pope Boniface VIII (Latin: Bonifatius PP. VIII; born Benedetto Caetani, c. 1230 – 11 October 1303) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 24 December 1294 until his death in 1303. Caetani was a learned canon lawyer whose pontificate asserted the fullest claims of papal supremacy of the medieval period, most famously in the bull Unam sanctam (1302).

Early life and election

Benedetto Caetani was born into a minor noble family at Anagni. He studied law at Bologna, served as a papal diplomat in France and England, and was made cardinal in 1281. He succeeded Celestine V, whose unprecedented abdication in 1294 — and subsequent imprisonment by Boniface, who feared a rival focus of loyalty — shadowed his reign; Celestine died in confinement in 1296.

Pontificate

Boniface proclaimed the first Roman Jubilee (Holy Year) in 1300, drawing enormous crowds of pilgrims to Rome and greatly enriching the Church. He founded the University of Rome La Sapienza in 1303 and intervened widely in European politics, feuding with the Colonna cardinals in Rome and confronting Edward I of England and, above all, Philip IV of France.

Conflict with Philip IV

The struggle with France began over clerical taxation (the bull Clericis laicos, 1296) and escalated with the arrest of the bishop of Pamiers. In Unam sanctam (1302), Boniface declared that "it is absolutely necessary for salvation that every human creature be subject to the Roman pontiff." Philip's minister Guillaume de Nogaret responded with charges of heresy and, in September 1303, led an armed band that seized the pope at Anagni — the famous "outrage of Anagni," during which Boniface was allegedly slapped by Sciarra Colonna. Freed by the townspeople after three days, he died in Rome a month later, on 11 October 1303. Dante, whose Florentine faction Boniface had opposed, placed him in the Divine Comedy's circle of simoniac popes.
