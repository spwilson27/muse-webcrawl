Punctuated equilibrium
Proposed byNiles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould
Year proposed1972
FieldEvolutionary biology, palaeontology
Key claimSpecies typically persist in morphological stasis for most of their duration; most change is concentrated in geologically rapid speciation events
Contrasts withPhyletic gradualism

Punctuated equilibrium is a theory in evolutionary biology which proposes that once a species appears in the fossil record, its population shows little net evolutionary change for most of its geological history — a state called stasis — and that significant evolutionary change is concentrated in rare, geologically rapid speciation events (cladogenesis). The theory was proposed in 1972 by palaeontologists Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould as an alternative to phyletic gradualism, the expectation that species evolve by slow, steady transformation of whole lineages.

History

Eldredge's doctoral work on the trilobite Phacops rana showed subspecies appearing abruptly in different regions rather than grading into one another, consistent with allopatric speciation in small peripheral populations. Gould's work on the land snail Poecilozonites showed a similar pattern. In their 1972 paper "Punctuated equilibria: an alternative to phyletic gradualism", Eldredge and Gould argued that the fossil record's characteristic pattern — abrupt appearance and prolonged stasis — is real biological signal, not merely an artefact of an incomplete record, as Darwin had supposed.

Mechanisms

The theory rests on Ernst Mayr's model of allopatric (peripatric) speciation: new species arise in small, geographically isolated populations where genetic drift and selection act quickly, in geological terms within thousands to tens of thousands of years — too fast and too localised to be captured typically by fossil sequences. Once established and spread, large populations are buffered against further change by gene flow, stabilising selection and habitat tracking, producing stasis. Later elaborations linked the theory to hierarchical views of evolution, including species selection.

Evidence and debate

Palaeontological studies have documented many cases of long-term stasis punctuated by rapid change, alongside others showing gradual trends; the relative frequency of the two patterns remains debated, and the theory's early claims that it overturned the modern synthesis were tempered — most evolutionary biologists regard punctuated equilibrium as a claim about the tempo and fossil expression of evolution, compatible with population genetics, rather than a new mechanism. The idea nevertheless transformed palaeontology by making stasis a phenomenon requiring explanation rather than absent data.
