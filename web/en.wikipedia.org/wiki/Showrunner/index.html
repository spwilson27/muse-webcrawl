Showrunner

A showrunner is the leading authority of a television series in the United States and Canada, and is almost always its head writer and executive producer. The showrunner has creative and management responsibility for a television series' production, combining the roles of writer, producer, and often series creator. The term came into common use in the late 20th century to distinguish the writer-producer who effectively runs a series from the often rotating directors who shoot individual episodes.

Role and responsibilities

The showrunner oversees all creative aspects of a series: breaking stories in the writers' room, assigning scripts, rewriting drafts, casting, budgets, and final approval of editing, music, and visual effects. Unlike film, where the director is usually the principal creative figure, television's production schedule — producing many episodes per season under tight deadlines — places the continuing creative authority in the producer-writer's hands. Most showrunners hold the title of executive producer, and many have overall deals with the producing studio.

Origins of the term

Trade magazine Variety is credited with popularizing "showrunner" in the early 1990s, though the role itself is as old as American television: figures such as Rod Serling on The Twilight Zone and Gene Roddenberry on Star Trek exercised showrunner-like authority decades earlier. The recognition of the showrunner as an auteur figure grew during the so-called Golden Age of Television of the 2000s, associated with creator-showrunners such as David Chase (The Sopranos), David Simon (The Wire), Matthew Weiner (Mad Men), Shonda Rhimes (Grey's Anatomy, Scandal), and Ryan Murphy (Glee, American Horror Story).

Outside the United States

In British television, the closest equivalent is often the lead writer or executive producer; series such as Doctor Who formalized the role in the 21st century under Russell T Davies and Steven Moffat, who were explicitly styled as showrunners. In other television industries, creative control may rest with a head writer, a director, or a commissioning network.
