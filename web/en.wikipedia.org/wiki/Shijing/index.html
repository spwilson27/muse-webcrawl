Classic of Poetry

The Classic of Poetry, also known as the Shijing, Book of Songs, Book of Odes, or simply The Odes, is the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry, comprising 305 works dating from the 11th to 7th centuries BCE. It is one of the Five Classics of Chinese literature traditionally said to have been compiled or edited by Confucius, and has been studied and memorised in China for over two millennia, remaining part of the classical education curriculum well into the 20th century.

Contents

The collection is traditionally divided into four parts. The Guofeng ("Airs of the States", 160 songs) are folk songs from fifteen regions of the Zhou realm, dealing with courtship, marriage, harvest, work, and grievances against rulers. The Xiaoya ("Lesser Court Hymns", 74 songs) and Daya ("Greater Court Hymns", 31 songs) are court poems — banquet songs, complaints of officials, and dynastic legends of the Zhou kings. The Song ("Hymns", 40 pieces) are sacrificial and temple hymns, including some of the oldest pieces, praising the ancestors of the Zhou, Lu, and Shang houses. The poems are predominantly written in four-character lines, employing rhyme, repetition, and the techniques later classified as fu (exposition), bi (comparison), and xing (evocative image, often drawn from nature, opening a song).

Transmission and commentary

Tradition held that the Odes were selected from some 3,000 songs by Confucius himself, though modern scholars regard the collection as having crystallised gradually through court and ritual use. During the Han dynasty, three early schools of interpretation (the Lu, Qi, and Han schools) were largely lost, and the surviving text derives from the Mao school, whose commentary — the Maoshi prefaces attributed in part to Wei Hong — read the poems allegorically as moral and political instruction, sometimes far removed from their apparent sense. Zheng Xuan's commentary and later Kong Yingda's subcommentary fixed the received interpretation for the imperial era, while Song-dynasty scholar Zhu Xi reread many of the "airs" as genuine folk love songs. The traditional system of quoting the Odes also played a role in diplomacy: in Spring and Autumn period statecraft, envoys conveyed delicate messages by reciting appropriate stanzas.

Influence

The Shijing set foundational patterns for Chinese poetry — its imagery, diction, and four-character metre influenced literature for three thousand years — and its songs remain a primary source for the social history, religion, agriculture, and language of early China. Many of its lines survive as set phrases (chengyu) in modern Chinese, and the collection has been translated into English repeatedly, including versions by Arthur Waley (1937) and Ezra Pound.
