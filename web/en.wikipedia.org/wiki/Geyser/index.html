Geyser

A geyser is a spring characterised by an intermittent discharge of water ejected turbulently and accompanied by steam. As a fairly rare phenomenon, the formation of geysers is due to particular hydrogeological conditions that exist only in a few places on Earth. Generally all geyser field sites are located near active volcanic areas, and the geyser effect is due to the proximity of magma. Surface water works its way down to an average depth of around 2,000 metres (6,600 ft), where it contacts hot rocks. The resultant boiling of the pressurised water results in the geyser effect of hot water and steam spraying out of the geyser's surface vent.

Formation and mechanism

Geysers require three ingredients: abundant water, an intense heat source, and a special plumbing system of constrictions. Water percolating into the conduit system is heated by magma; because of the pressure in the depths, the water can exceed its surface boiling point without boiling (superheating). Eventually rising bubbles and expansion displace water from the vent, pressure drops, and the superheated water flashes to steam, driving the eruption. Afterward the system recharges and the cycle repeats. The word "geyser" derives from Geysir, the famous Icelandic spring in Haukadalur, from the Icelandic verb geysa, "to gush".

Distribution

Over a thousand geysers are known worldwide, roughly half of them in Yellowstone National Park in the United States, which hosts the world's tallest active geyser, Steamboat Geyser, and the famous predictable Old Faithful. Other major fields include the Valley of Geysers on Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula, El Tatio in Chile, the Taupō Volcanic Zone of New Zealand, and several fields in Iceland. Iceland's Great Geysir, documented since 1294, gave all geysers their name, though it is now mostly dormant; nearby Strokkur erupts every few minutes.

Biology and human impact

Geyser basins host thermophilic bacteria and archaea that form colourful microbial mats and thrive in near-boiling water; enzymes from Yellowstone's hot-spring microbes enabled the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) used in DNA analysis. Geysers are fragile: earthquakes can change their behaviour, and throwing objects into vents, geothermal energy extraction, or vandalism has destroyed or diminished several. Many historic geysers in New Zealand were lost under hydroelectric reservoirs.

Extraterrestrial geysers

Geyser-like water-vapour plumes occur elsewhere in the Solar System, notably on Saturn's moon Enceladus and Jupiter's moon Europa, and nitrogen geysers have been observed on Neptune's moon Triton.
