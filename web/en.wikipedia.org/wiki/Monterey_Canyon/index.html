Monterey Canyon is one of the largest submarine canyons in the world. It begins at Moss Landing, at the centre of the Monterey Bay shoreline, and extends horizontally about 400 km seaward, cutting across the continental shelf, slope and rise. The canyon's walls reach heights of about 1,700 m, and its floor descends to more than 3,600 m below sea level — a depth comparable to that of the Grand Canyon.

Formation

The canyon's origins are debated. It is thought to have been carved partly by an ancient course of a major river — possibly the ancestral Colorado or a paleo-Salinas River — when sea levels were lower, and to be maintained today by turbidity currents: sediment-laden avalanches of water that periodically rush down its axis and transport sand and mud to a deep-sea fan. The canyon's position has also shifted over geological time with movement along the San Andreas Fault system and the uplift of the region's granitic basement.

Oceanography and biology

Monterey Canyon is a focus of intensive research by the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI), whose remotely operated vehicles have explored its ecosystems since the 1990s. Nutrient-rich upwelling along the canyon supports extraordinary biodiversity, from cold-water coral gardens and sponge fields on its walls to the krill concentrations that draw blue whales into Monterey Bay. Sediment transport events recorded by instruments on the canyon floor have documented turbidity currents moving heavy equipment hundreds of metres.
