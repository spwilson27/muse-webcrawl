Wood finishing

Wood finishing refers to the process of refining or protecting a wooden surface, especially in the production of furniture. Finishing is the final step of the manufacturing process that gives wood surfaces desirable characteristics, including enhanced appearance and increased resistance to moisture and other environmental agents.

Purposes

A finish serves three main purposes: protection (against water, heat, chemicals, and wear), sanitation (sealed surfaces are easier to clean), and aesthetics (enhancing the grain and colour of the wood, or changing its appearance with stain and paint).

Surface preparation

Before finishing, the surface is sanded through progressively finer abrasive grits, cleaned of dust, and sometimes bleached, filled, or stained. Grain filling is used on open-pored woods such as oak and mahogany when a perfectly smooth finish is desired.

Types of finish

  • Oil finishes — linseed oil, tung oil, and Danish oil penetrate the wood and harden within it, giving a low-sheen, close-to-the-wood feel.
  • Varnish — a resin (historically natural, now often polyurethane) dissolved in a solvent, forming a hard, durable film.
  • Shellac — a natural resin secreted by the lac insect, dissolved in alcohol; prized for French polishing.
  • Lacquer — fast-drying film finishes, including nitrocellulose and modern water-based and catalysed lacquers.
  • Wax — paste waxes of beeswax and carnauba, usually applied over another finish.
  • Paint — opaque coatings that conceal the grain entirely.

Application methods

Finishes are applied by brush, rag ("wiping"), spray gun, or pad. French polish is a traditional hand technique for shellac using a rubber of wadding wrapped in cloth. Industrial finishing uses spray booths, curtain coaters, and UV-cured coatings.
