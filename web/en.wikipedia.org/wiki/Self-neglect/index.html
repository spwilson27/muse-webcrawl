Self-neglect

Self-neglect is a behavioural condition in which an individual neglects to attend to their own basic needs, such as personal hygiene, appropriate clothing, feeding, medical care, and maintaining a safe living environment. It is most often described in older adults and is a frequent reason for referral to adult safeguarding and social services.

Features and causes

Self-neglect may manifest as malnutrition or dehydration, poor hygiene and untreated wounds or illness, wearing unsuitable clothing, living in squalor with accumulated clutter, refuse, or animal hoarding, and refusing help from family, carers, or professionals. The extreme form is sometimes called Diogenes syndrome or senile squalor syndrome, though the terms are not synonymous. Causes and risk factors include dementia and other cognitive impairment, depression, schizophrenia and other mental illness, substance misuse, frailty, bereavement, social isolation, and executive dysfunction from frontal-lobe disease. Intentional self-neglect — where a person with mental capacity chooses a lifestyle others regard as neglectful — is distinguished from cases where illness prevents self-care, and raises ethical questions about autonomy.

Assessment and intervention

Assessment involves evaluating mental capacity, nutrition, health risks, home safety, and the person's own wishes. Interventions are usually multi-agency, involving GPs, district nurses, occupational therapists, social workers, housing and environmental health services, and sometimes mental-health teams or the fire service. In England, self-neglect is recognised under the Care Act 2014 as a category within safeguarding adults work, though practitioners must balance protection against the person's right to make unwise decisions. Outcomes are often poor when engagement is refused; gradual trust-building and practical help — rather than abrupt clearance of the home, which can be traumatic — are generally recommended.
