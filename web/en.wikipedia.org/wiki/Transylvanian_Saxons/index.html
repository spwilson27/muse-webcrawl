Transylvanian Saxons

The fortified church of Biertan, a hallmark of Saxon Transylvania.

The Transylvanian Saxons (German: Siebenbürger Sachsen; Hungarian: Erdélyi szászok; Romanian: Sași) are a people of mainly German ethnicity who settled in Transylvania (then part of the Kingdom of Hungary, now in Romania) in various waves, starting in the mid-12th century and continuing for centuries. Their traditional territory, the Königsboden ("Royal Soil"), lay in southern and northeastern Transylvania around cities such as Sibiu (Hermannstadt), Brașov (Kronstadt), Mediaș, Sighișoara (Schäßburg) and Bistrița (Bistritz).

History

Invited by King Géza II of Hungary to colonise and defend the frontier, the settlers — despite their name, drawn largely from the Moselle and Rhine regions — received extensive privileges confirmed in the Diploma Andreanum of 1224. They founded towns and built the famous fortified churches of the region, now several of them UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Most became Lutheran in the Reformation, and the Saxons formed one of the three recognised "nations" of Transylvania (alongside the Hungarian nobility and the Székelys) under the Union of the Three Nations.

Decline and emigration

After the Second World War, many Transylvanian Saxons were deported to the Soviet Union or expropriated; under Communist Romania, most of the remaining population emigrated to West Germany, often through payments negotiated by the German government. From some 750,000 before the war, the community in Romania had fallen to roughly 12,000 by the 2010s, though its churches, schools and cultural heritage remain central to the region's identity.
