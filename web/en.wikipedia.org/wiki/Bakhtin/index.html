Mikhail Mikhailovich Bakhtin (17 November 1895 – 7 March 1975) was a Russian philosopher, literary critic and scholar who worked on literary theory, ethics and the philosophy of language. His writings on the novel — especially the concepts of dialogism, heteroglossia, polyphony, the carnivalesque and the chronotope — became enormously influential in Western literary studies after his rediscovery in the 1960s and 1970s, and his ideas now permeate cultural studies, linguistics and anthropology.

Life

Born in Oryol to a family of the old nobility, Bakhtin studied classics at Odessa and St Petersburg. From 1918 he worked in Nevel and Vitebsk, where the informal "Bakhtin Circle" — including Valentin Voloshinov and Pavel Medvedev — debated language, Marxism and psychoanalysis; several books published under circle members' names in the 1920s (notably Voloshinov's Marxism and the Philosophy of Language) have been attributed wholly or partly to Bakhtin, a matter of continuing scholarly dispute. Arrested in 1929 for involvement with a religious-philosophical circle, he was exiled to Kazakhstan, where he worked as a bookkeeper. He taught for many years at the Mordovian Pedagogical Institute in Saransk, largely forgotten, until young Moscow scholars championed him in the 1960s; his revised Dostoevsky book (1963) and his Rabelais study (published 1965) restored his reputation. He died in Moscow in 1975.

Ideas

Dialogism and heteroglossia

For Bakhtin, meaning is created in dialogue: every utterance responds to earlier utterances and anticipates a response. Language in real life is stratified into competing social voices — heteroglossia — and the novel, his privileged genre, orchestrates these voices rather than imposing a single authorial monotone.

Polyphony

In Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics (1929; revised 1963), Bakhtin argued that Dostoevsky created the polyphonic novel, in which characters are not objects of the author's finalising word but independent consciousnesses with their own unmerged voices and ideas.

Carnival

Rabelais and His World analysed the medieval and Renaissance culture of carnival: festive laughter, the grotesque body, feasting and the temporary suspension and inversion of hierarchy, in which the people's "second life" mocked official seriousness and fear.

Chronotope

The chronotope ("time-space") denotes the intrinsic connectedness of temporal and spatial relationships in literature — the road, the salon, the provincial town — which organises narrative and defines genres.

Legacy

Translated widely from the 1970s onward, Bakhtin's work reshaped narratology, postcolonial theory, performance studies and the philosophy of language, and dialogic concepts drawn from him appear in fields as distant as education and psychotherapy.
