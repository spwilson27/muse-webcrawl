Fallibilism

Charles Sanders Peirce formulated fallibilism in the 19th century

Fallibilism is the philosophical principle that propositions concerning empirical knowledge can be accepted even though they cannot be proved with certainty, and that, in principle, any of one's beliefs might turn out to be mistaken. The doctrine was formulated by the American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce in the late 19th century and is a cornerstone of his pragmatism.

Peirce's doctrine

Peirce held that "our knowledge is never absolute but always swims, as it were, in a continuum of uncertainty and of indeterminacy." Fallibilism was for him both a description of the human epistemic condition — our perceptual judgements, measurements and inferences are all subject to error — and a methodological prescription: inquiry should remain self-correcting, treating conclusions as provisional and open to revision by further experience. He contrasted this attitude with the search for indubitable foundations associated with Cartesian philosophy.

Later developments

Karl Popper's philosophy of science is strongly fallibilist: scientific theories can never be verified, only falsified, and knowledge grows by conjecture and refutation. In contemporary epistemology, fallibilism about knowledge is the widely held view that a person can know a proposition even though their evidence does not guarantee its truth; its opposite, infallibilism, ties knowledge to certainty. Fallibilism also underpins arguments for intellectual humility, free inquiry and the open society, since if any belief may be wrong, institutions should protect criticism and revision.
