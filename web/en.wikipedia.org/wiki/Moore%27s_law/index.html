Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit doubles approximately every two years. It is named after Gordon Moore, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, who proposed the trend in a 1965 paper and revised the doubling period to about two years in 1975. Although often called a law, it is an empirical observation and projection rather than a physical law, and it became a guiding target for the semiconductor industry's research and development planning.

History

Moore's original 1965 article, 'Cramming more components onto integrated circuits', noted that the number of components per chip had doubled every year since 1959 and predicted the trend would continue for at least a decade. In 1975 he revised the doubling interval to approximately two years, the form in which the observation is usually quoted.

For decades the trend held remarkably well, tracking the progress from thousands of transistors in early microprocessors such as the Intel 4004 (1971) to tens of billions in modern processors.

Self-fulfilling character

Because chipmakers, equipment suppliers and software developers planned their roadmaps around the expected doubling, Moore's law functioned partly as a self-fulfilling prophecy and an industry coordination device. The International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors explicitly used it to set targets for feature sizes and manufacturing capability.

Limits and slowing

Physical limits — including transistor sizes approaching atomic scales, leakage currents and heat dissipation — have slowed the classic scaling described by Moore's law and by Dennard scaling, which ended around 2005. The industry has responded with multicore designs, three-dimensional transistor structures such as FinFETs and gate-all-around devices, chiplet architectures and specialised accelerators, keeping effective performance growth going even as simple transistor scaling has become harder.
