Drinking culture


Social drinking in a British pub.

Drinking culture is the set of traditions, rituals and social behaviours surrounding the consumption of alcoholic beverages within a society. Because alcohol is used across cultures for celebration, bonding, ceremony and relaxation, the norms governing who may drink, what, where, when and how much vary enormously.

Social functions

Alcohol commonly serves as a social lubricant. Toasting rituals, rounds-buying in pubs, communal drinking vessels and drinking songs structure social interaction and affirm group membership. In business cultures such as those of Japan and South Korea, after-hours drinking parties (such as the Japanese nomikai) have traditionally been venues for building trust outside workplace hierarchy. Pubs, taverns, beer gardens and cafés act as "third places" between home and work.

Variation by culture

Anthropologists commonly contrast "wet" cultures, where alcohol is integrated into daily life and meals (for example around the Mediterranean), with "dry" cultures where drinking is concentrated in episodic bouts, sometimes associated with higher rates of intoxication. Religious norms also shape drinking: Islam proscribes alcohol, while wine plays ritual roles in Judaism and Christianity, and rice wine in East Asian ancestor rites.

Problems and responses

Heavy drinking cultures face burdens of alcoholism, binge drinking, drink-driving and alcohol-related violence. Responses have included temperance movements, prohibition (as in the United States from 1920 to 1933), minimum legal drinking ages, taxation and, more recently, sober-curious movements and the growth of low- and no-alcohol drinks.
