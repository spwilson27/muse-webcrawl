Clydesdale horse

Clydesdale
Country of origin: Scotland
Use: Draught, showing
Height: 163–183 cm (16–18 hands)
Colour: Usually bay, also black, brown, chestnut; white markings common

The Clydesdale is a Scottish breed of draught horse. It takes its name from its area of origin, the Clydesdale — the valley of the River Clyde in Lanarkshire. The breed was developed in the 18th and 19th centuries for agricultural work and for hauling heavy loads, including coal and freight in cities.

Characteristics

Clydesdales are large horses, typically standing 16 to 18 hands high and weighing 820–1,040 kg. They are distinguished by profuse feathering — long hair covering the lower legs and hooves — and by frequent white markings on the face and legs. The most common colour is bay, though black, brown, and roan also occur.

History and use

The Clydesdale Horse Society was formed in 1877. The breed was exported worldwide, including to Australia and North America, where it was used in forestry and farming; in Australia the breed is sometimes called "the breed that built Australia". Numbers declined with mechanisation after the Second World War, and the breed is listed as at risk by the Rare Breeds Survival Trust. Clydesdales are famously used as the Budweiser Clydesdales hitch teams in the United States, and are still shown in harness and used for logging and promotional work.
