Chromogenic print

A chromogenic print made from a colour negative

A chromogenic print, also known as a C-print or C-type print, is a photographic print made from a colour negative, transparency (slide), or digital image using a chromogenic printing process. Chromogenic prints are composed of three emulsion layers, each sensitive to a primary colour of light, in which dye couplers form cyan, magenta, and yellow dyes during development. For most of the late twentieth century, the chromogenic process was the dominant method of producing colour photographic prints, both in commercial minilabs and in professional darkrooms.

Process

Chromogenic paper consists of silver halide emulsion layers sensitised to red, green, and blue light, each containing dye couplers corresponding to the complementary colours cyan, magenta, and yellow. When the paper is exposed through a colour negative and processed in a colour developer, oxidised developer reacts with the couplers to form dyes in proportion to the silver image; the silver is then bleached and fixed away, leaving only the dye image. The standardised processing chemistry is known as RA-4 for prints from negatives.

History

The chromogenic principle derives from research in the early twentieth century, including the work of Rudolf Fischer, who patented dye-coupler development in 1912. Kodak introduced Kodacolor film in 1942, and chromogenic papers became widespread after the Second World War. Fujifilm and Agfa produced competing papers and chemistries. From the 1990s onward, digital exposure of chromogenic paper using laser and LED printers allowed digital image files to be printed as C-prints, a service offered by many professional laboratories.

Archival properties

Chromogenic prints are susceptible to fading: the dyes degrade with exposure to light and with time, typically shifting in colour balance as the dye layers fade at different rates, and older prints may develop an orange or reddish cast. Dark storage slows deterioration, and later paper formulations improved stability considerably. Many landmark colour photographs of the twentieth century exist as chromogenic prints, making their preservation a concern for museums and archives.
