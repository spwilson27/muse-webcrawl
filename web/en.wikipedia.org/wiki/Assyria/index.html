Assyria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Stone relief from Nineveh showing Assyrian soldiers and siege engines attacking a walled city]

Assyria was a major civilisation of the ancient Near East centred on the upper Tigris river in northern Mesopotamia (modern northern Iraq). Named for the city and god Ashur, it passed through Old (c. 2000–1364 BC), Middle (1363–912 BC) and Neo-Assyrian (911–609 BC) phases; in its final phase it became the largest empire the world had yet seen, ruling from Egypt to western Iran. The Assyrians were famed — and feared — as soldiers and administrators, and their palace reliefs and royal libraries preserve an unmatched record of the age.

The Neo-Assyrian Empire

From Ashurnasirpal II (r. 883–859 BC), who built a vast palace at Nimrud, Assyrian kings campaigned almost yearly, using professional armies, siege engines and mass deportation of conquered peoples to break resistance. Tiglath-Pileser III (r. 745–727 BC) reorganised the empire into provinces under royal governors. Sargon II founded a new capital at Dur-Sharrukin; his son Sennacherib moved it to Nineveh, destroyed rebellious Babylon in 689 BC, and besieged Jerusalem. Esarhaddon conquered Egypt in 671 BC, and Ashurbanipal (r. 668–627 BC) both campaigned from Thebes to Elam and assembled a great library of cuneiform tablets at Nineveh.

Fall

After Ashurbanipal's death civil wars weakened the empire; a coalition of Babylonians under Nabopolassar and Medes destroyed Assur in 614 BC and Nineveh in 612 BC. The last Assyrian army was defeated at Harran in 609 BC, ending Assyria as a state, though Assyrian identity and language survived in the region into modern times.
