Negative concord

Negative concord, popularly known as double negation, is a phenomenon of grammar in some languages in which multiple negative items occur in a single clause, but they express a single semantic negation. It is found in many varieties of English, including African American Vernacular English and many non-standard dialects, as well as in languages such as Spanish, Italian, French, Russian, Polish, Greek and others.

Examples

In Spanish, No vi a nadie ("I didn't see anyone", literally "I didn't see nobody") requires the negative nadie alongside the preverbal negative marker no. In standard English, by contrast, two negatives in a clause are traditionally interpreted as cancelling each other out, or are replaced by a negative plus an indefinite such as anyone.

Types

Linguists distinguish strict negative concord, in which the negative marker must appear even when the negative indefinite precedes the verb, from non-strict negative concord, in which the marker appears only when the indefinite follows the verb. Slavic languages generally show strict concord, while Romance languages such as Spanish and Italian show non-strict concord.

Negative concord should not be confused with the litotes-like use of two negatives to express a weakened positive, as in "not uncommon".
