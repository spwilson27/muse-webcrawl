The menorah (Hebrew: מְנוֹרָה, mənōrā) is a seven-branched candelabrum of pure gold described in the Hebrew Bible, made for the Tabernacle by the artisan Bezalel according to instructions given to Moses on Mount Sinai (Exodus 25:31–40). It stood in the Holy Place opposite the Table of Showbread, its lamps — kept burning from evening to morning with pure olive oil — tended by the priests. Successor menorahs stood in Solomon's Temple and in the Second Temple in Jerusalem.

Description

Exodus describes the menorah as hammered from a single talent of gold, with a central shaft and six branches curving out from its sides, ornamented with cups shaped like almond blossoms, bulbs and flowers. Rabbinic tradition and the first-century historian Josephus elaborated its dimensions and symbolism; Josephus interpreted the seven lamps as the seven planets, and later thinkers treated it as an emblem of divine light, wisdom — and, in modern readings, as resembling a stylised tree of life or burning bush.

History

The original menorah was carried through the wilderness and installed in the Tabernacle at Shiloh and later in the Temple in Jerusalem. The menorah's rekindling after the desecration of the Temple by Antiochus IV is bound up with the origins of Hanukkah — whose own nine-branched lamp, the hanukkiah, is a distinct object. After the Roman sack of Jerusalem in 70 CE, the Second Temple's menorah was carried in triumph to Rome, as depicted on the Arch of Titus, and placed in the Temple of Peace; its later fate — variously reported as taken by Vandals to Carthage, recovered to Constantinople, and lost — is unknown.

Symbolism and modern use

The menorah is among the most enduring symbols of Judaism and of the Jewish people, appearing on ancient synagogue mosaics, coins and tombstones. A seven-branched menorah forms the central device of the emblem of the modern State of Israel, flanked by olive branches, and a large bronze menorah based on the Arch of Titus relief stands outside the Knesset in Jerusalem.
