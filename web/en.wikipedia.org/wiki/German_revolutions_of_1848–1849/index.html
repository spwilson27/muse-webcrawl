German revolutions of 1848–1849

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The German revolutions of 1848–1849 were a series of loosely coordinated protests and rebellions across the states of the German Confederation, part of the wider revolutions of 1848 that swept Europe. Inspired by events in France and driven by demands for national unification, constitutional government, freedom of the press and assembly, and relief from economic hardship, the uprisings forced rulers to concede liberal reforms — but by mid-1849 conservative forces had regained control.

Course of events

In March 1848 ("the March Days"), demonstrations and barricade fighting in Berlin, Vienna and other capitals compelled princes to appoint liberal "March ministries" and promise constitutions. In May 1848 an elected national assembly, the Frankfurt Parliament, convened in St. Paul's Church to draft a constitution for a united Germany. Its work, the Frankfurt Constitution of 1849, proclaimed basic rights and offered the imperial crown to King Frederick William IV of Prussia — who refused it as a "crown from the gutter".

Failure and aftermath

Divided between advocates of a "greater Germany" including Austria and a Prussian-led "lesser Germany", and lacking an army of its own, the parliament collapsed as Prussian and Austrian troops crushed remaining uprisings in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden in the summer of 1849. Many disappointed liberals — the "Forty-Eighters" — emigrated, notably to the United States. Though the revolutions failed, they abolished most remaining feudal burdens, produced lasting constitutional gains in some states, and left national unification as the central question of German politics, achieved two decades later under Bismarck by very different means.
