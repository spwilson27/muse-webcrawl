Carbanion

A carbanion is an anion in which carbon carries a formal negative charge and an unshared pair of electrons; the carbon is trivalent (bonded to three other atoms) with eight valence electrons. Carbanions are conjugate bases of carbon acids and are important reactive intermediates and reagents in organic chemistry, acting as strong bases and nucleophiles. Familiar examples include the methyl anion (CH3−), enolates, acetylides, the cyclopentadienyl anion, and the carbanionic carbon of organolithium and Grignard reagents (whose carbon–metal bonds are strongly polarised toward carbon).

Geometry and stability

In the absence of conjugation, a carbanion is pyramidal, like ammonia, with the lone pair in an orbital of substantial s-character; increased s-character (as in sp-hybridised acetylide anions) stabilises the negative charge because the electrons are held closer to the nucleus. Carbanion stability therefore increases with the acidity of the conjugate acid (lower pKa): alkynes (pKa ≈ 25) form more stable anions than alkenes (≈ 44) or alkanes (≈ 50). Resonance stabilisation is powerful: enolates, benzyl anions, and the aromatic cyclopentadienyl anion are planar and delocalised. Inductive electron-withdrawing groups and adjacent carbonyls, nitriles, or sulfones also stabilise carbanions.

Formation and reactions

Carbanions are generated by deprotonation with strong bases (e.g., LDA, NaH, butyllithium), by metal–halogen exchange, by direct reaction of organic halides with metals (giving organolithiums and Grignard reagents), and by decarboxylation in special cases. They react as nucleophiles with electrophiles (alkylation, addition to carbonyls as in the aldol and Grignard reactions), as bases in eliminations, and in umpolung strategies where an acyl anion equivalent reverses normal carbonyl polarity. The acidity of C–H bonds adjacent to functional groups underlies much of carbonyl chemistry, including enolate formation and the Wittig and Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons olefinations (via phosphorus ylides, which are carbanion-like).

Historical note

The existence of carbanions was established in the early 20th century through studies of triphenylmethyl salts and the conductivity of organometallic compounds; the concept became central to mechanistic organic chemistry through the work of Christopher Ingold and others on reaction mechanisms.
