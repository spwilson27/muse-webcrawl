Giants (Greek mythology)

In Greek mythology, the Giants, also called Gigantes (Greek: Γίγαντες, Gígantes, singular Γίγας Gígas), were a race of great strength and aggression, though not necessarily of great size. They were born of Gaia (Earth), impregnated by the blood of the castrated Uranus (Sky), according to Hesiod's Theogony. The Giants are famous for the Gigantomachy, their war against the Olympian gods for supremacy of the cosmos, in which they were defeated with the help of the mortal hero Heracles.

The Gigantomachy on the Pergamon Altar frieze.

Origins and characteristics

Hesiod says that Gaia, impregnated by the blood of Uranus, bore the Giants along with the Erinyes and the Meliae. Later sources name individual Giants — among them Alcyoneus, Porphyrion, Enceladus, Polybotes, Pallas, Ephialtes and the Aloads — and give them varying parentage. In early Greek art the Giants appear as heavily armed hoplite warriors of human form; from about the late 5th century BC, and most famously on the great frieze of the Pergamon Altar (2nd century BC), they are depicted with serpent-like legs (anguipedes). The Giants are often confused with other large beings of myth, such as the Titans, the Cyclopes and the hundred-handed Hecatoncheires, but they are a distinct race.

The Gigantomachy

The Gigantomachy was one of the most frequently depicted subjects in Greek art, symbolising the victory of order (the Olympians) over chaos. An oracle had declared that the Giants could not be killed by gods alone but only with a mortal's aid, so Zeus enlisted Heracles. Gaia sought a magical herb (pharmakon) that would make the Giants invulnerable, but Zeus kept it from her by forbidding the sun and moon to shine until he found it himself. In the battle the gods each fought individual Giants: Zeus struck Porphyrion, Apollo and Heracles slew Ephialtes, Athena buried Enceladus beneath Sicily (whence his fiery breath was said to cause Mount Etna's eruptions) and flayed Pallas, and Poseidon crushed Polybotes beneath part of the island of Kos, creating Nisyros. Heracles finished off the wounded Giants with his arrows.

Interpretation

Like the Titanomachy, the war of the Giants expresses a cosmic succession myth and was interpreted politically in the classical period as an image of Greek victory over barbarism, notably after the Persian Wars; the Gigantomachy decorated the Parthenon's metopes and the shield of Athena Parthenos. In later tradition the Giants' defeat is sometimes located in the volcanic Phlegraean Fields of Italy, associating them with subterranean fire.
