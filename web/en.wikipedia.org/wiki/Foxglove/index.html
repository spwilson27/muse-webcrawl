Foxglove

KingdomPlantae
FamilyPlantaginaceae
GenusDigitalis
Speciesabout 20, including D. purpurea and D. lanata

Foxglove is the common name for plants of the genus Digitalis, a group of about twenty species of herbaceous perennials, shrubs, and biennials native to Europe, western Asia, and northwestern Africa. The best known is the common or purple foxglove, Digitalis purpurea, widely grown as an ornamental for its tall spikes of tubular, thimble-shaped flowers, usually purple, pink, or white with spotted throats.

Toxicity and medicine

All parts of the plant are poisonous, containing cardiac glycosides such as digitoxin and digoxin. In 1785 the physician William Withering published his Account of the Foxglove, documenting its use for dropsy (oedema due to heart failure), learned from folk medicine. The purified glycoside digoxin, obtained mainly from Digitalis lanata, remains in clinical use for heart failure and certain arrhythmias. Poisoning produces nausea, visual disturbances (including a yellow tinge to vision), and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias.

Ecology and cultivation

Digitalis purpurea is typically biennial, forming a rosette of soft, wrinkled leaves in its first year and flowering in its second. Its flowers are adapted to pollination by bumblebees, which crawl inside the bells. The plant has naturalised in many temperate regions beyond its native range and is a classic of cottage gardens.
