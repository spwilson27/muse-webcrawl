Counterintelligence
PurposeProtecting against foreign espionage and intelligence operations
ActivitiesDetection of spies, double agents, deception, security investigations
Key agenciesFBI (US), MI5 (UK), CSIS (Canada), ASIO (Australia)

Counterintelligence (also counter-intelligence, often abbreviated CI) is the activity of protecting a state, organisation or its own intelligence services against foreign espionage, sabotage, subversion and intelligence collection. It includes identifying and neutralising hostile spies, running double agents, feeding deception to adversaries, and securing personnel, information and installations against penetration.

Scope

Defensive counterintelligence focuses on security: vetting, classification, communications security and investigations of leaks and insider threats. Offensive counterintelligence seeks to penetrate hostile services, recruit or turn their officers, and manipulate their operations. In the United States, the FBI is the lead counterintelligence agency domestically, while the CIA's Counterintelligence Center handles foreign operations; in the United Kingdom the role belongs principally to MI5.

History

Modern counterintelligence grew with the professional spy services of the early twentieth century. During the Second World War, Britain's Double-Cross System turned every German agent in the country into a controlled channel of deception, feeding the Fortitude deception that protected the Normandy landings. The Cold War produced celebrated successes and notorious moles — Kim Philby inside British intelligence and Aldrich Ames and Robert Hanssen inside the CIA and FBI — whose betrayals drove major reforms. James Jesus Angleton, the CIA's long-serving counterintelligence chief, became emblematic of both the discipline's rigour and its risks of obsessive suspicion.

Methods

Techniques include surveillance, interrogation, polygraph screening, analysis of communications and travel, "dangles" and false-flag recruitment attempts, honeypots, and the systematic investigation of anomalies that may betray a penetration. Counterintelligence failures — missing a mole, or wrongly destroying careers through mole hunts — are among the most damaging episodes in intelligence history.
