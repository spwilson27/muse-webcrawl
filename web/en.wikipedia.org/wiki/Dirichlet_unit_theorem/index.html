Dirichlet unit theorem

In algebraic number theory, the Dirichlet unit theorem is a fundamental result describing the structure of the group of units in the ring of integers of a number field. Proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, it states that the unit group is a finitely generated abelian group consisting of a finite cyclic group of roots of unity together with a free abelian part of rank r₁ + r₂ − 1, where r₁ is the number of real embeddings of the field and r₂ the number of pairs of complex embeddings.

Statement and consequences

For the rationals the unit group of ℤ is just {±1}; for a real quadratic field the rank is 1, so the units are generated (up to sign) by a single fundamental unit, closely related to solutions of Pell's equation; for an imaginary quadratic field the rank is 0 and the unit group is finite. The regulator of the field, defined as a determinant of logarithms of a system of fundamental units, measures the size of the unit lattice and appears in the analytic class number formula.

Proof ideas

Standard proofs embed the units into Euclidean space via the logarithmic embedding and use Minkowski's theorem on convex bodies (the geometry of numbers) to produce units of bounded size and to show the logarithmic image is a full lattice.
