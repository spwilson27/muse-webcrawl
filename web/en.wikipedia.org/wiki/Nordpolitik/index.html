Nordpolitik

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nordpolitik (German for "North politics") was the foreign policy of South Korean president Roh Tae-woo, announced in 1988, which sought to improve relations with North Korea while simultaneously establishing diplomatic ties with the Soviet Union, China, and other socialist states traditionally aligned with Pyongyang.

The policy was modeled in part on West Germany's Ostpolitik of the 1970s. It marked a decisive break from the previous anti-communist posture of the Republic of Korea, which had refused formal contact with communist governments.

Background

When Roh Tae-woo took office in February 1988, South Korea remained without diplomatic relations with any communist state. The approaching 1988 Summer Olympics in Seoul gave Seoul an opportunity: many socialist nations would send delegations, and the South Korean government sought to use the Games as a bridge to the communist world.

Implementation

On 7 July 1988, Roh issued the Special Declaration for National Self-Esteem, Unification and Prosperity, calling for exchanges of goods, people, and information between the two Koreas and declaring that South Korea would no longer oppose its allies improving relations with North Korea. South Korea subsequently normalized relations with Hungary (1989), Poland (1989), Yugoslavia (1989), Czechoslovakia (1990), the Soviet Union (1990), and finally China (1992).

Nordpolitik contributed to both Koreas joining the United Nations simultaneously in September 1991 and to the signing of the Agreement on Reconciliation, Nonaggression and Exchanges and Cooperation (the Basic Agreement) in December 1991.

Legacy

Nordpolitik is generally considered to have set the stage for the later Sunshine Policy of president Kim Dae-jung. Critics argue that the economic benefits conferred on Pyongyang were not matched by concessions, but most historians regard the policy as a strategic success that ended South Korea's diplomatic isolation within the socialist bloc.
