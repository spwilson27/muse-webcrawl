Neuromuscular-blocking drug

Neuromuscular-blocking drugs, or neuromuscular blockers, are medications that block transmission at the neuromuscular junction, causing paralysis of skeletal muscles. They are used in anaesthesia to facilitate tracheal intubation and to optimise surgical conditions, and in intensive care to facilitate mechanical ventilation.

Classification

Blockers are divided by mechanism into depolarising agents, such as succinylcholine (suxamethonium), which activate then desensitise nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and non-depolarising agents, such as rocuronium, vecuronium and atracurium, which competitively antagonise those receptors. The original agents derive from curare, the South American arrow poison.

Clinical use

Depth of blockade is monitored with a peripheral nerve stimulator, commonly using the train-of-four pattern. Because blockers paralyse the muscles of respiration, ventilation must be supported. Residual paralysis after surgery is associated with respiratory complications.

Reversal

Non-depolarising blockade can be reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine (given with an antimuscarinic), or, for rocuronium and vecuronium, with sugammadex, which encapsulates the blocking molecule. Succinylcholine is broken down by plasma cholinesterase and is short acting.
