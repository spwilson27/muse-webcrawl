Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), was a landmark United States Supreme Court case in which the Court ruled that it is unconstitutional for state officials to compose an official school prayer and encourage its recitation in government schools, due to it violating the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.

Background

The New York State Board of Regents had composed a nondenominational prayer — "Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee, and we beg Thy blessings upon us, our parents, our teachers and our country" — and recommended that school districts adopt it as part of a "moral and spiritual training" programme. The school district in New Hyde Park, New York directed that the prayer be recited aloud each morning. Steven Engel and other parents challenged the practice.

Decision

Writing for the 6–1 majority, Justice Hugo Black held that government-directed prayer in public schools breached the constitutional wall of separation between church and state, regardless of the prayer's nondenominational character or the fact that students could be excused from participating. Justice Potter Stewart dissented, arguing that the Court had misapplied the Establishment Clause.

Legacy

The decision was controversial and was followed a year later by Abington School District v. Schempp (1963), which struck down mandatory Bible reading in public schools. Engel remains a foundational case in Establishment Clause jurisprudence and a recurring reference point in debates over religion in American public schools.
