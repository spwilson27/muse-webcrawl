Swashbuckler film

The climactic duel is the genre's signature set piece

A swashbuckler film is a genre of adventure film characterised by sword fighting, heroic protagonists, and romanticised historical settings — typically Europe of the 16th to 18th centuries, the age of musketeers, pirates, and highwaymen. The genre takes its name from the "swashbuckler", a swaggering swordsman who made his shield (buckler) ring with his sword.

History

The genre flourished first in the silent era with Douglas Fairbanks in The Mark of Zorro (1920) and Robin Hood (1922), whose athletic stunts set the template. Sound-era classics starred Errol Flynn in Captain Blood (1935), The Adventures of Robin Hood (1938), and The Sea Hawk (1940), with elaborate fencing choreographed by Fred Cavens and Ralph Faulkner and duels against Basil Rathbone, himself an expert fencer. The source material came from novels by Alexandre Dumas (The Three Musketeers), Rafael Sabatini, Baroness Orczy (The Scarlet Pimpernel), and Robert Louis Stevenson.

Decline and revivals

The genre waned in the 1950s as tastes changed, though Richard Lester's irreverent The Three Musketeers (1973) revived it, and later successes include The Princess Bride (1987), The Mask of Zorro (1998), and the Pirates of the Caribbean series (from 2003), which moved the swashbuckling duel to the Spanish Main.

Conventions

Staples include a charming, honourable hero; a hissable villain; a damsel or spirited heroine; duels on staircases, tables, and rigging; and a tone of wit and derring-do rather than gritty realism.
