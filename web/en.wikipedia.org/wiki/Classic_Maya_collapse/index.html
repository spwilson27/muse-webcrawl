Classic Maya collapse

The Classic Maya collapse refers to the decline and abandonment of the Classic Period Maya cities of the southern Maya lowlands of Mesoamerica during the 8th and 9th centuries CE, at the end of the Classic Period (c. 250–900 CE). Monument construction and inscriptions ceased city by city — the last Long Count date, at Toniná, corresponds to 909 CE — and populations in the southern lowlands fell dramatically. It was not the end of Maya civilisation: northern cities such as Chichen Itza flourished, and Maya peoples and culture persist to the present day.

Abandoned Maya temples were reclaimed by forest.

Evidence

Archaeologists trace the collapse through the cessation of carved stelae and dated monuments, the end of palace construction, and settlement surveys showing steep population decline in regions such as Guatemala's Petén. Climate records from lake sediments and cave speleothems indicate a series of severe multiyear droughts coinciding with the Terminal Classic decline.

Proposed explanations

No single cause commands consensus. Leading hypotheses include prolonged drought undermining rain-fed agriculture; intensifying warfare between rival kingdoms such as Tikal and Calakmul; overpopulation and soil exhaustion; the collapse of trade routes; peasant revolts against divine kingship; and combinations of these stresses. The collapse unfolded unevenly over more than a century, with some centres (Seibal, for example) briefly flourishing late, suggesting political and environmental factors interacted regionally.
