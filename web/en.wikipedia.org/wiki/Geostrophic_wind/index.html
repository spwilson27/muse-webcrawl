Geostrophic wind

Geostrophic wind
FieldMeteorology, atmospheric dynamics
Forces balancedPressure gradient force, Coriolis force
DirectionParallel to isobars

The geostrophic wind is the theoretical wind that results when the pressure gradient force on an air parcel is exactly balanced by the Coriolis force arising from the Earth's rotation. Under geostrophic balance the air flows parallel to the isobars (lines of constant pressure) rather than directly from high to low pressure.

Derivation

Air initially accelerates down the pressure gradient, from high toward low pressure. In a rotating frame the Coriolis effect deflects moving air — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. The deflection increases with wind speed until the Coriolis force, which acts at right angles to the motion, exactly opposes the pressure gradient force. The parcel then moves at a steady speed along the isobars.

The geostrophic wind speed is proportional to the pressure gradient and inversely proportional to the Coriolis parameter (and hence to the sine of latitude) and air density: where isobars are closely spaced, the geostrophic wind is strong; near the equator, where the Coriolis parameter approaches zero, geostrophic balance breaks down. In the Northern Hemisphere the wind blows with low pressure on its left — the rule of thumb known as Buys Ballot's law.

Applicability

Geostrophic balance is a good approximation for large-scale, slowly varying flow in the free atmosphere, above the friction layer and away from the equator — conditions under which friction, curvature and acceleration are small. In practice forecasters routinely estimate upper-air winds from the spacing of height contours on constant-pressure charts using the geostrophic relationship. Deviations from balance are captured by refinements such as the gradient wind (which adds the centrifugal effect of curved flow around highs and lows) and the thermal wind (which describes how the geostrophic wind changes with height in response to horizontal temperature gradients).

Oceanography

The same balance governs large-scale ocean currents: geostrophic currents flow along lines of constant pressure, and oceanographers calculate them from the slope of the sea surface and from density (temperature and salinity) profiles measured by hydrography and satellites.
