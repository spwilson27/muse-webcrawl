Amplitude modulation (AM) is a modulation technique used in electronic communication, most commonly for transmitting messages with a radio wave. In amplitude modulation, the amplitude (signal strength) of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the message waveform being transmitted, such as an audio signal, while its frequency and phase remain constant.

Principle

For a carrier of frequency fc and a sinusoidal message of frequency fm, the modulated signal contains three spectral components: the carrier and two sidebands at fc ± fm. Standard AM therefore occupies twice the bandwidth of the message. The depth of modulation is measured by the modulation index. Because the information is carried in the envelope of the wave, a simple diode envelope detector can demodulate it, which made early radio receivers cheap and simple — one reason AM dominated early broadcasting.

Uses and limitations

AM is still used for long-, medium-, and short-wave radio broadcasting, aviation voice communications, and citizens band radio. Its disadvantages are poor power efficiency (much of the transmitted power resides in the information-free carrier) and susceptibility to noise, since most natural and man-made noise is amplitude-based — the chief reason FM, which varies frequency instead, delivers higher-fidelity audio. Variants such as single-sideband (SSB) modulation suppress the carrier and one sideband to save power and bandwidth, at the cost of more complex receivers.
