Gharial

Scientific nameGavialis gangeticus
FamilyGavialidae
LengthMales 3–6 m
HabitatRivers of the northern Indian subcontinent
IUCN statusCritically Endangered

The gharial (Gavialis gangeticus), also known as the Indian gavial, is a large crocodilian native to freshwater rivers of the Indian subcontinent. It is distinguished by its extremely long, thin snout lined with sharp interlocking teeth — an adaptation for catching fish — and by the bulbous growth (the ghara, named after an Indian earthen pot) on the tip of adult males' snouts.

Distribution

Once widespread in the Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra and Mahanadi river systems, the gharial survives mainly in a few protected stretches of India and Nepal, notably the Chambal, Girwa, Rapti–Narayani, Karnali and Babai rivers.

Biology and behaviour

Gharials are the most thoroughly aquatic crocodilians and rarely leave the water except to bask and nest on sandbanks. They cannot lift their bodies clear of the ground like other crocodiles. Females lay 20–95 eggs in sand nests in the dry season; the species has temperature-dependent sex determination.

Conservation

Hunted for skins, trophies and traditional medicine, and harmed by fishing nets, sand mining and river engineering, gharial numbers crashed by an estimated 98% during the 20th century. India's Project Crocodile (from 1975) established captive breeding and reintroduction, but the species remains Critically Endangered, with only a few hundred breeding adults.
