Disturbance (ecology)

Regrowth after wildfire, a major natural disturbance in many ecosystems.

In ecology, a disturbance is a temporary change in environmental conditions that causes a pronounced change in an ecosystem. Disturbances often act quickly and with great effect, altering the physical structure or arrangement of biotic and abiotic elements. Examples include wildfires, hurricanes, floods, droughts, insect outbreaks and volcanic eruptions, as well as human activities such as logging and ploughing.

Effects

Disturbance can create opportunities for new organisms to colonise an area by removing dominant competitors and freeing resources. The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes that species diversity is highest when disturbance is neither too rare nor too frequent: too little disturbance allows competitive exclusion, while too much prevents all but the hardiest species from persisting. After a disturbance, ecosystems undergo ecological succession, a gradual process of recovery and change in species composition.

Disturbance regimes

Ecosystems are characterised by disturbance regimes — the spatial and temporal patterns of disturbance, including their frequency, intensity, extent and seasonality. Many species are adapted to particular regimes; for example, some pine species have serotinous cones that open only after fire. Changes to a regime, such as fire suppression or increased storm frequency under climate change, can transform ecosystems profoundly.
