Deverbal noun

A deverbal noun (also called a verbal noun, though usage of the terms varies) is a noun that is derived from a verb, typically by adding a suffix, and that denotes the action, process or result of the verb. English examples include construction from construct, decision from decide, writing from write, and zero-derived forms such as a walk from to walk. Deverbal nouns are a major word-formation type in many languages and sit at the centre of the linguistic study of nominalisation.

Formation

In English, common deverbal suffixes include -tion/-sion (Latinate: creation, expansion), -ment (development, agreement), -al (refusal, arrival), -age (breakage), -ance/-ence (performance) and -ing. The -ing forms straddle the boundary between noun and verb: fully nominal gerunds ("the singing of the anthem") take determiners and of-phrases, while participial uses retain verbal syntax. Other languages form deverbal nouns with rich morphology — Arabic's maṣdar, the verbal nouns of Celtic languages, and Japanese and Turkish nominalising suffixes are well-studied examples.

Semantics

Deverbal nouns are semantically diverse. A classic distinction, due to Barbara Grimshaw, separates complex event nominals, which denote events and preserve the argument structure of the base verb ("the destruction of the city by the army"), from result nominals, which denote the outcome or product ("the building is tall"). Agent and instrument nouns derived from verbs (writer, opener) are related formations, often treated alongside deverbals in morphology.
