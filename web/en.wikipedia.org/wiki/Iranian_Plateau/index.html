The Iranian plateau, also called the Persian plateau, is a geological formation in Southwest and Central Asia covering most of modern Iran together with parts of Afghanistan and western Pakistan. Formed by the collision of the Arabian plate with the Eurasian plate, it is ringed by mountain ranges — the Zagros to the west and south, the Alborz along the Caspian shore, and the Hindu Kush to the east — and its interior is dominated by high, arid basins.

Geography and climate

The plateau's interior contains two great salt deserts, the Dasht-e Kavir and the Dasht-e Lut, among the hottest and driest places on Earth. Most settlement concentrates along the mountain foothills, where qanats (underground aqueducts) tap groundwater; historic cities such as Isfahan, Shiraz, Yazd and Kerman grew up at such oasis sites on the plateau's edge.

History

The plateau was home to some of the earliest farming and urban societies in the region, including the Elamites of the southwest lowlands and the cultures of Jiroft and Shahr-e Sukhteh. It formed the heartland of successive empires — Median, Achaemenid, Parthian, Sasanian and the later Persian dynasties — and a crossroads of the Silk Road linking China, India, Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean. Because of its geography, historians treat "Greater Iran" as a cultural sphere extending across the plateau and beyond.
