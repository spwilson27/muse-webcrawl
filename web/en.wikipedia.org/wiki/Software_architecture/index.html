Software architecture is the set of structures needed to reason about a software system, comprising the system's elements, the externally visible properties of those elements, and the relationships among them. Architecture concerns the fundamental organisation of a system — its decomposition into components, their interfaces and interactions, and the principles guiding its design and evolution. Decisions made at the architectural level are typically the most consequential and the most difficult to change.

History

The concept emerged from research in the 1960s and 1970s, notably Edsger Dijkstra's work on structured programming and David Parnas's 1972 paper on modular decomposition, which argued that systems should be split according to design decisions likely to change. The term gained currency in the late 1980s and 1990s as Perry and Wolf's 1992 paper and the work of Mary Shaw and David Garlan — whose 1996 book Software Architecture: Perspectives on an Emerging Discipline codified the field — established architecture description languages, architectural styles, and analysis methods. The standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 now defines architecture description concepts internationally.

Architectural styles and patterns

An architectural style is a named collection of design decisions applicable to many systems. Widely used styles and patterns include:

  • Layered architecture — organising the system into layers (presentation, business logic, data) each using only the layer below.
  • Client–server and n-tier architectures.
  • Pipe-and-filter — data flowing through successive processing components, as in Unix pipelines and compilers.
  • Event-driven architecture and publish–subscribe messaging.
  • Model–view–controller (MVC) and related presentation patterns.
  • Microservices — decomposing a system into small, independently deployable services, contrasted with the monolith.
  • Service-oriented architecture (SOA), space-based, and peer-to-peer styles.

Quality attributes

Architecture is the primary vehicle for achieving quality attributes — non-functional requirements such as performance, availability, modifiability, security, scalability, and testability. These goals often conflict, and architectural work consists largely of negotiating trade-offs: for example, adding layers improves modifiability but may cost performance. Methods such as the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) systematise the evaluation of these trade-offs.

Views and documentation

Because no single diagram can capture a system, architectures are documented in multiple views, each addressing a stakeholder concern. Kruchten's "4+1" view model (logical, process, physical, development, plus scenarios) and the C4 model of Simon Brown are popular frameworks. Lightweight architecture decision records (ADRs) capture the rationale behind significant choices.

Role of the software architect

The software architect is responsible for high-level structure, technology selection, and ensuring the architecture supports business goals. Contemporary practice often distributes these duties among senior engineers, team-level "tech leads", and enterprise architects, with agile methods favouring emergent, incremental architecture over heavyweight up-front design.
