A spark discharge occurs when the electric field exceeds the dielectric strength of air, about 3 million volts per metre.

In physics, the term dielectric strength has the following meanings: for an insulating material, the maximum electric field that a pure material can withstand under ideal conditions without breaking down (i.e., without experiencing a failure of its insulating properties); and for a specific piece of dielectric material and location of electrodes, the minimum applied electric field that results in breakdown. The SI unit of dielectric strength is volts per metre (V/m).

Electrical breakdown

When the field across an insulator becomes strong enough, electrons are torn from their atoms or accelerated to energies that ionise further atoms in an avalanche, and the material suddenly becomes conductive — a breakdown spark in gases, or a puncture through a solid. Breakdown is influenced by impurities, defects, moisture, sample thickness, temperature and the duration and waveform of the applied voltage, so tabulated values are idealised figures measured under standard conditions.

Typical values

Dry air breaks down at about 3 MV/m; transformer oil at roughly 10–25 MV/m; common plastics and glass in the range of tens of MV/m; and thin films of materials such as silicon dioxide or mica can exceed 100 MV/m. Dielectric strength determines the insulation design of cables, capacitors, transformers and high-voltage transmission equipment.
