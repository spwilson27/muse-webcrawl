Shared lane marking

A shared lane marking, commonly called a sharrow (a portmanteau of share and arrow), is a road marking consisting of a bicycle pictogram combined with two chevrons. It indicates a lane that is shared by bicycles and motor vehicles, guiding cyclists to a safe lateral position and alerting drivers to expect bicycles in the lane.

Use and placement

Sharrows are used on streets where dedicated bicycle lanes cannot be provided, typically where the roadway is too narrow. Guidance in the United States, where the marking was standardised in the 2009 edition of the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices, places the symbol so that cyclists riding over it are outside the "door zone" of parked cars — at least about 3.3 metres (11 feet) from the kerb where parking is present. They are commonly placed on downhill streets where cyclists approach traffic speed, on gaps in bicycle-lane networks, and on designated bicycle boulevards.

Effectiveness

Studies of sharrows have found modest benefits: cyclists tend to ride slightly further from parked cars, and some cities report small reductions in wrong-way and pavement riding. However, sharrows provide no physical protection and are widely regarded by cycling advocates as among the weakest forms of bicycle facility, far less effective than separated lanes at increasing cycling rates or safety. The marking originated in Denver, Colorado, in the 1990s and has since been adopted, with variations, in Canada, Australia, Japan and parts of Europe.
