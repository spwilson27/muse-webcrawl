Nazi eugenics

Nazi eugenics refers to the eugenics policies of Nazi Germany, rooted in the regime's ideology of racial hygiene (Rassenhygiene). The Nazis sought to "improve" the German population by encouraging the reproduction of those deemed racially and biologically valuable, and by preventing or eliminating those deemed inferior — through compulsory sterilisation, forced abortion, and ultimately mass murder, including the Aktion T4 "euthanasia" programme and the Holocaust.

Background

Eugenics, named by Francis Galton in the 1880s, was an international movement in the early 20th century; compulsory sterilisation laws existed in the United States and Scandinavia before the Nazis came to power. In Germany, Alfred Ploetz founded the journal Archiv für Rassen- und Gesellschaftsbiologie in 1904 and coined the term "racial hygiene". After 1933 the Nazi regime fused eugenics with its antisemitic and racial ideology, and figures such as Ernst Rüdin, Eugen Fischer, and Otmar Freiherr von Verschuer gave the programme scientific cover through the Kaiser Wilhelm Institutes.

Compulsory sterilisation

The Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring, passed on 14 July 1933, mandated sterilisation for people with conditions deemed hereditary, including schizophrenia, epilepsy, Huntington's disease, hereditary blindness and deafness, severe physical deformity, and chronic alcoholism. Hereditary Health Courts decided cases, overwhelmingly in favour of sterilisation. An estimated 300,000 to 400,000 people were sterilised under the law by 1945.

The Nuremberg Laws and population policy

The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 prohibited marriage and sexual relations between Jews and "citizens of German or related blood" and required marriage fitness certificates. Positive eugenics measures included the Lebensborn programme (founded 1935), marriage loans for "racially valuable" couples, the Mother's Cross for large families, and bans on abortion and contraception for "Aryan" women — while abortion was compelled for those classified as unfit.

Aktion T4 and mass murder

In 1939 the regime began the systematic killing of institutionalised people with disabilities, centralised at Tiergartenstraße 4 in Berlin (hence "T4"). Adults were gassed in six killing centres, including Hadamar and Hartheim; a parallel "child euthanasia" programme murdered disabled children. Although the centralised programme was officially halted in August 1941 after public protest, notably Bishop Clemens August Graf von Galen's sermons, decentralised killing continued until 1945. In total some 250,000 to 300,000 people were murdered under the various "euthanasia" programmes. T4 personnel and gassing technology were later transferred to the extermination camps of Operation Reinhard.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg Doctors' Trial (1946–47) some perpetrators were prosecuted, but many racial hygienists resumed academic careers in West Germany. Nazi eugenics discredited the broader eugenics movement and reshaped postwar medical ethics, informing the Nuremberg Code's requirement of voluntary consent.
