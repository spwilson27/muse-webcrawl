The Saratoga campaign in 1777 was an attempt by the British to gain military control of the strategically important Hudson River valley during the American Revolutionary War. It ended in the surrender of a British army under General John Burgoyne at Saratoga on 17 October 1777, an event frequently described as a turning point of the war, because it convinced France to enter the conflict openly on the American side.

Background

The British plan, largely devised by Burgoyne, called for three forces to converge on Albany, New York: Burgoyne's army would advance south from Canada along the Lake Champlain–Hudson corridor; a smaller force under Barry St. Leger would move east from Lake Ontario through the Mohawk Valley; and forces under General William Howe would advance north from New York City. Control of the Hudson would isolate New England from the other rebellious colonies.

The campaign

Burgoyne's force of some 8,000 British and German troops recaptured Fort Ticonderoga in July 1777 but then made slow progress through difficult terrain while American forces under Philip Schuyler felled trees, destroyed bridges, and stripped the countryside of supplies. St. Leger's expedition was turned back after the Battle of Oriskany and the siege of Fort Stanwix, and a foraging expedition toward Bennington was defeated in August, costing Burgoyne nearly 1,000 men he could not replace. Howe, meanwhile, had taken most of his army south to capture Philadelphia rather than advancing up the Hudson.

Battles of Saratoga and surrender

Crossing the Hudson near Saratoga in September, Burgoyne confronted the entrenched American Northern Army, now commanded by Horatio Gates, at Bemis Heights. In the Battle of Freeman's Farm (19 September) and the Battle of Bemis Heights (7 October) the British were repulsed, with American forces energized by fighters including Daniel Morgan's riflemen and Benedict Arnold. Surrounded, outnumbered, and out of supplies, Burgoyne surrendered his remaining army of about 6,000 men on 17 October 1777 under the terms of the Convention of Saratoga.

Consequences

News of the surrender reached Paris in December 1777 and helped convince the French government to conclude an alliance with the United States, formalized in February 1778. The "Convention Army" of surrendered troops remained in American custody for years amid disputes over the convention's terms. The campaign is commemorated at Saratoga National Historical Park.
