American theatre

Theatre in the United States, often called American theatre, encompasses theatrical production and drama in the United States from colonial times to the present. Its commercial centre is Broadway in New York City, complemented by Off-Broadway, Off-Off-Broadway and a network of regional theatres.

LocationUnited States; centred on New York City
FormsDrama, musical theatre, regional theatre

Early history

Professional theatre arrived with British touring companies in the 18th century; Lewis Hallam's company performed from 1752. Early American plays included Royall Tyler's The Contrast (1787). In the 19th century, melodrama, minstrel shows, vaudeville and touring stars dominated.

20th century

Eugene O'Neill transformed American drama from the 1910s, followed by Tennessee Williams, Arthur Miller, Thornton Wilder and Edward Albee. The musical matured through Rodgers and Hammerstein's Oklahoma! (1943) and later Stephen Sondheim. The Federal Theatre Project (1935–39), the Actors Studio and Method acting, and postwar regional theatres such as Arena Stage decentralized production.

Contemporary theatre

Broadway remains a major economic and cultural force, with long-running musicals and star-led revivals. Playwrights such as August Wilson, Tony Kushner, Suzan-Lori Parks and Lin-Manuel Miranda, creator of Hamilton (2015), have expanded whose stories American theatre tells.
