The coastline paradox is the counterintuitive observation that the coastline of a landmass does not have a well-defined length. This results from the fractal-like properties of coastlines: the finer the scale of measurement, the more detail is captured and the longer the measured length becomes, without converging to a fixed value.

History

Lewis Fry Richardson first documented the effect while studying how measurement scale influenced reported lengths of international borders. Benoit Mandelbrot later examined Richardson's data in his 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension", showing that coastlines approximate fractals with a characteristic fractal dimension — about 1.25 for Britain's west coast — so that measured length increases predictably as the ruler shortens.

Implications

Because of the paradox, published figures for the lengths of coastlines and borders depend on the method and scale used, and comparisons between sources can be misleading. Practical measurements adopt agreed conventions, but the paradox remains a classic illustration of fractals in nature.
