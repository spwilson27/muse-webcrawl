Perjury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Perjury is the offence of wilfully telling an untruth or making a misrepresentation under oath in a judicial proceeding (or in certain other sworn contexts: affidavits, depositions, legislative testimony, statutory declarations). The classic elements are a lawful oath administered by a competent authority, a false statement, on a matter material to the proceeding, made with knowledge of its falsity and intent to deceive — honest mistake, confusion and literally-true-but-misleading answers (the defendant in Bronston v. United States, 1973, was acquitted on that ground) fall outside it.

Function and enforcement

Perjury law guards the epistemics of adjudication: a system that decides by testimony must threaten the liar. Prosecutions are nonetheless selective — materiality and provable falsity are demanding, "he said/she said" oath-against-oath cases are disfavoured (some jurisdictions historically required corroboration by two witnesses), and prosecutors weigh deterrence against the chill on testimony. Its political salience is outsized because it is the charge that attaches to scandals: perjury (and obstruction) supplied the legal core of the Clinton impeachment and innumerable lesser downfalls, where the underlying conduct was uncharged or unchargeable — the cover-up literally becoming the crime.
