Temperance (virtue)

Temperance, in its classical use, is the practice of self-control, moderation, and restraint — in particular, voluntary abstention from indulgence in pleasures and appetites. It is one of the four cardinal virtues of classical antiquity and Christian theology, alongside prudence, justice, and fortitude.

Classical and religious traditions

The Greek concept of sophrosyne — soundness of mind, moderation, and self-knowledge — was central to the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle. In Plato's Republic, temperance is the virtue by which the appetitive part of the soul submits to reason. Aristotle treated temperance as the mean between the excess of self-indulgence and the deficiency of insensibility, chiefly regarding the pleasures of taste and touch.

Thomas Aquinas developed temperance extensively in the Summa Theologiae, treating it as the virtue that moderates attraction to pleasures and ensures the will's mastery over instincts. Analogous virtues of restraint appear in many traditions: in Buddhism, the Five Precepts counsel abstention from intoxicants and sensual misconduct; in Islam, moderation and fasting are central practices; and in Hinduism and Jainism, self-discipline (tapas) is a core spiritual discipline.

Temperance movements

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, "temperance" became strongly associated with the temperance movement, a social campaign against the consumption of alcoholic beverages that led to Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) and restrictions in several other countries. Organisations such as the Woman's Christian Temperance Union promoted abstinence as a moral and public-health reform.
