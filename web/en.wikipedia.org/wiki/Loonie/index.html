The loonie (French: huard) is the common name for the Canadian one-dollar coin, introduced by the Government of Canada in 1987. It is a gold-coloured coin made of nickel electroplated with bronze, and later of brass-plated steel. The coin's reverse depicts a common loon, a bird found throughout Canada, from which the nickname derives. By extension, "loonie" is also used informally to refer to the Canadian dollar itself, especially in foreign-exchange commentary.

History

The coin replaced the one-dollar banknote, which was withdrawn from circulation in 1989, and the earlier silver-coloured Voyageur dollar coin. The introduction of a durable coin was intended to save money over the long term, since coins last far longer than low-denomination notes. The original dies depicting a voyageur design were lost (believed stolen) in transit in 1986, so the loon design by artist Robert-Ralph Carmichael was adopted as a replacement.

Because the coin is eleven-sided (a hendecagon/Reuleaux-like curve of constant width), it rolls smoothly in vending machines. In 1996 the two-dollar coin, nicknamed the "toonie", was introduced following the loonie's success.

Lucky loonie

At the 2002 Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City, a Canadian ice-maker embedded a loonie beneath the centre ice; both the Canadian men's and women's hockey teams won gold, and the coin — the "lucky loonie" — became a celebrated piece of Canadian sporting folklore, later displayed at the Hockey Hall of Fame. The tradition of burying a loonie in the ice was repeated at subsequent events.
