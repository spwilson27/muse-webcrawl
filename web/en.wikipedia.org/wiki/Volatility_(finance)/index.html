Volatility (finance)

In finance, volatility (symbol σ) is the degree of variation of a trading price series over time, usually measured by the standard deviation of logarithmic returns. It quantifies the risk of an asset: highly volatile instruments swing widely in price. Volatility is annualised for comparability and is a central input to option pricing in the Black–Scholes model.

Types

Historical (realised) volatility is computed from past prices. Implied volatility is backed out from market option prices and reflects expectations of future movement; the term structure and "smile" or "skew" of implied volatility across strike prices encode information about perceived tail risk. Future realised volatility is what subsequently occurs, traded via volatility derivatives.

Properties

Volatility is not constant: it clusters (large moves follow large moves), spikes during crises, and tends to mean-revert. It correlates negatively with equity returns — markets usually fall faster than they rise — producing the "leverage effect". Models such as ARCH/GARCH (Engle, Bollerslev) and stochastic volatility models are used to forecast it.

Indices and instruments

The best-known measure is the CBOE VIX, the implied volatility of S&P 500 index options, often called the "fear gauge". Tradable products include VIX futures and options, variance swaps and exchange-traded volatility notes. Traders "long volatility" profit from large moves in either direction, for example through straddles.

Relation to risk

Volatility is a convenient but imperfect risk measure: it treats gains and losses symmetrically and ignores liquidity and fat tails. It feeds into measures such as beta, value at risk and the Sharpe ratio's denominator.
