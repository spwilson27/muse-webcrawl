Supersonic flight

Supersonic flight is flight at a speed greater than the speed of sound (Mach 1). At sea level in standard conditions sound travels at about 343 metres per second (1,235 km/h); the speed of sound decreases with altitude as temperature falls. Objects moving at supersonic speeds generate shock waves that coalesce into the audible phenomenon known as a sonic boom.

History

Although bullets and whip tips had exceeded the speed of sound for centuries, sustained controlled supersonic flight began on 14 October 1947, when Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 past Mach 1. Military jets routinely became supersonic in the 1950s, and the Anglo-French Concorde (in service 1976–2003) and the Soviet Tupolev Tu-144 carried passengers at about Mach 2. No supersonic airliner has operated since Concorde's retirement, though several companies have announced plans for new designs.

Aerodynamics

Supersonic aircraft use thin, swept or delta wings, slender fuselages shaped according to the area rule, and powerful engines — often with afterburners — to overcome the sharp rise in drag near Mach 1 (transonic drag rise). Air intakes must slow incoming air to subsonic speed before it reaches the compressor, and flight controls must cope with the rearward shift of the centre of pressure at supersonic speed. Heating from air friction becomes a major design constraint above about Mach 2.

Sonic boom and regulation

The sonic boom produced by supersonic flight led many countries, including the United States in 1973, to ban routine supersonic flight over land, a restriction that confined Concorde mainly to transoceanic routes. Research into "low-boom" shaping, exemplified by NASA's X-59 experimental aircraft, aims to soften the boom to a quiet "thump" and open the way to lifting those bans.
