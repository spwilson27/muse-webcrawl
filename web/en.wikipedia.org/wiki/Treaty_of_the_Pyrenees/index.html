The Treaty of the Pyrenees was signed on 7 November 1659 on the Isle of Pheasants in the Bidassoa River, on the border between France and Spain, ending the Franco-Spanish War that had continued after the Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the Thirty Years' War. Negotiated by Cardinal Mazarin for France and Don Luis de Haro for Spain, the treaty is traditionally seen as the moment Spain's European predominance passed to France.

Terms

Spain ceded to France Roussillon and the northern part of Cerdanya in the Pyrenees — fixing the Franco-Spanish frontier essentially where it remains today — as well as Artois and a chain of fortified towns in the Spanish Netherlands, including Gravelines, Landrecies and Le Quesnoy. A notorious ambiguity in the wording, which ceded Cerdanya's "villages" but not its "towns", left the town of Llívia Spanish, creating the Spanish enclave within France that still exists; thirty-three villages of Cerdanya passed to France.

The marriage clause

The treaty's most fateful provision was the marriage of the young Louis XIV to the Spanish infanta Maria Theresa, elder daughter of Philip IV. She renounced her rights to the Spanish succession in return for a dowry of 500,000 gold écus — which Spain was never able to pay. Louis XIV later invoked the unpaid dowry and claimed the renunciation was void, using the issue to press French claims in the War of Devolution (1667) and ultimately to advance his grandson's candidacy in the War of the Spanish Succession.

Consequences

With Catalonia restored to Spain (the Reapers' War ended with the fall of Barcelona in 1652) but its northern counties lost, the treaty reshaped the Pyrenean border permanently and consolidated France as Europe's leading land power for the next half-century.
