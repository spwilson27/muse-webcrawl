Seafloor spreading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Seafloor spreading is a process that occurs at mid-ocean ridges, where new oceanic crust is formed through volcanic activity and then gradually moves away from the ridge. Seafloor spreading helps explain continental drift in the theory of plate tectonics.

Process

At a spreading ridge, magma rises from the mantle into the rift between separating plates and solidifies as basalt and gabbro, creating new oceanic lithosphere. The plates move apart at rates from about 1 cm per year at slow ridges such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to more than 15 cm per year at fast ridges such as the East Pacific Rise. As crust moves away it cools, thickens, and subsides, so ocean depth increases predictably with crustal age.

Evidence

The hypothesis, proposed by Harry Hess in the early 1960s, was decisively supported by:

  • Magnetic stripes — symmetric patterns of normal and reversed magnetic polarity in the crust on either side of ridges, explained by Vine, Matthews, and Morley in 1963 as a tape recording of Earth's geomagnetic reversals.
  • Age dating — deep-sea drilling by the Glomar Challenger (1968) confirmed that crust is youngest at ridges and oldest (no more than about 200 million years) near continental margins.
  • Heat flow and sediment thickness — both greatest near ridges and decreasing with distance.

Spreading explains why oceanic crust is far younger than continental crust: old seafloor is continually consumed by subduction at ocean trenches.
