Hurricane Otis was a compact but devastating tropical cyclone that made landfall in October 2023 near Acapulco, Mexico, as a Category 5 hurricane. Otis underwent explosive rapid intensification in the 24 hours before landfall, strengthening from a tropical storm to a Category 5 hurricane with maximum sustained winds of 165 mph (270 km/h) — an intensification that was not anticipated by forecast models, which had predicted only a modest hurricane at landfall.

Otis was the first Pacific hurricane to make landfall at Category 5 intensity on record, and the strongest landfalling Pacific hurricane in Mexico. The storm caused catastrophic damage in Acapulco and surrounding areas of Guerrero, killing at least 52 people and causing an estimated $12–16 billion in damage, making it the costliest Pacific hurricane on record at the time.

Meteorological history

A tropical wave moved off the coast of West Africa in early October 2023 and tracked westward across the Atlantic before entering the eastern Pacific. A broad area of low pressure developed south of Mexico on 18 October, and the system organized into Tropical Depression Eighteen-E on 22 October, becoming Tropical Storm Otis later that day.

Initially hampered by easterly wind shear, Otis moved slowly northward. On 23–24 October, as shear relaxed and the storm passed over very warm waters with high ocean heat content, Otis began a period of explosive intensification. In roughly 12 hours its winds increased from 65 mph to 145 mph, and by the evening of 24 October Otis reached Category 5 strength. Hurricane Hunter aircraft measured a minimum central pressure of 922 mbar. Otis made landfall near Acapulco around 06:25 UTC on 25 October at peak intensity, after which it weakened rapidly over the Sierra Madre del Sur mountains and dissipated the same day.

Preparations and impact

Because forecast models had predicted a much weaker storm, preparations in Acapulco were limited. The hurricane's eyewall passed directly over the city, destroying or damaging the vast majority of buildings, downing power lines and communications, and flooding streets. The city's hotels, hospitals, and the international airport sustained severe damage. Thousands of tourists were stranded, and looting was reported in the aftermath.

The Mexican government deployed thousands of soldiers and federal police under its disaster response plans. Electricity and water service took weeks to restore fully. The federal government announced a reconstruction and support program for affected households and businesses.

Aftermath and records

Otis's intensification — an increase of about 110 mph in winds within 24 hours — ranks among the fastest ever observed in the eastern Pacific. The storm prompted reviews of hurricane forecasting and emergency communication in Mexico. Otis surpassed Hurricane Patricia (2015) in damage totals for the basin despite making landfall at a populated resort city. The name Otis was retired by the World Meteorological Organization in 2024 and replaced with Olaf for future seasons.
