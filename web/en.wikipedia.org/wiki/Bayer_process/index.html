Bayer process

The Bayer process is the principal industrial means of refining bauxite to produce alumina (aluminium oxide), the feedstock for aluminium smelting by the Hall–Héroult process. It was invented in 1888 by the Austrian chemist Karl Joseph Bayer, working in Saint Petersburg, Russia.

The process

Crushed bauxite is digested in hot concentrated sodium hydroxide solution, which dissolves the aluminium-bearing minerals (gibbsite, boehmite, diaspore) as sodium aluminate while iron oxides and other impurities remain as an insoluble residue — the toxic alkaline tailings known as red mud — which is filtered off. The clarified liquor is cooled and seeded with aluminium hydroxide crystals, precipitating pure Al(OH)₃, which is washed and calcined at around 1,000 °C to produce alumina. The caustic soda is recovered and recycled.

Economics and environment

Typically about two tonnes of bauxite yield one tonne of alumina, and roughly two tonnes of alumina yield one tonne of aluminium. The process leaves 1–1.5 tonnes of red mud per tonne of alumina; its storage in impoundment ponds poses environmental hazards, dramatically shown by the 2010 Ajkai alumina plant accident in Hungary, when a red mud reservoir burst and flooded surrounding villages.
