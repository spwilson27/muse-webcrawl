1894 Australian shearers' strike

Date1894
LocationQueensland and New South Wales, Australia
PartiesShearers' unions vs pastoralists
OutcomeStrike defeated; union leaders imprisoned

The 1894 Australian shearers' strike was a major industrial dispute in colonial Australia, the second great shearers' strike after the better-known strike of 1891. Like its predecessor, it arose from pastoralists' attempts to cut shearers' pay and employ non-union labour under "freedom of contract" agreements during a severe economic depression.

Background

The defeat of the 1891 strike and the maritime strike of 1890 had weakened the union movement, but shearers in Queensland remained organised through the Queensland Shearers' Union. As wool prices fell and the depression of the 1890s deepened, pastoralists sought further reductions in shearing rates and insisted on the pastoralists' standard agreement, which unions saw as destroying collective bargaining.

Course of the strike

Strikers established camps in the bush districts of central Queensland and resorted to tactics including the burning of woolsheds and the destruction of fences. Violence flared at several stations; the most notorious episode was the burning of the Dagworth Station woolshed, an event later woven into the legend surrounding the composition of "Waltzing Matilda" by Banjo Paterson. Colonial governments deployed police and military forces, union leaders were arrested, and the strike collapsed. Several leaders were tried for conspiracy and sedition at Rockhampton and sentenced to terms of imprisonment on St Helena Island.

Legacy

Together, the defeats of 1891 and 1894 convinced many unionists that industrial action alone could not protect their interests, accelerating the turn toward parliamentary politics and the rise of the Australian Labor Party, which formed the world's first labour government in Queensland in 1899, albeit briefly.
