A disinfectant is a chemical substance or mixture applied to inanimate objects and surfaces to destroy or irreversibly inactivate bacteria, viruses and fungi. Disinfection does not necessarily kill all microorganisms, particularly resistant bacterial spores; it is less thorough than sterilisation but more aggressive than sanitising, which merely reduces microbial counts. Chemical agents used on living tissue, such as skin, are called antiseptics instead.

Types

Alcohols (ethanol and isopropanol, typically 70%) are fast-acting against bacteria and enveloped viruses but are not sporicidal and evaporate quickly. Chlorine compounds such as sodium hypochlorite (bleach) are broad-spectrum and inexpensive but corrosive and inactivated by organic matter. Hydrogen peroxide and peracetic acid serve as high-level disinfectants for medical instruments. Quaternary ammonium compounds ("quats") are common low-level surface disinfectants, and phenolic compounds — descendants of Joseph Lister's carbolic acid — remain in use in some settings.

Factors affecting efficacy

Effectiveness depends on the concentration of the agent, contact time, temperature, pH, the presence of organic soil (surfaces should usually be cleaned first), and the type and number of microorganisms. Regulatory bodies such as the US Environmental Protection Agency register disinfectants and publish lists of products effective against particular pathogens.

Resistance and safety

Microorganisms differ greatly in susceptibility: bacterial spores and non-enveloped viruses are hardest to kill, and biofilms protect embedded microbes. Overuse and under-dosing of disinfectants are debated as contributors to reduced microbial susceptibility, and many agents pose health or environmental hazards requiring ventilation and protective equipment.
