English monarchy

The monarchy of England was the institution of kingship in the Kingdom of England from its formation in the tenth century until 1707, when England united with Scotland to form the Kingdom of Great Britain. Its line runs from the West Saxon kings — traditionally from Æthelstan, first king of the English in 927 — through the Norman Conquest of 1066, the Plantagenet, Lancaster, York, Tudor and Stuart dynasties, to Queen Anne, the last monarch of England as a separate state. The modern British monarchy descends directly from it, and the English crown's constitutional evolution — Magna Carta, Parliament, civil war, the execution of Charles I, and the Glorious Revolution settlement of 1689 — shaped constitutional monarchy worldwide.

The English crown's regalia symbolised a monarchy increasingly limited by law and Parliament.

Anglo-Saxon and Norman foundations

England emerged from the union of Anglo-Saxon kingdoms; Æthelstan (r. 924–939) was the first to rule the whole territory recognisably as England. After Cnut the Great's North Sea empire and the restored West Saxon line under Edward the Confessor, William of Normandy's victory at Hastings in 1066 replaced the English aristocracy and bound England to French-speaking Norman kingship, introducing feudal tenure and the Domesday survey (1086). The Anarchy of Stephen's reign (1135–54) ended with the accession of Henry II, founder of the Plantagenet (Angevin) line and of much of English common law.

Constraint and Parliament

Conflict between crown and barons produced Magna Carta (1215), in which John accepted that the king was subject to law; under Henry III and Edward I, Parliament took institutional shape. The Hundred Years' War with France, the deposition of Edward II (1327) and Richard II (1399), and the Wars of the Roses (1455–87) between Lancaster and York all demonstrated both the strength and the vulnerability of the crown. The Tudor dynasty — Henry VII, Henry VIII, whose break with Rome made the monarch Supreme Head of the Church of England, and Elizabeth I — built a powerful "new monarchy" while still governing through Parliament.

Revolution and settlement

Stuart claims to divine-right rule led to the English Civil War (1642–51), the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649, and England's only republican period, the Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell. The monarchy was restored in 1660, but James II's Catholicism provoked the Glorious Revolution of 1688: William III and Mary II accepted the Bill of Rights 1689, which fixed parliamentary supremacy, required parliamentary consent to taxation and standing armies, and settled the Protestant succession, confirmed by the Act of Settlement 1701. Under Anne the crowns of England and Scotland were united (1707), ending the separate English monarchy.
