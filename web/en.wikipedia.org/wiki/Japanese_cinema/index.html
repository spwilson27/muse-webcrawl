Cinema of Japan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A black-and-white still from a Kurosawa film: samurai silhouetted against a windswept sky]
A black-and-white still from a Kurosawa film: samurai silhouetted against a windswept sky

The cinema of Japan has produced one of the world's richest film cultures since the 1890s. Its golden age of the 1950s — Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon and Seven Samurai, Yasujirō Ozu's family dramas, Kenji Mizoguchi's tragedies — established Japanese film's world reputation, and its contemporary industry, driven by Anime, genre cinema and auteurs such as Hirokazu Kore-eda, remains among the largest by output and box office.

Early cinema and the studio system

Films arrived in 1896–97; the industry developed with the benshi narrators who voiced silents into the 1930s, and consolidated into studios (Shochiku, Nikkatsu, Toho, Daiei, Toei) running a star-and-genre system. Period dramas (jidaigeki, including the chanbara sword film) alternated with contemporary stories (shomin-geki, dramas of ordinary people) in which Ozu and Mikio Naruse specialised.

Golden age and international discovery

Rashomon's Golden Lion at Venice (1951) opened Western eyes; Kurosawa's Seven Samurai (1954) and Yojimbo reshaped world action cinema, Mizoguchi's Ugetsu (1953) and Ozu's Tokyo Story (1953) entered the all-time canon, and Ishirō Honda's Godzilla (1954) founded the kaiju genre from the ashes of Hiroshima. A 1960s new wave (Nagisa Ōshima, Shōhei Imamura) attacked the studios from within as television eroded audiences.

Contemporary industry

Japan is one of the few markets where local films (led by anime — Studio Ghibli, Makoto Shinkai's Your Name, Demon Slayer) routinely beat Hollywood imports. Auteurs Kore-eda (Shoplifters, Palme d'Or 2018), Ryūsuke Hamaguchi (Drive My Car, 2021) and Takashi Miike sustain festival prestige alongside a vast commercial industry.
