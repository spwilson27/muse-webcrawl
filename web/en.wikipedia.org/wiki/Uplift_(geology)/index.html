Uplift (geology)

Uplift, in geology, is the vertical elevation of the Earth's surface in response to natural causes. Broad, gentle, warping or faulting of the Earth's crust leads to uplift; the large-scale changes in topography that result constitute orogenesis (mountain building). The opposite of uplift, subsidence, results in a decrease in elevation.

Mechanisms

Uplift can occur through several processes. In convergent plate boundaries, crustal shortening and thickening raise mountain ranges such as the Himalayas and the Andes. Isostatic uplift occurs when the crust rebounds after the removal of a heavy load — for example, post-glacial rebound following the melting of Pleistocene ice sheets, which continues to raise parts of Scandinavia and Canada by up to a centimetre per year. Erosion can also cause isostatic uplift of the remaining crust ("erosional unloading"), and dynamic topography driven by mantle convection produces broad, slow vertical motions over geological time.

Rates and measurement

Modern uplift rates are measured with GPS geodesy, precise levelling, tide gauges, and satellite radar interferometry (InSAR). Long-term rates are inferred from dated geological markers such as marine terraces, river terraces, and raised beaches. Tectonic uplift of individual mountain ranges commonly proceeds at rates of millimetres per year sustained over millions of years. The balance between uplift and erosion determines whether a landscape rises, and exhumation (the exposure of rocks that were once buried) is the combined result of uplift and erosion working together.
