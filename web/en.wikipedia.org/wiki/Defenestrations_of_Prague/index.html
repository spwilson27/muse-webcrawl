Defenestrations of Prague

The Defenestrations of Prague were three incidents in the history of Bohemia in which people were thrown out of windows (defenestration, from Latin de fenestra). Two are especially significant: the First Defenestration in 1419, which helped trigger the Hussite Wars, and the Third Defenestration in 1618, which precipitated the Thirty Years' War.

First Defenestration (1419)

On 30 July 1419, a mob of Hussites led by Jan Želivský marched on the New Town Hall in Prague demanding the release of imprisoned Hussites. When the town councillors refused, the crowd stormed the building and threw the judge, the burgomaster and several councillors from the windows onto spears below, killing them. King Wenceslaus IV is said to have suffered a stroke on hearing the news, dying weeks later; the event sparked the Hussite Wars.

Second Defenestration (1483)

In September 1483, during political and religious tension under King Vladislaus II, Catholic town councillors were attacked in Prague; the corpse of one magistrate was thrown from a window of the Old Town Hall. Unlike the other two, this incident is less often counted in the traditional numbering.

Third Defenestration (1618)

On 23 May 1618, Protestant Bohemian nobles, angered by the perceived violation of the Letter of Majesty guaranteeing religious freedom, confronted the Catholic imperial governors Jaroslav Bořita of Martinice and Vilém Slavata of Chlum, together with their secretary Philip Fabricius, in Prague Castle. After a heated exchange, the three men were thrown from a window some 21 metres (70 feet) above the ground. All three survived the fall — Catholics attributed this to the intervention of angels or the Virgin Mary, while Protestants pointed to the softening effect of a dung heap below. The event led directly to the Bohemian Revolt and the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War.
