Bootstrapping (statistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bootstrapping is a statistical method for estimating the sampling distribution of an estimator by resampling with replacement from the observed data. It was introduced by Bradley Efron in 1979 and is named after the idiom of "pulling oneself up by one's bootstraps", since the data are used to learn about the very population from which they were drawn.

Method

In the basic (nonparametric) bootstrap, one draws many new samples, each of the same size as the original dataset, by sampling observations with replacement. For each resample, the statistic of interest — a mean, median, regression coefficient, or almost any other estimator — is recomputed. The distribution of these recomputed values approximates the sampling distribution of the estimator, allowing standard errors, bias estimates and confidence intervals to be calculated without strong distributional assumptions. Interval methods include the percentile method and the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval.

Variants and applications

Variants include the parametric bootstrap, which simulates from a fitted model; the block bootstrap for time series; and Bayesian bootstrap. Bootstrapping has become routine in applied statistics and machine learning — for example, bagging (bootstrap aggregating) underlies random forest models — because it works for complicated estimators, such as medians or correlation coefficients, where analytic formulas are unavailable or unreliable. Its main limitations are that it cannot repair a small or unrepresentative sample, and it can behave poorly for extreme statistics such as the sample maximum.
