Papier-mâché

Papier-mâché
MaterialsPaper pulp or strips + adhesive (glue, starch, resin)
MeaningFrench for "chewed paper"
UsesMasks, piñatas, models, furniture, sculpture

Papier-mâché (French for "chewed paper") is a composite material consisting of paper pieces or pulp, sometimes reinforced with textiles, bound with an adhesive such as glue, starch, or wallpaper paste. It can be layered over a mould or armature and, when dry, forms a light, hard surface that can be sanded, painted, and lacquered.

History

Paper-based moulded objects were made in China soon after paper's invention, including lacquered helmets and vessels. In Europe, papier-mâché became commercially important in the 18th and 19th centuries for trays, snuffboxes, dolls' heads, and architectural ornament; in Victorian England, firms such as Henry Clay's produced japanned papier-mâché furniture inlaid with mother-of-pearl, and the material was even used for panels of railway carriages and small boats.

Modern uses

Today papier-mâché is ubiquitous in festivals and crafts: piñatas in Mexico, giant parade figures and Carnival masks in Europe and Latin America, school art projects, theatre props, and taxidermy and model-making forms.
