Moons of Haumea

The dwarf planet Haumea has two known moons, Hiʻiaka and Namaka, named after Hawaiian goddesses. Both moons were discovered in 2005 by Michael E. Brown and the Caltech Optical Observatories team at the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii. They are thought to have formed from debris ejected when Haumea was struck by another large body early in the history of the Solar System — the same collisional family that produced Haumea's extremely fast rotation and elongated shape.

Hiʻiaka

Hiʻiaka, provisionally designated S/2005 (136108) 1 and nicknamed "Rudolph" by the discovery team, is the larger and outer of the two moons, roughly 310 km in diameter. It orbits Haumea in about 49 days at a distance of about 49,500 km, in a nearly circular orbit inclined about 13° to Haumea's equator. Observations with the Hubble Space Telescope and adaptive-optics systems show its surface is covered in crystalline water ice, like Haumea itself. Hiʻiaka is named for the Hawaiian goddess of dance and a patroness of the Big Island of Hawaiʻi, a daughter of Haumea.

Namaka

Namaka, provisionally designated S/2005 (136108) 2 and nicknamed "Blitzen", is the smaller and inner moon, roughly 170 km in diameter (about one-tenth the mass of Hiʻiaka). It orbits in about 18 days at a distance of about 25,600 km, on an elliptical orbit strongly perturbed by Hiʻiaka; the two moons are close to an 8:3 orbital resonance, which makes their mutual gravitational interactions a sensitive tool for weighing the system. Namaka is named for a Hawaiian water spirit, another daughter of Haumea.

Origin

The Haumea collisional family — a group of trans-Neptunian objects with similar orbits and nearly pure water-ice surfaces — is believed to have been created when a proto-Haumea was struck by another body roughly 4.5 billion years ago. The impact spun Haumea up to its current 3.9-hour rotation period and threw off icy fragments, some of which accreted into Hiʻiaka and Namaka. Precise astrometry of the moons' orbits gives Haumea a mass of about 4 × 1021 kg, roughly 28% the mass of the Pluto system.
