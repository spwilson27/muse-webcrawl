A literary magazine is a periodical devoted to literature in a broad sense. Literary magazines usually publish short stories, poetry, essays, literary criticism, book reviews, biographical profiles of authors, interviews and letters. They are often called literary journals or literary reviews; small, avant-garde titles aimed at a limited audience are known as little magazines.

History

The Nouvelles de la république des lettres, first published in 1684, is one of the earliest literary journals. In the 19th century, periodicals such as The Edinburgh Review (1802) and The Quarterly Review shaped literary taste, while American magazines including The North American Review and later The Atlantic Monthly and Harper's published the era's leading writers.

The early 20th century saw an explosion of "little magazines" that championed modernism: Poetry (Chicago, founded 1912), The Little Review, The Egoist and Blast. Many major modernist works, including parts of Joyce's Ulysses, first appeared in such journals.

Notable magazines

Long-running and influential literary magazines include The Paris Review (founded 1953, famous for its author interviews), Granta, The Kenyon Review, The Sewanee Review (the oldest continuously published literary quarterly in the United States), Ploughshares, The Gettysburg Review, The New England Review, and Britain's London Review of Books and Times Literary Supplement. Many universities sponsor reviews, and since the 1990s numerous journals have moved partly or wholly online.

Role in literary culture

Literary magazines are a principal venue for emerging writers: many careers begin with short fiction or poems placed in journals, and agents and editors scout them for new talent. Most operate on small budgets, supported by subscriptions, universities, arts councils and donations.
