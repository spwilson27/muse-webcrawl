Dollarization

Dollarization occurs when the inhabitants of a country use a foreign currency in parallel to or instead of the domestic currency. The term is not only applied to the use of the United States dollar, but generally to the use of any foreign currency as the national currency. Dollarization may be de facto (unofficial, arising from public preference) or de jure (official, where a government formally adopts a foreign currency as legal tender).

Countries

Full official dollarization with the US dollar has been adopted by Panama (since 1904), Ecuador (2000), El Salvador (2001) and Zimbabwe (2009–2019), among others; several small island states also use the dollar, the euro, or the Australian dollar. Euroization describes the analogous adoption of the euro, as in Montenegro and Kosovo.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advocates argue dollarization eliminates currency risk, lowers inflation and interest rates, and integrates the economy with the issuer's. Critics note the loss of independent monetary policy, of seigniorage revenue, and of a lender of last resort in the national currency, and that the adopting country is exposed to the monetary policy of the issuing state, which is set without regard to its conditions.
