1977 Pakistani military coup

Date5 July 1977
LocationPakistan
Organised byGeneral Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, Chief of Army Staff
OutcomeOverthrow and arrest of Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto; martial law imposed; Zia becomes Chief Martial Law Administrator and later President

The 1977 Pakistani military coup (codenamed Operation Fair Play) was the overthrow on 5 July 1977 of the elected government of Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto by Chief of Army Staff General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq. Zia imposed martial law, suspended the constitution and ruled Pakistan for eleven years — first as Chief Martial Law Administrator and from 1978 as President — until his death in an air crash in 1988. It was Pakistan's second successful military coup, following Ayub Khan's in 1958.

Background

Bhutto's Pakistan Peoples Party won the general election of March 1977 by a large majority, but the opposition Pakistan National Alliance (PNA) alleged systematic rigging and launched a mass protest movement (Nizam-e-Mustafa, "the Prophet's system") demanding fresh elections and Islamic laws. Months of strikes and street violence killed several hundred people and paralysed the economy. Bhutto imposed martial law in three cities and opened negotiations with the PNA; by early July an agreement on new elections appeared close.

The coup

In the night of 4–5 July 1977, army units seized key installations in Islamabad, Karachi, Lahore and other cities, arresting Bhutto, his ministers and opposition PNA leaders alike. Zia announced that the armed forces had intervened only to prevent civil war, promised elections within ninety days, and insisted there was no political ambition involved. The elections were repeatedly postponed; the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Anwar-ul-Haq validated the coup under the "doctrine of necessity" in the Nusrat Bhutto case.

Aftermath

Bhutto was released, then rearrested in September 1977 on charges of conspiring to murder a political opponent, Nawab Muhammad Ahmed Khan Kasuri. Convicted by the Lahore High Court in March 1978 in a trial widely criticised as unfair, he was hanged on 4 April 1979 after the Supreme Court upheld the sentence by a narrow 4–3 verdict, despite international appeals for clemency.

Zia embarked on an Islamisation programme — Hudood Ordinances, zakat and ushr taxes, Shariat courts and expanded blasphemy laws — and aligned Pakistan closely with the United States and Saudi Arabia during the Soviet–Afghan War, funnelling aid to the Afghan mujahideen. Political parties were banned or restricted, the press censored, and public floggings and amputations introduced. Non-party elections were held in 1985, but Zia retained sweeping powers (including, under the Eighth Amendment, the power to dissolve the National Assembly). His sudden death in the crash of a C-130 near Bahawalpur on 17 August 1988 ended the longest period of military rule in Pakistan's history and opened the way to the restoration of parliamentary democracy under Benazir Bhutto.
