Branching process

In probability theory, a branching process is a type of stochastic process that models a population whose members, in each generation, produce a random number of offspring according to a fixed probability distribution. The best-known example is the Galton–Watson process, introduced in the 19th century to study the extinction of family surnames.

History

The problem of the extinction of aristocratic family names was posed by Francis Galton in 1873 and analysed mathematically by the Reverend Henry William Watson. Their joint paper of 1874, "On the probability of the extinction of families", introduced the model now called the Galton–Watson process. The subject was revived in the 20th century in connection with nuclear chain reactions, cosmic rays, genetics, and epidemiology, and was developed by mathematicians including Andrey Kolmogorov, Stanislaw Ulam, and Theodore Harris.

The Galton–Watson process

Let Z0 = 1 and let each individual in generation n produce a random number of children drawn independently from an offspring distribution {pk} with mean μ. Then Zn+1 is the sum of the offspring counts of the members of generation n. The process has three regimes:

  • Subcritical (μ < 1): the population dies out with probability 1.
  • Critical (μ = 1): the population dies out with probability 1 (unless each individual has exactly one child).
  • Supercritical (μ > 1): there is a positive probability that the population survives and grows indefinitely.

The extinction probability is the smallest non-negative root of the equation s = G(s), where G is the probability-generating function of the offspring distribution.

Types and applications

Generalizations include multi-type branching processes, in which individuals have types; continuous-time processes such as the Bellman–Harris process, in which individuals have random lifetimes; and branching random walks and superprocesses, in which particles also move in space. Branching processes are used to model cell division, the spread of infectious diseases (where the basic reproduction number plays the role of μ), chemical chain reactions, surname dynamics, and the proliferation of neutrons in nuclear reactors.
