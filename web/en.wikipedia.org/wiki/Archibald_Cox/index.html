Archibald Cox Jr. (May 17, 1912 – May 29, 2004) was an American lawyer and law professor who served as U.S. Solicitor General under President John F. Kennedy and as the first special prosecutor of the Watergate scandal. His dismissal by President Richard Nixon in the "Saturday Night Massacre" of 20 October 1973 became a defining moment of the crisis.

Early career

Cox was born in Plainfield, New Jersey, and educated at Harvard, where he graduated from Harvard Law School in 1937 and later became a professor, specialising in labour and constitutional law. Under Kennedy he served as Solicitor General (1961–1965), arguing the government's cases before the Supreme Court, before returning to Harvard.

Watergate special prosecutor

In May 1973, Attorney General Elliot Richardson — as a condition of his own confirmation — appointed Cox special prosecutor with guaranteed independence to investigate the Watergate break-in and cover-up. Cox built a large staff and moved aggressively; after Alexander Butterfield revealed the White House tapes, Cox subpoenaed nine recordings. When Nixon offered summaries instead, Cox refused the compromise and held a press conference saying he would press the subpoena in court.

The Saturday Night Massacre

On Saturday, 20 October 1973, Nixon ordered Cox fired. Attorney General Richardson resigned rather than obey; Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also refused and resigned (or was fired); Solicitor General Robert Bork, as acting head of the Justice Department, carried out the order and dismissed Cox, whose office staff were briefly barred from their files by the FBI. The public outcry — a "firestorm" of telegrams and editorial demands for impeachment — forced Nixon to appoint a new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, and surrender the tapes. Jaworski's pursuit culminated in United States v. Nixon and Nixon's resignation.

Later life

Cox returned to Harvard, wrote the influential book The Court and the Constitution (1987), and served as chairman of the citizens' lobby Common Cause (1980–1992). He died at his home in Brooksville, Maine, in 2004.
