Vagina

The vagina is an elastic, muscular reproductive organ of the female reproductive system in humans and other mammals. In humans it extends from the vulva to the cervix of the uterus. It is the canal through which menstrual fluid leaves the body, which receives the penis during sexual intercourse, and which forms part of the birth canal during childbirth. The word derives from the Latin vāgīna, literally "sheath" or "scabbard".

Structure

The vagina is a fibromuscular tube typically 7 to 9 cm long along its front wall, angled upward and backward within the pelvis. Its upper end surrounds the cervix, forming recesses called fornices; its lower end opens at the vaginal vestibule, partially covered in many individuals by the hymen. The wall consists of an inner mucosal layer with transverse folds (rugae), a middle muscular layer, and an outer adventitial layer. During sexual arousal the vagina lengthens and produces lubrication from transudation of the vaginal walls, supplemented by secretions from the Bartholin's glands near the opening.

Microbiome and health

The healthy vagina is colonised predominantly by Lactobacillus species, which produce lactic acid and maintain an acidic pH (roughly 3.8–4.5) that inhibits many pathogens. Disruption of this balance can lead to conditions such as bacterial vaginosis or candidiasis. The vagina is self-cleaning, and internal douching is generally discouraged by clinicians. Common medical concerns include vaginitis, vaginal atrophy after menopause, and pelvic organ prolapse.

Development and variation

The vagina develops largely from the Müllerian ducts and the urogenital sinus. Its size and shape vary between individuals and change with age, hormonal status and childbirth. It is distinct from the vulva, the external genital structures, with which it is often loosely conflated in everyday usage.
