Assignment (law)


Assignment transfers the assignor's rights to the assignee.

Assignment is a legal term used in the context of the laws of contract and of property. In a contract setting, assignment describes the transfer of rights held by one party — the assignor — to another party — the assignee. Most kinds of property rights, including rights under contracts and choses in action, are assignable unless prohibited by statute, by public policy, or by the terms of the underlying contract. Assignment differs from novation, in which an old contract is extinguished and replaced by a new one, and from delegation, in which a party's duties (rather than rights) are passed to another.

Requirements

Generally, a valid assignment requires a present intention to transfer the right, and the right assigned must be identified with reasonable certainty. No particular form is required for most assignments, though some — such as assignments of copyrights under United States law, or of interests required to be in writing under a statute of frauds — must be written. Notice to the obligor is not normally required to make the assignment effective between assignor and assignee, but an obligor who pays the assignor without notice of the assignment is discharged.

Assignment of contract rights and duties

Under the common law and the UCC in the United States, contractual rights are freely assignable unless the assignment would materially change the obligor's duty or burden, materially increase the obligor's risk, or impair the obligor's chance of obtaining return performance. Rights under contracts for personal services, and future rights based on a contract not yet in existence, may not be assignable. Contracts commonly include anti-assignment clauses; courts often construe these narrowly, treating an assignment made in breach as effective while giving the obligor a claim for damages, unless the clause expressly makes the assignment void.

Equitable assignment

Apart from legal assignment (such as under section 136 of the Law of Property Act 1925 in England and Wales, which requires writing and express notice to the debtor), equity recognises informal assignments where the assignor has done everything necessary to transfer the right. An assignee in equity generally must join the assignor in any action to enforce the right.

Effects and defences

The assignee "stands in the shoes" of the assignor, taking the right subject to the terms of the contract and to defences and set-offs the obligor could have raised against the assignor. A party who assigns rights does not thereby escape its own obligations under the contract unless released by novation.

Applications

Assignment is fundamental to modern commerce: it underlies the transfer of mortgages, the sale of receivables in factoring and securitisation, the assignment of insurance policies and of intellectual property, and the transfer of leases (assignment of lease, distinct from subletting).
