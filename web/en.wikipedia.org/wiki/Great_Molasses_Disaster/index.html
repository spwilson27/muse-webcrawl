The Great Molasses Disaster, also known as the Boston Molasses Disaster, occurred on 15 January 1919 in the North End neighbourhood of Boston, Massachusetts. A storage tank filled with 2.3 million US gallons (about 8,700 tonnes) of molasses, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, burst, and the resulting wave of molasses rushed through the streets at an estimated 35 mph (56 km/h), killing 21 people and injuring about 150.

The flood

The steel tank, 50 feet (15 m) high and 90 feet (27 m) in diameter, stood at 529 Commercial Street. It had leaked since its construction in 1915, and workers later testified that it groaned and shuddered as it was filled; the company had painted it brown to disguise the leaks. On the unusually warm January day of the disaster, the tank ruptured with a sound witnesses compared to a machine gun as rivets tore out. The wave — up to 25 feet (7.6 m) high at its front — demolished buildings, swept a firehouse off its foundation, buckled an elevated railway structure, and derailed a train. Rescue work by police, firefighters, soldiers, and Red Cross nurses was hampered by the viscous, waist-deep molasses; the clean-up took weeks, using salt water to wash the residue into the harbour.

Aftermath

Residents filed one of the first class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts history against the parent company, United States Industrial Alcohol. After years of hearings, the company settled out of court for about $628,000, and the court-appointed auditor attributed the failure to a combination of inadequate design, insufficient steel thickness, and the absence of proper testing — the tank had never been filled with water to test it. The disaster spurred reforms in engineering practice and regulation, including requirements that plans be filed and stamped by licensed engineers and inspected by city officials. Local legend long claimed that the North End smelled of molasses on hot days for decades afterwards.
