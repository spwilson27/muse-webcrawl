Ditransitive verb

In grammar, a ditransitive verb is a verb which takes a subject and two objects which refer to a theme and a recipient. According to certain linguistics considerations, these objects may be called direct and indirect, or primary and secondary. This is in contrast to monotransitive verbs, which take only one object. Examples of ditransitive verbs in English include give, send, show, lend, and offer.

In English

English ditransitive verbs typically appear in the double object construction:

  • Mary gave John the book.

Many — though not all — alternate with a prepositional construction in which the theme is the direct object and the recipient is expressed by the preposition to or for:

  • Mary gave the book to John.

This alternation is known as the dative alternation. Not all verbs permit both patterns: donate, for example, allows only the prepositional construction (*Mary donated the museum a painting is ungrammatical), while verbs such as envy and bet take two objects but do not alternate. The syntactic analysis of ditransitives has been a major topic in generative grammar and lexical semantics, with debate over whether the two objects occupy symmetric positions and which object counts as the "primary" object for passivisation and binding.

Cross-linguistically

Languages mark the arguments of ditransitive verbs in different ways: some use case marking or agreement to distinguish theme and recipient (indirective alignment), others treat the recipient like a direct object (secundative alignment), and others treat both objects alike (neutral alignment). Many languages possess applicative constructions that promote beneficiaries or recipients to object status, and serial verb constructions are used for giving-events in parts of West Africa and Southeast Asia.
