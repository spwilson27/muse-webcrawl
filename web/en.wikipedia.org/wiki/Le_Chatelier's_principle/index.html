Le Chatelier's principle

Le Chatelier's principle (pronounced UK: /lə ʃæˈtɛljeɪ/ or US: /ʃəˈtɑːtəljeɪ/), also called Chatelier's principle or the Equilibrium Law, is a principle of chemistry used to predict the effect of a change in conditions on chemical equilibria. The principle is named after French chemist Henry Louis Le Chatelier, who enunciated it in 1884 (with Karl Ferdinand Braun independently proposing a similar idea), and is commonly stated as:

If a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions, the position of equilibrium moves to counteract the change.

Effects of changes

Concentration

Increasing the concentration of a reactant shifts the equilibrium toward the products, partially consuming the added substance; removing a product likewise draws the reaction forward. Industrially, this is exploited in the Haber process, where ammonia is continually liquefied and removed.

Temperature

Raising the temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction — heat can be treated as a reactant in endothermic reactions and as a product in exothermic ones. Thus for the exothermic synthesis of ammonia, lower temperatures favour yield, though higher temperatures are used in practice to achieve acceptable rates.

Pressure and volume

For gas-phase equilibria, increasing pressure shifts the position toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Changing the volume of an inert-gas-containing system at constant pressure, or adding inert gas at constant volume, has subtleties that the simple statement of the principle can mis-handle.

Catalysts

A catalyst speeds the forward and reverse reactions equally and therefore does not change the position of equilibrium, only the time taken to reach it.

Limitations and rigorous basis

Le Chatelier's principle is a qualitative rule derived from the thermodynamics of equilibrium — more precisely, from the stability conditions on the Gibbs free energy. Careful analysis shows that naive applications can fail, for example when multiple changes are made simultaneously or when a reactant is added at constant pressure to a gas mixture (which dilutes the other species). The principle is nonetheless a reliable and widely taught heuristic for single perturbations. Analogous "moderation" principles have been formulated in economics (the Le Chatelier principle of Paul Samuelson) and other fields.
