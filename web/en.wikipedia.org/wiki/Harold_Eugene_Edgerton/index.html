Harold Eugene Edgerton (6 April 1903 – 4 January 1990), affectionately known as "Doc" Edgerton, was an American professor of electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and a pioneer of high-speed photography. He perfected the electronic stroboscope, allowing cameras to freeze events lasting microseconds, and produced some of the most famous scientific photographs of the twentieth century: a milk drop forming a coronet, a bullet piercing an apple, and the multiflash motion studies of athletes.

Career

Edgerton earned his doctorate at MIT in 1931 with work on synchronous motors, in which he used stroboscopic flashes to study rotating machinery. He joined the MIT faculty and in 1947 co-founded the company EG&G, which supplied high-speed photographic equipment for nuclear weapons testing, including the rapatronic camera that captured the first instants of atomic explosions. During the Second World War he developed powerful strobes for night aerial reconnaissance photography.

Photography and exploration

His 1936 photograph of a milk drop's splash and his bullet-through-apple image (1964) made the invisible visible and became staples of art and science alike; Gjon Mili used Edgerton's multiflash technique for celebrated studies of dancers and musicians. Later Edgerton turned to sonar: he developed side-scan sonar and sub-bottom profiling, worked with Jacques Cousteau aboard Calypso, and helped locate the sunken Civil War ironclad USS Monitor in 1973. He received the National Medal of Science in 1973 and remained an MIT institution, famous for demonstrations in his "Strobe Alley" laboratory, until his death.
