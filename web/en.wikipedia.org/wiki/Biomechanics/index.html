Biomechanics is the study of the structure, function, and motion of the mechanical aspects of biological systems, at any level from whole organisms to organs, cells, and cell organelles, using the methods of mechanics. It applies principles of physics — Newton's laws, continuum mechanics, fluid dynamics — to living tissues and movements, and draws on anatomy, physiology, and engineering.

History

The field's roots extend to Aristotle's On the Motion of Animals and Leonardo da Vinci's anatomical mechanics. Giovanni Alfonso Borelli's De Motu Animalium (1680) first applied rigorous mechanical analysis to muscle and joint action, earning him recognition as the father of biomechanics. In the nineteenth century, Étienne-Jules Marey and Eadweard Muybridge pioneered photographic motion analysis; in the twentieth, researchers such as Y. C. Fung established modern continuum biomechanics of soft tissues.

Applications

Sports biomechanics analyses technique to improve performance and reduce injury, using motion capture, force plates, and electromyography. Clinical and orthopaedic biomechanics studies gait, joint loading, implant design, and injury mechanisms, and informs prosthetics, orthotics, and rehabilitation. Cardiovascular biomechanics models blood flow and vessel-wall mechanics, contributing to stent and heart-valve design. Other branches study the mechanics of cells and molecules, plant growth and fluid transport, animal locomotion (including flight and swimming), and the design of bio-inspired robots. Forensic biomechanics assesses injury causation in accidents and litigation.
