Plaintiff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plaintiff (in English and Welsh practice since 1999, a claimant) is the party who initiates a civil lawsuit — the person or entity bringing a claim against another (the defendant) and seeking a legal remedy, typically damages, an injunction or specific performance. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving the claim on the balance of probabilities in most civil matters.

Standing and forms

To sue, a plaintiff needs standing (a sufficient connection to the claim — in US constitutional law, injury, causation and redressability) and capacity. Claims may be brought by individuals, companies, governments (as in state consumer-protection or antitrust suits, sometimes parens patriae for citizens), and collectively via class actions or group litigation where many plaintiffs share claims (mass torts, securities). Strategic plaintiffs and test cases — brought by advocacy organisations to change the law — are a recognised feature of public-interest litigation, from civil-rights era cases to modern climate suits against states and oil companies.
