Elfshot

Elfshot (Old English: ylfa gescot) is a condition in Anglo-Saxon and later British and Scandinavian folklore in which a person or animal suffers a sudden, sharp internal pain attributed to invisible arrows shot by elves. The Old English medical text Wið færstice ("Against a sudden stitch"), preserved in the Lacnunga, describes a charm against "elf shot", "god shot", and "witch shot", picturing loud riders passing over a burial mound and prescribing feversfew, red nettle, and song as the cure.

Beliefs and evidence

Sudden cramps, rheumatic pains, and illnesses of livestock were explained as the work of elf arrows. Neolithic and Bronze Age flint arrowheads found in fields were taken as the physical missiles of the elves and were worn as amulets or dipped in water given to afflicted cattle as a remedy; in Scotland such stones were also called "elf bolts" or "elf arrows". The same word-stock appears in the Old English poem Beowulf-era charms and in later Scots usage, where "elfshot" cattle were a stock explanation for unexplained animal deaths.

Interpretation

Folklorists read elfshot as a pre-scientific aetiology for abrupt internal pains, strokes, and appendicitis-like symptoms: an invisible projectile explained an invisible cause. The belief is part of a wider northern-European complex in which supernatural beings—elves, fairies, witches, and in Norse tradition the álfar—inflict illness by shooting, and it persisted in rural Britain into the nineteenth century.
