Leeches are segmented parasitic or predatory worms of the subclass Hirudinea in the phylum Annelida, closely related to earthworms (oligochaetes). About 680 species are described, of which roughly 540 are marine, 100 terrestrial and the rest freshwater. Most leeches live in freshwater habitats. The best-known species is the European medicinal leech, Hirudo medicinalis, used in medicine for millennia.

Anatomy and physiology

Leeches have soft, muscular, segmented bodies that can lengthen and contract, with a sucker at each end. Unlike other annelids they have a fixed number of segments (34 in most groups), no chaetae (bristles), and a reduced coelom largely filled with connective tissue. The anterior sucker surrounds the mouth; blood-feeding species have jaws armed with fine teeth or a protrusible proboscis. The gut of blood-feeders bears paired pouches (caeca) that store ingested blood — up to several times the animal's body weight — which symbiotic bacteria digest slowly, allowing the leech to survive many months between meals.

Leeches are hermaphrodites and reproduce sexually, depositing eggs in cocoons secreted by the clitellum. They move by looping ("leech-walking") with their suckers or by undulating swimming.

Feeding

Many leeches are predators of small invertebrates such as insect larvae, worms and snails, which they swallow whole or pierce and suck dry. Others are ectoparasites that feed on the blood of vertebrates — fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, including humans. A feeding leech's saliva contains hirudin, a powerful anticoagulant, together with other compounds that inhibit blood clotting and reduce inflammation; the bite is typically painless because the saliva also has anaesthetic properties.

Medical use

Leeches were a mainstay of bloodletting therapy from ancient Egyptian and Ayurvedic medicine through the 19th century, when France alone imported tens of millions of Hirudo medicinalis annually. In modern medicine, medicinal leeches have been cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration as a medical device (2004) for relieving venous congestion after reconstructive and reattachment surgery, and purified hirudin and its synthetic analogues (such as lepirudin and bivalirudin) are used as anticoagulant drugs.
