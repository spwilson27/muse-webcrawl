Inversion (discrete mathematics)


Field: Combinatorics
Related: Permutations, sorting

In computer science and discrete mathematics, an inversion in a sequence is a pair of elements that are out of their natural order. Formally, for a permutation or sequence A, a pair of indices (i, j) with i < j but A[i] > A[j] is an inversion. The total number of inversions, the inversion number, measures how far the sequence is from being sorted: a sorted sequence has 0 inversions, and a reverse-sorted sequence of n distinct elements has the maximum, n(n−1)/2.

Measures of presortedness

The inversion number (also called the Kendall tau distance from the identity permutation) is one of the standard measures of presortedness used in the analysis of adaptive sorting algorithms. Insertion sort runs in time proportional to n plus the number of inversions, which makes it fast on nearly sorted data. Other related notions use inversion vectors (Lehmer codes / factoradic representations), which encode a permutation uniquely.

Counting inversions

Inversions can be counted in O(n log n) time by a modification of merge sort, a classic divide-and-conquer exercise. The expected number of inversions in a random permutation of n elements is n(n−1)/4. The distribution of inversion numbers is given by the Mahonian distribution, whose generating function is the q-factorial.
