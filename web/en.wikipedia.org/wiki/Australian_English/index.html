Australian English (AusE, AusEng, AuE) is the set of varieties of the English language native to Australia. It began to differentiate from British English soon after the founding of the Colony of New South Wales in 1788 and was recognised as distinct by the 1820s. It differs from other varieties in accent, pronunciation, spelling, idiom, and vocabulary, while remaining highly mutually intelligible with them.

Phonology and accent

Australian English is non-rhotic and is distinguished by its vowel phonology, including wide diphthongs. Sociolinguists traditionally describe a continuum from "Broad" through "General" to "Cultivated" accents, correlated historically with class and attitude rather than region; regional variation within Australia is comparatively slight, though Queensland, South Australian, and Victorian features are sometimes noted.

Vocabulary

Australian vocabulary draws on British and Irish sources, thousands of loanwords from Aboriginal languages (such as kangaroo, boomerang, billabong), and numerous local coinages and diminutives (arvo, servo, bottle-o). Terms such as fair dinkum, mateship, and the bush carry particular cultural resonance. The Macquarie Dictionary, first published in 1981, is the standard reference.

Spelling

Spelling mostly follows British conventions (colour, organise acceptable alongside -ize), with notable exceptions: program is standard (not programme except historically for broadcast schedules), and the Australian Labor Party spells its name without the "u". Australian governments and publishers generally follow the Style Manual.

Grammar

Grammar is close to other English varieties, with informal features such as the generic second-person plural youse in some speech and the discourse particle but at the end of sentences in parts of Queensland and New South Wales.
