Activity (chemistry)

In chemical thermodynamics, the activity (symbol a) of a species is a measure of its "effective concentration" in a mixture, introduced by the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in 1907. Activity accounts for the non-ideal behavior of real solutions and gases, in which interactions between molecules cause thermodynamic properties to deviate from those predicted by concentration alone.

Definition

The activity appears in the expression for the chemical potential of a species i:

μi = μi⊖ + RT ln ai

where μi⊖ is the standard chemical potential, R the gas constant, and T the absolute temperature. Activity is dimensionless and is defined relative to a chosen standard state. For solutes it is commonly written as the product of a concentration term and an activity coefficient γ:

ai = γi · (ci / c⊖)

Activity coefficients

Activity coefficients measure deviation from ideality: in ideal behavior γ = 1. For electrolyte solutions, the Debye–Hückel theory and its extensions (such as the Pitzer equations) model how ionic interactions depress activity coefficients at low concentration. For gases, the analogous concept is fugacity.

Applications

Activities, rather than raw concentrations, properly appear in the equilibrium constant, in the Nernst equation for electrochemical cells, and in the definition of pH. Using activities allows equilibrium calculations to remain exact for concentrated and non-ideal systems.
