Spree killer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spree killings typically occur at two or more locations in rapid succession

A spree killer is someone who commits a murder of two or more victims in a short time, in a way that lacks a defined "cooling off" period between the murders. The term has been used by law enforcement, criminologists, and the media to describe offenders whose killings form a single continuous episode or a rapid sequence of episodes without an intervening return to normal life.

Definitions

Definitions vary. The United States Bureau of Justice Statistics traditionally defined a spree murder as "killings at two or more locations with almost no time break between murders". The Federal Bureau of Investigation distinguishes spree killers from serial killers (who kill in separate events separated by cooling-off periods) and from mass murderers (who kill multiple victims in a single event at one location). In practice the boundaries blur: some criminologists have argued that the spree category should be merged into a single "mass murder" or "multiple homicide" classification, since the cooling-off distinction is difficult to apply consistently.

Characteristics

Spree killings are frequently mobile: the offender moves from place to place, sometimes over great distances, and the sequence often ends only when the offender is captured, killed, or takes their own life. Motives may include revenge, financial gain (as in some robbery-homicide sequences), notoriety, or a final culminating act of violence. Because there is no cooling-off period, the offender's emotional state typically escalates throughout the episode.

Notable cases

Historical cases commonly classed as spree killings include the 1949 shooting in Camden, New Jersey, by Howard Unruh; the 1958 cross-country killings by Charles Starkweather; the 1982 Tampa-area killings by Bobby Joe Long were later reclassified; and various "going postal" and road-rage sequences. The 2011 Norway attacks and other combined mass-murder events are sometimes discussed in the same framework.

Classification debate

Some researchers, including the FBI's Behavioral Analysis Unit, have proposed abandoning the threefold mass/serial/spree typology in favour of a continuum of multiple homicide, arguing that the categories overlap and that cooling-off periods are poorly defined. Others retain the typology because it captures operationally useful differences for investigators and profilers.
