Synaptic pruning

Synaptic pruning, a phase in the development of the nervous system, is the process of synapse elimination that occurs between early childhood and the onset of puberty in many mammals, including humans. Pruning starts near the time of birth and continues into the mid-20s. During pruning, both the axon and dendrite decay and die off. It was traditionally considered complete by the time of sexual maturation, but this was discounted by modern neuroimaging studies.

The infant brain will increase in size by a factor of up to five by adulthood, reaching a final size of approximately 86 (± 8) billion neurons. Two factors contribute to this growth: the growth of synaptic connections between neurons, and the myelination of nerve fibers. The total number of neurons, however, remains approximately the same. After adolescence, the volume of synaptic connections decreases due to pruning.

Developmental course

Synaptic density follows an inverted-U trajectory that differs by brain region. In the visual cortex, synapse numbers peak within the first year of life; in the prefrontal cortex, peak density is reached later in childhood, and pruning continues into the third decade. Synapses that are frequently activated are strengthened and retained, while weak or unused connections are eliminated — a "use it or lose it" principle related to Hebbian plasticity and activity-dependent competition.

Mechanisms

Pruning is carried out in part by microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, which engulf synaptic elements tagged by complement proteins such as C1q and C3. Astrocytic phagocytosis also contributes. Molecular cues — including neuronal activity, neurotrophins, and guidance molecules — determine which connections survive.

Clinical relevance

Abnormal pruning has been hypothesized to contribute to neurodevelopmental conditions. Excessive pruning in adolescence has been proposed as a mechanism in schizophrenia (associated with complement component C4 variation), while insufficient pruning has been suggested in some forms of autism spectrum disorder. These hypotheses remain active areas of research.
