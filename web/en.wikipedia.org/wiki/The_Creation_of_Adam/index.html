The Creation of Adam (Italian: Creazione di Adamo) is a fresco painting by the Italian artist Michelangelo, forming part of the Sistine Chapel ceiling, painted c. 1508–1512. It illustrates the Biblical creation narrative from the Book of Genesis, in which God gives life to Adam, the first man. The fresco is part of a complex iconographic scheme and is chronologically the fourth in the series of panels depicting episodes from Genesis.

Composition

God, depicted as an elderly bearded man wrapped in a swirling cloak, reaches out with his right arm toward the languid Adam, who reclines on the earth at left. The two index fingers — God's straining with energy, Adam's limp and passive — nearly touch but do not meet, and this small gap has become one of the most famous and reproduced images in Western art. God is surrounded by a host of angels and figures, held within a shape that some modern observers have compared to a human brain or a uterus, though art historians treat such readings cautiously.

Context

The panel is one of nine central scenes from Genesis on the ceiling commissioned by Pope Julius II. Michelangelo, who considered himself primarily a sculptor, painted the ceiling largely single-handedly between 1508 and 1512, working on a specially designed scaffold.

Cultural impact

The near-touching hands have been endlessly parodied and referenced in popular culture, film posters, advertising, and other artworks, and the image is frequently used to symbolise humanity, creation, and the relationship between God and man.
