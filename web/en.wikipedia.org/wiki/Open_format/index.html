Open format

An open format can be implemented by many programs without restriction.

An open format is a file format for storing digital data, defined by a published specification (usually maintained by a standards organisation) that can be used and implemented by anyone. In general, open formats are royalty-free to implement, free of legal restrictions on their use, and are promoted to prevent vendor lock-in and ensure long-term access to stored data. The term is often used in contrast to proprietary formats, whose specifications are secret or encumbered.

Criteria

Definitions such as the one in the European Interoperability Framework typically require that the specification be public, that anyone may implement it without royalty or fee, that there be no discrimination against implementers, and that the format be maintained in an open standards process. A format may be an open standard (developed through a formal body such as ISO) or simply an open specification; debate surrounds formats associated with patented technology pools (as historically with some multimedia codecs).

Examples

  • Text and documents: HTML, plain text, OpenDocument (ODF), PDF (ISO 32000)
  • Images: PNG, JPEG, SVG, WebP
  • Audio/video: FLAC, Vorbis and Opus in Ogg containers; AV1/WebM
  • Data interchange: XML, JSON, CSV; fonts in OpenType
  • Archives: ZIP, tar, 7z

Policy

Governments and archives favour open formats for public records because they remain readable when a single vendor's product disappears. Several states have mandated open document formats for public administration, and digital-preservation institutions such as national libraries designate open, well-documented formats as preferred for long-term archiving.
