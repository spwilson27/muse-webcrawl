Subarachnoid hemorrhage

Specialty: Neurosurgery, neurology, emergency medicine

Symptoms: Severe "thunderclap" headache, vomiting, decreased consciousness

Causes: Head injury; ruptured cerebral aneurysm

Risk factors: High blood pressure, smoking, family history, alcohol abuse

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is bleeding into the subarachnoid space—the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. Symptoms may include a severe headache of rapid onset, vomiting, decreased level of consciousness, fever, weakness, numbness, and sometimes seizures. Neck stiffness or neck pain are also relatively common.

Causes

In general, head trauma is the most common cause. Spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage most often results from a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, a weakening in the wall of one of the arteries in the brain. Less common causes include arteriovenous malformations, bleeding disorders, and the use of blood thinners or sympathomimetic drugs such as cocaine.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is typically confirmed by computed tomography (CT scan) of the head, which shows blood in the subarachnoid space. If the CT is negative and suspicion remains, a lumbar puncture may reveal blood or its breakdown products (xanthochromia) in the cerebrospinal fluid. CT angiography can locate the source of bleeding, such as an aneurysm.

Treatment and prognosis

Management involves stabilization, control of blood pressure, and securing the aneurysm to prevent rebleeding, either by endovascular coiling or surgical clipping. Complications include rebleeding, vasospasm leading to delayed cerebral ischemia, and hydrocephalus. Subarachnoid hemorrhage is a medical emergency with high mortality: many people die before reaching hospital, and a substantial proportion of survivors have lasting neurological disability.
