The Taq Kasra (Persian: ایوان خسرو, Eyvan-e Khosrow; Arabic: طاق كسرى, "Arch of Khosrow") is the surviving monument of a Sasanian palace complex at Ctesiphon, in present-day Iraq, about 35 km south-east of Baghdad. Its immense brick vault — long the largest single-span vault of unreinforced brickwork in the world — is the only visible above-ground remnant of the ancient city that served for centuries as the royal capital of the Parthian and Sasanian empires.

Architecture

The monument is an iwan: a great barrel-vaulted hall open on one side, here some 37 metres high and spanning about 25 metres, flanked by a façade decorated with blind arcades. Traditionally attributed to the reign of Khosrow I (531–579 AD), though possibly begun earlier, the hall is thought to have served as the audience chamber of the palace, its floor once covered by the legendary Baharestan carpet. The vault was built without centring, using fast-setting gypsum mortar and inclined brick courses — a celebrated feat of Sasanian engineering.

History

Ctesiphon fell to the Arab armies in 637 after the Battle of al-Qādisiyyah, and the palace was gradually quarried for building materials; the southern wing was demolished in 1888 during a flood. The northern wing partially collapsed in 1909, and parts have been lost since. Floods, neglect and war have repeatedly threatened the structure: in 2019 a section of the vault's ceiling fell, and a section of the façade collapsed in 2023, prompting emergency stabilisation work by Iraqi and international teams.
