Spelling

Spelling is a set of conventions for representing the words of a language in writing. Where the spoken language has graphemes corresponding to its phonemes, spelling reflects an orthography — a standardised system of writing words. Spelling is one element of orthography, alongside capitalisation, hyphenation and punctuation.

Standardisation

Many languages developed standard spellings only with the spread of dictionaries and printing. In English, Samuel Johnson's A Dictionary of the English Language (1755) and Noah Webster's American dictionaries (from 1806) were major standardising forces; Webster deliberately introduced American spellings such as color and center in place of colour and centre.

Variation and reform

English spelling is famously irregular because it preserves layers of Old English, Norse, French and Latin borrowings and a largely fossilised pronunciation from before the Great Vowel Shift. Spelling reform movements — from the Simplified Spelling Board of 1906 to the Cut Spelling proposals — have repeatedly attempted, mostly unsuccessfully, to make English spelling more phonetic.

Depth of orthography

Linguists describe orthographies as "shallow" when spelling closely follows pronunciation (as in Finnish or Spanish) and "deep" when it encodes morphology and etymology at the expense of sound (as in English or French). Learning to spell is correspondingly harder in deep orthographies, and spelling bees — competitive spelling contests — arose in the United States partly as a response.
