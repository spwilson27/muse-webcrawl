Indian vulture crisis

Affected species: Indian vulture, white-rumped vulture, slender-billed vulture

Cause: Diclofenac poisoning

Period: 1990s–present

The Indian vulture crisis is a severe decline in populations of vultures on the Indian subcontinent that began in the 1990s. Populations of three species — the Indian vulture, the white-rumped vulture, and the slender-billed vulture — fell by between 97 and 99.9 percent, one of the fastest declines of any bird group ever recorded.

Cause

The collapse was traced to diclofenac, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug widely used to treat livestock. Vultures feeding on carcasses of cattle treated with diclofenac suffered kidney failure and died of visceral gout. A single contaminated carcass could kill many birds.

Consequences

With vultures gone, carcasses accumulated and feral dog populations rose sharply, increasing the risk of rabies. Parsi communities, whose funerary tradition of sky burial depends on vultures, were also affected.

Response

India, Nepal, and Pakistan banned the veterinary use of diclofenac in 2006, and captive breeding and vulture-safe zone programmes have since been established.
