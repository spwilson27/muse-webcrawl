The English Midlands is the central region of England, corresponding roughly to the early medieval kingdom of Mercia. It is bordered by Northern England to the north, Southern England to the south, East Anglia and the North Sea to the east, and Wales to the west. The Midlands is conventionally divided into the East Midlands and the West Midlands, which are also two of the nine official regions of England.

Geography

The region is largely low-lying plain, drained by the River Trent and the upper reaches of the River Severn and River Avon. The Peak District in the north-west rises to over 600 metres, and smaller uplands include the Charnwood Forest, Cannock Chase and the Malvern Hills. Major cities include Birmingham — England's second-largest city — along with Coventry, Leicester, Nottingham, Derby, Stoke-on-Trent, Wolverhampton, Lincoln and Northampton.

History

In the Anglo-Saxon period the region formed the powerful kingdom of Mercia, which under kings such as Penda and Offa dominated much of southern England. During the Industrial Revolution, the coalfields and metalworking trades of the Black Country and Birmingham made the Midlands a manufacturing heartland, earning Birmingham the title "workshop of the world" and "the city of a thousand trades". The Potteries around Stoke-on-Trent became the centre of the British ceramics industry.

Economy and culture

Traditional industries include metalworking, automotive manufacturing (Jaguar Land Rover, and historically British Leyland), ceramics, brewing, and hosiery and footwear in the East Midlands. Modern employment centres on logistics — the region's central location has made it a hub for warehousing and distribution — as well as professional services and advanced manufacturing. The Midlands accent and dialects vary considerably across the region, and local identities remain strongly tied to individual towns and cities rather than to the Midlands as a whole.
