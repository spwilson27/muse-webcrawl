Zero Defects (ZD) is a quality management concept and performance standard, associated with Philip B. Crosby, which holds that defects should be prevented by doing work right the first time, rather than merely inspected out, and that "zero defects" — not an "acceptable quality level" of some defects — is the only legitimate goal. It emerged in the United States defence industry in the early 1960s and became one of the most widely adopted (and debated) slogans of the quality movement.

Origins

The first Zero Defects programme was implemented in 1961–62 at the Martin Company's Orlando plant, which built the Pershing missile for the US Army. Confronted with delivery pressure and workmanship errors, management launched a motivational campaign asking every employee to pledge personal care in their work; deliveries reportedly achieved defect-free results, and the Department of Defense promoted the model across its contractors. Crosby, a quality manager at Martin, later codified the philosophy in his best-selling Quality Is Free (1979), arguing that the money wasted on rework — the "price of nonconformance" — far exceeds the cost of prevention.

Principles

In Crosby's formulation, quality means conformance to requirements; the system of quality is prevention; the performance standard is zero defects; and the measurement is the price of nonconformance. Management's task is to set clear requirements, supply the means to meet them, and spend time encouraging and helping people to do so — defects being caused by lack of knowledge or lack of attention, both of which management can address.

Criticism and legacy

Critics such as W. Edwards Deming objected that exhortations and slogans directed at workers ignore that most variation is built into the system, and that an absolute zero-defects target can distort reporting and behaviour. Statistical approaches, including Six Sigma's allowance of 3.4 defects per million opportunities, explicitly reject literal zero as a statistical standard. Nevertheless, ZD's central ideas — prevention over inspection, the cost of poor quality, and management responsibility — were absorbed into total quality management and modern quality practice, and the phrase remains a fixture of manufacturing culture.
