Melody

A melody (from Greek μελῳδία, melōidía, "singing, chanting"), also called a tune, voice or line, is a linear succession of musical tones that a listener perceives as a single, coherent entity. In its most literal sense, a melody is a combination of pitch and rhythm; more figuratively, the term can include successions of other musical elements such as tone color. Melody is typically the most memorable and recognisable aspect of a piece of music, contrasted with harmony (simultaneous pitches) and rhythm.

Elements

Melodies are often built from motifs and organised into phrases, analogous to sentences in language, frequently in balanced question-and-answer pairs (antecedent and consequent). The contour of a melody — its rise and fall in pitch — and its range, the distance between its highest and lowest notes, are key features. Movement between notes may be conjunct (by step) or disjunct (by leap).

Melody in musical traditions

In Western classical music, melody is usually supported by harmony and counterpoint, with composers developing themes through repetition, variation and sequence. Many folk and popular traditions are essentially monophonic or melody-led, with accompaniment subordinate. In jazz, a melody ("head") frames improvised solos over its chord progression. Non-Western systems organise melody through frameworks such as the Indian raga and the Arabic maqam.

Analysis and perception

Listeners group melodic notes into unified streams according to principles of Gestalt psychology — proximity, similarity and continuation. Expectation and memory for melody are central topics in music cognition; a melody that violates expectations can create tension and expression. Simple, singable melodies with clear phrasing tend to be most easily remembered.
