Chevron (heraldry)

A chevron is one of the ordinaries in heraldry, and is among the oldest charges borne on coats of arms. It consists of a broad inverted V shape, like the rafters or roof of a house, occupying roughly the middle third of the shield. In blazon it is described simply as a chevron followed by its tincture.

Argent, a chevron gules

Description

The chevron is formed by two diagonal bands meeting at an angle in the upper part of the shield, like a pair of rafters. Early heraldic writers offered various interpretations of its origin: it has been said to represent the roof of a house, a pair of boots, or the saddle of a knight. It is frequently found in the arms of families named Cheverell, and of builders and carpenters.

Variants include:

  • Chevron inverted — reversed, with the point downwards.
  • Chevronel — a diminutive of the chevron, narrower than usual; often borne in pairs.
  • Chevron couped — with its ends cut off so that they do not reach the edges of the shield.
  • Chevron rompu — broken, with the upper part displaced.
  • Chevron engrailed, invected, embattled, nebuly etc. — with one or both edges following the corresponding line of partition.

Usage

The chevron appears in civic, corporate and military heraldry as well as in family arms. In a number of armed forces, chevrons worn on the sleeve form the rank insignia of non-commissioned officers such as corporals and sergeants — a usage derived from the heraldic charge. When two or three chevrons appear on the same shield they are often blazoned as "chevronels" if reduced in size to fit.
