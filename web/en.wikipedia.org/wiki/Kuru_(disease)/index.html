Kuru (disease)

Kuru is a rare, incurable, and fatal neurodegenerative disorder that was formerly common among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea. It is a prion disease — a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy caused by an infectious protein (prion) — related to Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease. The word kuru comes from the Fore word kuria or guria ("to shiver" or "trembling"), a reference to the body tremors that are a classic symptom of the disease; it is also known among the Fore as the "laughing sickness" because of bursts of pathological laughter displayed by affected people.

Transmission and history

The disease spread among the Fore through funerary endocannibalism: relatives consumed the bodies of the dead, including the brain, in which prions are concentrated. Women and children, who ate the brain and organs, were affected far more often than men. At its peak in the late 1950s, kuru killed up to 2% of some villages' populations annually; over 2,700 deaths are recorded between 1957 and 2005. Researchers D. Carleton Gajdusek and Baruch Blumberg received the 1976 Nobel Prize for work showing kuru's transmissibility, and the disease declined steadily after Australian colonial authorities and missionaries suppressed cannibalism in the 1950s–60s. Because incubation can exceed 50 years, rare cases continued into the 2000s. Studies of survivors identified a protective prion-protein variant (G127V), evidence of recent human evolution under disease pressure.

Symptoms

Kuru progresses through ambulant, sedentary, and terminal stages: progressive cerebellar ataxia, tremor, and slurred speech give way to inability to sit or stand unsupported, dysphagia, incontinence, and dementia, with death usually resulting from infection or starvation within a year or two of symptom onset.
