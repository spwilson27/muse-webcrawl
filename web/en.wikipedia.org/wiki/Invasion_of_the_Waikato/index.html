The invasion of the Waikato was the largest and most consequential campaign of the New Zealand Wars. Between July 1863 and April 1864, British and colonial forces under Lieutenant-General Duncan Cameron invaded the territory of the Kīngitanga (the Māori King movement) in the Waikato region of the North Island, in a war promoted by Governor George Grey. The campaign ended with the Kīngitanga forces retreating into the rugged interior that became known as the King Country, and was followed by the confiscation (raupatu) of some 1.2 million acres (about 490,000 hectares) of Māori land under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863.

Background

The Kīngitanga had been established in 1858 to unify Māori resistance to land sales, with Pōtatau Te Wherowhero as the first Māori King, succeeded by his son Tāwhiao. After the Taranaki war of 1860–61, Grey rebuilt military roads south of Auckland — notably the Great South Road — and assembled the largest force yet seen in New Zealand, eventually some 12,000–14,000 imperial and colonial troops supported by gunboats on the Waikato River. In July 1863 Grey issued an ultimatum requiring Waikato Māori to swear allegiance to the Queen or retire beyond the Mangatāwhiri Stream; on 12 July 1863 imperial troops crossed the stream, and fighting began days later at Koheroa.

Campaign

Advancing along the river and the Great South Road, Cameron's forces bypassed or captured a series of fortified pā. Major engagements included the attack on the unfinished pā at Rangiriri (20 November 1863), where heavy British casualties accompanied the capture of the position and the surrender of some 180 defenders; the assault on the Orakau pā (31 March – 2 April 1864), famous for the defenders' refusal of an offer of safe passage and Rewi Maniapoto's stance; and the final battle at the Gate Pā near Tauranga (29 April 1864), a costly British repulse outside the main campaign area, followed by Te Ranga (21 June 1864). After Orakau, Tāwhiao and his followers withdrew across the Puniu River into the King Country, which remained effectively closed to Europeans for two decades.

Consequences

The confiscation of Waikato Māori land — including fertile districts awarded to military settlers who founded towns such as Hamilton — had lasting social and economic effects, and the invasion became a central grievance in Treaty of Waitangi claims. In 1995 the Crown formally apologized to Waikato-Tainui in a settlement acknowledging that the invasion and confiscations were unjust; the apology was delivered in the Waikato Raupatu Claims Settlement Act 1995, signed by Queen Elizabeth II. Historians such as James Belich have emphasized the sophistication of Māori fortifications and resistance in the campaign.
