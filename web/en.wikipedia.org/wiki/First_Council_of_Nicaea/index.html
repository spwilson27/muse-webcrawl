The First Council of Nicaea was a council of Christian bishops convened in the Bithynian city of Nicaea (now İznik, Bursa province, Turkey) by the Roman Emperor Constantine I. The Council of Nicaea met from May until the end of July 325, and was the first ecumenical council of the Christian Church.

Its main accomplishments were settlement of the Christological issue of the divine nature of God the Son and his relationship to God the Father, the construction of the first part of the Nicene Creed, establishing a uniform date for the celebration of Easter, and promulgation of early canon law.

The Arian controversy

The council was called to resolve the teaching of Arius of Alexandria, who held that the Son was created by the Father and thus subordinate — 'there was a time when he was not'. The council, influenced by figures including the young deacon Athanasius, declared the Son to be homoousios ('of the same substance') with the Father and issued anathemas against Arian propositions. Arius was exiled; controversy nonetheless continued for decades.

Other decisions

The council fixed Easter to be celebrated independently of the Jewish Passover calendar, addressed the Meletian schism in Egypt, and issued twenty canons on church discipline, including rules on clergy ordination and the standing of the lapsed.
