Signed by:Franklin D. Roosevelt

Subject:Forbidding the hoarding of gold coin, bullion and certificates
Superseded by:Gold Reserve Act of 1934; restrictions ended 1974

Executive Order 6102 is an executive order signed on April 5, 1933, by US President Franklin D. Roosevelt, "forbidding the hoarding of gold coin, gold bullion, and gold certificates within the continental United States". Issued under authority of the Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917 as amended by the Emergency Banking Act of March 1933, the order required all persons to deliver their gold coin, bullion and certificates to a Federal Reserve Bank or member bank by May 1, 1933, in exchange for payment at the statutory rate of $20.67 per troy ounce.

Provisions and exemptions

The order exempted gold used in industry, profession or art, and — following a clarifying amendment — gold coins having a recognised special value to collectors of rare and unusual coins. Jewellery was not covered. Violations were punishable by a fine of up to $10,000, imprisonment for up to ten years, or both. A handful of prosecutions followed, and the order became a lasting symbol, in American political folklore, of federal power over private wealth.

Consequences

EO 6102 was a step in taking the United States off the gold standard during the Great Depression. The Gold Reserve Act of January 1934 transferred the Federal Reserve's gold to the Treasury and authorised Roosevelt to devalue the dollar, which he did by raising the official gold price to $35 per ounce — a windfall that funded, among other things, the Exchange Stabilization Fund. Private ownership of monetary gold remained broadly prohibited for four decades. President Gerald Ford legalised it again by signing legislation in 1974, effective December 31 of that year, and his Executive Order 11825 revoked the Roosevelt-era orders. Gold clauses in contracts, abrogated in 1933, were upheld by the Supreme Court in the Gold Clause Cases of 1935.

Compare Executive Order 10340 (1952), Harry Truman's steel seizure order.
