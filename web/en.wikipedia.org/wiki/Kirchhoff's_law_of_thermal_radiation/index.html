Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation

At thermal equilibrium, emissivity equals absorptivity at each wavelength.

In heat transfer, Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation, formulated by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1859–1860, states that for a body in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings, the emissivity of the body equals its absorptivity at each wavelength and direction: a good absorber of radiation at a given wavelength is also a good emitter at that wavelength.

Statement

For radiation at wavelength λ, ε(λ) = α(λ), where ε is the spectral emissivity and α the spectral absorptivity. A perfect black body, which absorbs all incident radiation (α = 1), is therefore also the best possible emitter; this insight led Kirchhoff to define the universal black-body spectrum, later derived by Max Planck, and helped give rise to quantum theory.

Applications

The law underlies the design of thermal insulation, solar collectors, and radiant heating, and explains why a dark surface both heats up quickly in sunlight and cools quickly at night. It also applies in astrophysics, where it relates the absorption and emission spectra of gases in stars and nebulae.
