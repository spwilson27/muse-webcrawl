Lupus erythematosus

Lupus erythematosus
SpecialtyRheumatology, dermatology, immunology
TypesSystemic (SLE), cutaneous (CLE), drug-induced, neonatal
CausesAutoimmune; genetic and environmental factors

Lupus erythematosus is a collection of autoimmune diseases in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissues, producing widespread inflammation and tissue damage. The term is most often used for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which can affect the skin, joints, kidneys, blood cells, brain, heart, and lungs, but the family also includes skin-limited (cutaneous) forms, drug-induced lupus, and neonatal lupus. The name derives from the Latin lupus ("wolf"), a term applied in medieval medicine to ulcerating skin diseases thought to devour the flesh.

Classification

Systemic lupus erythematosus is the most common and most serious form. Cutaneous lupus erythematosus affects only the skin and includes chronic discoid lupus (DLE), subacute cutaneous lupus (SCLE), and acute cutaneous lupus (the butterfly rash). Drug-induced lupus, associated with medicines such as procainamide, hydralazine, and some TNF inhibitors, usually resolves when the drug is stopped. Neonatal lupus is a rare condition of newborns of mothers with anti-Ro/SSA antibodies.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms vary widely and come in flares. Common features include fatigue, fever, joint pain, and skin lesions — classically a butterfly-shaped (malar) rash across the cheeks and nose. Serious manifestations include lupus nephritis (kidney inflammation), serositis, cytopenias, and neuropsychiatric disease.

Cause and diagnosis

The cause involves genetic predisposition combined with environmental triggers such as ultraviolet light, infections, and smoking; the disease is about nine times more common in women. Diagnosis combines clinical criteria with immunological tests, particularly antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies.

Treatment

There is no cure, but hydroxychloroquine is recommended for nearly all patients with SLE, alongside corticosteroids and immunosuppressants such as mycophenolate for organ-threatening disease. Biologics including belimumab and anifrolumab are approved for SLE, and voclosporin for lupus nephritis.
