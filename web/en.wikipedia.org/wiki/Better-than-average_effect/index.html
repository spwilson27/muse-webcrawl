Better-than-average effect

Better-than-average effect
FieldSocial psychology
RelatedIllusory superiority, Dunning–Kruger effect, Lake Wobegon effect

The better-than-average effect (also known as illusory superiority) is a cognitive bias that causes people to overestimate their own qualities and abilities in relation to the same qualities and abilities of other people.

Evidence

In classic studies, large majorities of respondents rate themselves above the median in driving skill, intelligence, honesty, teaching ability and getting along with others — a statistical impossibility. In one survey of US high school students, most rated themselves above average in leadership and athletic ability, with many placing themselves in the top 1%.

Explanations

Proposed mechanisms include egocentrism (focusing on one's own performance), focalism, selective use of favourable comparisons and information asymmetries about peers. The effect is strongest for ambiguous, subjective traits and can reverse — a worse-than-average effect — for very difficult tasks. It is related to self-serving bias and unrealistic optimism.
