First Council of Constantinople

The First Council of Constantinople (Greek: Πρώτη σύνοδος της Κωνσταντινουπόλεως) was a council of Christian bishops convened in Constantinople in AD 381 by the Roman Emperor Theodosius I. This second ecumenical council, an effort to attain a consensus in the church through an assembly representing all of Christendom, except for the Western Church, confirmed the Nicene Creed by expanding it, forming the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, also known as the Nicene Creed.


The First Council of Constantinople (381), depicted in a later icon.

Background

The Arian controversy over the divinity of Christ had divided the church since the First Council of Nicaea (325). Theodosius, a Nicene Christian, became emperor of the East in 379 and sought to restore orthodox unity. New disputes had also arisen: Apollinaris of Laodicea denied that Christ had a human rational mind, while the Pneumatomachi ("Spirit-fighters", associated with Macedonius) denied the full divinity of the Holy Spirit.

Proceedings

About 150 Eastern bishops attended, initially presided over by Meletius of Antioch; after his death during the council, Gregory of Nazianzus — recently installed as bishop of Constantinople — presided, and after Gregory's resignation, Nectarius presided over the closing sessions. No Western bishops and no representatives of the pope were present; the council was recognised as ecumenical only later, notably at the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

Decisions

The council promulgated four canons and a revised creed:

  • The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed — an expansion of the 325 creed with an enlarged third article affirming the Holy Spirit as "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is adored and glorified". This is the creed still recited in most Christian liturgies.
  • Canon 1 reaffirmed the Nicene faith and anathematised the heresies of the Eunomians, Arians, Pneumatomachi, Sabellians, and Apollinarians.
  • Canon 3 declared that "the Bishop of Constantinople shall have the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is New Rome" — a ranking the Roman see long resisted.
  • The council condemned Apollinarianism and confirmed the condemnation of Macedonianism.

Significance

Together with Nicaea, the council defined classical Trinitarian orthodoxy: one God in three co-equal persons. The Cappadocian Fathers — Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus — supplied much of its theological vocabulary, especially the distinction between one ousia (essence) and three hypostases (persons).
