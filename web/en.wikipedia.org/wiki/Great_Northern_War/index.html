The Great Northern War (1700–1721) was a conflict in which a coalition led by the Tsardom of Russia successfully contested the supremacy of the Swedish Empire in Northern, Central and Eastern Europe. The war began with coordinated attacks on Sweden by Denmark–Norway, Saxony–Poland and Russia in 1700, when the fifteen-year-old Charles XII came to the Swedish throne. It ended with the Treaty of Nystad (1721), which established Russia as the dominant power in the Baltic and marked the beginning of Sweden's decline as a great power.

Background

By 1700 Sweden controlled a Baltic empire including Finland, Ingria, Estonia, Livonia and parts of northern Germany, won in the wars of the seventeenth century. Its neighbours resented these gains: Frederick IV of Denmark coveted Schleswig-Holstein, Augustus II of Saxony-Poland aimed at Livonia, and Peter I of Russia sought access to the Baltic Sea.

Course of the war

Swedish victories, 1700–1706

Charles XII knocked Denmark out of the war within weeks (Peace of Travendal, 1700), then crushed a larger Russian army at Narva (1700) in a snowstorm. Turning south, he campaigned in Poland for six years, installing Stanisław Leszczyński as king and forcing Augustus to renounce the Polish crown by the Treaty of Altranstädt (1706).

The Russian campaign and Poltava

In 1707 Charles invaded Russia with some 44,000 men. Peter's forces retreated, scorching the earth; the Swedish supply column under Lewenhaupt was mauled at Lesnaya (1708), and the Ukraine expedition failed to secure the support of Hetman Mazepa. Weakened by one of the coldest winters in European history, the Swedish army was decisively defeated at the Battle of Poltava (8 July 1709) and the remnant surrendered at Perevolochna. Charles escaped to Bender in Ottoman territory, spending five years in exile.

Swedish collapse, 1709–1721

The coalition re-formed: Russia overran the Baltic provinces and Finland (the "Great Wrath"), founded Saint Petersburg (1703) in conquered Ingria, and won naval victories at Gangut (1714). Prussia and Hanover joined the allies, taking Sweden's German possessions. Charles, returning in 1714, was killed by a projectile while besieging Fredriksten in Norway in 1718 — whether by enemy or friendly fire remains debated. His sister Ulrika Eleonora and then Frederick I made peace in stages: Stockholm (1719–20), Frederiksborg (1720) and finally Nystad (30 August/10 September 1721), by which Russia kept Ingria, Estonia, Livonia and part of Karelia.

Consequences

An estimated several hundred thousand soldiers died, most from disease, cold and starvation; the plague of 1710–13 devastated the Baltic lands. The war transformed the balance of power in eastern Europe: the Russian Empire was proclaimed in 1721, and Sweden entered its Age of Liberty with reduced territory and influence.
