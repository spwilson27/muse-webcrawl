Brehon law

Brehon law
Irish: Féineachas
Region: Gaelic Ireland
Period: Early medieval to 17th century
Early Irish law preserved in medieval manuscripts

Brehon law is the name given to the legal system of early Ireland, administered by professional jurists called brehons (Irish: brithem, plural brithemain). Written down in Old Irish from the seventh and eighth centuries in texts such as the Senchas Már, it governed Gaelic Irish society for nearly a thousand years, until it was finally displaced by English common law in the early seventeenth century.

Features

Brehon law was a civil rather than criminal system, enforced by sureties, distraint of property and community pressure rather than by a state. Wrongs — including injury and homicide — were typically compensated by fines: the éraic (body-price) and an honour-price (lóg n-enech) scaled to the victim's rank in a finely graded hierarchy of kings, nobles, freemen and dependents. The law tracts cover contracts, marriage and divorce, inheritance, land, clientship, bee-keeping and even the liabilities arising from cats and dogs. Judgments were given by brehons trained in hereditary law schools, and kings issued rulings but did not make law in the modern sense.

Decline

The system retreated with the Anglo-Norman and later Tudor conquests; the Statutes of Kilkenny (1366) forbade its use by the English settlers, and after the Flight of the Earls (1607) and legal decisions such as the Case of Tanistry, Brehon law ceased to operate, though its texts survive in later manuscripts.
