Airborne forces are military units, usually light infantry, transported by aircraft and dropped into battle by parachute or landed by glider or helicopter. Placed behind enemy lines, they are intended to seize key objectives — bridges, airfields, road junctions — and hold them until relieved by advancing ground forces. Paratroopers are typically volunteers trained to a high standard and regarded as elite troops.

History

The concept was developed between the world wars, notably in the Soviet Union, which staged large parachute exercises in the 1930s, and in Germany. In the Second World War airborne forces were used at scale: German Fallschirmjäger captured Fort Eben-Emael and seized Crete in 1941 (at such cost that Hitler curtailed further large drops), while the Allies mounted enormous airborne operations — the drops behind the Normandy beaches on D-Day in June 1944 and Operation Market Garden in the Netherlands that September, the largest airborne operation in history, which failed to hold its final objective at Arnhem.

Post-war development

Large combat jumps became rare after 1945, though airborne units remained prestigious rapid-reaction forces: the US 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions, the British Parachute Regiment, and Russia's VDV airborne troops are examples. Helicopters gave rise to the related concept of air assault or airmobile infantry, delivered directly onto the objective without parachutes.

Characteristics

Airborne troops trade heavy equipment for strategic mobility: dropped with only light weapons and limited supplies, they are vulnerable until link-up and depend on air superiority and accurate delivery. Modern airborne forces retain parachute capability for forced-entry operations while training also for air-landed and helicopter deployment.
