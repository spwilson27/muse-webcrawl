Ministry of International Trade and Industry

The Ministry of International Trade and Industry (通商産業省, Tsūshō-sangyō-shō), known by its English acronym MITI, was a ministry of the Government of Japan from 1949 to 2001. It was one of the most powerful agencies of the Japanese state during the postwar economic miracle, coordinating industrial policy, trade, energy and technology development.

History

MITI was created in 1949 from the wartime Ministry of Commerce and Industry and the Board of Trade. During Japan's high-growth era (roughly 1955–1973), MITI officials used tools such as foreign-exchange allocation, import licensing, tariff protection, directed credit and "administrative guidance" to nurture strategic industries — steel, shipbuilding, automobiles, consumer electronics and later semiconductors — while managing the decline of uncompetitive sectors. Its coordination of industry through deliberation councils and its role in crafting export-led growth made it a central exhibit in studies of the Japanese "developmental state."

Influence and debate

Economists debate how much of Japan's success MITI caused: some credit its industrial policy for building world-leading industries, while others note famous misjudgments, such as its early reluctance to support Sony's transistor licensing and its attempt to consolidate the auto industry, which Honda defied. MITI also played a major role in trade negotiations during periods of friction with the United States in the 1970s and 1980s.

Reorganization

In the central government reform of January 2001, MITI was reorganized and renamed the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI), which continues many of its functions.
