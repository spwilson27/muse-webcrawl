Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes

The Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes ("Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns") was a literary and artistic dispute that divided the Académie française and French letters from the 1680s into the early 18th century, over whether classical antiquity or the literature and arts of the present day represented the greater achievement.

The dispute

The quarrel began with a poem by Charles Perrault, Le Siècle de Louis le Grand (1687), which declared the writers of Louis XIV's age superior to Homer and Virgil. Jean de La Fontaine, Nicolas Boileau and Jean Racine defended the Ancients, arguing that true standards of beauty were established by antiquity; Bernard Le Bovier de Fontenelle and others sided with the Moderns, contending that human knowledge — and hence literature — progresses. Jonathan Swift satirised the English echo of the dispute in The Battle of the Books (1704), and the Ancients-versus-Moderns split re-emerged in Britain with figures such as Richard Bentley.

Significance

Historians treat the quarrel as a landmark of the early Enlightenment: a public debate over progress, authority and taste that prefigured later arguments about reason, tradition and modernity. The Moderns' victory in the literary field helped legitimise vernacular writing and new genres, including the novel.
