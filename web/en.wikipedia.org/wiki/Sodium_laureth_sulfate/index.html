Sodium laureth sulfate

IUPAC namesodium 2-(2-dodecoxyethoxy)ethyl sulfate (typical)
Other namesSLES, sodium lauryl ether sulfate
Chemical formulaCH3(CH2)11(OCH2CH2)nOSO3Na (variable n)
AppearanceColourless to pale yellow viscous liquid or paste

Sodium laureth sulfate (SLES), sometimes written sodium lauryl ether sulfate, is an anionic detergent and surfactant found in many personal care products such as shampoos, shower gels, toothpastes and liquid soaps. It is an inexpensive and very effective foaming agent.

Structure and production

SLES is prepared by ethoxylating dodecyl alcohol (lauryl alcohol), which is derived from coconut or palm kernel oil, and then converting the resulting ethoxylate to a sulfate ester and neutralizing with sodium hydroxide to give the sodium salt. The number of ethoxy units, commonly one to three, is indicated commercially by the degree of ethoxylation; the generic formula contains a variable number of ethylene oxide units.

Comparison with SLS

SLES is closely related to sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), but the ethoxylation step makes SLES considerably milder and less irritating to skin and eyes, which is why it has largely replaced SLS in consumer formulations. Both are effective surfactants that solubilize oils and suspend soil so it can be rinsed away.

Safety

Regulatory reviews, including assessments by the U.S. FDA and expert panels, have found SLES safe as used in cosmetics when formulated to be non-irritating. A recurring concern has been trace contamination with 1,4-dioxane, a by-product of ethoxylation; manufacturers can reduce it by vacuum stripping, and it is not considered a hazard at the trace levels found in finished products. SLES is not classified as a carcinogen by the IARC, NTP or OSHA. Like other detergents it can irritate skin and eyes with prolonged exposure and is moderately toxic to aquatic organisms before treatment in wastewater systems.
