Cost–benefit analysis

Cost–benefit analysis (CBA), sometimes also called benefit–cost analysis, is a systematic approach to estimating the strengths and weaknesses of alternatives. It is used to determine options which provide the best approach to achieving benefits while preserving savings in, for example, transactions, activities, and functional business requirements.

Process

A CBA typically involves: defining goals and alternatives; identifying all costs and benefits; assigning monetary values where possible; discounting future costs and benefits to present value; and computing indicators such as net present value, benefit–cost ratio and internal rate of return. Sensitivity analysis tests how results change with assumptions.

History and use

The concept is associated with French engineer Jules Dupuit in the 1840s and was formalised in U.S. water-project evaluation under the Flood Control Act of 1936. Since Executive Order 12291 (1981), major U.S. federal regulations have generally required CBA review by the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs.

Criticism

Critics note difficulties monetising health, life (the 'value of a statistical life'), environmental goods and distributional effects; the choice of discount rate heavily affects climate and long-term projects. CBA remains central to the policy cycle and project appraisal worldwide.
