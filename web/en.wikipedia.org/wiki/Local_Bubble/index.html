Local Bubble

The Local Bubble, or Local Cavity, is a relative cavity in the interstellar medium (ISM) of the Orion Arm in the Milky Way. It contains the closest stars and brown dwarfs, including the Solar System. It is estimated to be at least 300 light years across (some estimates give ~90 parsecs in radius, depending on direction) and its neutral-hydrogen density is less than one tenth that of the average ISM in the Milky Way (0.05 atoms/cm3 versus 0.5–1 atoms/cm3), and its gas temperature is comparatively high (millions of kelvin, making it a soft X-ray emitter).

Local Bubble
Size: at least 300 ly across
Density: ~0.05 atoms/cm3
Temperature: ~106 K
Location: Orion Arm, Milky Way
Formation: multiple supernovae, ~10–20 million years ago
Nearby features: Local Interstellar Cloud, G Cloud, Loop I Bubble

Properties

The Solar System has been traveling through this region for the last five to ten million years, and is currently inside the Local Interstellar Cloud (LIC), a smaller region of slightly denser gas embedded within the bubble. The LIC formed where the Local Bubble and the neighbouring Loop I Bubble met. The gas in the LIC has a density of ~0.3 atoms/cm3.

Formation

The Local Bubble is not spherical or spheroidal but seems to be narrower in the galactic plane, becoming more of a chimney shape extending into the galactic halo. It was created between ten and twenty million years ago by a series of supernova explosions — estimates have ranged from roughly 10 to 20 supernovae — most likely from stars in the Scorpius–Centaurus association moving into its current volume, which blasted out cavities in the diffuse interstellar medium. Ongoing star formation at the bubble's surface occurs in molecular clouds along its boundaries.

Contents

The bubble contains hundreds of stars within roughly 300 light years, including some of the brightest and nearest stars in the sky, such as Antares (in the Upper Scorpius association), Beta Eridani, and others. Direct measurements by Ulysses and other spacecraft of dust grains and interstellar helium flowing through the heliosphere sample the material of the LIC.
