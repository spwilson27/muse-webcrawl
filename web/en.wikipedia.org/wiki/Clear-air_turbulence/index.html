Clear-air turbulence (CAT) is the turbulent movement of air masses in the absence of any visual clues such as clouds. It occurs at high altitudes, typically near the tropopause, where aircraft cruising in apparently clear skies can be subjected to sudden, severe jolts. Clear-air turbulence is the leading cause of weather-related injuries in commercial aviation, most often to passengers and crew not wearing seat belts.

Causes

CAT arises primarily from wind shear — sharp gradients in wind speed and direction — especially along the edges of jet streams, where fast-moving air borders slower air. When the shear exceeds a threshold, Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities develop: wave-like overturning of the air that breaks down into turbulence. Mountain waves propagating into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere, and sharp temperature gradients near the tropopause, can also generate CAT away from jet streams.

Detection and forecasting

Unlike convective turbulence, CAT is invisible to conventional weather radar, which detects precipitation. Forecasts rely on numerical weather prediction diagnostics such as the Ellery index and Richardson number calculations, combined with pilot reports (PIREPs). Airlines dispatch flights to avoid forecast CAT regions, and researchers have tested forward-looking LIDAR systems that detect density fluctuations ahead of the aircraft.

Climate change

Studies by atmospheric scientists, including work by Paul Williams at the University of Reading, project that climate change will strengthen the vertical wind shear in the jet streams, significantly increasing the frequency of moderate-to-severe clear-air turbulence over the North Atlantic and other busy flight corridors by mid-century.
