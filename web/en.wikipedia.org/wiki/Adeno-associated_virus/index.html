Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is a small, non-enveloped virus of the family Parvoviridae that infects humans and other primates. AAV is a dependovirus: it can replicate productively only in cells co-infected with a helper virus such as an adenovirus or herpesvirus. Wild-type AAV is not known to cause disease and provokes only a mild immune response, properties that have made engineered AAV vectors one of the leading platforms for gene therapy.

Biology

The AAV genome is a single-stranded DNA molecule of about 4.7 kilobases flanked by inverted terminal repeats. It carries two main open reading frames, rep (encoding proteins involved in replication and site-specific integration into chromosome 19) and cap (encoding the capsid proteins VP1–VP3). Numerous natural serotypes (AAV1–AAV12 and many more) differ in tissue tropism, allowing vectors to be targeted to muscle, liver, retina, or the central nervous system.

Gene therapy vectors

In recombinant AAV (rAAV), the viral genes are removed and replaced with a therapeutic expression cassette of up to roughly 4.7 kb. The vector genome persists mainly as episomal concatemers in the nucleus, giving long-term expression in non-dividing cells. Approved AAV-based medicines include voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna, for inherited retinal dystrophy, 2017), onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma, for spinal muscular atrophy, 2019), and products for haemophilia. Limitations include pre-existing immunity to capsids, limited packaging capacity, and dose-related toxicity at high systemic doses.
