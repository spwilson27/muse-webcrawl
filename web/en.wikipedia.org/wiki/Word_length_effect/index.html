Word length effect

The word length effect is a phenomenon in cognitive psychology in which people remember lists of short words better than lists of long words. It was demonstrated systematically by Alan Baddeley, Neil Thomson, and Margaret Buchanan in 1975 and has become a cornerstone finding in the study of short-term and working memory.

Findings

Baddeley and colleagues showed that memory span was inversely related to the spoken duration of the words: participants could recall about as many words as they could say in roughly two seconds. Long words are forgotten more quickly than short ones because they take longer to rehearse subvocally, leaving less time before memory traces decay.

Interpretation

The word length effect is a central line of evidence for the phonological loop component of Baddeley's working memory model. Evidence includes:

  • The effect is abolished by articulatory suppression (repeating an irrelevant sound while learning), showing that it depends on subvocal rehearsal.
  • Patients with impaired phonological short-term memory show a reduced or absent word length effect.
  • The effect depends on spoken duration rather than the number of syllables per se.

Debate

Some researchers have argued that the effect is partly an output phenomenon — long words take longer to say at recall, and earlier items decay during that time — and others have questioned how cleanly it separates phonological storage from rehearsal. Nevertheless, the effect remains among the most robust findings in memory research and is often taught as evidence for a time-limited verbal rehearsal process.
