Greco-Buddhism

Greco-Buddhism is the cultural and religious syncretism between the Hellenistic culture of the Greek world and Buddhism that developed between the 4th century BC and the 5th century AD in the regions of Gandhara and the Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kingdoms of what is now Afghanistan and Pakistan.

Origins

Contact began with Alexander the Great's campaigns into Bactria and the Indus valley (334–323 BC). Greek settler kingdoms persisted for centuries; the Indo-Greek king Menander I (reigned c. 165–130 BC), known in Pali as Milinda, is remembered in Buddhist tradition for his dialogues with the monk Nāgasena, recorded in the Milinda Pañha.

Art

Greco-Buddhist art fused Greek naturalism with Buddhist subjects: the Buddha was depicted anthropomorphically for the first time, with wavy hair, classical drapery, and features reminiscent of Apollo. Figures such as Heracles appear as the Buddha's protector Vajrapani. The resulting Gandhara school influenced Buddhist art across Central and East Asia along the Silk Road, while the Bamiyan Buddhas were its monumental heirs.

Religious exchange

Under the Kushan emperor Kanishka, Mahayana Buddhism flourished and spread eastward. Scholars have debated possible Greek influence on Mahayana philosophy and Buddhist influence on Greek thought, including reported contacts with the Pyrrhonist tradition.
