Arrhenius acid

An Arrhenius acid is a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+) — more precisely, hydronium ions (H3O+) — when dissolved in water. The definition comes from the work of the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius, who proposed in the 1880s that acids and bases dissociate into ions in aqueous solution. Correspondingly, an Arrhenius base is a substance that increases the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH−) in water, such as sodium hydroxide.

Definition

Arrhenius defined an acid as a hydrogen-containing compound that ionises in water to give hydrogen ions, and a base as a compound that produces hydroxide ions in water. Neutralisation is then the combination of H+ and OH− to form water. Common Arrhenius acids include hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), nitric acid (HNO3) and acetic acid (CH3COOH).

History

Arrhenius presented his theory of electrolytic dissociation in his 1884 doctoral dissertation at Uppsala University and developed it in an 1887 publication. Although initially received sceptically, the theory explained the electrical conductivity of solutions and the near-constant heat of neutralisation of strong acids and bases. Arrhenius received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1903, in part for this work.

Limitations

The Arrhenius definition is confined to aqueous solutions and cannot explain the basicity of substances such as ammonia, which contain no hydroxide group, nor acid–base reactions in non-aqueous solvents or the gas phase. These limitations led to the broader Brønsted–Lowry theory (1923), in which an acid is a proton donor and a base a proton acceptor, and later to the Lewis definition based on electron pairs.
