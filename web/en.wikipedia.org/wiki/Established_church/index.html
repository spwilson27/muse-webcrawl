Established church

Established church
SynonymState church; state religion (broader term)
ExamplesChurch of England; Church of Denmark; Church of Greece; Roman Catholicism in Costa Rica and Liechtenstein; Islam in Saudi Arabia and Iran
OppositeDisestablishment; separation of church and state

An established church or state church is a religious organisation that has been officially endorsed by a sovereign state and enjoys a privileged constitutional position, often including state funding, a role in state ceremonies, and representation in the legislature. The broader term state religion covers any faith officially adopted by a state, whether or not it has a single institutional church.

Characteristics

Establishment typically involves mutual entanglement: the state confers privileges and protection on the church, while the church provides services to the state — coronations, national commemorations, chaplaincies to the armed forces and prisons — and may be subject to state control over appointments, doctrine or worship. In England, the monarch is Supreme Governor of the Church of England, bishops are appointed by the Crown on the advice of the prime minister, measures of the Church's General Synod require parliamentary approval, and 26 bishops (the Lords Spiritual) sit in the House of Lords, among them the Archbishop of York.

History

State sponsorship of religion is ancient — from Mesopotamian temple cults and the Roman imperial cult to Christianity's adoption under Constantine and Theodosius, who made Nicene Christianity the religion of the Roman Empire in 380. After the Reformation, the principle cuius regio, eius religio (the ruler's religion determines the realm's) structured much of Europe. The modern era has seen widespread disestablishment: the United States' First Amendment (1791), France's 1905 separation law, the disestablishment of the Church in Wales (1920) and of the Church of Sweden (2000), and the Church of Norway's loosened ties (2012–2017).

Present day

Surviving establishments range from the largely symbolic — England, Denmark, Greece, Iceland — to theocratic systems in which religious law is state law, as in Iran and Saudi Arabia. Many other countries give a particular religion official recognition, funding or concordats without full establishment. Debates continue over whether establishment preserves national heritage and a public moral voice, or privileges one faith in religiously plural societies.
