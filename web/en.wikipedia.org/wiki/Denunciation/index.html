Denunciation

Denunciation is the act of publicly reporting or condemning a person, group, or act, typically to an authority. The word derives from the Latin denuntiare, "to announce" or "to declare". Denunciation can be an act of civic duty, of moral protest, or — under repressive regimes — a tool of surveillance and persecution.

In politics and history

Authoritarian states have repeatedly relied on denunciations by citizens to identify supposed enemies. In Nazi Germany, the Gestapo depended heavily on voluntary denunciations from the public, many motivated by private grudges rather than ideology. In the Soviet Union, denunciation was institutionalised during the Great Terror, and the cult of Pavlik Morozov glorified a boy said to have denounced his own father. During China's Cultural Revolution, denunciation sessions and "struggle sessions" were used to humiliate and punish alleged class enemies.

Historians such as Robert Gellately have argued that such regimes functioned partly through the active cooperation of ordinary people who denounced neighbours, colleagues and rivals, which complicates simple accounts of terror imposed purely from above.

In law

In international law, denunciation has a technical meaning: it is the formal act by which a state withdraws from a treaty, under the treaty's own provisions or the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties. In some civil-law traditions, a denunciation (dénonciation) is a report of an offence to the public prosecutor by a person other than the victim.

Whistleblowing

The modern concept of whistleblowing is a form of denunciation directed against wrongdoing by organisations, but it is usually framed positively, as disclosure in the public interest, and many jurisdictions now give whistleblowers legal protection. The moral evaluation of denunciation thus depends heavily on what is reported, to whom, and with what consequences.
