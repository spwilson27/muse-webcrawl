Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children

Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C), or paediatric inflammatory multisystem syndrome (PIMS / PIMS-TS), is a rare systemic illness involving persistent fever and extreme inflammation following exposure to SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19. It can rapidly lead to medical emergencies such as insufficient blood flow around the body (a condition called shock). Failure of one or more organs can occur. A warning sign is unexplained persistent fever with severe symptoms following exposure to COVID-19. Prompt referral to paediatric specialists is essential, and families need to seek urgent medical help.

Signs and symptoms

Affected children always have a persistent fever. Other clinical features vary between children, and may include abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, a skin rash, red or cracked lips, a swollen tongue, bloodshot eyes (conjunctivitis), swollen hands and feet, joint pain, dizziness, and enlarged lymph nodes. In severe cases, blood pressure falls and the child can develop myocarditis (inflammation of the heart muscle) or shock requiring intensive care.

Cause

MIS-C is thought to result from a dysregulated immune response occurring several weeks after SARS-CoV-2 infection, rather than from the acute viral infection itself. Most affected children test negative for the virus by PCR but positive for antibodies, suggesting a post-infectious process. The precise immunological mechanisms remain under study; suggested factors include superantigen-like stimulation of T cells and autoimmunity.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria, laboratory evidence of inflammation (such as raised C-reactive protein, ferritin, and D-dimer), evidence of recent SARS-CoV-2 infection or exposure, and exclusion of other causes such as bacterial sepsis or toxic shock syndrome. The presentation overlaps with Kawasaki disease, and the two conditions are sometimes compared, although MIS-C typically affects older children and more often involves gastrointestinal symptoms and cardiac dysfunction.

Treatment

Treatment generally takes place in hospital and may involve intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), corticosteroids such as methylprednisolone, and in refractory cases biologic agents such as anakinra. Supportive care, including fluids, inotropic support for shock, and anticoagulation, is given as needed. Most children recover, though cardiac follow-up is recommended because of the risk of coronary artery abnormalities.

Epidemiology

The condition was first recognised in April and May 2020, when clusters of children with an unusual hyperinflammatory syndrome were reported in the United Kingdom, Italy, and the United States during the COVID-19 pandemic. MIS-C is rare, occurring in well under 1 in 1,000 children infected with SARS-CoV-2, but it is a serious complication. Incidence declined markedly after widespread vaccination and with later SARS-CoV-2 variants. A related condition in adults is called multisystem inflammatory syndrome in adults (MIS-A).
