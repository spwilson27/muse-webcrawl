Censorship in the United Kingdom has a long history shaped by laws on obscenity, blasphemy, sedition and official secrecy, though modern Britain has no general system of prior restraint for the press. Freedom of expression is protected under Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, given effect by the Human Rights Act 1998, but is qualified by laws protecting national security, public order and the rights of others.

History

From the 16th century the Stationers' Company licensing system controlled printing; Parliament let the Licensing Act lapse in 1695, ending systematic pre-publication censorship of the press — a milestone celebrated by Milton's Areopagitica tradition. The theatre remained licensed by the Lord Chamberlain under the Theatres Act 1843 until censorship of plays was abolished in 1968. Twentieth-century landmarks include the 1960 acquittal of Penguin Books in the Lady Chatterley's Lover obscenity trial, wartime censorship under the Defence Regulations, and the D-Notice (now DSMA) system of voluntary media restraint on security matters.

Film, broadcasting and online media

Films are classified by the British Board of Film Classification (founded 1912), whose age ratings are legally enforced for video works under the Video Recordings Act 1984 — the statute passed amid the "video nasties" controversy. Broadcasting is regulated by Ofcom under content standards. The Internet Watch Foundation maintains a blocklist of child sexual abuse material implemented by UK ISPs, and the Online Safety Act 2023 imposed duties on platforms regarding illegal and harmful content. Blasphemy offences were abolished in England and Wales in 2008 and in Scotland in 2021.

Criticism and debates

Debates continue over the breadth of official secrecy law, super-injunctions and privacy orders, counter-extremism duties in universities (the Prevent duty), and the balance between safety regulation and free expression online.
