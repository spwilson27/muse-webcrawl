AbbreviationMLS
IndustryReal estate
OriginUnited States, late 19th century
PurposeShared database of property listings and offers of compensation between brokers

A multiple listing service (MLS) is an organization and database through which real estate brokers share information about properties for sale and agree to cooperate with one another, including offers of compensation to brokers who bring buyers. MLSs originated in the United States around the end of the 19th century, when local associations of brokers met regularly to exchange listings, and they remain the backbone of residential real estate in North America; similar systems exist in many other countries.

How it works

A listing broker enters a property into the MLS with details such as price, size, features and the compensation offered to cooperating brokers. Other member brokers can search the database on behalf of buyers. MLS rules govern the accuracy and timeliness of listings, the obligations of members, and the sharing of data with public websites through Internet Data Exchange (IDX) feeds.

Governance and criticism

Most US MLSs are owned by local associations of Realtors affiliated with the National Association of Realtors (NAR), though independent and regional MLSs also exist, and consolidation has reduced their number to several hundred. The system has been criticised for commissions practices and restrictions on competition; antitrust litigation, including a series of class-action lawsuits over buyer-broker commission rules, led NAR to agree in 2024 to change its rules, removing offers of compensation from MLS listings and requiring written buyer agreements.

Outside North America

Many countries have no MLS tradition, with listings dispersed among agents or concentrated on public portals; others, such as Canada (whose systems closely parallel the US model) and the Philippines, operate MLS-like services.
