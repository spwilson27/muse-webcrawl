Split focus diopter

A split focus diopter, also known as a split diopter, is an auxiliary camera lens element — effectively half of a close-up lens — attached to the front of a primary lens so that only part of the frame is affected. It allows a cinematographer to keep a foreground subject on one side of the frame and a distant background subject on the other side simultaneously in sharp focus, achieving an extreme depth of field beyond what stopping down the lens could provide. A visible, slightly blurred dividing line runs through the image where the diopter's edge falls, which filmmakers usually conceal by placing the boundary along a natural vertical division or an out-of-focus area.

Use in cinema

The technique predates its famous 1970s heyday — Gregg Toland's deep-focus work and earlier split-field lenses anticipated it — but it became a signature of New Hollywood. Director Brian De Palma used split diopters extensively, often paired with split-screen, in films such as Carrie (1976), Dressed to Kill (1980) and Blow Out (1981), to stage voyeuristic compositions in which a character and a distant threat share the frame. Cinematographer Gordon Willis employed the device strikingly in All the President's Men. Because the lens's focal plane must be aligned so the diopter edge is unobtrusive, shots tend to be static and carefully composed.

Limitations

The blurred seam, the requirement that subjects sit on opposite sides of the frame, and the distortion of mid-ground elements limit the device. In the digital era, similar effects can be achieved by compositing two takes, though many directors still prefer the optical original for its authentic spatial tension.
