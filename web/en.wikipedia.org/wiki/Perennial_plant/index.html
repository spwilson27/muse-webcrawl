Perennial plant


Definition: Plant living for more than two years
Contrasts with: Annual plant, biennial plant

A perennial plant, or simply perennial, is a plant that lives more than two years. The term is most often used for herbaceous plants that die back to their roots in unfavourable seasons and regrow each year, but all woody plants—trees, shrubs, and vines—are also perennial by definition.

Life cycle and strategies

Herbaceous perennials survive winter or drought as underground structures such as bulbs, corms, tubers, rhizomes, or crowns, storing energy for the next season's growth. Unlike annuals, which reproduce once and die, perennials typically flower and set seed repeatedly over many seasons (polycarpy), though some, like the century plant, are monocarpic and die after a single flowering at the end of a long life.

Advantages and uses

Established root systems let perennials exploit soil resources early in the season, resist drought, and reduce erosion, which makes them valuable in landscaping, permaculture, and restoration planting: a perennial garden or food crop needs replanting only rarely, though it establishes more slowly than annuals. Many staple foods come from perennials—fruit and nut trees, asparagus, rhubarb, and most forage grasses and legumes—while grain agriculture relies mainly on annuals, a contrast motivating research into perennial grains such as Kernza.

Longevity

Perennial lifespans range from a few years for many garden flowers to millennia for clonal colonies and bristlecone pines, among the oldest known individual organisms on Earth.
