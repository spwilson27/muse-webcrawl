Fermi problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Fermi problem, also known as a Fermi question or Fermi estimate, is an estimation problem designed to teach dimensional analysis and approximation, and to show the importance of clearly identifying one's assumptions. It is named after physicist Enrico Fermi, who was famous for his ability to make good approximate calculations with little or no actual data — for example, estimating the strength of the first atomic bomb explosion from the distance travelled by scraps of paper he dropped as the blast wave passed.

The classic example: piano tuners in Chicago

The canonical Fermi problem asks: How many piano tuners are there in Chicago? It is solved by chaining together a series of reasonable estimates:

  1. Chicago's population is roughly 3 million.
  2. Average household size is about 2 persons, giving about 1.5 million households.
  3. Perhaps 1 household in 20 owns a regularly tuned piano: about 75,000 pianos.
  4. Pianos are tuned about once a year, so there are about 75,000 tunings per year.
  5. A tuner might complete about 2 tunings per working day, for some 250 working days a year — roughly 500 tunings annually.
  6. Therefore Chicago supports roughly 75,000 / 500 ≈ 150 piano tuners.

The result is surprisingly close to the true figure, because the estimates' errors tend to partially cancel and each factor need only be right to within an order of magnitude.

Method

Fermi estimation works by decomposing an unknown quantity into a product of factors, each easier to estimate than the whole. The technique is reliable when the factors are roughly independent and their logarithmic errors are distributed symmetrically, so overestimates and underestimates average out. Practitioners often bound each factor between plausible extremes and take the geometric mean. The method is widely used in physics, engineering, market sizing (job interviews in consulting and technology firms famously include such questions), and policy analysis where decisions must be made before data can be gathered.

Related ideas

The Drake equation, which estimates the number of detectable extraterrestrial civilizations, is a celebrated large-scale Fermi problem. "Back-of-the-envelope calculations" are the same practice in informal form. Fermi problems appear in physics education and in the Physics Olympiad to reward clear reasoning with sparse information.
