Circular breathing

A didgeridoo player storing air in puffed cheeks.

Circular breathing is a technique used by wind instrument players to produce a continuous, unbroken tone without pausing to inhale. The player stores air in the cheeks and squeezes it out through the instrument using the cheek muscles while simultaneously sniffing air in through the nose, and then resumes normal blowing from the lungs. Practised smoothly, the cycle can be repeated indefinitely: jazz saxophonist Kenny G famously held a single note for over 45 minutes using the technique in 1997.

Technique

To learn the technique, players usually practise by blowing a stream of bubbles through a straw into water: first filling the cheeks with air while blowing, then maintaining the bubble stream from cheek pressure alone while inhaling through the nose. The transition between lung air and cheek air is the difficult part and must be made seamless to avoid gaps or changes in volume and pitch.

Instruments and traditions

Circular breathing is essential to the continuous drone of the Aboriginal Australian didgeridoo and is used traditionally on instruments such as the Sardinian launeddas, the Persian sorna, the Indian shehnai and pungi, the Chinese suona, Armenian duduk, and various bagpipe-like drone instruments in which continuity is instead supplied by a bag reservoir. In Western classical and jazz performance it is occasionally used on flute, oboe, clarinet, saxophone, and brass instruments, sometimes for special effects such as the unbroken glissando ending of a phrase, or in contemporary repertoire that demands sustained tones.
