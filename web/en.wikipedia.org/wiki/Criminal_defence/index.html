Criminal defence

In criminal law, a defence is an argument raised by or on behalf of an accused person that seeks to challenge the validity of the prosecution's case and thereby lead to acquittal, or to a reduction of the charge or sentence. Defences fall broadly into two categories: those that deny an element of the offence (such as the actus reus or mens rea), and affirmative defences, which concede the conduct but assert a justification or excuse.

Types of defence

  • Denial and alibi — the defendant was elsewhere, or did not commit the act.
  • Mistake of fact — an honest (and sometimes reasonable) mistaken belief negating intent.
  • Self-defence — use of reasonable force against an unlawful attack; see self-defence in criminal law.
  • Necessity and duress — the act was committed to avoid a greater harm, or under threat of death or serious injury.
  • Insanity — under the M'Naghten rules and similar tests, a defect of reason from disease of the mind such that the defendant did not know the nature of the act or that it was wrong.
  • Diminished responsibility / capacity — partial defence reducing murder to manslaughter in some jurisdictions.
  • Automatism and intoxication — the act was involuntary, or intoxication negated the required specific intent.
  • Entrapment — state inducement of a crime the defendant was not predisposed to commit.
  • Consent and lawful authority (e.g. police, surgery, sport).

Burden and standard of proof

Generally the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, and the defendant need prove nothing. For most affirmative defences the defendant bears only an evidential burden — enough evidence to raise the issue — after which the prosecution must disprove it. For a few defences, notably insanity in English law, the legal burden rests on the defendant on the balance of probabilities. Constitutional protections such as the presumption of innocence, the right to counsel and protection against self-incrimination frame the defence function in modern systems.

Defence counsel

A criminal defence lawyer represents the accused, tests the prosecution's evidence, advises on pleas, and presents the defence case. Public defenders or legal aid schemes provide representation for those unable to afford it in many jurisdictions.
