Incunable

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An incunable (plural incunabula; also incunabulum) is a book, pamphlet, or broadside printed in Europe — or, in the extended sense, with the earliest printing technologies elsewhere — before the year 1501. The term, from the Latin incunabula meaning "swaddling clothes" or "cradle," was applied to these works in the seventeenth century to evoke the infancy of printing. The plural form is often used in English as though it were singular.

Characteristics

Incunabula were produced in the first decades after Johannes Gutenberg's development of printing with movable type in Mainz around 1450. Early examples closely imitate manuscripts: they often lack title pages, leave spaces for hand-painted initials and decoration (rubrication), and were frequently finished by hand after printing. In total, an estimated 30,000 or more distinct editions were printed, and surviving copies now number in the hundreds of thousands, held by libraries around the world.

Study and cataloguing

Because 1500 is a conventional rather than a technological boundary — printing practice changed little in 1501 — the category is essentially a bibliographical convenience for the first half-century of European print. The major union catalogue of incunabula is the Incunabula Short Title Catalogue (ISTC), maintained by the British Library, which records editions and the locations of surviving copies.
