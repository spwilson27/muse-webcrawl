Small-angle approximation

The small-angle approximations can be used to approximate the values of the main trigonometric functions, provided that the angle in question is small and is measured in radians:

sin θ ≈ θ,   cos θ ≈ 1 − θ²/2,   tan θ ≈ θ

These approximations have a wide range of uses in branches of physics and engineering, including mechanics, electromagnetism, optics, cartography, astronomy, and computer science. One reason for this is that they can greatly simplify differential equations that do not need to be answered with absolute precision.

Justification

Graphically, as the angle θ shrinks toward zero, the sine and tangent curves converge on the line y = θ, while the cosine flattens toward 1. Analytically, the approximations follow from the Taylor (Maclaurin) series expansions of the trigonometric functions around zero, keeping only the lowest-order terms. Limits in calculus give the same result: sin θ / θ approaches 1 as θ approaches 0.

Applications

In astronomy, the small-angle approximation underlies the angular diameter distance relation: the linear size of an object equals its angular size (in radians) multiplied by its distance. In physics it simplifies the analysis of the simple pendulum, whose period becomes independent of amplitude for small swings. In optics it leads to paraxial (Gaussian) optics.

Error

The relative error of sin θ ≈ θ remains below about 1 percent for angles under roughly 0.24 radians (about 14 degrees), making the approximation practical for many engineering purposes.
