Mexican muralism

Mexican muralism was a Mexican art movement that took place primarily in the 1920s to 1950s. The movement is perhaps best exemplified by the work of the "three greats" — Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco and David Alfaro Siqueiros — who painted large-scale murals on public buildings with social and political messages.

Origins

After the Mexican Revolution, the new government of Álvaro Obregón, through education minister José Vasconcelos, commissioned artists to decorate public buildings in order to promote national identity, literacy and revolutionary ideals to a largely illiterate population. Early commissions began in 1921 at the National Preparatory School in Mexico City. The muralists drew inspiration from Mexico's pre-Columbian art, Mexican folk traditions, Italian Renaissance fresco and European modernism.

The three greats

Rivera's murals, such as those at the National Palace and the Secretariat of Public Education, celebrate Mexican history and the dignity of labour. Orozco's work is darker and more tragic, meditating on violence and human suffering, while Siqueiros experimented with industrial materials and radical political themes. All three also worked in the United States in the 1930s, where Rivera's destroyed Man at the Crossroads at Rockefeller Center became a famous censorship controversy. Rufino Tamayo and Juan O'Gorman were other prominent muralists.

Legacy

Mexican muralism transformed public art in Mexico and strongly influenced later movements, including the Chicano mural movement in the United States and community muralism worldwide.
