Electra complex

In neo-Freudian psychology, the Electra complex is a theory of a girl's psychosexual development corresponding to the male Oedipus complex. It describes a daughter's sense of competition with her mother for the affections of her father, arising during the phallic stage of psychosexual development, roughly between the ages of three and six.

Origin of the term

The term was coined by Carl Jung in 1913, drawing on the Greek myth of Electra, daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, who persuaded her brother Orestes to help her avenge their father's murder by killing their mother and her lover Aegisthus. Sigmund Freud himself preferred to speak of the "feminine Oedipus attitude" and never adopted Jung's term, one of several points of disagreement that contributed to their split.

Theory

In Jung's formulation, the complex involves a girl's initial attachment to her mother giving way to attraction toward her father and rivalry with her mother. Resolution is held to occur when the girl represses these desires and identifies with her mother, which Freud's followers connected to the development of the superego and adult gender identity.

Criticism

Like much of classical psychoanalysis, the Electra complex has been criticised as unfalsifiable, culturally specific, and lacking empirical support. Feminist theorists have also challenged its assumptions about female development. It nonetheless remains influential in literary criticism and popular culture.
