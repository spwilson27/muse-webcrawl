Wilhelm Roux

Wilhelm Roux (9 June 1850 – 15 September 1924) was a German zoologist and pioneer of experimental embryology. He founded the discipline he called Entwicklungsmechanik ("developmental mechanics"), shifting embryology from descriptive observation toward experiment, and founded the journal Archiv für Entwicklungsmechanik der Organismen, later renamed Roux's Archives of Developmental Biology.

Work

Influenced by Ernst Haeckel, under whom he studied at Jena, and by Darwin's theory of natural selection, Roux proposed that development could be understood through a "struggle of the parts" within the organism. In his famous 1888 experiment, he used a hot needle to kill one cell of a two-cell frog embryo; the surviving cell developed into a half-embryo. Roux concluded that the frog's development followed a "mosaic" pattern, with the fate of each cell determined early — a result contrasted with Hans Driesch's later finding that separated sea urchin blastomeres could each form a complete larva, suggesting regulative development.

Legacy

Roux's insistence on experiment over description transformed developmental biology, and his program of Entwicklungsmechanik shaped the research of a generation, including Hans Spemann. The tension between mosaic and regulative models that his frog experiment opened remains a central theme of embryology.
