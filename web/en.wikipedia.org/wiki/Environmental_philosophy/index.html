Environmental philosophy

Environmental philosophy is a branch of philosophy concerned with the natural environment and humans' place within it. It asks crucial questions about human environmental relations: What are we saying when we speak of nature having intrinsic value? What is the moral status of non-human animals and plants? How should humans respond to environmental challenges such as pollution and climate change?

Scope

Environmental philosophy includes environmental ethics, environmental aesthetics, ecofeminism, environmental hermeneutics, and environmental theology. A central dispute opposes anthropocentrism, which values nature for its usefulness to humans, to non-anthropocentric views such as biocentrism and ecocentrism, which attribute inherent value to living things or to ecosystems as wholes.

History

The field emerged as an academic discipline in the 1970s, in parallel with the environmental movement. Important early contributions included Richard Routley's "last man" thought experiment, Arne Næss's distinction between deep ecology and shallow environmentalism, and Aldo Leopold's "land ethic" from A Sand County Almanac (1949), which Leopold summarised as: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community."

Key themes

  • Intrinsic value in nature
  • The moral considerability of animals, plants, species and ecosystems
  • Wilderness and the idea of the wild
  • Environmental justice and the distribution of environmental harms
  • Intergenerational obligations in the face of climate change
