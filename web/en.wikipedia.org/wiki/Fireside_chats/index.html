Fireside chats

The fireside chats were a series of evening radio addresses given by United States President Franklin D. Roosevelt between 1933 and 1944. Speaking in a calm, conversational tone directly to the American public, Roosevelt used the new medium of radio to explain his New Deal policies during the Great Depression and, later, the course of the Second World War. The chats — around thirty in total — are considered a landmark in political communication and in the rhetorical presidency.

The first chats

Roosevelt gave the first chat on 12 March 1933, eight days after taking office and during the national bank holiday he had declared to arrest a banking panic. Explaining in plain language what the banks did and why they could reopen safely, he urged listeners to have confidence; deposits exceeded withdrawals when the banks reopened, and the address is credited with helping to restore trust in the banking system. A second chat in May 1933 outlined the legislation of the First Hundred Days.

Style and audience

The name "fireside chat" was coined by a CBS executive, Harry Butcher, evoking the image of the President speaking informally by a fireside. Roosevelt spoke slowly, at roughly half the pace of normal conversation, used simple vocabulary and the pronoun "you", and typically began "My friends." Audiences were enormous for their time — tens of millions of listeners — and the volume of mail to the White House increased greatly after each broadcast.

Later chats

Subsequent chats explained the banking and securities reforms, the drought of 1936, the controversial Court-packing plan of 1937, and the recession of 1937–38. During the war years Roosevelt used the chats to report on the progress of the war, announce the fall of Rome, and call for sacrifice on the home front, including war bond purchases and scrap drives. The final chat was delivered on 12 June 1944.

Legacy

The fireside chats established radio — and by extension each new mass medium — as a direct channel between president and public, bypassing the press. Later presidents imitated the form, from televised addresses to weekly radio talks and social-media messages, though few matched Roosevelt's combination of intimacy and policy exposition.
