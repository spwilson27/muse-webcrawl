Grammar school

Historic grammar school buildings often date from the Victorian expansion of endowed schools.

A grammar school is one of several kinds of historically related but distinct schools. Originally a school teaching Latin grammar, the term now chiefly denotes selective secondary schools in the United Kingdom and some Commonwealth countries, while in the United States it was historically a synonym for an elementary school.

Origins

Grammar schools arose in medieval Europe attached to cathedrals and monasteries, where the curriculum centred on Latin — the language of the Church, law and scholarship. In England many were refounded or newly endowed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, some by royal charter (hence "Royal Grammar School"), and the Endowed Schools Act 1869 reorganised hundreds of them. Places such as Manchester Grammar School (1515) and the schools associated with Winchester and Eton exemplify the tradition, though grammar schools were separate from the elite "public schools".

Grammar schools in the modern UK

Under the Education Act 1944, state secondary education in England, Wales and Northern Ireland was organised on the "tripartite" model — grammar, secondary technical and secondary modern schools — with pupils allocated mainly by the eleven-plus examination at age 11. Grammar schools offered an academic curriculum leading to O-levels and A-levels and university entrance.

From the 1960s, most local authorities replaced selection with comprehensive schools. A minority of areas — including Kent, Buckinghamshire, Lincolnshire, Trafford and parts of London — retained fully or partly selective systems. About 163 state grammar schools remain in England, plus 69 in Northern Ireland; none remain in Wales or Scotland. Admission is still largely by an 11-plus style test. The schools consistently top GCSE and A-level league tables, but their social selectivity is politically contentious: critics argue they cream off affluent, tutored pupils, while supporters defend them as engines of social mobility. A statutory ban on new grammar schools in England, enacted in 1998, remains in force, though existing schools may expand and "annexes" have opened.

Elsewhere

  • United States — "grammar school" was the traditional name for elementary schools (grades 1–8, later 1–6); the Boston Latin School (1635) began as a grammar school in the older Latin sense.
  • Australia — many independent schools carry the name (e.g. Sydney Grammar School, Melbourne Grammar School), without the UK selective-state meaning.
  • Canada and New Zealand — historic secondary schools such as Auckland Grammar School retain the name and, in Auckland's case, a strong academic reputation.
  • Germany — the Gymnasium is the functional equivalent of the academic grammar school track.
