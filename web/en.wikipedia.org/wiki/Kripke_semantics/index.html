Kripke semantics

Kripke semantics (also known as relational semantics or frame semantics, and often confused with possible world semantics) is a formal semantics for non-classical logic systems created in the 1950s and 1960s by Saul Kripke, then still a teenager. Kripke semantics have found influential applications in modal logic, intuitionistic logic, multi-valued logics, computer science and linguistics. The core idea is to evaluate formulas at points of a structure consisting of a set of "worlds" (or states) together with relations between them and a valuation assigning truth values to atomic formulas at each world; modal operators are interpreted by quantifying over accessible worlds.

Basic definitions

A Kripke frame consists of a non-empty set W of worlds (nodes, states or moments) and binary accessibility relations on W — one for each modal operator (in intuitionistic logic, a partial order; in temporal logic, orderings on time points). A Kripke model adds a valuation V specifying, for each propositional variable and world, whether the variable is true at that world. Satisfaction is then defined recursively; for modal logic the crucial clauses are:

  • A world w satisfies □A ("necessarily A") iff every world u accessible from w (wRu) satisfies A.
  • A world w satisfies ◇A ("possibly A") iff some accessible world satisfies A.

A formula is valid on a frame if it is true at every world under every valuation, and valid in a logic if valid on all frames of the corresponding class. Kripke proved completeness results connecting the standard modal systems (K, T, S4, S5, B) with simple conditions on frames: for example, the axiom □A → A is valid exactly on reflexive frames; the S4 axioms correspond to reflexive, transitive frames; and S5 to frames whose accessibility is an equivalence relation.

Frame correspondence

The tight match between axioms and first-order conditions on frames gave rise to correspondence theory and to "canonical model" completeness proofs. The framework also supplies simple countermodels: to show that a formula is not derivable in a system, one exhibits a finite Kripke model in which it fails. Frame definability, the Sahlqvist theorem (1975), and duality theory between frames and modal algebras developed these ideas algebraically. Variations include neighbourhood semantics (a generalisation), two-dimensional semantics, and dynamic epistemic logic, in which models are transformed by informational events — extensively used in computer science for the verification of protocols and multi-agent systems.

Applications

  • Modal logic: alethic, deontic, epistemic, temporal and doxastic logics all use Kripke-style semantics, with accessibility read as epistemic possibility, temporal succession, obligation-ideality and so on.
  • Intuitionistic logic: Kripke's semantics (1963) models truth as persistence along an information order, with atomic truth monotone along the ordering; failure of excluded middle is visualised by worlds that may later decide a proposition.
  • Philosophy: the possible-worlds framework underlies Kripke's Naming and Necessity (lectures 1970; published 1972) and David Lewis's modal realism debates.
  • Computer science: Kripke structures are the standard model for model checking of hardware and software, where states are worlds and transition relations are accessibility.

History

Kripke developed modal model theory in papers from 1959 (age 18–19) onwards, including "A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic" (1959) and "Semantical Considerations on Modal Logic" (1963). Related ideas were developed independently by Stig Kanger, Jaakko Hintikka, E. J. Lemmon and others, but Kripke's formulation — especially the use of varying domains and accessibility relations — became canonical. Ruth Barcan Marcus had earlier pioneered quantified modal logic; Kripke semantics gave quantified modal logic its standard possible-worlds treatment.
