35 mm movie film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

35 mm movie film is a film stock format with a width of 35 millimetres (1.4 inches) that was the standard gauge for motion pictures for over a century. Introduced by William Dickson and Thomas Edison in the 1890s for the Kinetoscope, it became the universal medium of theatrical cinema and remained dominant until the transition to digital cinematography in the 2000s and 2010s.

Technical description

The film runs vertically through conventional cameras and projectors, with sprocket holes (perforations) along both edges used to advance the film. The standard frame, in the Academy format adopted in 1932, has an aspect ratio of 1.37:1 and a height of four perforations ("4-perf"). Anamorphic prints squeeze a 2.39:1 widescreen image into the frame, while formats such as VistaVision run the film horizontally for a larger image area. Sound is carried on an optical soundtrack printed along one edge of the film, later joined by digital sound data for systems such as Dolby Digital and DTS.

History

Edison's 35 mm standard, with four pairs of sprocket holes per image, was confirmed as the international norm at a 1909 congress of film makers. Early film base was highly flammable cellulose nitrate, replaced by safety acetate base around 1948–1951 after disastrous vault fires. Eastmancolor and other colour processes became standard in the 1950s. Reels were typically shipped in lengths of about 1,000 or 2,000 feet; at the standard sound speed of 24 frames per second, film runs at 90 feet per minute.

Decline and survival

From around 2009, digital projection rapidly displaced film in cinemas, and major manufacturers ceased mass production of motion picture cameras and, for a time, film stock. Nevertheless 35 mm survives in archival preservation, in repertory screenings, and in the work of directors who prefer to shoot on film; Kodak continues to manufacture 35 mm stocks for these markets.
