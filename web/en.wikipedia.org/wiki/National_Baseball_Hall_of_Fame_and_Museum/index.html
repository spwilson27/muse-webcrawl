National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum

The National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum is a history museum and hall of fame in Cooperstown, New York, operated by private interests. It serves as the central point of the history of baseball in the United States and displays baseball-related artefacts and exhibits, honouring those who have excelled in playing, managing and serving the sport. The first five inductees — Ty Cobb, Babe Ruth, Honus Wagner, Christy Mathewson and Walter Johnson — were elected in 1936, and the museum was dedicated on June 12, 1939.

History and the Cooperstown myth

The Hall was founded by Stephen Carlton Clark, a Cooperstown hotelier seeking to bring tourists to the village during the Great Depression. Its location rested on the Mills Commission's 1907 finding that Abner Doubleday had invented baseball in Cooperstown in 1839 — a story now regarded by historians as a myth, though it remains central to the Hall's founding legend; the spurious "Doubleday ball" was displayed at the dedication. The 1939 centennial celebrations, including the first induction ceremony, drew huge crowds and established Cooperstown as baseball's symbolic home.

Election

Players are elected principally by the Baseball Writers' Association of America (BBWAA), which votes on players retired for five years; candidates must receive 75 percent of ballots cast to be elected. A separate Era Committees process considers players overlooked by the writers, managers, umpires and executives. As of the most recent inductions, the Hall has more than 340 members, including over 270 former major league players, plus executives, managers, umpires and Negro leagues figures. Induction weekend, held each July, draws tens of thousands of fans to Cooperstown.

Collections

The museum holds some 40,000 artefacts, 140,000 baseball cards, and a vast photographic and media archive; its library is a leading research centre for baseball history. Notable items include the bat Babe Ruth used for his "called shot", the ball from Don Larsen's perfect game in the 1956 World Series, and exhibits on the Negro leagues, women's baseball, and the game's global spread. Exhibits have also addressed controversial eras, including the steroid era, whose leading figures, such as Barry Bonds and Roger Clemens, have not been elected by the writers.
