Stoning of the Devil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Stoning of the Devil (Arabic: رمي الجمرات ramy al-jamarāt, lit. "throwing of the jamarāt") is part of the annual Islamic Hajj pilgrimage to the holy city of Mecca in Saudi Arabia. During the ritual, Muslim pilgrims throw pebbles at three walls (formerly pillars), called jamarāt, in the city of Mina just east of Mecca. It is one of a series of ritual acts that must be performed in the Hajj.

Religious significance

The ritual re-enacts the pilgrimage of Abraham, in which the Devil (Shaytan) appeared three times to tempt Abraham, his wife Hagar, and his son Ishmael away from obedience to God's command. At each place, the angel Gabriel instructed them to throw stones at the Devil, and he fled. The stoning thus symbolizes the pilgrim's rejection of evil and temptation.

Performance

Pilgrims collect pebbles — traditionally 49 or 70, gathered at Muzdalifah or in Mina — and throw seven at the largest jamrah on the day of Eid al-Adha, then seven at each of the three jamarāt on the following days. Since 2004 the pillars have been replaced by long walls, and a vast multi-level Jamarat Bridge carries pilgrims to the stoning areas to ease congestion.

Safety

The ritual has historically been the scene of deadly crowd crushes, including the 1990 disaster and the 2015 Mina stampede, in which hundreds or more pilgrims died. Saudi authorities have repeatedly expanded the bridge and surrounding infrastructure and introduced scheduled stoning times to improve safety.
