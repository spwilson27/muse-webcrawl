Rubber bullet

Rubber bullet
Type: Less-lethal projectile
Used for: Riot control
First used: 1970s (British forces in Northern Ireland)

Rubber bullets (also called rubber baton rounds) are a type of less-lethal projectile intended for riot control and crowd dispersal. Despite the name, most modern "rubber" bullets consist of a metal core coated with rubber, or are made largely of plastic or other composite materials; solid rubber projectiles are now uncommon.

History

Baton rounds were developed by the British security forces during the Troubles in Northern Ireland, where rubber bullets were introduced in 1970 as a replacement for earlier wooden bullets used in Hong Kong. They were designed to be fired at the ground so as to ricochet into the legs of rioters, although in practice they were often fired directly. Rubber bullets were later replaced in British service by plastic bullets, and similar projectiles have been adopted by police and military forces worldwide.

Effects and controversy

Although classified as less-lethal, rubber bullets can cause serious injury and death, particularly when fired at the head, chest or at close range. Medical studies, including systematic reviews of injuries during protests, have documented permanent disability (especially eye loss) and fatalities. Their use has been controversial in many countries, and several jurisdictions restrict or prohibit firing them directly at people, requiring skip-firing off the ground instead.

Related munitions include bean bag rounds, sponge grenades and pepper-ball projectiles.
