Remoteness (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Graded remoteness along a timeline

In linguistics, remoteness is the conceptualization in some languages of a distance metaphor in the expression of modality and tense. A language might, for example, mark different degrees of distance (or remoteness) from the present moment in its past and future tenses, so that a "hodiernal" past refers to events of today, a "hesternal" past to events of yesterday, and a remote past to events of long ago.

Graded tense

Many Bantu languages and a number of other languages in Africa, the Americas and Papua New Guinea have graded tense systems with multiple past and future tenses distinguished by remoteness. The Yagua language of Peru, for instance, has been described as having five degrees of past tense, distinguishing events from earlier today through to events more than two months in the past. Similar fine-grained distinctions occur in some languages for the future.

Remoteness in modality

The metaphor of distance is also used for modality: in English, for example, the so-called "past" forms of modals (could, might, would) often signal not past time but modal remoteness — tentativeness, politeness or counterfactuality. Thus "I wanted to ask you…" uses past morphology to distance the request rather than to locate it in the past. Linguists sometimes describe this as a distinction between "primary" (actual) and "remote" forms.
