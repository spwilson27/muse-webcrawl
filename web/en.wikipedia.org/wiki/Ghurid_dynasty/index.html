Ghurid dynasty

The Ghurid dynasty (Persian: سلسله غوریان, Shansabānī) was a dynasty of eastern Iranian (Tajik) origin from the mountainous region of Ghor in present-day central Afghanistan, ruling from the mid-9th century as local princes and as a great empire from about 1173 to 1215. At its greatest extent under the brothers Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad and Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad (Muhammad of Ghor), the Ghurid Empire stretched from Khurasan in the west to Bengal in the east, and its conquests in northern India laid the foundations of Muslim rule there, leading directly to the Delhi Sultanate.

Origins

The Ghurids, the Shansabani family, ruled the rugged, largely inaccessible land of Ghor, whose inhabitants converted to Islam only around the 11th–12th centuries. For generations they were vassals in turn of the Ghaznavids, who they served and fought, and of the Seljuks. In 1150–51 the Ghurid Ala al-Din Husayn sacked and burnt Ghazni so thoroughly that he earned the epithet Jahansuz ("World-Burner").

Imperial expansion

Under Ghiyath al-Din (r. 1163–1202), ruling from Firozkoh and Herat, and his brother Mu'izz al-Din (Muhammad of Ghor), ruling from Ghazni, the dynasty reached imperial scale. The brothers conquered Multan and Peshawar, expelled the Ghaznavids from Lahore in 1186, and then turned on the Rajput kingdoms. Defeated by Prithviraj Chauhan at the First Battle of Tarain (1191), Muhammad returned to win the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), one of the decisive battles of Indian history, opening the Gangetic plain to conquest; his generals, notably Qutb al-Din Aibak and Bakhtiyar Khalji, extended Ghurid power to Delhi and Bengal by 1204.

Decline and legacy

Muhammad of Ghor was assassinated in 1206. Without a strong heir, the empire fragmented between rival Ghurid branches and the sultans' own Turkic slave-generals (mamluks), and its western lands fell to the Khwarazmshahs by 1215. In India, Aibak founded the Mamluk dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206, preserving the Ghurid achievement. Ghurid architecture includes the Minaret of Jam (1194), a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and early Indo-Islamic monuments such as Delhi's Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque and the Qutb Minar, begun under Aibak.
