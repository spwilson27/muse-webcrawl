Pulse pressure

Pulse pressure is the difference between the systolic and diastolic blood pressures. It is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and represents the force that the heart generates each time it contracts.

Pulse pressure = Systolic pressure − Diastolic pressure

A normal pulse pressure at rest is about 40 mmHg: for a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg, the pulse pressure is 40 mmHg. Pulse pressure is proportional to stroke volume and inversely related to the compliance (elasticity) of the arterial system.

Narrow pulse pressure

A pulse pressure below about 30 mmHg, or less than 25 percent of the systolic pressure, is considered narrow (low). Causes include hypovolemia and hemorrhagic shock, aortic stenosis, cardiac tamponade, and advanced heart failure. In trauma, a narrowing pulse pressure can be an early sign of blood loss.

Wide pulse pressure

A pulse pressure persistently above about 60 mmHg is wide (high). It increases with age as large arteries stiffen, and is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, particularly in older adults. Other causes include aortic regurgitation, aortic sclerosis, severe anemia, hyperthyroidism, fever, arteriovenous fistula, and raised intracranial pressure (in which widening pulse pressure accompanies bradycardia and irregular respirations as Cushing's triad).

Clinical significance

Because arterial stiffening raises systolic and lowers diastolic pressure, pulse pressure widens with age and has been shown in studies such as the Framingham Heart Study to predict coronary heart disease risk, sometimes independently of mean arterial pressure.
