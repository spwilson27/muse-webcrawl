Rotating wheel space station

A rotating wheel space station, also known as a von Braun wheel, is a concept for a wheel-shaped space station that rotates about its axis to create artificial gravity through centrifugal force for its occupants. The idea has appeared in scientific proposals since the early 20th century and was popularised by Wernher von Braun in the 1950s. No full-scale rotating wheel station has been built, though rotating artificial-gravity concepts remain a subject of study for long-duration human spaceflight.

History

The Russian theorist Konstantin Tsiolkovsky wrote in 1903 about using rotation to create artificial gravity in space. In 1928 the Austro-Hungarian engineer Herman Potočnik (writing as Hermann Noordung) described a rotating "habitat wheel" (Wohnrad) in his book The Problem of Space Travel, complete with a hub docking port and spokes — the first detailed engineering depiction of the form. In the 1950s Wernher von Braun, working with Willy Ley, developed the concept further for a popular American audience: his wheel, described in Collier's magazine in 1952 and in Disney television programmes, was 76 metres (250 ft) in diameter, rotated three times per minute, and would serve as an orbital laboratory, observatory and staging point for lunar and Mars expeditions.

Later studies considered larger and differently shaped designs, including the cylindrical and dumbbell configurations and, in the 1970s, the vast rotating habitats of the Stanford torus design. NASA's 1969 plans for a large rotating station were never funded, and actual stations such as Salyut, Skylab, Mir and the International Space Station were built without rotation.

Design and physics

The apparent gravity at the rim of a rotating station is given by the centripetal acceleration a = v²/r = ω²r, where ω is the angular velocity and r the radius. To produce Earth-like gravity (1 g) while keeping rotation slow enough to avoid motion sickness, the radius must be large: at 2 revolutions per minute, a radius of about 224 metres is needed for 1 g. Designers generally target rotation rates of 2 rpm or less, because higher rates produce disorienting Coriolis effects and vestibular disturbance in crew members turning their heads.

Typical designs include a non-rotating or counter-rotating docking hub on the axis (where gravity is near zero, easing spacecraft docking), spoke tubes with elevators connecting hub and rim, and a rim containing crew quarters, laboratories, hydroponic gardens and life-support machinery. Power is usually supplied by solar arrays mounted on the hub. Structural and operational challenges include the mass required, attitude control, micrometeorite protection, and the difficulty of performing extra-vehicular activity around a rotating structure.

In popular culture

The design became an archetype of spaceflight in the public imagination, most famously as Space Station V in Stanley Kubrick's film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). Rotating stations or rotating sections appear in works such as Elysium (2013), Interstellar (2014), and numerous novels and video games; the Babylon 5 station in the television series of that name uses a rotating cylindrical section for gravity.
