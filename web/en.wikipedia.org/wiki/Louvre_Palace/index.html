The Louvre Palace (French: Palais du Louvre) is a former royal palace located on the Right Bank of the Seine in Paris, between the Tuileries Gardens and the church of Saint-Germain l'Auxerrois. It is the home of the Louvre Museum, the most visited art museum in the world. The palace is a vast complex of wings and pavilions on four main levels, built in many phases from the Middle Ages to the 20th century.

History

The Louvre began around 1190 as a fortress built by Philip II to defend Paris's western flank; a keep (the Grosse Tour) stood where the south-west corner of the Cour Carrée now lies. Charles V converted it into a royal residence in the 14th century. Francis I began its rebuilding in Renaissance style — Pierre Lescot's wing (from 1546) set the tone of French classicism — and Catherine de' Medici added the Tuileries Palace to the west. Henry IV's Grand Dessein linked the two palaces along the river (the Grande Galerie). Under Louis XIV the palace was completed around the Cour Carrée, including Claude Perrault's famous east Colonnade (1667–1670), before the court moved to Versailles in 1682. Napoleon I and Napoleon III greatly extended the complex along the Rue de Rivoli; the burning of the Tuileries in 1871 left the Louvre as the surviving palace. In the 1980s, President Mitterrand's Grand Louvre project added I. M. Pei's glass Pyramid (1989) and converted the finance ministry's Richelieu wing to museum use.
