H. L. A. Hart

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herbert Lionel Adolphus Hart (18 July 1907 – 19 December 1992) was an English legal philosopher, Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford (1952–68), and author of The Concept of Law (1961) — the most influential work of legal philosophy in the twentieth-century English-speaking world. Hart refounded legal positivism: against Austin's command theory, he modelled law as a system of rules — primary rules imposing duties and secondary rules (of recognition, change and adjudication) conferring powers — whose ultimate rule of recognition exists as official practice. His account of rules' “open texture” explained judicial discretion at the penumbra of language.

The Hart–Fuller debate (1958, Harvard Law Review) over law and morality after Nazism, and his exchange with Lord Devlin on enforcing morals (Hart's Law, Liberty and Morality, 1963, defending the Millian position against Devlin's), made him a public philosopher of liberalism. Punishment and Responsibility (1968) and Causation in the Law (with Tony Honoré, 1959) extended his reach into criminal law. His student generation (Raz, Dworkin — his successor and great critic — Finnis, MacCormick) defined the field; the posthumous second edition of The Concept of Law (1994) replied to Dworkin in a famous postscript.
