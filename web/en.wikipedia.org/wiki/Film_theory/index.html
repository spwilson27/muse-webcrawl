Film theory

Film theory is the academic discipline that explores the essence of cinema and provides conceptual frameworks for understanding film's relationship to reality, the other arts, individual viewers and society at large. Since its beginning, film theory has researched the nature of the medium, switching between formalist approaches concerned with film's specific devices and realist approaches concerned with its photographic basis.

Early theory

Ricciotto Canudo called cinema the "seventh art" in 1911. In the 1920s, Soviet filmmakers-theorists such as Lev Kuleshov, Vsevolod Pudovkin and Sergei Eisenstein developed theories of montage, while French impressionists and German theorists explored photogénie and film's psychological effects. Rudolf Arnheim's Film as Art (1932) argued formalist principles: film art arises from the medium's limitations.

Realism and auteurism

After the Second World War, André Bazin defended the photographic image's ontological realism and favoured deep focus and the long take; Siegfried Kracauer argued in Theory of Film (1960) that film should record and reveal physical reality. In parallel, critics at Cahiers du Cinéma formulated the auteur theory, treating the director as author — a doctrine that reshaped film criticism and the emerging academic film studies.

Structuralism, psychoanalysis and beyond

From the late 1960s, film theory drew on structuralism, semiotics (Christian Metz), Marxism (apparatus theory), psychoanalysis and feminism — notably Laura Mulvey's 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema", which coined the "male gaze". Later decades brought cognitivist critiques (David Bordwell, Noël Carroll), Deleuze's philosophy of the cinema, and approaches based on phenomenology, postcolonial theory and digital media.
