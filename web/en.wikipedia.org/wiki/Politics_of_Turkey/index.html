The politics of Turkey take place within the framework of a unitary presidential republic. The Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923 by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk from the remnants of the Ottoman Empire. Following the 2017 constitutional referendum, Turkey replaced its parliamentary system with an executive presidency, which took full effect after the 2018 elections: the office of prime minister was abolished and executive power vested in the president.

The Grand National Assembly of Turkey in Ankara, the country's unicameral legislature.

Institutions

The President is head of state and head of government, elected directly for a five-year term (maximum two terms), and appoints ministers, vice presidents and senior officials, and may issue presidential decrees. The Grand National Assembly (Türkiye Büyük Millet Meclisi) has 600 members elected by proportional representation for five-year terms. The judiciary includes the Constitutional Court, the Court of Cassation and the Council of State; Turkey is divided into 81 provinces administered by appointed governors alongside elected metropolitan and district mayors.

Political parties

Major parties include the Justice and Development Party (AKP), founded in 2001 and in government since 2002 under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan; the Republican People's Party (CHP), founded by Atatürk and the principal opposition; the Nationalist Movement Party (MHP), the AKP's ally in the People's Alliance; the İYİ Party; and the pro-Kurdish DEM Party (successor to the HDP). The 2023 general elections returned Erdoğan to the presidency in a run-off against Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu, while in the March 2024 local elections the CHP finished first nationwide for the first time since 1977, retaining Istanbul and Ankara under Ekrem İmamoğlu and Mansur Yavaş.

History

Turkey held its first competitive multi-party elections in 1950, won by the Democrat Party. The armed forces intervened in politics repeatedly — coups in 1960, 1971, 1980 and a "postmodern coup" in 1997 — and the 1982 constitution was drafted under military rule. A further coup attempt in July 2016 was defeated, after which a state of emergency and wide-ranging purges followed. Long-running issues include the Kurdish question, the role of religion in public life (shaped by the founding principle of secularism, laiklik), relations with the European Union, and the state of judicial independence and media freedom, which international observers have criticised as deteriorating since the 2010s.
