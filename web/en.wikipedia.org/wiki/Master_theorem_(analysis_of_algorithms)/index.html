Master theorem (analysis of algorithms)

In the analysis of algorithms, the master theorem provides a "cookbook" method for solving recurrence relations of the form that arise in divide-and-conquer algorithms:

T(n) = a·T(n/b) + f(n)

where a ≥ 1 is the number of subproblems, b > 1 is the factor by which the problem size is divided, and f(n) is the cost of dividing and combining the results.

The three cases

Let c = logb a, the exponent of the work done at the leaves of the recursion tree. Comparing f(n) with nc gives three cases:

  1. If f(n) is polynomially smaller than nc, the leaves dominate and T(n) = Θ(nc).
  2. If f(n) is comparable to nc (within a factor of logk n), every level contributes equally and T(n) = Θ(nc logk+1 n).
  3. If f(n) is polynomially larger than nc and satisfies a regularity condition, the root dominates and T(n) = Θ(f(n)).

Applications and limits

The theorem gives immediate running times for algorithms such as merge sort, binary search, and the Karatsuba and Strassen multiplication algorithms. It does not apply to all recurrences: if f(n) falls between cases (e.g., n log n vs n in some borderline settings) or subproblem sizes are uneven, more general methods such as the Akra–Bazzi theorem or the recursion-tree and substitution methods are needed. The theorem is presented in the textbook Introduction to Algorithms by Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, and Stein.
