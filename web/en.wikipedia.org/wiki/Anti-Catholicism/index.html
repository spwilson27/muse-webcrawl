Anti-Catholicism

Anti-Catholicism, also known as Catholicophobia, is hostility towards Catholics or opposition to the Catholic Church, its clergy, and its adherents. At various points after the Reformation, many majority-Protestant countries had anti-Catholic sentiments, laws restricting Catholic worship, and episodes of violence against Catholics.

Historical forms

In Britain and Ireland, penal laws after the Reformation restricted Catholic civil rights for centuries, and anti-Catholic panics erupted around events such as the Gunpowder Plot (1605) and the Gordon Riots (1780). In Germany, Bismarck's Kulturkampf of the 1870s targeted the Church's influence.

United States

Anti-Catholicism was a powerful force in 19th-century America, fuelled by mass Catholic immigration; the nativist Know Nothing movement, convent-burning riots, and Blaine Amendments restricting aid to Catholic schools reflected it. It resurfaced in the 1928 presidential campaign against Al Smith and, more muted, in concerns about John F. Kennedy in 1960. Anti-communist regimes and revolutionary governments (e.g. in Mexico and Spain) also persecuted the Church in the 20th century.

Contemporary discussion

Commentators debate how far criticism of Church doctrine and institutions differs from prejudice against Catholics as people; violence against Catholics persists in some sectarian contexts such as Northern Ireland.
