Theban hegemony

The Theban hegemony was the period of Greek history, roughly 371–362 BC, during which the city-state of Thebes was the dominant power in Greece. It followed the Spartan hegemony and was established by the Theban victory at the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BC, which shattered Spartan military prestige, and it effectively ended with the Battle of Mantinea in 362 BC, where the Theban general Epaminondas was killed.

Rise

Thebes' ascendancy was built by the statesman Pelopidas and the general Epaminondas after the liberation of the Cadmeia from a Spartan garrison in 379 BC. At Leuctra, Epaminondas used revolutionary tactics — a massively deepened left wing and an oblique advance — to crush the Spartan army and kill King Cleombrotus. Thebes then invaded the Peloponnese repeatedly, liberated Messenia from Spartan control and refounded Messene, permanently weakening Sparta.

Decline

Theban power depended heavily on its two great leaders: Pelopidas died in 364 BC and Epaminondas fell at Mantinea in 362 BC, even as his army won the battle. Xenophon concluded his history with the remark that Greece after Mantinea fell into "even greater confusion". Within a generation, Philip II of Macedon — who had been a hostage in Thebes and learned from its military innovations — imposed Macedonian supremacy at Chaeronea (338 BC).
