The chamois (Rupicapra rupicapra), pronounced sham-wah, is a species of goat-antelope native to the mountains of Europe, from west to east, including the Pyrenees, the Alps, the Apennines, the Dinarides, the Tatra and the Carpathian Mountains, the Balkan Mountains, the Caucasus, and parts of Turkey. It has also been introduced to the South Island of New Zealand. Some subspecies of chamois are strictly protected in the EU under the European Habitats Directive.

Description

The chamois is a very strong and agile animal, well adapted to steep, rocky terrain. Both males and females have short, dark horns that curve backwards like hooks. The summer coat is a smooth reddish-brown, turning a rich chocolate brown in winter, with a distinctive dark stripe along the back and pale cheeks marked by dark bands running from the eyes to the muzzle.

Behaviour and ecology

Chamois live in herds of up to 100 animals, although adult males are usually solitary outside the rutting season in late autumn. They feed on grasses, herbs, leaves, buds, shoots and fungi. Their natural predators include golden eagles, Eurasian lynx, wolves and brown bears. Chamois can jump up to 2 metres in height and 6 metres in length, and run at speeds of up to 50 km/h on uneven ground.

Chamois leather

The animal gives its name to chamois leather, a soft, absorbent leather traditionally made from the skin of the chamois but today usually produced from sheepskin; it is widely used for cleaning and polishing, notably for cars and optical lenses.
