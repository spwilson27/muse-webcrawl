Rescission (contract law)

In contract law, rescission is an equitable remedy that unmakes a contract between parties, restoring them, as far as possible, to the position they occupied before the contract was made (the status quo ante). A rescinded contract is treated as void from the beginning (void ab initio), as distinct from termination, which discharges future obligations but leaves accrued rights intact.

Grounds

Rescission may be available where a contract was induced by misrepresentation (fraudulent, negligent or innocent), mistake, duress or undue influence, or where a party lacks capacity. It may also arise by agreement of the parties (mutual rescission) or under statutory cooling-off provisions for certain consumer contracts.

Bars to rescission

Because rescission is equitable and discretionary, it may be refused where:

  • the parties cannot be restored substantially to their former positions (restitutio in integrum is impossible);
  • the claimant has affirmed the contract after discovering the defect;
  • there has been undue delay (laches); or
  • third parties have acquired rights under the contract in good faith.

Effects

On rescission, benefits transferred under the contract must generally be returned: money paid is recoverable and property re-vests. Courts may also award an indemnity for obligations necessarily incurred under the contract, but traditionally not damages for the misrepresentation itself, which is the province of a separate claim in deceit or under statute.
