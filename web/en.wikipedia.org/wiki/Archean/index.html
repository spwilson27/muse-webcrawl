The Archean Eon (also spelled Archaean; from Greek arkhē, "beginning") is the second of Earth's four geological eons, spanning from 4,000 million years ago (Ma) to 2,500 Ma. It followed the Hadean and preceded the Proterozoic. During the Archean the Earth's crust cooled enough to form rocks and continental plates, oceans condensed, and life appeared in the seas.

Earth environment

The early Sun was about 70–75% as luminous as today (the "faint young Sun"), yet geological evidence shows liquid water at the surface, implying a strong greenhouse effect from carbon dioxide and methane in an atmosphere essentially free of oxygen. Solar UV flux was high, and the Moon, much closer, raised powerful tides. Radiogenic heat production was two to three times the modern value, and mantle temperatures were higher, so Archean volcanism produced unusual magnesium-rich lavas called komatiites.

Geology

Surviving Archean crust consists of granite–greenstone terrains and high-grade gneiss complexes preserved in cratons — among them the Pilbara (Australia), Kaapvaal (South Africa), Slave (Canada), and the Isua supracrustal belt of Greenland. The oldest dated terrestrial material is the 4.4-billion-year-old Jack Hills zircon; the oldest intact rock units, such as the Acasta Gneiss (c. 4.03 Ga), date to the Eoarchean. Economically, Archean greenstone belts host major gold and nickel deposits.

Life

Life was established by at least 3.5 billion years ago: stromatolites — layered structures built by microbial mats — occur in the Pilbara and Barberton successions, and carbon isotope signatures suggestive of biology extend to c. 3.8–4.1 Ga. Archean life was entirely unicellular (bacteria and archaea). Oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria gradually transformed the oceans, precipitating banded iron formations, and set the stage for the Great Oxidation Event at the start of the Proterozoic.
