Human–computer interaction (HCI) is a multidisciplinary field of study and design concerned with the interfaces through which people interact with computers. It combines computer science, behavioural sciences, design and media studies to make technology usable, accessible and suited to human needs.

History

The field emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s as personal computing spread beyond specialists. Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) and Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demonstration of windows, hypertext and the mouse laid early foundations, while the Xerox Alto and later commercial graphical user interfaces brought direct manipulation to a wide public. The ACM's CHI conference, first held in 1982, became the field's flagship venue.

Principles

HCI research addresses usability — effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction — through methods such as user-centred design, prototyping, usability testing and field studies. Foundational models include Fitts's law for pointing, Norman's action cycle, and heuristic evaluation. Modern HCI extends beyond the desktop to mobile and wearable devices, voice and gesture interfaces, virtual and augmented reality, and the design of interactions with artificial intelligence systems.

Goals

Because a system that is hard to use imposes costs in errors, training and exclusion, HCI aims both to improve existing interfaces and to invent new interaction techniques that match human capabilities and contexts of use.
