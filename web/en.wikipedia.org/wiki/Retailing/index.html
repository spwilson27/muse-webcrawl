Retailing

Retailing is the sale of goods and services to consumers, in contrast to wholesaling, which is sale to business or institutional customers. A retailer purchases goods in large quantities from manufacturers, directly or through a wholesaler, and then sells in smaller quantities to consumers for a profit. Retailers are the final link in the supply chain from producers to consumers.

Types of retailers

Retail formats include department stores, supermarkets and grocery stores, convenience stores, discount stores, warehouse clubs, specialty stores, drugstores, and big-box retailers. Non-store retailing includes e-commerce, mail order, vending machines, and direct selling. Retailers are also classified by ownership: independent stores, chains, franchises, and consumer cooperatives.

History

Retail markets and shops have existed since ancient times; rural markets in China are documented from antiquity, and the bazaars of the Middle East and the agora of ancient Greece served similar functions. The modern era of retailing began in the nineteenth century with the department store — establishments such as Le Bon Marché in Paris (1852) introduced fixed prices, browsing without obligation to buy, and money-back guarantees. The twentieth century brought self-service supermarkets, suburban shopping malls, and chain stores; the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries saw the rise of electronic commerce, which reshaped the industry and pressured traditional brick-and-mortar formats.

Retail strategy

Retail management involves decisions about product assortment, pricing, location, store layout, merchandising, and customer service. The traditional "marketing mix" of retailing considers product, price, place, and promotion, extended in service-oriented accounts by people, process, and physical evidence. Key performance measures include sales per square foot, inventory turnover, conversion rate, and gross margin return on investment.
