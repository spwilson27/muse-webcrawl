Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) was a German polymath active as a mathematician, philosopher, scientist, and diplomat. Along with Isaac Newton, he is credited with the independent invention of infinitesimal calculus; the notation he devised — ∫ for integrals and d for differentials — remains standard today. He also refined the binary number system that underlies modern digital computing, and ranks among the most important logicians between Aristotle and the 19th century.

Life

Born in Leipzig, the son of a professor of moral philosophy, Leibniz taught himself Latin and Greek as a child and entered university at fourteen. After legal studies he entered the service of the Elector of Mainz, then spent four formative years in Paris (1672–1676), where he met Huygens and began the work that produced his calculus, first published in 1684. From 1676 he served the dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg in Hanover as librarian, counsellor, and historian — a post that frustrated him but that he held for forty years. He founded the Berlin Academy of Sciences in 1700 and advised rulers across Europe, including Peter the Great.

Philosophy

Leibniz's metaphysics centres on monads: simple, indivisible, "windowless" substances whose perceptions mirror the whole universe from individual points of view, coordinated by a pre-established harmony ordained by God. His Théodicée (1710) argued that this is the "best of all possible worlds" God could create — a doctrine satirised by Voltaire in Candide. He formulated the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, and envisioned a characteristica universalis, a universal formal language in which disputes could be settled by calculation.

Mathematics and science

Besides calculus, Leibniz contributed the product rule and the notation still used for derivatives, developed determinants, designed a stepped reckoner calculating machine, and formalised binary arithmetic (inspired partly by the Chinese I Ching). The calculus priority dispute with Newton's followers embittered his final years. He died in Hanover in 1716, his scientific reputation overshadowed in England but immense on the Continent.
