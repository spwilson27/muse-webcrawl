Quinidine

Class:Class Ia antiarrhythmic agent
Source:Cinchona bark

Quinidine is a class Ia antiarrhythmic agent used in the treatment of abnormal heart rhythms. It is a cinchona alkaloid obtained from the bark of the cinchona tree and is the stereoisomer (dextrorotatory diastereoisomer) of quinine, the antimalarial drug.

Medical uses

Quinidine was historically one of the most widely used drugs for atrial fibrillation and other supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias. Its use has declined substantially because of its proarrhythmic side-effect profile and the availability of safer alternatives, but it retains niche roles, including the treatment of Brugada syndrome, short QT syndrome, and certain cases of idiopathic ventricular fibrillation. Intravenous quinidine gluconate has also been used for severe malaria in the United States when artesunate was unavailable.

Mechanism of action

Quinidine blocks the fast inward sodium current (INa) in cardiac muscle, slowing conduction and prolonging the action potential and refractory period. It also blocks several potassium currents, has anticholinergic (vagolytic) effects, and produces alpha-adrenergic blockade, which can cause vasodilation and hypotension.

Adverse effects

Important adverse effects include QT prolongation with torsades de pointes, drug-induced thrombocytopenia, gastrointestinal upset and diarrhoea, and cinchonism (tinnitus, headache, and visual disturbance). Quinidine is a potent inhibitor of the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP2D6 and interacts with many other drugs, notably raising digoxin levels.

History

The antiarrhythmic properties of cinchona alkaloids were recognised in the early 20th century; Karel Wenckebach reported in 1914 that a patient self-treated his atrial fibrillation with quinine, and quinidine — identified as the more potent isomer — entered clinical use in the following years.
