Latvian mythology

Latvian mythology is the collection of myths that have emerged throughout the history of Latvia, elaborated by successive generations, and preserved chiefly in folk songs (dainas), legends, and seasonal customs. It forms part of the wider Baltic mythology shared in part with Lithuanian tradition, though the two developed distinct pantheons and emphases.

Sources

Unlike some neighbouring traditions, pre-Christian Latvian religion left no written scriptures; Latvia was Christianised only in the 12th–13th centuries during the Northern Crusades. Knowledge of the mythology derives from chronicles by foreign observers, from folklore collections — above all the vast corpus of dainas assembled by Krišjānis Barons in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — and from continuing folk practice, especially the midsummer festival of Jāņi. Scholars caution that the reconstructed mythology is likely a layered mixture of genuine archaic material, later folk invention, and some 19th-century romantic fabrication.

Deities

Dievs, the sky god whose name is cognate with the Indo-European *Dyēus, presides over the celestial order. Saule, the sun goddess, is one of the most prominent figures in the dainas, associated with fertility, warmth, and care for orphans and the unfortunate. Her daughters, the Saules meitas, appear in numerous songs. Mēness, the moon god, is often in rivalry or conflict with Saule. Pērkons, the thunder god, battles the devil (Velns) and is among the most frequently invoked deities. Laima is the goddess of fate and childbirth, who decrees each newborn's destiny. Māra is a complex earth and mother figure associated with the material world, cattle, and childbirth; her role has been much debated, partly because the name was also applied to the Virgin Mary in folk Catholicism.

Ūsiņš is associated with horses, bees, and the spring. Jumis, a fertility spirit connected with the harvest, is symbolised by a double ear of grain, and the Jumja rite of leaving the last sheaf in the field was performed at harvest's end.

Cosmology and spirits

The world tree — commonly a great oak or birch with the sun resting in its branches — appears in the dainas as an image of cosmic order. Below the chief gods stood a multitude of "mother" spirits (mātes), personifications of natural domains: the Forest Mother (Meža māte), Sea Mother (Jūras māte), Wind Mother, and dozens of others, more than a hundred recorded in folklore. Household and farm spirits, and the souls of the dead (veļi), who were honoured especially in the autumn season of Veļu laiks, completed the spiritual world.

Revival

In the 20th century, the Latvian neopagan movement Dievturība, founded in the 1920s by Ernests Brastiņš, sought to reconstruct and practise the old religion; it survived Soviet suppression and continues today, with Jāņi celebrations and folklore ensembles keeping much of the mythology in public life.
