Dulcimer

The term dulcimer refers to two families of stringed musical instruments: the hammered dulcimer, in which the strings are struck with small hand-held hammers, and the Appalachian dulcimer (or mountain dulcimer), a fretted instrument that is plucked or strummed and held on the lap. Both belong to the zither family, with strings stretched over a resonating soundboard.

Hammered dulcimer

The hammered dulcimer has a trapezoidal body with many strings stretched in pairs of courses over bridges. The player strikes the strings with two light wooden hammers. The instrument descends from the Persian santur and is related to the Hungarian cimbalom, the Romanian țambal, and the Chinese yangqin, which travelled along trade routes into Europe and Asia. It was popular in Britain and America in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and enjoyed a revival in the folk music of the twentieth century.

Appalachian dulcimer

The Appalachian dulcimer developed in the Scots-Irish settlements of the Appalachian Mountains in the early nineteenth century. It typically has three or four strings, an hourglass or teardrop body, and a diatonic fretboard. Traditionally played with a quill or pick while noting a melody string with the fingers or a noter stick, it produces a soft, droning sound suited to ballads and hymns. Performers such as Jean Ritchie popularised it during the American folk revival.
