Oboe d'amore

The oboe d'amore (Italian for "oboe of love") is a double-reed woodwind instrument in the oboe family, pitched a minor third below the ordinary oboe, in A. It is distinguished by its pear-shaped (bulb) bell, which gives it a softer, more veiled and lyrical tone than the brighter standard oboe. The instrument was developed in the early 18th century and is about a third longer than a soprano oboe.

History and repertoire

The oboe d'amore flourished in the Baroque era. Johann Sebastian Bach wrote extensively for it, using it in numerous cantatas, the Christmas Oratorio, and as a soloist in concertos, valuing its tender middle-register colour between the oboe and the cor anglais. After falling out of use in the Classical period, it was revived in the late 19th and 20th centuries; notable later uses include Ravel's Boléro (in its original scoring), Richard Strauss's Sinfonia Domestica, and works by Debussy and Britten.

Construction

Like the oboe, the instrument is usually made of grenadilla wood, is played with a double reed, and has a mostly conical bore. Its music is written in the treble clef as a transposing instrument in A, sounding a minor third lower than written.
