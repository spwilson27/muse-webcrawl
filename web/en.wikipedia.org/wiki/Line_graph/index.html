Line graph

In graph theory, the line graph of an undirected graph G, written L(G), is the graph whose vertices represent the edges of G, with two vertices adjacent in L(G) when the corresponding edges of G share a common endpoint. The concept was studied by Whitney (1932), who showed that connected graphs with isomorphic line graphs are themselves isomorphic, with a single exception (the triangle K3 and the claw K1,3).

Properties

Line graphs are claw-free: no line graph contains an induced K1,3 subgraph. Beineke characterised line graphs by a set of nine forbidden induced subgraphs. Every line graph is also a block graph of a special kind, and the edges of any graph can be partitioned so that the line graph is the union of cliques, one for each vertex of positive degree in the original graph.

Recognition and applications

Line graphs can be recognised, and a root graph reconstructed, in linear time (algorithms by Roussopoulos and Lehot). Line graphs model adjacency relationships in scheduling, chemistry (where they relate to molecular structure), and network analysis; the line graph of a bipartite graph is a perfect graph.

Related concepts

Iterated line graphs grow quickly in size. The line graph of a complete graph Kn is the triangular graph. Directed and multigraph versions of the definition also exist.
