Human microbiome


Distribution of microbial communities across the human body.

The human microbiome is the aggregate of all microbiota that reside on or within human tissues and biofluids along with the corresponding anatomical sites in which they reside, including the gastrointestinal tract, skin, mammary glands, oral cavity, lungs, and other sites. The human microbiome includes bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists, and viruses.

Composition and importance

Though micro-organisms reside in the body, they have historically been understudied, but research has flourished since the launch of the Human Microbiome Project in 2007. The gut microbiota in particular play crucial roles in digestion, production of vitamins, training of the immune system, and protection against pathogens. Disruptions of the microbiome (dysbiosis) have been associated with conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, and some mental health disorders, though causal relationships remain an active area of research.

Numbers

Estimates suggest the human body contains roughly as many microbial cells as human cells — on the order of 38 trillion bacteria — with the largest population residing in the colon.
