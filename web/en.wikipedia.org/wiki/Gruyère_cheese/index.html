Gruyère (French pronunciation: [ɡʁɥijɛʁ]) is a hard Swiss cheese that originated in the cantons of Fribourg, Vaud, Neuchâtel, Jura, and Berne in Switzerland. It is named after the town of Gruyères in the canton of Fribourg. In 2001, Gruyère gained the appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC), now AOP, status under Swiss law.

Gruyère is made from unpasteurised cow's milk, curdled, cut, and cooked at about 43 °C before being pressed into wheels of 25–40 kg and matured for at least five months. The cheese is sweetish, slightly salty, and creamy when young, developing a firmer, nuttier, more complex flavour with age; small cracks and tiny crystals form in well-aged wheels. Unlike Emmental, it has few or no holes.

Uses

Gruyère is prized as a melting cheese and is essential to classic Swiss fondue (often in the moitié-moitié blend with Vacherin Fribourgeois), French onion soup, croque-monsieur, and gratins. It regularly won top honours at the World Cheese Awards, and in 2022 a Swiss Gruyère AOP surchoix was named World Champion Cheese.

Name disputes

The name is protected in the European Union and Switzerland, but a US court ruled in 2023 that "gruyere" had become a generic term for a style of cheese in the United States, a decision the Swiss and French interprofessional bodies contested but did not overturn on appeal.
