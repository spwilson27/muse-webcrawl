Trade union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trade union (British English) or labor union (American English), often simply a union, is an organization of workers whose purpose is maintaining or improving the conditions of their employment: wages, hours, benefits, safety and voice at work, through collective bargaining and, when necessary, strike action. Unions arose with industrial capitalism (combinations long criminalised — Britain's Combination Acts 1799–1800, France's Le Chapelier Law 1791; legality won through the 19th century) and became mass institutions of the 20th-century settlement.

Structures and fortunes

Forms include craft, industrial, general and public-sector unions, federations (AFL–CIO, TUC, DGB, ITUC globally) and the Ghent-system and works-council variants of continental Europe, where unions' roles in unemployment insurance and co-determination sustain high membership (Nordic densities remain the world's highest). Density has declined across most advanced economies since the 1970s–80s (US private-sector density now in the single digits; defeats and restrictive laws — PATCO 1981, the UK miners' strike 1984–85 and subsequent statutes — mark the Anglo-American turn), while coverage by collective agreements often exceeds density where extension mechanisms exist (France: low density, near-universal coverage). Research associates unions with compressed wages, safer workplaces, political mobilisation of working-class voters and productivity effects both ways; the 2020s show an organising revival among young service and tech workers in some countries, against algorithmic management as the newest frontier of an old argument about power at work.
