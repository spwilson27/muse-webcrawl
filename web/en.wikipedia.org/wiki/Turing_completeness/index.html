Turing completeness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Assorted devices — a typewriter, a game console, a spreadsheet grid — all linked to one infinity symbol loop]

A system is Turing complete if it can simulate any Turing machine — equivalently, compute anything that is computable at all, given enough time and memory. Almost all general-purpose programming languages are Turing complete, and so, surprisingly, are many systems never designed for computing.

Famous accidental examples include Conway's Game of Life, the card game Magic: The Gathering, spreadsheet formulas with iteration, and the pattern-matching of some text editors. Turing completeness requires, roughly, conditional branching and a way to loop or recurse without a fixed bound.

Caveats

Turing completeness says nothing about speed or practicality: a complete system may take exponential time where another takes seconds. Real computers also have finite memory, making them technically finite-state machines — completeness is an idealisation recovered by imagining unbounded storage.

Languages deliberately not Turing complete (such as regular expressions in their pure form, or total functional languages) gain guarantees in exchange: programs in them always terminate.
