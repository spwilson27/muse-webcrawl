Kālua

Kālua is a traditional Hawaiian cooking method that utilises an imu, a type of underground oven. The word kālua, which literally means "to cook in an underground oven", may also be used to describe the food cooked in this manner, such as kālua pig or kālua turkey, which are commonly served at luaus.

Method

To build an imu, a pit is dug in the earth and lined with volcanic rocks, over which a fire is kindled. When the rocks are heated, a whole pig seasoned with Hawaiian sea salt is placed on banana leaves or ti leaves, covered with more leaves and wet burlap or sand to trap steam, and left to cook slowly for many hours. The result is tender, smoky, easily shredded meat.

Kālua pig

Kālua pig (kālua puaʻa) is the best-known dish prepared by this method and a staple of the luau feast, traditionally served with poi, lomi-lomi salmon and haupia. Commercial and home cooks often imitate the dish using a conventional oven or slow cooker with liquid smoke and cabbage.
