Squamous cell

A squamous cell is a flat, scale-like epithelial cell. Squamous cells form the main part of the epidermis of the skin and line many internal passageways and organs, including the mouth, esophagus, lungs, and cervix. The word "squamous" comes from the Latin squama, meaning a scale of a fish or serpent.

Types

Epithelia composed of squamous cells are classified by the number of cell layers. Simple squamous epithelium consists of a single layer of flat cells and is found where rapid diffusion or filtration occurs, such as the alveoli of the lungs, the lining of blood vessels (endothelium), and the lining of body cavities (mesothelium). Stratified squamous epithelium has many layers and provides protection against abrasion; it forms the outer layer of the skin and lines the mouth, throat, esophagus, and vagina. Stratified squamous epithelium may be keratinized, as in skin, or non-keratinized, as in the lining of the mouth.

Clinical significance

Squamous cells can give rise to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), a common form of cancer that occurs in the skin and in many other organs, including the lungs, cervix, head and neck, and esophagus. On the skin, SCC is the second most common form of skin cancer after basal cell carcinoma and is strongly associated with cumulative ultraviolet radiation exposure. Abnormal squamous cells detected on a Pap test of the cervix may indicate precancerous changes (squamous intraepithelial lesions) usually driven by human papillomavirus infection.
