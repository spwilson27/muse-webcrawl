The Day of the Dead (Spanish: Día de los Muertos or Día de Muertos) is a Mexican holiday in which families honour and remember their dead, welcoming their spirits back to the world of the living with altars, food, flowers and festivity. Celebrated principally on 1 and 2 November — the Catholic feasts of All Saints and All Souls — it fuses pre-Hispanic Mesoamerican rituals for the dead with Spanish Catholic tradition.

Origins

Aztec and other Nahua peoples held festivals for the dead, including rites dedicated to the goddess Mictecacíhuatl, "Lady of the Dead", and believed the dead travelled to Mictlán, the underworld, in stages. Under Spanish colonisation these observances were moved to the All Saints/All Souls dates and absorbed Catholic prayers for souls in purgatory, producing the syncretic festival known today. Scholars caution that the modern holiday is as much a product of 20th-century Mexican nationalism and cultural revival as of unbroken pre-Hispanic survival.

Celebrations

The heart of the festival is the ofrenda, a home or graveside altar bearing photographs of the departed, candles, incense of copal, water, salt, their favourite foods and drinks, pan de muerto (a sweet anise bread), and heaps of orange marigolds (cempasúchil), whose scent and colour are said to guide spirits home. Families clean and decorate graves and keep night vigils in cemeteries — most famously on the island of Janitzio in Lake Pátzcuaro, Michoacán — eating, singing and telling stories among the tombstones.

Children who have died, the angelitos, are honoured on 1 November (Día de los Angelitos / All Saints), and adults on 2 November (All Souls, Día de los Difuntos). Humorous verses called calaveras literarias mock the living and the dead alike, and images of grinning skeletons — above all La Calavera Catrina, created by printmaker José Guadalupe Posada around 1910 — have become the holiday's emblem. Since 2016 Mexico City has staged a vast Day of the Dead parade, inspired, ironically, by a fictional one in the James Bond film Spectre.

Beyond Mexico

Related observances include Guatemala's kite festivals of Sumpango and Santiago Sacatepéquez, Bolivia's Día de las Ñatitas, and the Día de los Difuntos of Ecuador and Peru. In the United States, Chicano artists of the 1970s revived Día de los Muertos in California and the Southwest as an assertion of Mexican-American identity, and it has since entered mainstream American culture — a process accelerated by Pixar's film Coco (2017) — sometimes raising questions of commercialisation and appropriation.

Recognition

In 2008 UNESCO inscribed the "Indigenous Festivity dedicated to the Dead" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising the festival's role in Mexican identity and community cohesion.
