Common ownership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Common ownership is the holding of the means of production, land, or other assets in common by a community, society, or the state, such that no individual or separate group has exclusive title. It is a foundational concept in socialist theory, where it is proposed as the successor to private ownership of the means of production, and it must be distinguished from collective ownership (by a defined group such as a co-operative's members) and from public ownership (by a state body on the public's behalf).

In socialist thought

For Karl Marx and later socialist thinkers, social ownership of the means of production was the defining feature of a post-capitalist society: production for use rather than profit, with the "free association of producers" managing the common stock of resources. Different traditions have filled in the institutional details differently — central state ownership, municipal ownership, workers' self-management, and commons-based or decentralised models — producing long-running debates about efficiency, incentives, and democratic control.

Historical and legal forms

Common ownership long predates socialism: village commons, open-field systems, monastic property, and many indigenous land tenure systems held land in common, and modern law recognises related arrangements such as co-ownership, community land trusts, and the global commons (the high seas, Antarctica, outer space). The economist Elinor Ostrom demonstrated that common-pool resources can be sustainably governed by their users under suitable institutions, challenging the simple "tragedy of the commons" narrative.

Modern antitrust usage

In contemporary competition economics, "common ownership" also denotes the separate phenomenon of institutional investors holding minority stakes in several competing firms in the same industry, which some studies associate with softened competition and higher prices. This usage is distinct from the socialist concept.
