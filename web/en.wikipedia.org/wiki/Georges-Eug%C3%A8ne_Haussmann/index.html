Georges-Eugène Haussmann (27 March 1809 – 11 January 1891), commonly known as Baron Haussmann, was a French official who, as prefect of the Seine under Emperor Napoleon III, directed the vast renovation of Paris between 1853 and 1870.

The renovation of Paris

Haussmann's programme — known as Haussmann's renovation — demolished much of medieval Paris and replaced it with wide, tree-lined boulevards, uniform stone apartment blocks, new parks such as the Bois de Boulogne and Bois de Vincennes, railway stations, markets, and modern water and sewer systems. Avenues such as the Boulevard Saint-Germain and the Avenue de l'Opéra were cut through dense old quarters, and the city's boundaries were expanded in 1860 to twenty arrondissements.

Legacy and criticism

The works improved sanitation, traffic, and light but displaced many working-class residents and were attacked for their cost and for destroying historic neighbourhoods. Haussmann was dismissed in 1870 amid financial scandal, shortly before the fall of the Second Empire, but his design permanently shaped the image of Paris and influenced urban planning worldwide.
