Gjon Mili

Gjon Mili (November 28, 1904 – February 14, 1984) was an Albanian-American photographer and filmmaker best known for his pioneering work with stroboscopic and stop-action photography, much of it published in Life magazine. Working with Harold Eugene Edgerton of MIT, Mili used the electronic flash to capture movement in ways never before seen, photographing dancers, musicians, athletes and artists.

Early life

Mili was born in Korçë, Albania, and emigrated to the United States in 1923. He studied electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology but did not complete a degree, instead pursuing photography while working commercially. In 1937 he was introduced to Edgerton, whose stroboscopic techniques Mili adapted to artistic and editorial photography.

Career

Mili became a freelance photographer for Life in 1939 and remained associated with the magazine for decades. His strobe photographs of the dancer Martha Graham, ice skaters, jugglers and musicians rendered motion as flowing, segmented patterns of light. In 1949 he photographed Pablo Picasso at Vallauris in southern France, where Picasso, given a small flashlight, "drew" centaurs and other figures in the darkened room while Mili made long exposures — the famous series of Picasso "light drawings."

Mili also made films, including Jammin' the Blues (1944), a short featuring Lester Young, Red Callender and other jazz musicians, which was nominated for an Academy Award and later added to the National Film Registry. His book Picasso's Third Dimension (1970) collected his work with the artist.

Legacy

Mili's photographs helped popularize high-speed and stroboscopic photography as an art form, influencing generations of photographers interested in the representation of motion.
