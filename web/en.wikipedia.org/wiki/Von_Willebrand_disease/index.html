Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common hereditary blood-clotting disorder in humans. It arises from a qualitative or quantitative deficiency of von Willebrand factor (VWF), a multimeric protein required for platelet adhesion to sites of vascular injury and which also carries and stabilises coagulation factor VIII. An acquired form can occur in association with other medical conditions.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are usually mild and mucocutaneous: easy bruising, frequent nosebleeds, bleeding gums, heavy menstrual bleeding and prolonged bleeding after dental work, surgery or childbirth. Severe deficiency (type 3) can cause joint and muscle bleeds resembling haemophilia.

Types

Type 1, the commonest (about 70–80% of cases), is a partial quantitative deficiency with mild symptoms. Type 2 comprises qualitative defects, subdivided into 2A, 2B, 2M and 2N. Type 3 is a virtually complete absence of VWF, inherited recessively, and is the most severe form. The Finnish physician Erik Adolf von Willebrand described the condition in 1926 in a family from the Åland Islands.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses VWF antigen and activity assays, factor VIII levels and multimer analysis. Many patients need no regular treatment; desmopressin (DDAVP) raises VWF levels in type 1, and VWF-containing concentrates are used for major bleeding or surgery. Antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid help with mucosal bleeding.
