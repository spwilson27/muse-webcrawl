Decarburization is the process of reducing the carbon content of steel at or near its surface, opposite to carburization. It occurs when steel is heated to high temperatures in an oxidizing atmosphere: carbon in the metal reacts with oxygen, hydrogen, or carbon dioxide in the surrounding gas and escapes as carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide, leaving a soft, low-carbon layer at the surface.

Causes and mechanism

Decarburization typically occurs during heat treatment, hot working, or annealing of steel in air or in atmospheres containing water vapor or CO₂. The depth of the decarburized layer depends on temperature, time at temperature, and the composition of both the steel and the furnace atmosphere; higher temperatures accelerate carbon diffusion to the surface. The affected zone may be fully decarburized (essentially ferrite) or partially decarburized, with a gradient of carbon content.

Effects and prevention

Because hardness and wear and fatigue resistance in steel depend strongly on carbon content, decarburization degrades springs, fasteners, tools, and bearing surfaces, and quality standards limit permissible decarburization depth for many products. Prevention methods include heating in controlled or inert atmospheres, vacuum furnaces, salt baths, protective coatings, and leaving extra stock ("machining allowance") so the decarburized layer is removed in finishing. Decarburization is measured by metallographic examination or microhardness traverses of a cross-section.

Deliberate decarburization

The term also describes intentional carbon removal in steelmaking, as in the refining of pig iron—whose carbon content is reduced in converters and electric furnaces to produce steel—and in processes such as the argon oxygen decarburization (AOD) converter used for stainless steel, where argon dilution allows carbon removal while minimizing chromium oxidation.
