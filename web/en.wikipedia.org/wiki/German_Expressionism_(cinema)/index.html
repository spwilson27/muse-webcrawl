German Expressionism (cinema)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

German Expressionism was a movement in German cinema of the 1920s, part of the wider Expressionist current in the arts, that emphasised distorted sets, stark chiaroscuro lighting, oblique camera angles and psychological themes of madness, betrayal and dread. Emerging after the First World War in the studios of Weimar Berlin, it produced some of the most influential films of the silent era.

Characteristics and key films

The movement's landmark work is Robert Wiene's The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), whose painted, jagged sets externalised a madman's worldview. Other classics include F. W. Murnau's Nosferatu (1922), an unauthorised adaptation of Dracula; Paul Wegener's The Golem (1920); and Fritz Lang's Dr. Mabuse the Gambler (1922), Die Nibelungen (1924) and Metropolis (1927). Rather than realism, directors pursued stylisation: sets were built or painted to embody inner states, and acting was exaggerated and gestural.

Context and decline

Germany's wartime isolation from foreign films, inflation-driven investment in the film industry, and the resources of the Ufa studio sustained the movement. It waned in the later 1920s as budgets tightened, the New Objectivity favoured realism, and sound arrived. Many of its practitioners emigrated to Hollywood after 1933, carrying its lighting and design techniques into film noir and the horror film.
