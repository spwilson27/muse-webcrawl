Gilt (finance)

Gilts are bonds issued by the British government, formally known as gilt-edged securities. The name derives from the gilded edges of the paper stock certificates once issued. Gilts are denominated in sterling, pay interest (the coupon) half-yearly, and are issued and managed on the government's behalf by the United Kingdom Debt Management Office (DMO), an executive agency of HM Treasury created in 1998.

Types

Conventional gilts pay a fixed coupon and a fixed redemption value at maturities ranging from a few years to over 50 years; they are named by coupon and redemption year, e.g. "4¼% Treasury Gilt 2034". Index-linked gilts, first issued in 1981, have both coupon and principal adjusted in line with inflation — historically the Retail Prices Index, with newer issues and reforms moving toward CPIH. A small number of undated gilts, descendants of the consolidated stock ("Consols") created in the 18th century, were redeemed in 2015.

Market

Gilts are traded on the London Stock Exchange and are held heavily by pension funds and insurers, whose long-term liabilities they match, as well as by the Bank of England — whose Asset Purchase Facility held a large share of the stock under quantitative easing after 2009 and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Gilt yields are the benchmark risk-free rate for sterling interest-rate markets and anchor the pricing of mortgages and corporate debt in the UK.

The gilt market drew global attention in September–October 2022, when sharp rises in long-dated yields following the government's "mini-budget" triggered a crisis in liability-driven investment (LDI) pension strategies and forced the Bank of England to intervene with temporary bond purchases to restore market functioning.
