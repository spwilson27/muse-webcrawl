Dark Lady (Shakespeare)

The Dark Lady is a woman described in Shakespeare's sonnets (sonnets 127–152), part of the sequence first published in 1609. She is so called because the poems describe her as having dark hair and dark features, in deliberate contrast to the fair beauty conventionally praised in Renaissance love poetry. Unlike the Fair Youth addressed in the first 126 sonnets, the Dark Lady is the speaker's mistress in a plainly sexual relationship marked by desire, deception, and mutual infidelity.

The sonnets

In Sonnet 130, the most famous of the group, Shakespeare mocks the blazon tradition of Petrarchan poetry:

My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;
Coral is far more red than her lips' red;
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.

The sequence depicts a tangled affair: the Dark Lady betrays the speaker with the Fair Youth, and the speaker, though knowing her false, remains bound to her — "For I have sworn thee fair and thought thee bright, / Who art as black as hell, as dark as night" (Sonnet 147 paraphrases this fever of desire).

Proposed identities

Because nothing certain is known about her, the Dark Lady has attracted more speculation than perhaps any other unnamed figure in English literature. Proposed candidates include Emilia Lanier (née Bassano), a poet and musician of Italian-Jewish descent, championed by A. L. Rowse; Mary Fitton, a maid of honour at Elizabeth's court; the London brothel-keeper known as "Lucy Negro"; and Shakespeare's fellow actor's wife, Jane Davenant. None of these identifications commands scholarly consensus, and many critics hold that the Dark Lady may be a literary construction rather than a portrait of a single historical person.
