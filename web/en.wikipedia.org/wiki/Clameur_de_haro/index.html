Clameur de haro

Raising the clameur de haro, an ancient Norman legal remedy still available in the Channel Islands.

The clameur de haro (French pronunciation: [klamœʁ də aʁo]) is an ancient legal injunction of restraint employed in the Channel Islands, particularly on the islands of Jersey and Guernsey. It also survives as a fully ratified law on the island of Sark, where it was last used on 28 June 2023 — and again on 11 August 2025 — for issues pertaining to Seigneurial land rights.

A person can raise the clameur by kneeling and reciting the Lord's Prayer in French, then crying out:

Haro! Haro! Haro! À l'aide, mon Prince, on me fait tort.
("Haro! Haro! Haro! To my aid, my Prince, I am being wronged.")

After the clameur is raised, the offending act — usually some interference with land or property — must cease immediately until the matter can be heard by the court. It is an offence to continue the disputed activity once the clameur has been raised.

History

The clameur is a relic of Norman customary law. "Haro" is traditionally said to derive from an appeal to Rollo, the first Duke of Normandy: the cry Ha! Rollo supposedly invoked the duke's protection. According to legend, the clameur was raised at the funeral of William the Conqueror in 1087, when a citizen of Caen interrupted the burial by claiming the church stood on land stolen from his family; the claim was investigated and the man compensated.

In Guernsey, the clameur must be raised in the presence of witnesses, and within 24 hours the person raising it must register it at the Greffe (the island's registry). The Royal Court then holds a hearing to decide whether the complaint is well founded; an unjustified clameur can result in a fine.

Modern usage

Despite its antiquity, the clameur has been raised several times in modern Guernsey and Sark, typically in disputes over building works encroaching on property. In Guernsey it was invoked in cases in the 21st century, including a 2017 dispute over a garden boundary. In Jersey the remedy is also still available under customary law.
