Conditional expectation

In probability theory, the conditional expectation of a random variable is its expected value given that a certain set of conditions or information is known. If the random variable can take only a limited number of outcomes given the condition, the conditional expectation reduces to a weighted average of those outcomes with the conditional probabilities as weights.

Conditional expectation averages a random variable over the outcomes consistent with known information.

Formal definition

In the measure-theoretic formulation of Andrey Kolmogorov, the conditional expectation of an integrable random variable X with respect to a sub-σ-algebra G, written E[X | G], is itself a random variable — the G-measurable function whose integral over every event in G equals the integral of X over that event. Conditioning on another random variable Y gives E[X | Y]. Key properties include the law of total expectation, E[E[X | Y]] = E[X], "taking out what is known", and linearity.

Applications

Conditional expectation is fundamental to statistics, where regression functions are conditional expectations; to stochastic processes, where martingales are defined as processes whose conditional expectation given the past equals their current value; and to filtering, finance and decision theory, where optimal predictions minimise mean squared error by taking conditional expectations.
