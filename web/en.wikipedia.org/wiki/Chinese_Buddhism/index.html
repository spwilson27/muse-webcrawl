Chinese Buddhism

A Mahayana Buddhist temple in China

Chinese Buddhism (漢傳佛教, Hànchuán Fójiào) is Buddhism in its Chinese form, shaped by Chinese culture and philosophy since its introduction from India during the Han dynasty, around the 1st century CE. It is the largest institutionalized religion in mainland China and a major form of Mahayana Buddhism, whose canon of scriptures in Chinese translation influenced Buddhism throughout East Asia, including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.

History

Buddhism arrived in China along the Silk Road. According to tradition, Emperor Ming of Han sponsored its introduction around 67 CE. During the following centuries, monks such as Kumārajīva and Xuanzang translated vast numbers of Sanskrit texts into Chinese; Xuanzang's 7th-century pilgrimage to India became the basis for the classic novel Journey to the West. Indigenous schools arose during the Sui and Tang dynasties, including Tiantai, Huayan, Chan (Zen), and Pure Land, which interacted with Confucianism and Daoism—the "three teachings" that together shaped Chinese religion.

Schools and practice

Chinese Buddhism is predominantly Mahayana, centered on the Chinese Buddhist canon (the Tripiṭaka in Chinese). Important practices include recitation of the Buddha's name (nianfo), meditation, chanting of sutras and mantras such as the Great Compassion Mantra, vegetarianism within monasteries, and the worship of bodhisattvas such as Guanyin, the Chinese form of Avalokiteśvara. Major temple complexes include Shaolin Temple, associated with Chan and martial arts.

Modern period

Buddhism in China suffered suppression during the Taiping Rebellion, the early Republican era, and especially the Cultural Revolution, but has revived since the 1980s, with temples restored and millions of lay adherents. In Taiwan and overseas Chinese communities, "Humanistic Buddhism" movements such as Fo Guang Shan and Tzu Chi have become globally influential.
