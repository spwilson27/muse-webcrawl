Charles J. Fillmore

Charles John Fillmore (August 29, 1929 – February 13, 2014) was an American linguist and Professor of Linguistics at the University of California, Berkeley. He is best known for originating case grammar, a theory of the deep semantic roles borne by sentence constituents, and for developing frame semantics, which became a foundation of cognitive linguistics and of the FrameNet lexical database project he led in his later career.

Case grammar

Fillmore gained his doctorate from the University of Michigan in 1961 and taught at Ohio State University before moving to Berkeley in 1971. His celebrated 1968 paper "The Case for Case", published in the wake of Chomsky's transformational grammar, argued that sentences are built from a verb and a set of semantic "cases" — deep roles such as Agentive, Instrumental, Dative, Locative and Objective — and that grammatical relations like subject and object are derived from these roles. Case grammar strongly influenced later work on thematic roles and valency.

Frame semantics and FrameNet

From the mid-1970s Fillmore developed frame semantics: the idea that a word's meaning can only be understood relative to a structured background of experience, a semantic frame. Understanding buy, for example, requires the commercial transaction frame with its roles of buyer, seller, goods and money. At the International Computer Science Institute in Berkeley, Fillmore founded the FrameNet project (1997), which analyses corpus examples of English words and records the frames they evoke and the ways their participants are expressed, a resource widely used in natural language processing.

Other contributions

With Paul Kay and others, Fillmore also contributed to construction grammar, arguing that grammar consists of learned pairings of form and meaning at every level of complexity. He was president of the Linguistic Society of America in 1984 and received the society's lifetime achievement recognition; he remained an emeritus professor at Berkeley until his death in 2014.
