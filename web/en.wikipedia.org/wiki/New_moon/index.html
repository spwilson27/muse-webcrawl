New moon

In astronomy, a new moon is the first lunar phase, when the Moon and Sun have the same ecliptic longitude. At this phase the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, so the side facing Earth is not illuminated and the Moon is generally invisible to the naked eye, appearing as a dark disc against the sky.

Lunar phases and calendars

The new moon marks the beginning of a lunation, lasting on average about 29.5 days until the next new moon. It defines the first day of the month in lunar and lunisolar calendars: the Islamic calendar month begins at the sighting of the crescent after new moon, Chinese New Year falls on the second (occasionally third) new moon after the winter solstice, and Rosh Chodesh marks the new moon in the Hebrew calendar. A solar eclipse can occur only at new moon, when the Moon passes directly between Earth and Sun.

The opposite phase, when the whole Earth-facing side is lit, is the full moon. The thin crescent seen a day or two after new moon is the waxing crescent, traditionally watched for by calendar keepers.
