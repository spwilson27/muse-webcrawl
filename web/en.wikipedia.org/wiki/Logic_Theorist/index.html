Logic Theorist

The Logic Theorist is a computer program written in 1955–56 by Allen Newell, Herbert A. Simon and Cliff Shaw at the RAND Corporation and Carnegie Institute of Technology. It is widely regarded as the first artificial intelligence program: it was designed to mimic human problem-solving and to prove theorems of mathematical logic automatically.

Description

Logic Theorist attempted to prove the theorems of propositional logic in Chapter 2 of Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica. Working from the axioms, it applied heuristic rules — substitution, detachment (modus ponens) and chaining — searching backwards from the theorem to be proved, and using heuristics modelled on the authors' observations of human reasoning to control the search. Of the first 52 theorems of the Principia, the program proved 38. For theorem 2.85 it found a proof shorter and more elegant than Whitehead and Russell's own; Simon wrote to Bertrand Russell, who responded with delight. A journal submission of the machine proof was, however, rejected.

To express the program the authors developed the Information Processing Language (IPL), one of the first list-processing languages and a precursor of Lisp. The program first ran in the summer of 1956 (a hand simulation was demonstrated earlier), and was presented at the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, the founding event of artificial intelligence research.

Significance

Logic Theorist introduced core ideas of AI: heuristic search, list processing, and the concept of a program that manipulates symbols to simulate reasoning. Newell and Simon generalised its approach in the General Problem Solver (1957) and in the "physical symbol system hypothesis", which held that symbol manipulation is the essential basis of intelligent action — work for which Simon won the Nobel Prize in Economics (1978) and both men shared the Turing Award (1975).
