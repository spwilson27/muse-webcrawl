Diocese

In church governance, a diocese (from Greek dioíkēsis, "administration") is an ecclesiastical district under the jurisdiction of a bishop. The term originally denoted an administrative division of the late Roman Empire, and was adopted by the early Church as its own territorial organisation spread through the empire. Dioceses form the basic territorial unit of many Christian churches, including the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Anglican Communion, Lutheran churches and others.

History

The word derives from the Roman administrative reforms of Diocletian and Constantine, under which the empire was divided into large units called dioceses, each grouping several provinces. As Christianity became established, the Church organised itself along similar lines, with bishops overseeing cities and their surrounding territory. Over time the ecclesiastical meaning became primary in the West, where each diocese centred on its cathedral city.

Organisation

A diocese is normally divided into parishes, each served by a parish priest, and parishes may be grouped into deaneries. The diocesan bishop governs with the assistance of diocesan offices — the curia — including a chancellor, judicial vicar and tribunals. Dioceses are in turn grouped into ecclesiastical provinces headed by a metropolitan archdiocese while remaining largely self-governing in their internal affairs. In the Roman Catholic Church, dioceses are erected, altered or suppressed only by the pope, and the diocesan bishop is described in canon law as governing his particular church in the name of Christ.

Other traditions

In the Anglican Communion each diocese is a constituent part of a national or regional church, and its bishop works with elected synods of clergy and laity. Eastern Orthodox dioceses are often called eparchies. Some Lutheran churches, such as the Church of Sweden, retain dioceses and bishops; other Protestant churches use equivalent districts under different names, such as presbyteries or conferences.
