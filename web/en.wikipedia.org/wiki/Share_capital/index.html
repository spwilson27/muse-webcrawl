Share capital

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Share capital is the portion of a corporation's equity that has been obtained by the issue of shares in the corporation to a shareholder, usually for cash. In accounting terms, share capital is the nominal (par) value of the issued shares, while amounts paid above par are typically recorded separately as share premium (additional paid-in capital). Together with retained earnings and other reserves, share capital forms part of shareholders' equity on the balance sheet.

Components

A company's capital structure in relation to its shares may be described by several related figures: authorized share capital, the maximum amount the company is permitted to issue under its constitutional documents; issued share capital, the nominal value of shares actually issued; subscribed capital, shares taken up by shareholders; called-up capital, the amount the company has demanded from shareholders; and paid-up capital, the amount actually received. In some jurisdictions companies must maintain a minimum share capital as creditor protection.

Legal aspects

Rules on share capital differ between legal systems. Many European civil-law jurisdictions historically required a statutory minimum capital and restrict distributions and share buybacks to protect creditors, while modern United States corporate law largely relies on par value (often nominal) and solvency tests. Shares may be issued at a premium but, in many systems, not at a discount to their nominal value.

Changes in share capital

Companies can increase share capital by new share issues, rights issues, or capitalization of reserves (bonus issues), and reduce it by cancellation of shares, share buybacks, or formal capital reduction procedures, often requiring shareholder approval and sometimes court or regulatory consent.
