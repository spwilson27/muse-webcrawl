A battle rifle is a military service rifle chambered for a full-power rifle cartridge and typically fed from a detachable box magazine, generally with selective-fire or semi-automatic operation. The term distinguishes these weapons from assault rifles, which use intermediate cartridges, and from earlier bolt-action service rifles.

Characteristics

Battle rifles fire cartridges such as the 7.62×51mm NATO, derived from full-power rounds like the .30-06 Springfield and 7.62×54mmR. They offer long effective range and strong barrier penetration, but automatic fire is difficult to control because of recoil, and the ammunition is heavier than intermediate rounds, reducing the load an infantryman can carry.

History

Early self-loading full-power rifles such as the Soviet SVT-40 and the American M1 Garand appeared before and during the Second World War. With the standardisation of the 7.62×51mm NATO cartridge in the 1950s, battle rifles became the standard infantry arm of most Western armies: the Belgian FN FAL ("the right arm of the free world"), the German Heckler & Koch G3, the American M14 and the Italian Beretta BM 59 were issued in large numbers.

Decline and modern roles

From the 1960s onward, experience in Vietnam and elsewhere showed that most infantry combat occurred at short ranges, and assault rifles in intermediate calibres such as 5.56×45mm NATO largely replaced battle rifles. Full-power rifles survive in designated marksman and sniper-support roles — for example the Mk 14 EBR and HK417 — and in the arsenals of many states, where FAL and G3 rifles remain in front-line or reserve service.
