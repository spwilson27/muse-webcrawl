Soichiro Honda (本田 宗一郎, Honda Sōichirō; 17 November 1906 – 5 August 1991) was a Japanese engineer and industrialist who founded the Honda Motor Company, which grew from a small maker of motorised bicycles into one of the world's largest manufacturers of motorcycles and automobiles.

Early life

Honda was born in a village in Shizuoka Prefecture, the son of a blacksmith who also repaired bicycles. Fascinated by machinery from childhood — he famously skipped school to watch an aeroplane — he left home at 15 to apprentice at an automobile repair shop in Tokyo, Art Shokai. By 1928 he had opened his own branch workshop in Hamamatsu and had patented metal wheel spokes. He later founded a piston-ring company, Tōkai Seiki, which struggled with quality before he sold it to Toyota in 1945; the factory was destroyed in wartime bombing and the 1945 Mikawa earthquake.

Founding Honda

In 1946 Honda established the Honda Technical Research Institute in Hamamatsu, fitting war-surplus generator engines to bicycles. The company was incorporated as Honda Motor Co., Ltd. in 1948, with Takeo Fujisawa as his business partner. The 1949 Dream D-Type was followed by the 1958 Super Cub — still the best-selling motor vehicle in history — and success at the Isle of Man TT races established Honda internationally. Honda entered the automobile market in 1963 with the T360 mini truck and S500 sports car, and defied official MITI pressure to merge with existing carmakers. Its 1972 Civic, with the CVCC engine meeting US emissions rules without a catalytic converter, was a landmark achievement.

Later life

Honda stepped down as president in 1973 but remained a director and "supreme advisor". He was inducted into the Automotive Hall of Fame in 1989. He died of liver failure in Tokyo in 1991. His management philosophy — emphasising engineer-led decision-making, joy in work, and respect for the individual — remains central to Honda's corporate culture.
