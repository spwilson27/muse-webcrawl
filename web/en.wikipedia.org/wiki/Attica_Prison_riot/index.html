The Attica Prison riot, also known as the Attica Prison rebellion, the Attica uprising, or the Attica Prison massacre, took place at the state prison in Attica, New York, from September 9 to September 13, 1971. It was one of the most well-known and significant uprisings of the prisoners' rights movement and remains the deadliest prison riot in United States history: of the 43 men who died, 33 were inmates and 10 were correctional officers and civilian employees taken hostage, and all but one guard and three inmates were killed by law enforcement gunfire when the state retook the facility.

Background

By 1971 Attica Correctional Facility was severely overcrowded, holding about 2,200 men in a prison built for 1,600. Inmates complained of chronic overcrowding, censorship of letters and reading material, inadequate medical care, poor food, and limits on religious freedom; Muslim inmates in particular protested restrictions on their worship. Prisoners were allowed one shower per week and one roll of toilet paper per month, and many worked in prison industries for as little as a few cents a day. Racial tension also ran high: the inmate population was majority Black and Puerto Rican, while the guard force was almost entirely white.

In the summer of 1971, a group of inmates presented a set of demands to Commissioner of Corrections Russell Oswald. On August 21, the killing of Black Panther inmate George Jackson at San Quentin Prison in California further inflamed tensions; hundreds of Attica inmates observed a silent hunger strike in his memory. A violent altercation on September 8 between two inmates and guards, and confusion over whether the men would be punished, set the stage for the uprising the next morning.

The uprising

On the morning of September 9, 1971, a scuffle during the movement of inmates through a tunnel area known as "Times Square" escalated when a defective gate gave a group of prisoners access to the central control area. Inmates armed with makeshift weapons seized control of four cellblocks and took 42 staff members hostage. The prisoners consolidated in D Yard, where they organized themselves, elected spokesmen, dug defensive trenches and latrines, and protected the hostages. A negotiating committee presented a list of 27 practical demands and, later, a set of broader political "15 Practical Proposals" covering food, medical care, religious freedom, education, an end to censorship, and amnesty from criminal prosecution for the uprising.

Over four days, a committee of outside observers — including journalist Tom Wicker, congressman Herman Badillo, civil rights lawyer William Kunstler, and Black Panther Bobby Seale — attempted to mediate. Commissioner Oswald agreed to most of the practical demands but refused amnesty and the removal of Superintendent Vincent Mancusi. Governor Nelson Rockefeller declined to come to Attica and, on September 13, ordered the prison retaken by force.

The retaking

On the morning of September 13, state troopers and correctional officers stormed the yard after helicopters dropped tear gas. Troopers fired thousands of rounds into the gas-filled yard; 39 people were killed in the assault — 29 inmates and 10 hostages — and more than 80 men were wounded. Initial official statements falsely claimed that hostages had had their throats slit by inmates; autopsies showed every hostage who died that day had been killed by police bullets. After the retaking, inmates were stripped, beaten, and forced to run a gauntlet of officers, and allegations of torture and denial of medical care followed.

Aftermath

Four inmates had died earlier in the uprising, bringing the total death toll to 43. Indictments were brought against 62 inmates, while only one trooper was indicted and no officials were prosecuted; Governor Hugh Carey closed the investigations in 1977 with pardons and commutations. The McKay Commission, appointed to investigate, concluded in 1972 that the assault was mishandled and that, except for the Indian uprisings of the nineteenth century, the retaking was the bloodiest one-day encounter between Americans since the Civil War.

After decades of litigation, New York State agreed in 2000 to pay $8 million to surviving inmates and the families of those killed, and in 2005 settled with the families of the slain employees for $12 million. The riot became a lasting symbol in debates over mass incarceration, prisoners' rights, and the use of force by the state, and it prompted reforms in New York's prison system, though many of the inmates' demands remained contested for years afterward.
