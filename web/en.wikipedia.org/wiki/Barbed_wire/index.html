Barbed wire, also known as barb wire, is a type of steel fencing wire constructed with sharp edges or points arranged at intervals along the strands. Its modern form was patented in the United States in 1874 by Joseph Glidden of DeKalb, Illinois. Cheap and easy to install, barbed wire transformed the fencing of the American Great Plains, enclosed the open range, and later became a defining material of trench warfare, borders and prisons.

History

Farmers on the treeless plains lacked wood or stone for fences, and smooth wire could not restrain cattle. Several inventors produced thorny wire designs in the 1860s–70s; Glidden's design — barbs crimped onto a double-strand twisted wire, made with a modified coffee grinder — proved practical and manufacturable. It was commercialised by Isaac Ellwood and mass-produced by firms such as Washburn and Moen. Fencing of the open range sparked "fence-cutting wars" in the 1880s between ranchers and farmers, but barbed wire ultimately enabled the settlement and subdivision of the West.

Uses

Agricultural barbed wire is still widely used for livestock fencing. In warfare, from the Boer War and the First World War onward, coils of barbed wire and its razor-edged successor, concertina/razor wire, formed obstacles protecting trenches, camps and borders. Barbed wire became a universal symbol of confinement, oppression and divided lands, appearing in the iconography of concentration camps and the Korean Demilitarized Zone. Glidden's invention is often called "the devil's rope" by those whose open-range way of life it ended.

Construction

Standard barbed wire consists of two galvanised steel strands twisted together, with two-point or four-point barbs spaced at intervals of roughly 8–15 cm. High-tensile versions use thinner, stronger wire. Related products include woven wire fencing, chain-link, and razor wire, which substitutes stamped sharp blades for wrapped barbs.
