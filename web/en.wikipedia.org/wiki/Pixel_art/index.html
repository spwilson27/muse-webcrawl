Pixel art

Pixel art is a form of digital art in which images are built at the level of individual pixels, using limited color palettes and deliberate placement of each pixel. It originated in the constraints of early computer and video game graphics — 8-bit and 16-bit arcade, console, and home computer systems — and survives as a popular aesthetic in indie games, the demoscene, and online communities.

History

The term "pixel art" was in print by 1982 (Adele Goldberg and Robert Flegal of Xerox PARC), though the practice emerged through the 1970s as games like Space Invaders (1978) and Pac-Man (1980) defined iconic sprite-based characters within strict hardware palettes and resolutions. Artists on the Commodore 64, ZX Spectrum, NES, and Amiga refined techniques — dithering to simulate extra colors, careful cluster shapes, and selective outlining — under tight memory limits. As 3D graphics took over mainstream games in the late 1990s, pixel art moved into mobile games, web graphics, and a nostalgic revival in indie titles such as Fez, Hyper Light Drifter, Stardew Valley, and Celeste.

Techniques and conventions

Purists distinguish pixel art from other raster images by the intentional control of every pixel, limited palettes, and avoidance of automatic filters, gradients, and scaling artifacts. Common practices include dithering patterns for shading, manual anti-aliasing, "banding" avoidance, and hue-shifting in color ramps. Community tools include Aseprite, GraphicsGale, and Pro Motion NG.

In culture

Pixel art aesthetics appear in advertising, fashion, emoji-like icons, and large collaborative projects such as Reddit's r/place (2017, 2022, 2023), where millions of users placed individual pixels on a shared canvas. Isometric pixel scenes ("eBoy"-style cityscapes) became a recognizable commercial illustration style in the 2000s.
