Runoff election

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A runoff election is the second round of an election conducted when no candidate achieves the required threshold (usually an absolute majority) in the first round. In the most common form, the two-round system, the top two candidates from the first round contest the runoff — the system used for presidential elections in France and most of Latin America and post-communist Europe, and for many US primaries and local offices in the South (a legacy institution whose origins historians connect to the one-party Democratic era and, in part, to diluting Black voting power — a contested history).

Variants include exhaustive ballots (rounds with eliminations, used by legislatures electing officers), supplementary vote (voters' second preferences redistribute once), and instant runoff, which simulates sequential runoffs on one ballot. Runoffs are credited with producing majority-mandated winners and letting first-round voters “vote with the heart, then with the head” (the French formula), and criticised for cost, lower second-round turnout, and incentives for first-round fragmentation. Georgia's US Senate runoffs of 2021 (which decided control of the chamber) are the modern American showcase of the institution's stakes.
