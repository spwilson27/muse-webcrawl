Fortifications of Quebec

The fortifications of Quebec City are the defensive walls, gates, bastions, and citadel surrounding Old Quebec in Quebec City, Canada. Quebec is the only city in North America north of Mexico whose historic city walls survive substantially intact; together with the surrounding historic district they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site (designated 1985). The defences crown the heights of Cape Diamond above the St. Lawrence River, a naturally strong position chosen by Samuel de Champlain when he founded the city in 1608.

French period

Early defences were palisades and small forts. After the English Kirke brothers briefly seized Quebec in 1629 and as threats grew, permanent works developed; major masonry fortifications were planned under Governor Frontenac in the 1690s, notably after the failed English attack of 1690. Following the fall of Louisbourg in 1745, engineer Gaspard-Joseph Chaussegros de Léry directed construction of a full enceinte on the landward side in 1745–1748 — works that were in place when the British attacked in 1759, though the decisive Battle of the Plains of Abraham was fought outside the walls, and the city surrendered after bombardment.

British period and the Citadel

The British maintained and improved the walls, regarding Quebec as the linchpin of Canada's defence, particularly during and after the American Revolution and the War of 1812. The star-shaped Citadel of Quebec was built between 1820 and 1850 on the highest point of Cape Diamond to designs influenced by Vauban's principles; it remains an active garrison of the Royal 22e Régiment and an official residence of the Governor General of Canada. Existing city gates were rebuilt in their present picturesque form in the late 19th century, after demolition pressures — resisted by Governor General Lord Dufferin — instead turned the walls into a celebrated heritage asset with promenades atop the ramparts.

Today

About 4.6 kilometres of ramparts survive, with gates such as Saint-Louis, Kent, Saint-Jean, and the Prescott Gate. Parks Canada administers the fortifications as a National Historic Site, and the walls define the boundary of Old Quebec.
