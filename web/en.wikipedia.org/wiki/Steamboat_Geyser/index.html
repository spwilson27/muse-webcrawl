Steamboat Geyser, in Yellowstone National Park's Norris Geyser Basin, is the world's tallest currently active geyser. During major eruptions, water is thrown more than 300 feet (90 m) into the air, with reports of some eruptions exceeding 375 feet (114 m) — taller than Old Faithful and any other active geyser. Minor eruptions of 10 to 40 feet (3 to 12 m) are far more frequent.

Eruption behaviour

Steamboat's major eruptions are entirely unpredictable, with intervals ranging from three days to fifty years. After a period of activity in the 1960s and 1980s, it erupted only once between 1991 and 2013, then sporadically in 2013–2014, before entering an exceptionally active phase in March 2018: it erupted 32 times in 2018, 48 times in 2019 and 48 times in 2020, setting records for the number of major eruptions in a calendar year. An eruption consists of a water phase of 3 to 40 minutes, followed by a powerful, noisy steam phase lasting hours.

History

The geyser was named in 1878 by Philetus Norris, the park superintendent, for the steamboat-like sound of its steam phase. In 1878–79 it erupted frequently, but the development of nearby features, including the lowering of Cistern Spring's water level during eruptions (the two features are connected), has long modulated its behaviour. Automobiles in the parking area have occasionally been coated with mineral-laden spray, and the geyser's discharges killed trees around its vent. In 2018 park scientists and the USGS Yellowstone Volcano Observatory installed additional monitoring to study the reactivation; the cause of the active phase remains debated but does not appear to signal volcanic unrest.

Geology

Steamboat sits in the Norris Geyser Basin, the hottest and most acidic thermal area in Yellowstone. Its plumbing likely extends to unusual depth, which may explain the exceptional height of its jets: the boiling water source and reservoir geometry allow a much larger volume of superheated water to flash to steam than in shallower geysers.
