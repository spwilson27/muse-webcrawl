Vehicle armour

Vehicle armour
Sloped armour on a modern armoured vehicle

Vehicle armour comprises protective coverings fitted to military and civilian vehicles — including tanks, armoured personnel carriers, ships, aircraft and trains — to resist damage from projectiles, explosives, fragments and other threats. The design of vehicle armour is a continual contest against the development of anti-armour weapons.

Materials

Traditional vehicle armour is made of steel, often rolled homogeneous armour (RHA), the protection of other materials being conventionally expressed as an equivalent thickness of RHA. Aluminium alloys are used where weight is critical, as on many infantry fighting vehicles. Modern composite armour combines layers of metals, ceramics, plastics and air gaps to defeat shaped-charge and kinetic-energy threats at lower weight; famous examples include British Chobham armour. Ceramics such as alumina and boron carbide are hard and light but brittle, and are used in combination with backing materials.

Design features

  • Sloped armour increases the effective thickness a projectile must penetrate and raises the chance of deflection.
  • Spaced armour uses separated plates to disrupt shaped-charge jets and strip penetrators.
  • Reactive armour (explosive reactive armour, ERA) sandwiches explosive between plates that explode outward when struck, disrupting the incoming threat.
  • Slat armour uses a cage of bars to prematurely detonate or crush the warheads of rocket-propelled grenades.
  • Spall liners reduce the cone of fragments produced inside the vehicle when the armour is struck.

Applications

Armoured fighting vehicles carry the heaviest protection, main battle tanks concentrating armour on the turret and hull front. Warships historically carried belt and deck armour, largely abandoned in the missile age in favour of compartmentation and active defences. Civilian applications include armoured cars for cash transport, police vehicles, and protected limousines for officials, which use ballistic steel, aramid composites and bullet-resistant glass.
