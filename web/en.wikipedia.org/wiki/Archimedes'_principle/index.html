Archimedes' principle

A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.

Archimedes' principle (also spelled Archimedes's principle) states that the upward buoyant force exerted on a body immersed in a fluid, whether fully or partially submerged, is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. It is a fundamental law of fluid mechanics, formulated by Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BC) in his work On Floating Bodies.

Fb = ρ · V · g

where Fb is the buoyant force, ρ is the density of the fluid, V is the volume of displaced fluid, and g is the acceleration due to gravity.

The "Eureka" story

According to the Roman architect Vitruvius, Archimedes discovered the principle while taking a bath. King Hiero II had asked him to determine whether a votive crown was made of pure gold without damaging it. Noticing the water level rise as he entered the bath, Archimedes realised that the volume of water displaced equalled the volume of the submerged part of his body, giving a way to measure the crown's volume and hence its density. He is said to have run naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!" ("I have found it!"). The story, first recorded two centuries later, is doubted in its details, but the principle itself is genuinely Archimedes'.

Explanation

The buoyant force arises from the pressure gradient in a fluid under gravity: pressure increases with depth, so the upward force on the bottom of an immersed body exceeds the downward force on its top. The net upward force equals the weight of the fluid that would occupy the submerged volume. If the body's average density is less than the fluid's, it floats with a fraction submerged such that the displaced fluid's weight equals the body's total weight; this explains why ships float and why a hydrometer can measure fluid density.

Applications

  • Ship design and loading (displacement tonnage)
  • Submarines and buoyancy control in diving
  • Hot-air balloons and airships (buoyancy in air)
  • Hydrometers and densitometry
  • Geology: isostatic floating of the Earth's crust
