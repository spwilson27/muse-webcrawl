Wireless telegraphy

A shipboard wireless operator sending Morse code, c. 1910.

Wireless telegraphy, or radiotelegraphy, is the transmission of text messages by radio waves using Morse code or other telegraph codes, without connecting wires. It was the first form of radio communication, developed in the 1890s and dominant for the first three decades of radio.

History

Building on the experiments of Heinrich Hertz, Oliver Lodge, Alexander Popov, and Guglielmo Marconi, wireless telegraphy was commercialised around 1900. Marconi's company established transatlantic signalling in 1901 and built a network of shore and ship stations. Early transmitters used spark gaps, later replaced by arc converters, high-frequency alternators, and vacuum-tube continuous-wave transmitters, which allowed narrower bandwidths and greater range.

Use

Radiotelegraphy transformed maritime safety: distress calls sent by wireless, notably during the sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912, led to requirements that large ships carry wireless equipment and maintain a continuous watch. It was used extensively by navies, point-to-point commercial services, press agencies, and amateur radio operators.

Decline

Voice radiotelephony, teleprinter networks, and later digital modes displaced Morse telegraphy. The maritime Morse distress service ended in 1999 with the full implementation of the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System, though amateur operators still use Morse code (CW) today.
