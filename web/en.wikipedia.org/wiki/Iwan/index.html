Iwan

TypeVaulted hall open on one side
OriginPersian / Parthian–Sasanian architecture
RegionsIran, Central Asia, Islamic world

An iwan (Persian: ایوان, eyvān; Arabic: إيوان) is a rectangular hall or space, usually vaulted, walled on three sides and entirely open on the fourth, serving as a monumental entrance or audience space. It is a hallmark of Persian and later Islamic architecture.

Origins

The form developed in Mesopotamia and Iran under the Parthians and Sasanians; grand examples survive at Ctesiphon — the Tāq Kasrā, whose great brick vault is among the largest single-span vaults of antiquity — and in palace complexes such as Firuzabad and Sarvestan.

Islamic architecture

The iwan became central to Seljuk, Timurid and Safavid design. The four-iwan plan arranges iwans facing each other across a courtyard in mosques, madrasas, caravanserais and palaces — as in Isfahan's Shah Mosque and the Friday Mosque, and across Mughal India (e.g. the Taj Mahal's facades). Often framed by a raised pishtaq portal and flanked by minarets, the iwan is richly decorated with tilework, muqarnas and calligraphy.
