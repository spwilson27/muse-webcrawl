Existentialism is a family of philosophical views, prominent in 19th- and 20th-century Europe, holding that thinking begins with the experiencing individual: each person, thrown into a world without built-in meaning, defines themselves through free choices. The label was embraced by Jean-Paul Sartre in the 1940s and applied retrospectively to Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche; some associated thinkers, such as Martin Heidegger and Albert Camus, rejected the term.

Themes

Sartre's formula 'existence precedes essence' denies a fixed human nature; with radical freedom come anguish, responsibility and the temptation of 'bad faith'. Camus explored the absurd — the clash between our demand for meaning and the world's silence — in The Myth of Sisyphus. Religious existentialists (Kierkegaard, Karl Jaspers, Gabriel Marcel) made faith central; atheistic existentialists drew opposite conclusions. The movement shaped post-war literature, theatre, psychology (Viktor Frankl, Rollo May) and theology.
