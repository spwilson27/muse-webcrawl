Emphysema

Emphysema

Specialty: Pulmonology

Symptoms: Shortness of breath, chronic cough, wheezing

Causes: Tobacco smoking, air pollution, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Risk factors: Smoking, occupational dusts and fumes

Treatment: Smoking cessation, bronchodilators, oxygen therapy, rehabilitation

Emphysema is a long-term lung disease in which the alveoli — the tiny air sacs at the ends of the bronchioles — become damaged and enlarged, reducing the surface area available for gas exchange and trapping air in the lungs. Together with chronic bronchitis, it is one of the two main conditions that make up chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Signs and symptoms

The main symptom is progressive shortness of breath, initially on exertion and later at rest, often accompanied by chronic cough, wheezing, and reduced exercise tolerance. Because exhaling becomes difficult, some patients develop a "barrel chest". Classically, emphysema-predominant patients were nicknamed "pink puffers" for their laboured breathing and relatively preserved blood oxygen, in contrast to the "blue bloaters" of chronic bronchitis.

Causes and pathophysiology

The overwhelming cause is cigarette smoking; other causes include air pollution, occupational dusts, and the inherited disorder alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, which can cause early-onset disease even in non-smokers. Inhaled oxidants provoke inflammation that destroys alveolar walls and the elastic fibres (elastin) that give the lung its recoil, largely through an imbalance between proteases such as neutrophil elastase and protective antiproteases. Loss of elastic recoil lets small airways collapse during exhalation, trapping air and hyperinflating the lungs.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on pulmonary function tests (spirometry) showing airflow obstruction with air trapping and an increased total lung capacity, together with a reduced diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). Chest CT can show areas of destroyed lung (bullae) and low-attenuation regions.

Treatment

There is no cure, and destroyed alveoli cannot regenerate. Smoking cessation is the most important step to slow progression. Management includes inhaled bronchodilators and corticosteroids, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus, and long-term oxygen therapy in advanced disease. Selected patients may benefit from lung volume reduction surgery or endobronchial valves, and end-stage disease may require lung transplantation. Alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation therapy is available for patients with the genetic deficiency.
