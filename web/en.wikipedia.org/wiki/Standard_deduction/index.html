Standard deduction

The standard deduction is a fixed dollar amount in the United States federal income tax system that reduces the amount of income on which a taxpayer is taxed. It is available to taxpayers who do not itemise their deductions, and its amount depends on filing status, age, blindness, and dependency status. Because it is available without substantiation of expenses, the standard deduction greatly simplifies filing for most taxpayers.

History

The standard deduction was introduced in 1944 as part of wartime tax simplification, when the number of Americans required to file returns grew dramatically. Its value rose steadily with periodic legislation and inflation adjustments. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 roughly doubled it (to $12,000 for single filers and $24,000 for married couples filing jointly at the time), while suspending the personal exemption; as a result, only about one in ten taxpayers itemised in the years that followed. The amounts are adjusted annually for inflation — for tax year 2024 they were $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married filing jointly, with additional amounts for taxpayers aged 65 or older or blind.

Mechanics

A taxpayer compares the standard deduction with the sum of itemised deductions (such as state and local taxes subject to the SALT cap, mortgage interest, and charitable contributions) and claims whichever is larger. Dependent taxpayers have a limited standard deduction: the greater of a minimum amount or earned income plus a small addition, capped at the regular amount for a single filer. Most U.S. states with an income tax offer their own standard deduction, which may differ substantially from the federal amount.
