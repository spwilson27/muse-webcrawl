Sandhi

Sandhi (Sanskrit: सन्धि, saṃdhi, "joining") is a cover term for a wide variety of phonological changes that occur at morpheme or word boundaries across languages. Examples are the fusion of sounds across word boundaries, as in Sanskrit, the alteration of a final consonant before a vowel in French liaison, and tone sandhi in Chinese languages. The term derives from the grammatical tradition of Sanskrit, codified by the ancient grammarian Pāṇini.

Sandhi describes the sound changes that occur where words or morphemes join.

Types

Sandhi is conventionally divided into internal sandhi, occurring within a word between morphemes, and external sandhi, occurring between separate words. Changes may involve assimilation, elision, insertion, or fusion of adjacent sounds.

In Sanskrit

Sanskrit sandhi rules are extensive and obligatory in prose and verse: a final sound and the following initial sound combine according to precise rules — for example, a + i becomes e (guṇa), and a + e becomes ai (vṛddhi). Visarga sandhi, vowel coalescence, and rules for final consonants were exhaustively catalogued in Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī. Because Sanskrit texts are written with sandhi applied, learners must "undo" the changes to identify words.

In other languages

Tone sandhi is widespread in Chinese languages, such as the change of the third tone in Mandarin before another third tone, and the complex tone circles of Taiwanese Hokkien. In French, liaison realizes a latent final consonant before a vowel; in English, the alternation of the indefinite article a/an is a simple sandhi effect. Celtic initial mutations are another related family of boundary phenomena.
