Chaldean Neo-Aramaic

Chaldean Neo-Aramaic, also called Chaldean or Kaldāya, is a Neo-Aramaic language spoken by the Chaldean Catholics, an Eastern Catholic community historically concentrated in the Nineveh Plains and the cities of northern Iraq, with large diaspora communities in the United States (notably metropolitan Detroit), Canada, Australia, and Europe. It belongs to the Northeastern Neo-Aramaic (NENA) group and is a modern descendant of the Aramaic language family spoken in northern Mesopotamia for over two millennia.

Background

The label "Chaldean" for the language reflects the identity of the Chaldean Catholic Church, which formed in the 16th–17th centuries when groups of the Church of the East entered communion with Rome. Linguistically, Chaldean Neo-Aramaic is one of a cluster of closely related, sometimes mutually intelligible Christian NENA dialects — its varieties include those of Alqosh, Tel Keppe, Batnaya, Karamlesh, and other Nineveh Plains towns — and it sits alongside Assyrian Neo-Aramaic, from which some speakers distinguish it for ethnic and religious rather than strictly linguistic reasons. Scholars generally treat the two as closely related varieties within NENA.

Features

Chaldean Neo-Aramaic preserves the Aramaic consonantal inventory augmented by loanwords from Arabic, Kurdish, Persian, Turkish, and (in the diaspora) English. Like other NENA varieties, it has undergone extensive restructuring: the old prefix and suffix conjugations have been reworked into a system built on participial forms, word order has become more rigid, and the emphatic state has lost many of its classical functions. It is written in the East Syriac (Madnhāyā) script, read right to left, while Classical Syriac remains the liturgical language of the Chaldean Church.

Status

The language is considered endangered. Persecution and displacement — from the Assyrian genocide (Sayfo) of 1915, through the Iraq wars, to the 2014 ISIS invasion of the Nineveh Plains — scattered its speakers worldwide. In the diaspora, younger generations commonly shift to English or other host-country languages, though community schools, churches, satellite media, and online initiatives work to maintain the language.
