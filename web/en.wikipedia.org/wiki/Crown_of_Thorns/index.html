According to the New Testament, a woven crown of thorns (Greek: στέφανος ἐξ ἀκανθῶν, stephanos ex akanthōn) was placed on the head of Jesus during the events leading up to his crucifixion. It was one of the instruments of the Passion, employed by his captors both to cause him pain and to mock his claim of authority as "King of the Jews."

A relic venerated as the Crown of Thorns was brought from Constantinople to Paris in 1239 by King Louis IX of France, who built the Sainte-Chapelle to house it. Kept for centuries at Notre-Dame Cathedral, it was famously rescued from the cathedral's April 2019 fire and has been returned to display at Notre-Dame following the cathedral's restoration. Individual thorns, distributed as gifts across medieval Europe, are venerated at many other churches.

In the Gospels

The crown of thorns is mentioned in the Gospels of Matthew (27:29), Mark (15:17), and John (19:2, 5). After Jesus was scourged, Roman soldiers "twisted together a crown of thorns and set it on his head," put a staff in his hand and a purple robe on his shoulders in parody of royalty, and struck and mocked him: "Hail, king of the Jews!" In John's account, Pilate presents the crowned and robed Jesus to the crowd with the words Ecce homo — "Behold the man." The plant used is not specified; candidates proposed include the spiny jujube (Ziziphus spina-christi), Paliurus spina-christi (Jerusalem thorn), and various acacias found around Jerusalem.

The Paris relic and its history

Pilgrims reported a crown of thorns at Jerusalem in the early centuries and later at Constantinople, where it was kept in the imperial palace chapel among the Passion relics. In 1238 the Latin emperor Baldwin II pawned the crown to Venetian merchants; Louis IX redeemed it and received it in France in 1239 with legendary solemnity, carrying it barefoot into Paris himself. The purchase price — 135,000 livres, more than half the French crown's annual revenue — plus the construction of the Sainte-Chapelle (consecrated 1248) made the relic the jewel of French royal devotion.

The Paris relic is a circlet of woven rushes (about 21 cm in diameter) from which the thorns were detached and distributed over the centuries; during the French Revolution it was transferred to the Bibliothèque nationale and later, in 1806, to Notre-Dame, where it was displayed in a rock-crystal reliquary tube on first Fridays and during Lent. On 15 April 2019, firefighters and the cathedral's chaplain saved the relic from the burning cathedral. After Notre-Dame's restoration and December 2024 reopening, the crown was returned in procession to the cathedral.

Other thorns and rival claims

Thorns said to be from the crown were prized diplomatic gifts: Louis IX and his successors distributed them across Europe, and dozens survive — many held in reliquaries such as the Holy Thorn Reliquary in the British Museum. Other claimants to the crown (or a crown) have included collections in Rome, Constantinople's successor institutions, and churches in Spain and Germany; the multiplicity reflects medieval relic practice rather than any documentary continuity.

Veneration and iconography

The crown of thorns became one of the Arma Christi (instruments of the Passion) in medieval devotion and art, appearing in countless depictions of the crucifixion, the Man of Sorrows, and Christ as the "Ecce Homo." It also gave its name to plant species (the crown-of-thorns plant, Euphorbia milii; Christ's thorn, Paliurus spina-christi), to starfish, and to numerous churches and religious orders.
