Adit

An adit (from Latin aditus, "entrance") is a horizontal or nearly horizontal passage driven from the surface into the side of a hill or mountain to reach a mine. Adits are used to access ore bodies, drain water from workings by gravity, ventilate a mine, and haul material out without the need for vertical hoisting.

TypeMine entrance / tunnel
PurposeAccess, drainage, ventilation, haulage
ContrastShaft (vertical access)

Use

Unlike a shaft, which is sunk vertically from the surface, an adit exploits topography: where mineral veins crop out on a slope, miners can drive inward at a slight upward gradient (typically a fraction of a percent) so that water flows out and loaded wagons roll downhill. Great drainage adits — such as the "Great County Adit" in Cornwall, which grew to tens of miles of branches — dewatered whole mining districts. In mountainous mining regions such as the Alps, the Andes and the American West, adits were often the primary access before mechanised hoisting.

Related terms

A horizontal passage with only one opening to the surface, driven to explore or develop a mine, is a "drift" in some terminologies, while in American usage a drift follows the vein underground. A short adit may be called a "day level". Horizontal passages driven purely for drainage are sometimes called soughs in Derbyshire lead mining.

Modern use

Adits remain in use in tunnelling, hydroelectric schemes (as access to underground works) and in mining where terrain permits. Abandoned adits pose safety hazards and may be gated to protect roosting bats while excluding people.
