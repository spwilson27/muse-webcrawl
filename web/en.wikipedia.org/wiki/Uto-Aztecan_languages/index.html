Uto-Aztecan languages

The Uto-Aztecan languages are a family of indigenous languages of the Americas, consisting of over 30 languages traditionally spoken in the western United States, Mexico, and Central America. The family's northernmost members, such as Ute and Shoshone, were spoken in the Great Basin and as far north as Idaho, while its southernmost language, Pipil (Nawat), reached El Salvador. The most widely spoken Uto-Aztecan language today is Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec (Mexica) and the lingua franca of much of Mesoamerica at the time of the Spanish conquest, with roughly 1.7 million speakers.

Branches

The family is conventionally divided into northern and southern groupings:

  • Northern Uto-Aztecan: Numic (Ute, Shoshone, Comanche, Paiute), Takic (Luiseño, Cahuilla), Tübatulabal, and Hopi.
  • Southern Uto-Aztecan: Tepiman (O'odham/Pima, Tepehuán), Tarahumaran (Tarahumara, Guarijío), Cahitan (Yaqui, Mayo), Opata (extinct), Corachol (Cora, Huichol), and the Aztecan (Nahuan) languages, including Nahuatl and Pipil.

Homeland and history

The location of the Proto-Uto-Aztecan homeland is debated; proposals include the highlands of central Mexico and the border region between the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. The family began to diversify several thousand years ago, and its southern expansion carried Nahua-speaking peoples into central Mexico, where Nahuatl became the language of the Aztec Empire. Many English and Spanish loanwords derive from Nahuatl, including chocolate, tomato, coyote, and avocado.

Characteristics

Uto-Aztecan languages are typically agglutinative, with complex verb morphology. Several, including Classical Nahuatl, are noted for features such as reduplication, absolutive noun suffixes, and a distinction between animate and inanimate categories in some branches. Many languages of the family are endangered, though Nahuatl, Yaqui, and Huichol retain substantial speaker communities.
