Loyalty program

Loyalty program
TypeMarketing strategy
PurposeEncourage repeat business and customer retention
MechanismsPoints, miles, stamps, tiers, cashback
ExamplesAAdvantage, Nectar, Tesco Clubcard, Starbucks Rewards

A loyalty program is a marketing strategy designed to encourage customers to continue to shop at or use the services of one or more businesses associated with the program. Customers earn points, miles, stamps or other currency for purchases, which can be redeemed for discounts, free products, upgrades or other rewards; many programs also use status tiers that unlock additional benefits.

History

Precursors include 18th-century copper tokens, trading stamps such as S&H Green Stamps in the United States and Green Shield Stamps in Britain, and Betty Crocker box tops. The modern era began with frequent-flyer programs: American Airlines launched AAdvantage in 1981, quickly imitated by rival airlines and hotels. Supermarket programs such as Tesco Clubcard (1995) and coalition schemes such as Nectar (2002) extended the model to everyday retail.

Types

Programs may be points-based, tiered, subscription-based (paid memberships such as Amazon Prime), cashback schemes, or coalition programs in which several businesses share one currency. Digital apps and stored customer data let firms personalise offers, though the collection of purchase data raises privacy questions.

Economics and criticism

Unredeemed points ("breakage") represent a liability for issuers; businesses sell points to partners such as credit-card issuers, making large programs highly profitable. Critics argue that programs can lock in consumers, inflate prices, and primarily serve as data-harvesting tools.
