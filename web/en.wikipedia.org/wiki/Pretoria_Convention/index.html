The Pretoria Convention was a treaty signed on 3 August 1881 between the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and representatives of the Boer population of the Transvaal, formally ending the First Boer War (1880–81). Britain had annexed the Transvaal in 1877; after Boer victories at Bronkhorstspruit, Ingogo, and decisively at Majuba Hill (27 February 1881), the Gladstone government chose negotiation over escalation.

Terms

Under the convention, the Transvaal regained self-government — complete internal self-rule — subject to the suzerainty of the British Crown. Britain retained control over the Transvaal's foreign relations, including the making of treaties with other states (except the Orange Free State) and with African peoples beyond its borders, reserved the right to move troops through the territory in wartime, and kept a British resident in Pretoria. The convention defined the Transvaal's borders and required protection of the rights of the Indigenous population, while the Transvaal accepted financial obligations arising from the annexation period.

Aftermath

The settlement was ratified by the Transvaal Volksraad in October 1881 and government was formally transferred. Paul Kruger, a leading negotiator, became president of the restored South African Republic in 1883. Boer dissatisfaction with the suzerainty clauses and frontier disputes led to renegotiation, and the Pretoria Convention was replaced by the London Convention of 1884, which dropped the word "suzerainty" from the operative text while keeping British control of foreign affairs. The ambiguity of the Transvaal's status remained a source of friction that contributed to the Second Boer War (1899–1902).
