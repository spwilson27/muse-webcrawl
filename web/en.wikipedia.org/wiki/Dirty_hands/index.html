Dirty hands

Dirty hands is a phrase and a problem in political and moral philosophy concerning whether political actors can ever be justified in committing morally wrong acts in order to achieve good outcomes — and, if so, what follows for their moral standing. The phrase is associated with Jean-Paul Sartre's 1948 play Les Mains sales (Dirty Hands), set in a fictional Eastern European country during the Second World War, in which a young party member assassinates a pragmatic party leader.

The problem

Michael Walzer's influential 1973 essay "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" argued that a good politician may sometimes be required to do wrong — for example authorising torture or deceiving the public — for the community's sake, but that the act remains wrong and the politician should acknowledge guilt rather than claim clean justification. The dirty hands problem thus resists both simple consequentialism (the end fully justifies the means) and simple absolutism (wrong acts are never permitted).

Related discussions

The problem is closely related to debates over the ticking time bomb scenario, supreme emergency in just war theory, and Machiavelli's advice in The Prince that rulers must learn "how not to be good".
