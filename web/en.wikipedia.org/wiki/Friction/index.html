Friction

Friction opposes the relative motion of surfaces in contact.

Friction is the force resisting the relative motion of solid surfaces, fluid layers, and material elements sliding against each other. When surfaces in contact move relative to each other, the friction between the two surfaces converts kinetic energy into thermal energy (heat). Friction can also be a useful force: it provides the traction that allows walking, driving, and gripping objects, and without it everyday tasks and machines would not function.

The study of friction falls within tribology, the science of interacting surfaces in relative motion, which also covers wear and lubrication.

History

Leonardo da Vinci first recorded the basic laws of friction in his notebooks in the 15th century, but his findings were unpublished. Guillaume Amontons rediscovered them in 1699, and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb further investigated friction in 1785, distinguishing static from kinetic friction. In the 20th century, work by Bowden and Tabor explained friction in terms of adhesion and deformation of microscopic surface asperities.

Kinds of friction

Dry friction

Dry friction acts between two solid surfaces in contact and is subdivided into static friction, which acts between surfaces at rest relative to each other, and kinetic (dynamic) friction, which acts between moving surfaces. The maximum static friction force is proportional to the normal force:

Fs,max = μs · N

and kinetic friction is approximately:

Fk = μk · N

where μ is the coefficient of friction, a dimensionless quantity depending on the materials and surface conditions, and N is the normal force. Typically μk is slightly less than μs, which is why a stuck object starts moving with a jerk. These relations, known as Amontons' laws and Coulomb's law of friction, are approximations: friction is roughly independent of the apparent contact area and of sliding speed.

Fluid friction

Fluid friction, or viscous drag, occurs between layers of a fluid moving relative to each other and between a fluid and a solid surface. It is central to aerodynamics and hydrodynamics, and in engineering machines it is exploited through lubrication with oils and greases, which replace dry contact with a low-shear-strength film.

Rolling friction

Rolling resistance is the force resisting the motion when a body such as a wheel or ball rolls on a surface. It is much smaller than sliding friction because it arises mainly from deformation of the rolling body and surface rather than surface sliding.

Origin

Even apparently smooth surfaces are rough at the microscopic scale. Contact occurs only at the tips of asperities, so the true contact area is a small fraction of the apparent area and grows in proportion to the load; adhesive junctions formed at these contacts must be sheared for sliding to occur. Frictional heating at the contacts can produce very high local temperatures ("flash temperatures") that influence wear and material behaviour.

Applications and consequences

  • Friction between tyres and road surfaces enables vehicles to accelerate, brake, and corner.
  • Brakes, clutches, and belt drives work by exploiting friction.
  • Friction causes wear and wastes energy in machinery; an appreciable fraction of the world's energy consumption is used to overcome it, motivating lubricant development.
  • Friction matches and frictional heating can spark fires; conversely, controlled friction ignites lighters and starts traditional fire-making.
