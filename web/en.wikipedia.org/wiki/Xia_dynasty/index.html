Xia dynasty

The Xia dynasty (Chinese: 夏朝; pinyin: Xiàcháo) is the first dynasty in traditional Chinese historiography. According to tradition it was established by the legendary Yu the Great after Shun, the last of the Five Emperors, yielded the throne to him, and it ruled from roughly 2070 to 1600 BC before being overthrown by Tang, founder of the Shang dynasty. The Xia is traditionally credited with some 17 kings over 14 generations.

Traditional accounts

Accounts of the Xia appear in texts such as the Bamboo Annals, Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian and the Classic of History. Yu the Great was famed for controlling the floods of the Yellow River, a feat said to have taken thirteen years. The dynasty's last ruler, Jie, is portrayed as corrupt and tyrannical, justifying his overthrow at the Battle of Mingtiao — a narrative pattern repeated with the fall of later dynasties and foundational to the Mandate of Heaven concept. The dynasty also features the story of Yi the Archer and the rebellion of Han Zhuo.

Archaeology and historicity

No contemporary written records have been confirmed for the Xia period — Chinese writing first clearly appears with Shang oracle bones — and its historicity was questioned by the Doubting Antiquity School in the 20th century. Many scholars associate the Xia with the Bronze Age Erlitou culture (c. 1900–1500 BC) in the Yellow River valley of Henan, where excavations have revealed a large urban centre with palace foundations and bronze workshops. China's state-supported Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project (1996–2000) concluded the Xia existed roughly 2070–1600 BC, though its methods remain debated among international scholars.
