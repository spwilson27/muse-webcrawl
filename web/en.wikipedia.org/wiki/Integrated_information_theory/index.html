Integrated information theory

Integrated information theory (IIT) is a theoretical framework, first proposed by neuroscientist Giulio Tononi in 2004 and developed in later versions (IIT 3.0, IIT 4.0), that attempts to explain the nature of consciousness and to provide a mathematical measure of it. The theory begins from the essential properties of experience — that it exists intrinsically, is structured, specific, unified and definite — and derives requirements that any physical substrate of consciousness must satisfy.

Phi

The central quantity of IIT is Φ (phi), a measure of the integrated information generated by a complex of elements above and beyond the information generated by its parts independently. According to the theory, a system is conscious to the extent that it has a high value of Φ, and the specific character of an experience corresponds to the structure of cause–effect relations the system specifies. A system that can be partitioned into independent parts with no loss has Φ = 0 and is unconscious, however sophisticated its behaviour might be.

Implications and criticism

IIT implies a form of panpsychism: even very simple systems may possess a minimal degree of consciousness if they integrate information. It also implies that a digital computer simulating a brain could have negligible Φ despite equivalent behaviour, and that consciousness is largely absent in deep sleep and anaesthesia because the cortex loses its capacity to integrate information — predictions that have motivated empirical measures such as the perturbational complexity index. Critics, including many philosophers and scientists, object that Φ is computationally intractable for realistic brains, that the theory's predictions are hard to falsify, and that counter-intuitive verdicts about simple logic-gate systems count against it. In 2023 the theory was the subject of an open letter, signed by over a hundred scholars, calling it pseudoscience — a characterization its defenders vigorously dispute.
