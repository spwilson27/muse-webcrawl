Gleason score

The Gleason grading system is used to help evaluate the prognosis of men with prostate cancer using samples from a prostate biopsy. Together with other parameters, it is incorporated into a strategy of prostate cancer staging which predicts prognosis and helps guide therapy. The Gleason score (or Gleason sum) is calculated from the grades of the two most common growth patterns of the tumour.

History

The system was devised by Donald Gleason, a pathologist at the Minneapolis Veterans Affairs Hospital, in the 1960s and 1970s, in collaboration with George Mellinger of the Veterans Administration Cooperative Urological Research Group. It was modified by the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) in 2005 and again in 2014, tightening the definitions of the patterns so that many tumours once graded pattern 3 are now regarded differently and cribriform patterns are classified as pattern 4.

Method

A pathologist examines biopsy tissue under the microscope and assigns a Gleason pattern from 1 to 5 according to how abnormal the glandular architecture appears: pattern 1 glands are well-formed and closely resemble normal tissue, while pattern 5 shows sheets or single infiltrating cells with essentially no gland formation. Because prostate cancers are heterogeneous, the score is the sum of the most prevalent pattern and the second most prevalent pattern (or the highest-grade pattern present, under modern rules), giving a score from 2 to 10, conventionally written e.g. 3 + 4 = 7.

Grade groups

Since 2014–2016, scores have also been reported in five Grade Groups (ISUP grades): Grade Group 1 corresponds to Gleason score ≤ 6; Group 2 to 3 + 4 = 7; Group 3 to 4 + 3 = 7 (a worse prognosis than Group 2 despite the same sum); Group 4 to score 8; and Group 5 to scores 9–10. In contemporary practice, scores below 6 are not assigned on biopsy, so clinically reported scores effectively range from 6 to 10. The score strongly predicts biochemical recurrence and mortality and, combined with PSA level and clinical stage, determines risk categories and treatment choices such as active surveillance, surgery or radiotherapy.
