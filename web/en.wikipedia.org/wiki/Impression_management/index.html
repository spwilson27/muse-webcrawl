Impression management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: theatre masks — the presented self]

Impression management is a conscious or subconscious process in which people attempt to influence the perceptions of other people about a person, object or event by regulating and controlling information in social interaction. The concept was developed by sociologist Erving Goffman in The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1956/1959), which analysed social life with a theatrical metaphor of front-stage and back-stage performance.

Tactics

Common strategies, catalogued by Jones and Pittman, include ingratiation (flattery and agreeableness), intimidation, self-promotion, exemplification (displaying moral worthiness) and supplication (displaying helplessness to elicit aid). Research also distinguishes assertive tactics from defensive ones used to repair a spoiled identity — excuses, justifications, apologies and accounts.

Applications

Impression management is studied in job interviews, organisations, politics and sport, and in online settings, where social media profiles are curated self-presentations. Related ideas include self-monitoring, the spotlight effect, and impression formation in social psychology. Critics note the model can overstate calculation: much self-presentation is habitual rather than strategic.
