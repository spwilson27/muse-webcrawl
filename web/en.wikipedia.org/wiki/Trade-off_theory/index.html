Trade-off theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The trade-off theory of capital structure is the idea that a company chooses how much debt finance and how much equity finance to use by balancing the costs and benefits of each. The classic version of the theory, which grew out of the debate over the Modigliani–Miller theorem, states that the optimum capital structure is obtained where the marginal value of the tax shield on additional debt is just offset by the marginal increase in the present value of the costs of financial distress.

Origins

Modigliani and Miller's 1958 theorem showed that, in a frictionless market without taxes or bankruptcy costs, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. Their 1963 correction recognised that, because interest on debt is tax-deductible, debt creates value through the corporate tax shield — implying a corner solution of nearly 100% debt. Robichek and Myers (1966), and Kraus and Litzenberger (1973), formalised the resolution: firms weigh the tax benefit of debt against expected bankruptcy costs, producing an interior optimum target leverage ratio.

Costs of financial distress

The relevant costs are the expected costs of financial distress, which include the direct costs of bankruptcy or reorganisation (legal and administrative fees) and the indirect costs (lost sales, customers and suppliers demanding harsher terms, fire-sale liquidation of assets, and underinvestment incentives). A related refinement adds agency costs: debt creates conflicts between shareholders and creditors, such as the incentive to take excessive risks (asset substitution) or to pass up good projects when existing debt is high (debt overhang, described by Myers in 1977).

Static and dynamic versions

The static trade-off theory implies that firms have a target capital structure and gradually adjust toward it; the speed of adjustment is limited by transaction and adjustment costs. Dynamic trade-off models (for example, Fischer, Heinkel and Zechner 1989, and Strebulaev 2007) recognise that firms do not continuously rebalance, so observed leverage drifts within a band around the target, and cross-sectional differences in leverage can reflect firms' histories as much as their targets.

Evidence and criticism

Empirically, profitable firms tend to borrow less than trade-off theory predicts — a fact that supports the rival pecking order theory of Myers and Majluf (1984), in which firms prefer internal finance. Trade-off theory does, however, explain why firms in stable industries with tangible assets carry more debt, why leverage is mean-reverting, and why tax rates influence financing. Modern research generally treats trade-off and pecking-order considerations as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.
