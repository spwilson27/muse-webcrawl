Edward "Ned" Kelly (December 1854 – 11 November 1880) was an Australian bushranger, outlaw, gang leader and convicted police-murderer. One of the last bushrangers, he is known for wearing a suit of bulletproof armour made from stolen ploughshares during his final shootout with the police at Glenrowan in June 1880.

Early life

Kelly was born in Beveridge, Victoria, the third of eight children to Irish parents. His father, John "Red" Kelly, was a transported convict who died when Ned was twelve, leaving the family in poverty. As a teenager Kelly was arrested several times, associating with the bushranger Harry Power and serving prison sentences for assault and horse theft. His family's long-running conflict with the police, and the arrest and imprisonment of his mother, deepened his grievances against the authorities.

The Kelly gang

In April 1878, after an incident at the Kelly home involving Constable Alexander Fitzpatrick, Ned and his brother Dan went into hiding and were joined by Joe Byrne and Steve Hart. In October 1878 the gang ambushed a police party at Stringybark Creek, killing three policemen. Declared outlaws, they went on to rob banks at Euroa (December 1878) and Jerilderie (February 1879), where Kelly dictated the famous Jerilderie Letter, a long manifesto setting out his grievances against the police and the colonial establishment.

Glenrowan and execution

In June 1880 the gang planned to derail a police train at Glenrowan and declare a "Republic of North-Eastern Victoria". Forewarned, the police besieged the gang at the Glenrowan Inn. Kelly advanced on the police wearing his armour — a helmet and breastplate hammered from plough mouldboards — but was wounded in the exposed legs and captured. The inn was burned, killing Dan Kelly, Byrne and Hart. Ned Kelly was tried in Melbourne for the murder of Constable Lonigan, convicted, and hanged at the Old Melbourne Gaol on 11 November 1880. His reported last words were "Such is life."

Legacy

Kelly remains a divisive figure in Australian culture: to some a murderous criminal, to others a folk hero and symbol of resistance by poor Irish-Australian settlers against colonial authority. His story has inspired novels, paintings by Sidney Nolan, and many films, and his armour is displayed at the State Library of Victoria. In 2011 his remains were identified among those exhumed from Pentridge Prison and were returned to his family for burial in 2013.
