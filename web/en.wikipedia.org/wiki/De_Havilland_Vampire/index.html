de Havilland Vampire

Vampire
Role: Fighter / fighter-bomber
Manufacturer: de Havilland
First flight: 20 September 1943
Introduced: 1946
Produced: 1943–1964 (3,268 built)

The de Havilland Vampire is a British jet fighter developed by the de Havilland Aircraft Company during the Second World War. It was the second jet fighter operated by the Royal Air Force, after the Gloster Meteor, and is readily recognised by its distinctive twin-boom tail, an arrangement that kept the tail surfaces clear of the jet exhaust of its single Goblin turbojet.

Design and service

Unusually for the time, the Vampire's central nacelle was built largely of moulded plywood, a technique inherited from the Mosquito. Armed with four 20 mm Hispano cannon, later fighter-bomber variants could also carry bombs and rockets. The Vampire entered RAF service in 1946, just too late for combat in the war, but became an export success, serving with the air forces of some 31 countries, and was built under licence in Australia, France, India, Italy, Switzerland and elsewhere. Two-seat trainer and night-fighter variants extended its career; the related naval Sea Vampire made the first carrier landing by a jet aircraft in December 1945, and in 1948 a Vampire became the first jet to cross the Atlantic.

Legacy

Production totalled 3,268 aircraft. The type was succeeded in de Havilland's line by the larger, swept-wing Venom. Many Vampires survive in museums and a number remain airworthy with private operators.
