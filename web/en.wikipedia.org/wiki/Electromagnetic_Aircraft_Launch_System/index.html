The Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS) is a type of aircraft launching system developed by General Atomics for the United States Navy. It uses a linear induction motor to accelerate aircraft along a track, replacing the steam catapult used on earlier generations of aircraft carriers.

EMALS was first installed on the aircraft carrier Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), the lead ship of the Gerald R. Ford class. The system offers smoother acceleration, reduced stress on airframes, a wider range of launchable aircraft weights, and lower maintenance requirements compared with steam catapults.

Design and description

The system uses a linear induction motor (LIM) built into the flight deck track. Electrical energy, stored in motor-generator flywheels, is released in a precisely controlled pulse lasting about two to three seconds, accelerating the aircraft to launch speed. Because the launch profile is computer controlled, EMALS can apply more gradual acceleration at the start of the stroke, reducing peak loads on the aircraft's structure.

Key claimed advantages include the ability to launch both very light unmanned aircraft and heavy strike aircraft, reduced manpower, an expected 30-year service life, and an end speed capability of about 240 kilometres per hour (150 mph) for a fully loaded fighter.

History

Development began in the 2000s, with shore-based testing carried out at Naval Air Systems Command facilities at Lakehurst, New Jersey. The first shipboard launches took place aboard USS Gerald R. Ford in 2017, when an F/A-18 Super Hornet was launched using EMALS. The system has also been selected for other navies' future carriers, and China tested a comparable electromagnetic catapult aboard its carrier Fujian.
