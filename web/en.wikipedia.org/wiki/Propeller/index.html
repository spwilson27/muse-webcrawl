Propeller

A propeller is a device with a rotating hub and radiating blades set at a pitch to form a helical spiral that, when rotated, exerts linear thrust upon a fluid such as water or air. Propellers convert rotational energy supplied by an engine or motor into propulsive force, and are used to power most ships, boats, submarines and propeller-driven aircraft, as well as in fans, pumps and turbines.

Principles of operation

Each propeller blade is an aerofoil (or hydrofoil) whose cross-section generates lift as it moves through the fluid; because the blade is angled and twisted along its length, the lift has a forward component — thrust — that propels the craft. Blade twist compensates for the faster motion of the outer blade relative to the hub, seeking to keep the angle of attack roughly constant along the radius. Thrust can be analysed by momentum theory (the propeller accelerates a stream of fluid backwards) and by blade-element theory. Efficiency is limited by tip losses, drag and, in water, cavitation — the formation of vapour bubbles that causes noise, vibration and erosion.

History

The screw principle goes back to Archimedes' water screw. Practical screw propulsion for ships developed in the 1830s: Francis Pettit Smith's screw propeller patent (1836) and John Ericsson's designs led to the demonstration voyage of SS Archimedes (1839) and the famous 1845 tug-of-war in which the screw-driven HMS Rattler out-pulled the paddle steamer HMS Alecto. For aircraft, the Wright brothers treated propellers as rotating wings and achieved high efficiencies with twisted blades in 1903. The variable-pitch and constant-speed propeller, developed in the 1920s–30s, greatly improved aircraft performance.

Types

  • Fixed-pitch — blades at a single built-in angle; simple and common on small boats and light aircraft.
  • Variable-pitch / constant-speed — blade angle adjusts in operation; feathering and reverse pitch are possible on aircraft and controllable-pitch ships.
  • Contra-rotating — two coaxial propellers turning in opposite directions to recover swirl energy.
  • Ducted — the propeller runs inside a nozzle or duct (e.g., Kort nozzle), increasing thrust at low speed.
  • Supercavitating — designed to operate with the blades enveloped in a vapour cavity at very high speeds.

Materials

Marine propellers are typically cast in bronze alloys (such as nickel-aluminium bronze) for strength and corrosion resistance; aircraft propellers are made of wood laminates, aluminium, or composite materials.
