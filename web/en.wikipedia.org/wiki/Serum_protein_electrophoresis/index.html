Serum protein electrophoresis

Serum protein electrophoresis (SPE or SPEP) is a laboratory test that separates the proteins in blood serum into fractions according to their size and electrical charge, using an electric field applied across a gel or other support medium. It is commonly used to screen for abnormal protein patterns associated with plasma cell disorders such as multiple myeloma.

Fractions

The separated proteins typically form five fractions: albumin (the largest, migrating furthest towards the anode), alpha-1 globulins, alpha-2 globulins, beta globulins, and gamma globulins. The gamma region contains the immunoglobulins (antibodies).

Interpretation

A tall, narrow spike in the gamma region — an M-spike or monoclonal component — suggests a monoclonal gammopathy produced by a single clone of plasma cells. A broad, diffuse increase in the gamma region indicates polyclonal immune activation, as seen in chronic infection or liver disease. Decreased albumin occurs with malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome and chronic illness. Results are often followed up with immunofixation electrophoresis and a serum free light chain assay.

Method

Serum is applied to agarose gel or a capillary system, an electric current is applied, and the proteins migrate at rates determined by their charge-to-size ratio. After staining, the bands are quantified by densitometry.
