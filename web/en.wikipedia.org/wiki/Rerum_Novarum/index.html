Rerum Novarum (from its incipit, Latin for "of revolutionary change"), or Rights and Duties of Capital and Labor, is an encyclical issued by Pope Leo XIII on 15 May 1891. It is an open letter, passed to all Catholic patriarchs, primates, archbishops and bishops, that addressed the condition of the working classes.

The encyclical is considered the foundational document of modern Catholic social teaching and a cornerstone of Christian democracy. It was written in response to the industrial revolution and the rise of socialist movements in nineteenth-century Europe.

Contents

Leo XIII rejected both unregulated capitalism and socialism. He defended the right to private property as natural and inviolable, condemning the socialist remedy of common ownership, yet insisted that wealth carries social obligations. The encyclical affirmed the right of workers to form trade unions, supported a just wage sufficient to support a worker and his family in reasonable comfort, called for limits on working hours and the regulation of child labour, and assigned the state a duty to protect the poor and to intervene where the common good required.

"Hence, by degrees it has come to pass that working men have been surrendered, isolated and helpless, to the hardheartedness of employers and the greed of unchecked competition."

Legacy

Rerum Novarum was commemorated and developed by later encyclicals, notably Pius XI's Quadragesimo Anno (1931), John XXIII's Mater et Magistra (1961), and John Paul II's Centesimus Annus (1991), published for its centenary. Its principles — human dignity, solidarity, subsidiarity and the preferential option for the poor — continue to structure Catholic social doctrine.
