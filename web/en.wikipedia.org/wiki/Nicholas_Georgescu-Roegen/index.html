Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (born Nicolae Georgescu; 4 February 1906 – 30 October 1994) was a Romanian-American mathematician, statistician and economist, regarded as the founder of bioeconomics and a founding figure of ecological economics. His masterwork, The Entropy Law and the Economic Process (1971), argued that the second law of thermodynamics — the entropy law — governs economic scarcity because all production irreversibly degrades available energy and materials.

Life and career

Georgescu-Roegen studied mathematics in Bucharest and at the Sorbonne, then worked at Harvard with Joseph Schumpeter in the 1930s. After the Second World War he emigrated from Romania to the United States in 1948, joining Vanderbilt University, where he taught until his retirement in 1976. His early work on consumer theory and production theory earned high praise — Paul Samuelson called him a "scholar's scholar" — but he grew increasingly critical of the mechanical analogies of mainstream economics.

Ideas

Against the "circular flow" picture of the economy, Georgescu-Roegen insisted that production is a one-way entropic process: low-entropy energy and materials are irrevocably transformed into high-entropy waste. From this he drew pessimistic conclusions about the limits of recycling, the impossibility of perpetual material growth, and the need for what he called a "minimal bioeconomic program", including population stabilisation, reduced consumption and durable goods. His ideas influenced Herman Daly and the steady-state economics and degrowth movements.
