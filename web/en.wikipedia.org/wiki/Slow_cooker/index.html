Slow cooker

A slow cooker, also known as a crock-pot (after the trademark owned by Sunbeam Products), is a countertop electrical cooking appliance used to simmer at a relatively low temperature (hence "slow cooking") compared to other cooking methods such as baking, boiling, and frying. This facilitates unattended cooking for many hours of dishes that would otherwise be boiled: pot roast, soups, stews, and other dishes (including beverages, desserts and dips).

History

The slow cooker was developed from the bean pot and from Jewish cholent traditions of slow-cooked Sabbath meals. Irving Naxon patented an early electric slow cooker, the "Naxon Beanery", in 1940. Rival Manufacturing acquired the design and introduced the Crock-Pot in 1971; the appliance became hugely popular in the 1970s, when many women began working outside the home and valued a cooker that could prepare a meal unattended during the day. Rival's trademark was sold to Sunbeam Products (later Newell Brands).

Design

A typical slow cooker consists of a lidded stoneware or ceramic pot ("crock") seated inside a housing containing an electric heating element. Most have at least two settings, low (around 77–90 °C, 170–195 °F) and high (around 120–150 °C at the element), while some add a "keep warm" setting. Sizes range from about 0.5 to 8.5 litres (roughly 1 to 9 US quarts). Because the lid traps steam, little liquid is lost during cooking, so recipes use less added water than stovetop equivalents.

Cooking

Slow cooking tenderises tough cuts of meat by holding them for hours at temperatures that convert collagen to gelatin. Dried beans, root vegetables and whole grains also suit the method. Food safety guidance recommends cooking on high for the first hour for some dishes, keeping the lid closed to maintain temperature, and ensuring food reaches a safe internal temperature; thawed ingredients are preferred over frozen ones in older models without sufficient heating power.

Comparison with pressure cooking

Electric pressure cookers and multicookers such as the Instant Pot achieve similar results in much less time by cooking above atmospheric pressure, and many include a slow-cook function. Traditional slow cookers remain popular for their simplicity, low energy use and ability to hold food warm for serving.
