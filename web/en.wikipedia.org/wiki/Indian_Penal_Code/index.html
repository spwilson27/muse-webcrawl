Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code (IPC) was the official criminal code of India for more than 160 years. Drafted in the 1830s under the chairmanship of Thomas Babington Macaulay and enacted in 1860, it came into force on 1 January 1862 during British rule. It defined offences and prescribed punishments across India (except Jammu and Kashmir, which had its own code until 2019). On 1 July 2024 the IPC was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), though it continues to apply to offences committed before that date.

History

The First Law Commission, constituted under the Charter Act 1833 and chaired by Macaulay, submitted a draft penal code in 1837. After revision by later law commissions, the code was enacted by the Imperial Legislative Council in 1860. Macaulay's code, influenced by English law and the French Code pénal but written in a deliberately accessible, systematic style, was widely admired and later served as a model for criminal codes throughout the British Empire, including in Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia and Nigeria.

Structure

The IPC comprised 511 sections arranged in 23 chapters, covering general explanations and exceptions, offences against the state, public tranquillity offences such as unlawful assembly, offences against the human body (including culpable homicide and murder under sections 299–304), property offences, defamation (section 499), and attempt (section 511). Famous provisions included section 377 (criminalising "unnatural offences", read down by the Supreme Court in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, 2018, insofar as it covered consensual adult relations), section 497 on adultery (struck down in 2018), and sections 124A (sedition) and 295A.

Replacement

In 2023 Parliament passed the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, replacing the IPC, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act respectively with effect from 1 July 2024, as part of a stated programme of removing colonial-era legislation.
