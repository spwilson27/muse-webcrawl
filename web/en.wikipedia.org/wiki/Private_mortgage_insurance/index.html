Private mortgage insurance

PMI allows borrowers to obtain a mortgage with a smaller down payment.

Private mortgage insurance (PMI), also called lender's mortgage insurance, is a type of insurance purchased by a borrower in connection with a conventional mortgage loan in the United States. It protects the lender against loss if the borrower defaults on the loan.

How it works

Lenders typically require PMI when a borrower's down payment is less than 20 percent of the home's purchase price, that is, when the loan-to-value ratio exceeds 80 percent. The borrower pays the premiums — usually as part of the monthly mortgage payment — but the insurance benefits the lender, compensating it for part of its losses in the event of foreclosure.

Cancellation

Under the United States Homeowners Protection Act of 1998, PMI on most loans must be terminated automatically when the loan balance is scheduled to fall to 78 percent of the original value of the home, and borrowers can request cancellation once the balance reaches 80 percent, provided payments are current and other conditions are met. Loans insured by the Federal Housing Administration use a similar but distinct charge called a mortgage insurance premium (MIP).

Alternatives

Borrowers sometimes avoid PMI through "piggyback" arrangements, in which a second loan covers part of the down payment, or through lender-paid mortgage insurance, in which the lender pays the premium in exchange for a higher interest rate on the loan.
