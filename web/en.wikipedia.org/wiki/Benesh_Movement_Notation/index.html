Benesh Movement Notation (BMN), also known as Benesh dance notation or choreology, is a system for recording human movement, principally ballet and other forms of dance, on a five-line stave analogous to that used in music notation. It was devised in the 1950s by the English mathematician and artist Rudolf Benesh (1916–1975) together with his wife Joan Benesh, a dancer with the Sadler's Wells Ballet.

History

Rudolf Benesh reportedly conceived the system while working during the Second World War and wondering how he might write a visual record of movement for his dancer wife. After several years of development, the notation was publicly demonstrated in 1955 and published the following year as An Introduction to Benesh Dance Notation. In 1957 Joan Benesh was invited to notate for the company that became The Royal Ballet, becoming its first choreologist.

In 1963 the Benesh Institute of Choreology was founded to teach the system and maintain standards; it later became part of the Royal Academy of Dance. Trained specialists in the system, known as choreologists, are employed by major ballet companies to record, teach and reconstruct repertoire.

The system

A Benesh score is written on a five-line stave read from left to right and aligned with the musical score, which is usually placed above it. The stave represents the dancer's body seen from behind: the top line corresponds to the top of the head, the second line to shoulder height, the middle line to the waist (or hips), the fourth line to the knees and the bottom line to the floor.

Simple symbols plot the positions of the hands and feet in three dimensions: marks placed on, above or below the appropriate stave line show whether a limb is in front of, level with, or behind the body, while the height of a symbol on the stave shows its level. Additional signs record turns, jumps, travelling steps, contacts with the floor or a partner, and details of phrasing. Because the score is fundamentally a visual "map" of body positions against time, experienced readers can reconstruct a ballet accurately from a score even without having seen it performed.

Applications

Benesh notation is used by more than fifty dance companies internationally, including The Royal Ballet, the Birmingham Royal Ballet, the Royal Danish Ballet and the National Ballet of Canada. It has been applied not only to ballet but also to folk dance, ice skating, physiotherapy and anthropological studies of movement. Computer software, such as the MacBenesh editor, has been developed to produce neat scores and convert them into animated figures.

Comparison with other systems

The other major system of dance notation is Labanotation, developed by Rudolf Laban, which uses a vertical three-line staff and abstract symbols for directions of movement. Benesh notation is generally considered more pictorial and is favoured by many ballet companies; Labanotation is more widely used in North America and in academic and modern-dance contexts.
