List of paradoxes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A paradox is a statement or situation that, despite apparently valid reasoning from true premises, leads to a seemingly self-contradictory or unacceptable conclusion. This article lists well-known paradoxes grouped by theme: logic, mathematics, probability, physics, philosophy and economics. Some items listed are more properly antinomies, dilemmas or counter-intuitive results than strict paradoxes, but all have traditionally been discussed under the name.

Logic and self-reference

ParadoxDescription
Liar paradox"This statement is false." If true, it is false; if false, it is true. Known from Epimenides and Eubulides of Miletus.
Barber paradoxA barber shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves. Who shaves the barber? Popularized by Bertrand Russell as an illustration of his set-theoretic paradox.
Curry's paradox"If this sentence is true, then Germany borders China" — conditional self-reference lets anything be "proved".
Pinocchio paradoxPinocchio says "my nose will grow now": if it grows he lied (so it shouldn't), if it doesn't he told the truth (so it should).
Crocodile dilemmaA crocodile promises to return a stolen child if the father correctly guesses whether it will do so; the father says "you will not return him."
Unexpected hanging paradoxA condemned prisoner reasons he cannot be hanged by surprise on a given week — until he is.
Sorites paradox (paradox of the heap)One grain is not a heap; adding one grain never makes a heap — so heaps cannot exist (and baldness cannot begin).
Ship of TheseusIf every plank of a ship is replaced, is it the same ship? A classical problem of identity.

Mathematics, infinity and probability

ParadoxDescription
Russell's paradoxThe set of all sets that do not contain themselves both contains and does not contain itself; it forced the axiomatization of set theory.
Zeno's paradoxesAchilles and the tortoise, the dichotomy, and the arrow: motion appears impossible because space and time can be divided indefinitely.
Banach–Tarski paradoxA sphere can be decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two spheres of the same size, using the axiom of choice.
Hilbert's paradox of the Grand HotelA fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms can still accommodate infinitely many new guests.
Gabriel's hornA solid with finite volume but infinite surface area: it can be filled with paint but not painted.
Birthday paradoxIn a group of just 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday exceeds 50%.
Monty Hall problemSwitching doors doubles your chance of winning (from 1/3 to 2/3), contrary to most people's intuition.
Simpson's paradoxA trend present in several groups can disappear or reverse when the groups are combined.
Braess's paradoxAdding a road to a network can make everyone's journey slower.
St. Petersburg paradoxA lottery with infinite expected value that no one will pay much to enter — a founding problem of decision theory.
Two envelopes problemAn argument suggests you should always switch envelopes, whatever you find.
Berry paradox"The smallest positive integer not definable in under sixty letters" is itself a definition in under sixty letters.
Cantor's paradoxThe set of all sets would have to be larger than itself, since its power set is larger still.

Physics

  • Grandfather paradox — a time traveller kills his own grandfather before his parent's conception.
  • Twin paradox — a travelling twin returns younger than the stay-at-home twin (resolved by relativity).
  • Schrödinger's cat — a cat in a box is, under the Copenhagen interpretation, simultaneously alive and dead until observed.
  • EPR paradox — quantum entanglement seems to imply "spooky action at a distance" incompatible with local realism; resolved in practice by Bell's theorem experiments.
  • Maxwell's demon — a being that sorts molecules without work would violate the second law of thermodynamics.
  • Olbers's paradox — in an infinite, eternal universe the night sky should be bright.
  • Fermi paradox — the universe should be full of alien civilizations, yet we see no evidence of them.
  • Heat-death (Loschmidt's) paradox — reversible microscopic laws seem incompatible with irreversible macroscopic entropy increase.
  • D'Alembert's paradox — inviscid flow theory predicts zero drag on a body.
  • Hydrostatic paradox — the pressure at the bottom of a vessel depends only on the height of liquid, not its weight.

Philosophy, decision theory and social choice

  • Omnipotence paradox — can an omnipotent being create a stone it cannot lift?
  • Paradox of hedonism — happiness is best attained by not aiming directly at it.
  • Paradox of tolerance — a tolerant society that tolerates intolerance may be destroyed by it (Popper).
  • Newcomb's problem — a predictor's near-perfect record splits decision theorists between one-boxing and two-boxing.
  • Prisoner's dilemma — individually rational defection leaves both players worse off (a dilemma rather than a strict paradox).
  • Arrow's impossibility theorem — no voting system can satisfy a short list of fairness criteria simultaneously.
  • Condorcet paradox — majority preferences can cycle even when individual preferences are consistent.
  • Buridan's ass — an ass exactly between two equal haystacks starves for want of a reason to choose.
  • Paradox of value (diamond–water paradox) — water, essential to life, is cheap; diamonds, frivolous, are dear — resolved by marginal utility.
  • Abilene paradox — a group unanimously takes a trip nobody wanted, each believing the others did.
  • Catch-22 — Heller's bureaucratic trap: asking to be relieved of combat duty proves the sanity that makes you fit for it.
