Nuclear envelope

The nuclear envelope, also known as the nuclear membrane, is made up of two lipid bilayer membranes in eukaryotic cells that surround the nucleus, which carries the genetic material. It separates the contents of the nucleus from the cytoplasm and regulates the passage of molecules between the two compartments.

Structure

The nuclear envelope consists of an inner nuclear membrane and an outer nuclear membrane, separated by the perinuclear space of about 20 to 40 nanometres. The outer membrane is continuous with the membrane of the rough endoplasmic reticulum and is studded with ribosomes. The inner membrane is lined on its nucleoplasmic side by the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of intermediate filaments made of lamin proteins that provides structural support.

Nuclear pores

The envelope is perforated by thousands of nuclear pore complexes, large protein assemblies that mediate the selective transport of proteins and RNA between the nucleus and cytoplasm. Small molecules diffuse freely, while larger molecules require active transport mediated by nuclear localization signals and transport receptors such as importins and exportins.

Breakdown and reassembly

During mitosis in most higher eukaryotes, the nuclear envelope breaks down into small membrane vesicles, allowing the mitotic spindle access to the chromosomes. At the end of mitosis, the envelope reassembles around the separated sets of chromosomes, a process central to cell division.
