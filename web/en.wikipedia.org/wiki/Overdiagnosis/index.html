Overdiagnosis

Overdiagnosis is the diagnosis of disease that will never cause symptoms or death during a patient's lifetime. It is a side effect of screening for early forms of disease: although screening can find treatable cancers and other conditions earlier, it also detects abnormalities that meet the pathological definition of disease but are so slow-growing, indolent, or self-resolving that they would never have harmed the person. Because clinicians usually cannot tell which detected abnormalities are dangerous, overdiagnosis typically leads to overtreatment — surgery, medication, or other interventions whose harms (and costs) are borne without any compensating benefit.

Mechanism

Overdiagnosis arises from the heterogeneity of disease progression. Screening preferentially detects slowly progressive disease (length-time bias), including lesions that are non-progressive or regress spontaneously, because fast-growing diseases are more likely to present clinically between screens. As diagnostic technologies become more sensitive and disease thresholds are lowered, ever milder abnormalities are labelled as disease, enlarging the reservoir of overdiagnosed cases. Unlike a false positive result, an overdiagnosed condition is a "true" abnormality by current definitions — the error lies in the expectation that it matters.

Examples

The phenomenon has been studied most extensively in cancer screening. Estimates suggest a substantial fraction of screen-detected prostate cancers (via PSA testing), breast cancers (mammography), thyroid cancers (notably in South Korea, where ultrasound screening drove a fifteen-fold rise in incidence with no change in mortality), lung cancers (low-dose CT), and melanomas may be overdiagnosed. Outside oncology, debate surrounds the expansion of diagnoses such as attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, chronic kidney disease defined by estimated glomerular filtration rate thresholds, prediabetes, and subclinical hypothyroidism.

Responses

Proposed remedies include raising diagnostic thresholds, renaming indolent lesions to remove alarming labels such as "cancer," active surveillance instead of immediate treatment, shared decision-making that communicates the risk of overdiagnosis to screening participants, and research into biomarkers that distinguish progressive from indolent disease. Campaigns such as Choosing Wisely and journals' "Too Much Medicine" series seek to reduce low-value testing that feeds overdiagnosis.
