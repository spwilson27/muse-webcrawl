Priority to the right

At an uncontrolled junction, traffic from the right goes first.

Priority to the right (French: priorité à droite) is a right-of-way rule in traffic law, observed in most continental European countries and codified in the Vienna Convention on Road Traffic. At an intersection where no signs, signals, or road markings assign priority, a driver must yield to vehicles approaching from the right.

TypeTraffic rule / right of way
Applies atUnmarked, uncontrolled intersections
Found inFrance, Belgium, Germany (Rechts vor Links), the Netherlands, and most of continental Europe

Operation

The rule serves as the default where priority roads have not been designated. In practice, most through-roads are designated priority roads with signs and markings, so the rule chiefly governs residential streets, rural junctions, and minor roads. In France, priorité à droite retains a wider scope, famously applying at some major junctions (historically at the Place de l'Étoile in Paris traffic already on the roundabout must even yield to entering traffic at certain giratoires, though most modern roundabouts reverse this).

History

The rule descends from 19th-century French practice for horse-drawn traffic and was adopted across continental Europe as motor traffic grew; it was written into the 1968 Vienna Convention, which provides that, absent other indications, drivers must yield to vehicles coming from the right in countries driving on the right (and the mirror-image rule applies in left-hand-traffic states).

Exceptions

Priority to the right does not apply, in most jurisdictions, to vehicles emerging from private drives, unpaved tracks, pedestrian zones, or parking areas, nor over trams in many systems. Emergency vehicles on call override the rule.
