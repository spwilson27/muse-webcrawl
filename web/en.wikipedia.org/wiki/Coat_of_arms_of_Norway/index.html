Coat of arms of Norway

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The coat of arms of Norway consists of a golden lion rampant holding a silver axe on a red shield, surmounted by a crown. It is one of the oldest national coats of arms in Europe, with origins in the thirteenth century. In Norwegian it is called Norges riksvåpen (the national arms of Norway).

Description

The blazon is: Gules, a lion rampant Or, crowned and holding an axe Argent with shaft Or. The axe is associated with Saint Olaf (Olav II of Norway), the patron saint of Norway, whose attribute was a battle axe. The crown on the shield signifies that the arms are those of the Norwegian state and monarchy.

History

The lion first appeared on the seals of Norwegian kings in the 1200s; the axe was added during the reign of Haakon IV or his successors, and by the late thirteenth century the lion with the axe had become the established royal arms. The arms continued through the unions with Denmark and later Sweden, and were retained after Norway's independence in 1905. A modernised drawing by the artist Sverre Morken was approved in 1992 and is the version used by the state today; a distinct version is used by the Royal Court.

Use

The coat of arms is used by the King, the Storting (parliament), the government, the courts and other state authorities. Its use by private parties is restricted under Norwegian law.
