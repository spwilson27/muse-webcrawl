Totum pro parte

Totum pro parte (Latin: "the whole for a part") is a type of synecdoche in which the name of the whole is used to refer to only a part of it — the reverse of pars pro toto. It is a figure of speech catalogued in classical and modern rhetoric.

Examples

  • "Germany beat Argentina in the final" — the country's name stands for its football team.
  • "The United States invaded Iraq" — the nation stands for its armed forces and government.
  • "Hired hands" (the opposite direction, pars pro toto, is often contrasted) versus "the factory announced layoffs", where the institution's name stands for its management.
  • Referring to a city's police force simply as "the city" in sentences like "the city investigated the complaint".

In linguistics, totum pro parte constructions are studied as cases of regular polysemy and active-zone phenomena: the whole entity is named, but only a salient subpart participates in the described event. The distinction between totum pro parte and pars pro toto is also relevant in textual criticism, translation studies, and the philosophy of language, where such transfers raise questions about reference and truth conditions.
