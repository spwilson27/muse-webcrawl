Aztec warfare

Aztec warfare concerns the military practices, weapons, and campaigns of the Mexica people of the Aztec Empire in central Mexico (1428–1521). Warfare was central to Aztec society, serving to expand the empire, extract tribute, and capture prisoners for ritual sacrifice.

Aztec warriors in the Codex Mendoza

Organization

All Aztec males received military training; commoners trained at the telpochcalli and nobles at the calmecac. Advancement depended on taking captives in battle. Elite orders included the eagle warriors (cuauhtli) and jaguar warriors (ocelotl), as well as the shorn ones (cuachicqueh). Armies were commanded by the tlatoani and his deputy, the cihuacoatl, and were supplied by an extensive system of porters and roadside depots.

Weapons and armour

The principal weapon was the macuahuitl, a wooden club edged with obsidian blades. Warriors also used spears and spear-throwers (atlatl), bows, slings, and obsidian- or flint-tipped darts. Protection consisted of quilted cotton armour (ichcahuipilli), wooden shields (chimalli) often decorated with featherwork, and elaborate feathered battle suits for high-ranking warriors.

Flower wars

The xochiyaoyotl or "flower wars" were ritualized conflicts, traditionally with Tlaxcala and other states, arranged to train warriors and obtain sacrificial captives rather than to conquer territory.

Conquest and collapse

Aztec campaigns built a tributary empire stretching from the Gulf Coast to the Pacific. The system collapsed during the Spanish conquest of 1519–1521, when Hernán Cortés, allied with Tlaxcala and other enemies of the Aztecs, besieged and destroyed Tenochtitlan.
