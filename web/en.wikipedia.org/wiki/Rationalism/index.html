Rationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rationalism is the view that reason, rather than sensory experience, is the primary source and test of knowledge. In early-modern philosophy, "the Rationalists" names the continental trio René Descartes, Baruch Spinoza and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who held that significant knowledge of reality is innate to the mind or deducible from self-evident first principles, in the way mathematics proceeds from axioms. Their position is the mirror image of British empiricism, and the quarrel between them set the agenda that Kant later tried to settle.

Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz

Descartes (1596–1650) sought certainty by methodical doubt: everything could be doubted except the doubting itself — cogito ergo sum, "I think, therefore I am" — and rebuilt knowledge on "clear and distinct ideas", innate truths and a proof of God. Spinoza (1632–77) wrote his Ethics in geometrical form, deducing a monist universe of one substance, God-or-Nature. Leibniz (1646–1716) distinguished truths of reason (necessary, known a priori) from truths of fact, peopled reality with windowless "monads" harmonised by God, and co-invented the calculus. Rationalist themes — innate structure, a priori knowledge, deduction — recur in modern dress: in Chomsky's nativism about language, in the logicist programme for mathematics, and in everyday uses of "rationalism" for confidence in reason over tradition, faith or emotion (a looser sense the philosophical tradition also carries).
