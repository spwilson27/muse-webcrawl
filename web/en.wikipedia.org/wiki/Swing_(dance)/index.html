Swing dance is a group of social dances that developed with the swing style of jazz music in the United States during the 1920s–1940s. The most well-known forms include the Lindy Hop, popularised in Harlem's Savoy Ballroom, and later adaptations such as East Coast Swing and West Coast Swing.

History

Swing dancing evolved from African-American vernacular dance traditions — including the Charleston and tap — blended with ballroom forms. The Lindy Hop, named after Charles Lindbergh's transatlantic flight, became a national craze in the 1930s with the rise of big bands led by Duke Ellington, Count Basie, and Benny Goodman. Shorty George Snowden and Frankie Manning were among its defining innovators. After fading in the rock era, swing experienced a major revival in the 1980s and 1990s, and today there are active Lindy Hop scenes in hundreds of cities worldwide.

Characteristics

Swing is a partner dance based on an elastic, bouncing connection between leader and follower, with improvisation and playful "styling" as core values. Many forms use six- or eight-count basic patterns, breaks, and aerial steps (in Lindy Hop competitions).
