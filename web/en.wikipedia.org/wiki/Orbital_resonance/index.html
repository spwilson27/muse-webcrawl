Orbital resonance

In celestial mechanics, an orbital resonance occurs when orbiting bodies exert a regular, periodic gravitational influence on each other, usually because their orbital periods are related by a ratio of small integers. The mutual gravitational forces can stabilise the orbits — protecting them from close encounters — or destabilise them, clearing gaps.

Examples

The moons Io, Europa and Ganymede form a Laplace resonance with period ratios 1:2:4, the only known triple resonance. Neptune and Pluto are in a 3:2 resonance, which keeps Pluto's eccentric, inclined orbit from ever colliding with Neptune. In Saturn's rings, the Cassini Division is maintained by a 2:1 resonance with Mimas, while the Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt correspond to resonances with Jupiter that eject asteroids. Many exoplanet systems, such as TRAPPIST-1, contain chains of resonant planets.

Types

Mean-motion resonances concern orbital periods; secular resonances involve the slow precession of perihelia and nodes; and spin–orbit resonances — such as Mercury's 3:2 state and the Moon's 1:1 tidal locking — couple a body's rotation to its revolution. Resonances generally arise when bodies migrate through a disc or are captured by tidal evolution.
