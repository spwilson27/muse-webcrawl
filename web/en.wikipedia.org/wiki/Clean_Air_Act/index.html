Clean Air Act

Smog over Los Angeles: urban air pollution helped drive the federal Clean Air Act.

The Clean Air Act (42 U.S.C. §7401 et seq.) is the primary federal law in the United States governing air pollution. It was first enacted in 1963 and has been amended many times, most comprehensively in 1970, 1977 and 1990. The Act authorises the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to establish National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) to protect public health and the environment, and it regulates emissions from stationary sources such as power plants and factories as well as mobile sources such as cars and trucks.

History

The original Clean Air Act of 1963 was a modest measure funding research and state programmes. The Clean Air Amendments of 1970 — passed overwhelmingly and signed by President Richard Nixon the same year he created the EPA — transformed federal policy: they required national air quality standards, strict deadlines for states to meet them through implementation plans, technology-forcing emission standards for new cars (a roughly 90 per cent reduction in pollutants by the mid-1970s), and citizen-suit provisions allowing the public to enforce the law.

The 1977 amendments added programmes to prevent significant deterioration of air quality in clean areas and tightened requirements for areas not attaining the standards. The landmark 1990 amendments, signed by President George H. W. Bush, addressed acid rain through a pioneering cap-and-trade system for sulphur dioxide, created an operating-permit programme, set controls for 189 hazardous air pollutants, and mandated the phase-out of ozone-depleting chlorofluorocarbons.

Programmes

  • NAAQS — standards for six "criteria" pollutants: particulate matter, ground-level ozone, carbon monoxide, sulphur oxides, nitrogen oxides and lead.
  • New Source Performance Standards and prevention of significant deterioration (PSD) permitting for major new sources.
  • Acid Rain Program — Title IV cap-and-trade for SO2 and NOx from power plants, credited with cutting acid-rain emissions faster and more cheaply than projected.
  • Mobile source controls — tailpipe standards, fuel regulations and the catalytic-converter era; California's stricter standards operate under a special statutory waiver.

Greenhouse gases and litigation

In Massachusetts v. EPA (2007), the Supreme Court held that greenhouse gases are "air pollutants" under the Act and that the EPA has authority to regulate them; the agency's 2009 endangerment finding led to rules for vehicles and power plants. The scope of that authority has remained contested, notably in West Virginia v. EPA (2022), which limited the EPA's ability to restructure the power sector under an existing provision. EPA analyses credit the Act's programmes with preventing hundreds of thousands of premature deaths and delivering benefits many times their compliance costs.
