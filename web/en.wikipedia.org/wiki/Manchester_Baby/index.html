The Manchester Baby, also known as the Small-Scale Experimental Machine (SSEM), was the first electronic stored-program computer. It was built at the University of Manchester by Frederic C. Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill, and ran its first program on 21 June 1948.

Design

The Baby was built as a testbed for the Williams tube, an early form of computer memory using a cathode-ray tube that could store 32 words of 32 bits. The machine had just seven instructions and used paper tape for input. On 21 June 1948 it successfully ran a 52-minute program, written by Kilburn, to find the highest proper factor of 218.

Legacy

Having proved the stored-program concept and the Williams tube store, the machine was developed into the Manchester Mark 1, which led in turn to the Ferranti Mark 1 — the first commercially available general-purpose computer. A working replica of the Baby, built from original components where possible, is displayed at the Science and Industry Museum in Manchester and was operated to mark the machine's 50th anniversary in 1998.
