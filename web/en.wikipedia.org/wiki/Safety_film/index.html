Safety film

Safety film is photographic and motion-picture film whose base is made of a slow-burning or non-flammable material — first cellulose acetate, later polyester — in contrast to the dangerously flammable cellulose nitrate base used in the early film industry. The transition to safety film, completed for professional cinema in the early 1950s, eliminated one of the great hazards of early filmmaking and projection.

Nitrate and its dangers

Cellulose nitrate film burns fiercely, cannot be extinguished easily (it carries its own oxygen), and can ignite spontaneously as it decays. Nitrate fires destroyed studios, cinemas and archives; the 1929 Glen Cinema disaster in Paisley, Scotland, killed 71 children, and vast proportions of silent-era cinema were lost to fire and decomposition. Acetate "safety" stock existed from the 1910s for amateur gauges (16 mm film was never sold on nitrate), and Eastman Kodak discontinued nitrate roll film in 1951.

Acetate decay and polyester

Cellulose acetate base has its own archival flaw: "vinegar syndrome", in which the base releases acetic acid, shrinking and embrittling the film. Modern stocks use polyester (PET) base, which is stronger and far more stable. Archivists still distinguish safety film from nitrate in cataloguing, and surviving nitrate prints are handled under strict fire codes — and prized, since nitrate's image quality is often considered superior.
