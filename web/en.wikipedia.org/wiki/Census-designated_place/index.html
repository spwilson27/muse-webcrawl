Census-designated place

CDP boundaries are statistical, not municipal.

A census-designated place (CDP) is a concentration of population defined by the United States Census Bureau for statistical purposes only. CDPs are populated areas that lack a separate municipal government but otherwise resemble incorporated places such as cities or villages.

Purpose

Many settled communities in the United States are unincorporated: they have names, identities, and substantial populations but no local government of their own. To provide census data for such places comparable to that for incorporated municipalities, the Census Bureau delineates CDPs in cooperation with local officials, typically for each decennial census.

Characteristics

CDP boundaries have no legal status and may not coincide with residents' understanding of the community's extent; they can change between censuses, and places may gain or lose CDP status as populations shift or communities incorporate. Some very large CDPs have populations exceeding 100,000. CDPs are especially common in states such as Hawaii, where there are no incorporated municipalities below the county level outside Honolulu, and in fast-growing suburban areas of the South and West.
