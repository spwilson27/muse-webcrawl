Deimatic behaviour

Deimatic behaviour or startle display is any pattern of bluffing behaviour in an animal that lacks strong defences, such as suddenly displaying conspicuous eyespots, to scare off or momentarily distract a predator, thus giving the prey animal an opportunity to escape. The term deimatic originates from the Greek δειματόω (deimatόō), meaning "to frighten". Displays are classified as deimatic (rather than aposematic) when the animal is not genuinely defended and the predator, if it called the bluff, could safely eat the prey.

Mechanism

Deimatic displays exploit predators' innate or learned startle responses and neophobia: sudden movement, noise, or the appearance of "eyes" causes the predator to hesitate or retreat. Unlike warning coloration backed by toxins (aposematism), the deimatic signal is a bluff — though some animals combine both, first startling and then advertising genuine defences. Experiments with artificial prey and with predators such as birds have shown that eyespots and sudden unveilings increase prey survival, and that the displays work best when novel.

Examples

Moths such as the eyed hawk-moth flash large eyespots on their hindwings when disturbed. Mantises rear up, spread their wings and forelegs, and in some species (e.g., Hierodula) display vivid colours. The frilled-neck lizard of Australia erects its frill and gapes. Cuttlefish and octopuses produce dramatic deimatic patterns — darkening, paling, or forming eye-like spots — and the blue-ringed octopus flashes iridescent rings (in its case backed by potent venom). Frogs such as the four-eyed frog display eyespots on their hindquarters; some snakes hiss, flatten their heads, or expose brightly coloured undersides; and mammals including skunks (handstands) and porcupines perform threat displays before deploying real defences.

Relation to other defences

Deimatic behaviour sits within a spectrum of anti-predator adaptations including crypsis, mimicry, mobbing, and thanatosis (playing dead). Some displays blur categories: the hognose snake first bluffs fiercely, then plays dead if the bluff fails.
