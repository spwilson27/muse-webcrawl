Anglo-Catholicism comprises the beliefs and practices of Anglicans — sometimes called "high church" Anglicans — who emphasise the Catholic heritage and identity of the Anglican churches. It understands Anglicanism not as a Protestant denomination founded at the Reformation, but as a continuing part of the one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church whose historic episcopate and sacraments survived the English Reformation.

History

The movement is usually dated from the Oxford Movement (from 1833), when John Keble's sermon on "National Apostasy" and the Tracts for the Times by John Henry Newman, Edward Pusey and others argued for the catholic character of the Church of England. Newman's Tract 90 (1841), contending that the Thirty-nine Articles were compatible with Catholic teaching, provoked controversy; in 1845 Newman converted to Roman Catholicism. Those who remained, led by Pusey, nonetheless transformed Anglican worship over the following century.

Beliefs and practices

Anglo-Catholics stress apostolic succession, the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist (without necessarily defining it as transubstantiation), the seven sacraments, prayers for the dead, invocation of the saints and veneration of Mary, auricular confession, and ceremonial worship: eucharistic vestments, incense, candles, processions and reservation of the Blessed Sacrament. In the later nineteenth century the movement became associated with slum parishes and social action ("ritualism" prosecutions under the Public Worship Regulation Act 1874 led to the imprisonment of several priests), and in the twentieth century with the parish communion movement and Anglo-Papalism.

Today

Anglo-Catholic parishes and societies (such as the Society of the Holy Cross and Forward in Faith) exist throughout the Anglican Communion. Disagreements over the ordination of women led some Anglo-Catholics to Rome, notably through the personal ordinariates erected under Anglicanorum Coetibus (2009), while others remain a distinctive tradition within Anglicanism, nowadays divided between traditionalist and liberal/affirming currents.
