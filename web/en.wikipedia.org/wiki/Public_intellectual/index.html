Public intellectual

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A public intellectual is an intellectual who contributes to public discourse on a society's political and ideological questions, generally as an avowed expert in a particular field of knowledge who addresses a broad audience rather than only fellow specialists. Historically, the term was first used to describe intellectuals who intervene in public debate on specific political issues, as in the Dreyfus affair in France, when Émile Zola's open letter J'Accuse…! (1898) made "intellectual" a noun in common use.

Definition and role

The public intellectual differs from the academic specialist by writing or speaking for a lay public, and by taking positions on questions of public concern — war, justice, culture, science policy — rather than remaining within technical debate. In Representations of the Intellectual (1993), Edward Said argued that the intellectual's role is to "speak truth to power", challenging orthodoxies and representing the marginalised, even at personal cost. Others, such as Richard Posner in Public Intellectuals: A Study of Decline (2001), have criticised the quality of public-intellectual commentary, arguing that the market for it rewards celebrity and confidence more than accuracy.

History

The modern notion emerged in late nineteenth-century France and Russia (where the intelligentsia formed a recognised social stratum). In the twentieth century figures such as Jean-Paul Sartre embodied the "engaged" intellectual, while in the English-speaking world writers from Bertrand Russell to Noam Chomsky and Susan Sontag addressed the public on war, nuclear weapons, and civil rights. Television, and later the internet and social media, reshaped the role, shortening the distance between intellectuals and mass audiences while subjecting them to new commercial pressures.

Debates

Discussion continues about whether the public intellectual is in decline — displaced by academic specialisation, think tanks, and media punditry — or simply transformed. Rankings and polls of "top public intellectuals" periodically revive the debate about who legitimately occupies the role.
