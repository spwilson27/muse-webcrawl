The Camargue horse is an ancient breed of horse indigenous to the Camargue region of southern France, the marshy delta where the Rhône River meets the Mediterranean Sea. The horses are traditionally ridden by the gardians, the mounted herdsmen of the region, who use them to manage herds of semi-feral black Camargue cattle. The breed is famous for its hardiness, its white-grey coat, and iconic images of herds galloping through the shallow lagoons of the delta.

History

Horses have lived in the Camargue wetlands for centuries, and the local breed is often claimed to descend from prehistoric horses of the region, possibly with admixture from horses brought by successive invaders and traders — Phoenicians, Romans, and Arabs among them. For most of its history the Camargue horse lived semi-feral in manades (herds), rounded up periodically by gardians. The breed was officially recognised in 1978, and a stud book and breed association (the Association des Éleveurs de Chevaux de Race Camargue) were established to preserve it.

Characteristics

Camargue horses are small, standing roughly 1.35–1.50 metres at the withers, with a heavy, square head, short strong neck, deep chest, and exceptionally hard hooves adapted to wet terrain — they are rarely shod. Foals are born black or dark brown and lighten with age to the characteristic white-grey adult coat. The breed is noted for stamina, agility, sure-footedness, and a calm, willing temperament.

Uses

The horses remain the traditional mount of the gardians for working cattle and for the course camarguaise and other regional festivals. They are also used for trail riding and tourism in the delta, and their striking appearance makes them popular subjects for photographers and filmmakers — most famously in the 1953 short film Crin-Blanc (White Mane).
