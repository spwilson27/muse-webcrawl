Patagonian toothfish

Scientific classification
OrderPerciformes
FamilyNototheniidae
GenusDissostichus
SpeciesD. eleginoides
Market nameChilean sea bass

The Patagonian toothfish (Dissostichus eleginoides) is a species of notothen found in cold waters of the southern Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans and the Southern Ocean around Antarctica, generally at depths of 300 to 2,500 metres. A large, slow-growing predator that can exceed two metres in length and live for up to fifty years, it is prized for its rich, white, flaky flesh and sold in restaurants and markets — especially in the United States and East Asia — under the marketing name Chilean sea bass, although it is neither a bass nor exclusively Chilean.

Fishery

Commercial longline fisheries developed from the 1970s and expanded rapidly in the 1990s as demand for "Chilean sea bass" boomed. The fishery is managed internationally by the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), which sets catch limits for most Southern Ocean areas; other fisheries fall under national jurisdictions such as those of Chile, Argentina, Australia (Heard Island and McDonald Islands), and France (Kerguelen and Crozet).

Illegal fishing and conservation

High prices made the species a prime target of illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) "pirate" fishing in the 1990s and 2000s, which at its peak may have taken as much fish as the legal fleet and depleted some stocks. Countermeasures included CCAMLR's Catch Documentation Scheme, vessel blacklists, satellite tracking, naval patrols — famously Australia's 2003 pursuit of the Uruguayan vessel Viarsa 1 across the Southern Ocean — and campaigns urging consumers to "take a pass on Chilean sea bass." Several fisheries have since earned Marine Stewardship Council certification, though conservation groups still rate some toothfish fisheries cautiously because the fish's late maturity makes stocks slow to recover from overfishing.
