Ōnin War

The Ōnin War (応仁の乱, Ōnin no Ran), also known as the Ōnin–Bunmei War, was a civil war fought in Japan from 1467 to 1477, during the Muromachi period. A dispute over the succession to the Ashikaga shogunate, aggravated by rivalries between powerful shugo daimyo families (notably the Hosokawa and Yamana), escalated into a nationwide conflict. The war devastated Kyoto, destroyed the authority of the Ashikaga shogunate, and is traditionally taken as the beginning of the Sengoku period, a century of fragmentation and competing warlords.

Causes

Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa, lacking an heir, adopted his brother Yoshimi as successor, but the subsequent birth of his son Yoshihisa created a rival claim. The two candidates were backed by the great deputy (kanrei) families: Hosokawa Katsumoto supported Yoshimi, while Yamana Sōzen supported Yoshihisa. Simultaneous succession disputes within the Hatakeyama and Shiba families drew in the same rival camps, and tensions over the shogunate's growing weakness and famine-era unrest brought armies into the capital.

Course of the war

Fighting began in Kyoto in 1467. The Hosokawa-led "eastern" army and Yamana-led "western" army entrenched themselves in the northern part of the city, burning large districts; much of Kyoto was reduced to ruins and its population fled. Campaigns spread to the provinces as local lords pursued their own disputes. Both Katsumoto and Yamana died in 1473, and Yoshimasa abdicated in favour of Yoshihisa, but skirmishing continued until a negotiated settlement in 1477, after which the Ōnin era name passed into Bunmei.

Consequences

Neither faction achieved its aims, and the shogunate emerged powerless outside Kyoto. Provincial authority passed to daimyo who owed little to the centre, inaugurating the Sengoku ("warring states") period. The destruction of the capital also dispersed court culture and craftsmen to the provinces, reshaping Japanese society.
