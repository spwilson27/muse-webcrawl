Treasure Act 1996

Citation: 1996 c. 24

Enacted by: Parliament of the United Kingdom

Royal assent: 4 July 1996

Commencement: 24 September 1997

Territorial extent: England, Wales and Northern Ireland

The Treasure Act 1996 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which defines and governs the treatment of finds of "treasure" in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. It replaced the ancient common law of treasure trove and came into force on 24 September 1997. Anyone who finds an object they believe to be treasure must report it to the local coroner within 14 days.

Definition of treasure

Under the Act, treasure includes objects (other than coins) at least 300 years old with a metallic content of at least 10 per cent precious metal by weight; groups of two or more coins from the same find, at least 300 years old and meeting the precious-metal threshold (or ten or more coins of lesser metal content); objects found in association with treasure; and certain prehistoric base-metal assemblages. A reform that took effect in 2023 added a significance-based criterion, allowing exceptionally important objects more than 200 years old to be classed as treasure regardless of material.

Procedure and rewards

Reported finds are investigated by a coroner, who holds an inquest to determine whether they are treasure. Treasure vests in the Crown (or a franchisee such as the Duchy of Lancaster), and museums may acquire it, in which case a reward at full market value — determined by the Treasure Valuation Committee — is normally paid, shared between the finder and the landowner. The Act is administered alongside the voluntary Portable Antiquities Scheme, which records non-treasure archaeological finds.

Notable finds

Major finds declared under the Act include the Staffordshire Hoard (2009), the largest hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold ever found, and the Vale of York Hoard. Scotland has its own system of treasure trove, and the Act does not extend there.
