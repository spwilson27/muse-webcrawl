Macrolide

Macrolides are a class of natural products and drugs consisting of a large macrocyclic lactone ring (typically 14, 15, or 16 atoms) to which one or more deoxy sugars are attached. The antibiotic macrolides — including erythromycin, clarithromycin, and azithromycin — are widely used to treat respiratory tract and soft-tissue infections.

Mechanism and uses

Macrolide antibiotics inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding reversibly to the P site of the 50S subunit of the bacterial ribosome, blocking translocation. They are particularly useful against atypical pathogens such as Mycoplasma, Legionella, and Chlamydia, and as alternatives for patients allergic to penicillin. Erythromycin, the prototype, was isolated in 1952 from Saccharopolyspora erythraea found in a Philippine soil sample.

Other macrolides

The macrolide family also includes antifungals such as nystatin and amphotericin B (polyenes) and the immunosuppressants tacrolimus and sirolimus. Adverse effects of the antibiotic macrolides include gastrointestinal upset, QT prolongation, and interactions via the cytochrome P450 system; bacterial resistance, often through methylation of the ribosomal target, is a growing concern.
