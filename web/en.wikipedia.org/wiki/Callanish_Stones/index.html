The Callanish Stones (or Calanais in Scottish Gaelic) are an arrangement of standing stones placed in a cruciform pattern with a central stone circle, near the village of Callanish on the west coast of Lewis in the Outer Hebrides, Scotland. They were erected in the late Neolithic era, and were a focus for ritual activity during the Bronze Age.

Description

The main site, Callanish I, consists of a circle of thirteen stones with a monolith near the middle, a small chambered cairn inside the circle, and avenues of stones extending to the north and single rows to the south, east and west, giving the whole a cross-like plan. The tallest stones reach about 5 metres. The stone is locally quarried Lewisian gneiss, banded and weathered. Numerous smaller sites (numbered up to Callanish XXII and beyond) are scattered nearby, earning the area comparison with Stonehenge and Carnac.

History

The stones were raised around 2900–2600 BC. Ritual use continued for some two thousand years before the site was abandoned, and peat gradually buried the bases of the stones; in 1857, about 1.5 metres of peat was cleared away. Local tradition held that the stones were giants turned to stone for refusing to convert to Christianity.

Astronomy

Alexander Thom and later researchers proposed that the setting-out of the stones marks alignments on the Moon and Sun, particularly the extreme southern moonrise ("major lunar standstill"), an interpretation that remains debated.
