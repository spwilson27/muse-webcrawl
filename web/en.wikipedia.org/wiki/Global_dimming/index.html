Global dimming

Global dimming
CauseAirborne particulate matter (aerosols)
EffectReduction in sunlight reaching Earth's surface
Observedc. 1950s–1980s; partial reversal ("brightening") since

Global dimming is the reduction in the amount of sunlight (direct irradiance) reaching the surface of the Earth, observed from the 1950s to the 1980s. It is attributed mainly to an increase in airborne particulate matter — aerosols such as sulfate particles from fossil-fuel burning — which scatter and absorb sunlight and brighten clouds, increasing their reflectivity.

Observations

Measurements of pan evaporation and surface solar radiation in many regions showed declines of a few percent per decade over the second half of the twentieth century. Dimming was strongest over industrialised regions of the Northern Hemisphere. From about 1990, clean-air legislation in Europe and North America reduced aerosol pollution, and many regions have since experienced "global brightening".

Climatic significance

Global dimming partially masked the warming effect of greenhouse gases: the cooling influence of aerosols offset some greenhouse warming, so cleaning up air pollution without cutting greenhouse gases can unmask additional warming. Dimming has also been linked to shifts in rainfall, including possible contributions to Sahel drought and weakened monsoons, since reduced surface sunlight alters evaporation and atmospheric circulation.
