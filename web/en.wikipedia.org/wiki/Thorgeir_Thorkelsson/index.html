Þorgeir Þorkelsson (Old Norse: Þorgeir Ljósvetningagoði Þorkelsson; c. 940 – after 1000), known as Thorgeir the Lawspeaker, was an Icelandic chieftain (goði) from Ljósavatn in northern Iceland who served as lawspeaker of the Althing from 985 to 1001. He is remembered for the decision by which Iceland adopted Christianity.

The conversion decision

Around the year 1000, pressure from the Christian king Olaf Tryggvason of Norway and the arrival of Christian missionaries had split the Althing into pagan and Christian factions that threatened to break the Icelandic Commonwealth's legal unity. The assembly turned the matter over to Thorgeir, himself a pagan, who, according to Ari Þorgilsson's Íslendingabók, lay down under a cloak for a day and a night in silent deliberation.

Emerging, he proclaimed that Icelanders should all have "one law and one religion": all should be baptised and become Christian, while private pagan practices such as infant exposure and the eating of horse meat — and, initially, secret sacrifice — would remain tolerated. His reasoning was pragmatic: if the law was torn apart, the peace would be too. The compromise was accepted, and Iceland converted peacefully.

Legacy

Thorgeir's arbitration is one of the most celebrated episodes of Icelandic history, remembered as a triumph of law and compromise over civil strife. He is sometimes called Ljósvetningagoði after his chieftaincy at Ljósavatn.
