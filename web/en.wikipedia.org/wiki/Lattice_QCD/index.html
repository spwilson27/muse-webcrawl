Lattice QCD

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Visualisation of gluon field fluctuations in the QCD vacuum from a lattice simulation]
The QCD vacuum as computed on a space-time lattice.

Lattice QCD is a non-perturbative approach to Quantum chromodynamics in which calculations are performed on a discrete grid of points in space and time, introduced by Kenneth G. Wilson in 1974. Quark fields live on the lattice sites and gluon fields on the links between them; the path integral of the theory is then evaluated numerically by Monte Carlo sampling on supercomputers, with results extrapolated to zero lattice spacing and physical quark masses.

Lattice QCD computes the masses of the light hadrons from the QCD Lagrangian alone to within a few percent — a striking confirmation of QCD — and provides essential Standard Model inputs (quark masses, CKM matrix elements, the hadronic contributions to the muon's anomalous magnetic moment) that no other method can supply. Its limits are computational: real-time dynamics, finite-density matter (as in neutron stars) suffers from the 'sign problem' that defies Monte Carlo — one of computational physics' famous open problems.

Practice

Modern calculations use lattices of tens of points per side, gauge ensembles generated over months on top supercomputers, and chiral fermion formulations preserving the symmetries needed for light pions. The steady march of computing power and algorithms has brought many quantities from 20% to sub-percent errors in four decades — a programme of 'precision QCD' that precision experiments at the LHC and elsewhere depend on.
