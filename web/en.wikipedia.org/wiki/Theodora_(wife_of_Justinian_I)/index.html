Theodora (wife of Justinian I)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Theodora and her court in the apse mosaic of San Vitale, Ravenna]

Theodora (c. 500 – 28 June 548) was Byzantine empress as wife and co-ruler of Justinian I, among the most powerful women of the ancient world. Born, by the hostile tradition of Procopius' Secret History, into the circus factions — daughter of the Greens' bear-keeper, actress and courtesan in her youth — she returned from travels in the eastern provinces, met Justinian (then heir apparent), and overcame the legal bar on senators marrying actresses (a law changed for her by Justin I) to marry him in 525. Crowned Augusta in 527, she ruled as partner: her defiance saved the throne in the Nika riots (532), her networks shaped church policy (protecting Miaphysite dissenters against her husband's orthodoxy), and legislation of the reign — on women's property, marriage, and the traffic in girls — bears her mark.

Nika and power

When the Nika revolt burned Constantinople and crowned a rival emperor in January 532, and Justinian's council resolved on flight, Procopius (in the public Wars) gives Theodora the speech that decided otherwise: 'royalty is a good burial-shroud' — the purple makes a fine shroud. The generals Belisarius and Mundus then massacred the rioters in the Hippodrome. Thereafter her power was institutional: receiving ambassadors, corresponding with kings, holding her own court and estates, her name joined with Justinian's in laws and dedications (the Anthemius church of Saints Sergius and Bacchus). Her religious protection sheltered the Miaphysite church — its hierarchy, consecrated under her roof in Constantinople, survives today as the Syriac Orthodox Church.

Sources and memory

Theodora died in 548 — of cancer, by tradition — and Justinian never remarried; her image in mosaic (San Vitale, Ravenna, facing his) proclaims the partnership. Everything about her early life comes filtered through Procopius' invective (the Secret History's pornographic polemic) against the respectful official record — a source problem modern biographers (from Diehl's romantic rehabilitation to current scholarly readings) must navigate openly. Orthodox tradition venerates her as a saint alongside Justinian.
