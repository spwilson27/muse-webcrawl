Omakase

Japaneseお任せ
Transliterationomakase
Literal meaning"I leave it up to you" / "entrusting"
CuisineJapanese; principally sushi

Omakase (Japanese: コース お任せ, omakase, literally "I leave it up to you") is a form of Japanese dining in which guests leave the selection of dishes entirely to the chef. The chef presents a series of dishes, usually beginning with the lightest and proceeding to richer, more heavily flavoured courses. The term derives from the verb makaseru (任せる), "to entrust."

The omakase style is most closely associated with sushi restaurants, where diners sit at a counter directly in front of the chef, but it is also offered in kaiseki, tempura, and other Japanese restaurants, and has been widely imitated outside Japan.

History

Omakase dining rose to prominence in Tokyo in the 1990s, as sushi became popular with a broader clientele who did not know enough about seafood to order for themselves and found it convenient — and fashionable — to let the chef decide. The format also suited chefs, who could showcase seasonal fish at its best and manage their inventory of prized ingredients. It subsequently spread through the Japanese diaspora and was adopted by high-end restaurants in cities such as New York, Los Angeles, and London, where multi-course chef's-counter meals became a hallmark of fine dining.

Characteristics

An omakase meal is seasonal and is structured by the chef according to the freshest ingredients available that day. Courses commonly include sashimi, several pieces of nigiri sushi served one at a time, cooked or grilled dishes, a hand roll, soup, and a small dessert such as tamago or fruit. Because the chef controls pacing and portioning, the meal is often compared to a performance, with the chef preparing each piece in view of the guests. Prices are typically fixed per person or vary with the market cost of the ingredients, and omakase meals at renowned restaurants rank among the most expensive dining experiences in the world.

Etiquette

Diners are expected to eat each piece promptly as it is served, ideally in one bite and with the fish side down on the tongue. It is acceptable to eat nigiri with the hands. Guests usually avoid wearing strong perfume, which interferes with the aromas of the fish, and are expected to inform the chef of allergies or dislikes in advance. Unlike a tasting menu in Western restaurants, omakase etiquette places trust in the chef's judgment; substitutions are generally discouraged.
