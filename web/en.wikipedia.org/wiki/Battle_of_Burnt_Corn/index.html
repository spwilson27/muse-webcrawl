Battle of Burnt Corn

The Battle of Burnt Corn, also known as the Battle of Burnt Corn Creek, was fought on 27 July 1813 in present-day Escambia County, Alabama, between United States militia and Red Stick Creek warriors. It was the first significant engagement of the Creek War (1813–1814), a conflict that grew out of a civil war within the Creek Nation and became entangled with the War of 1812.

The ambush at Burnt Corn Creek, July 1813.

Background

The Creek Nation was dividing between the accommodationist Lower Towns and the nativist "Red Sticks," inspired in part by Tecumseh's call for pan-Indian resistance. In July 1813 a party of Red Sticks led by Peter McQueen travelled to Pensacola in Spanish Florida to obtain arms and supplies from the British and Spanish. Mississippi Territory militia under Colonel Joseph Carson learned of the expedition and moved to intercept the returning party at Burnt Corn Creek.

The battle

On 27 July the militia — about 180 men under Colonel James Caller in the advance — attacked the Red Stick encampment while the warriors were at their midday meal, scattering them. Instead of pressing the advantage, many militiamen stopped to loot the packhorses. The Red Sticks regrouped and counterattacked, driving the militia in disorder across the creek. Casualties were light on both sides — roughly a dozen killed in total — but the skirmish was a humiliation for the militia and a Red Stick victory.

Aftermath

The battle inflamed the frontier. A month later, on 30 August 1813, Red Stick warriors attacked Fort Mims and killed several hundred settlers and militia, an event that brought the United States fully into the Creek War and led to Andrew Jackson's campaigns culminating at Horseshoe Bend in 1814.
