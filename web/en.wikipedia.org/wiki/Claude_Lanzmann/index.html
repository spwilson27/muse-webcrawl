Claude Lanzmann (French: [klod lɑ̃dzman]; November 27, 1925 – July 5, 2018) was a French filmmaker, journalist, and professor, best known for the nine-and-a-half-hour documentary film Shoah (1985), regarded as one of the greatest documentaries of all time and a landmark in the representation of the Holocaust.

Life

Lanzmann was born in Bois-Colombes to a Jewish family that went into hiding during the Second World War; as a teenager in Clermont-Ferrand he joined the French Resistance. After the war he studied philosophy, became close to Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, and joined the editorial board of Sartre's journal Les Temps modernes, which he later directed. His first film, Pourquoi Israël (1973), addressed the early years of the Israeli state.

Shoah and later films

Shoah took eleven years to make (1974–1985). Built entirely from interviews with survivors, bystanders, and perpetrators — and present-day footage of the sites of the camps — it contains no archival imagery whatsoever, a method Lanzmann called an "incarnation" rather than a representation of the genocide. Several additional films were assembled from unused Shoah footage, including A Visitor from the Living (1997), Sobibor, October 14, 1943, 4 p.m. (2001), and The Last of the Unjust (2013), centered on Benjamin Murmelstein, the last elder of the Theresienstadt ghetto. His memoir The Patagonian Hare appeared in 2009. Lanzmann died in Paris in 2018.
