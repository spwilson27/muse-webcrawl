Agave

Agave is a genus of monocots native to the hot and arid regions of the Americas. The genus is known primarily for its succulent and xerophytic species that typically form large rosettes of strong, fleshy leaves. Agave now includes species formerly in several other genera.

Description

Agaves are rosette-forming succulents with thick, water-storing leaves that usually end in a sharp spine and often have toothed margins. Most species are monocarpic: the mature rosette flowers once, producing a tall stalk (quiote) with clusters of yellow or greenish flowers, and then dies, though it commonly leaves offsets ("pups"). Some species are bat-pollinated. The plants use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), opening their stomata at night to reduce water loss.

Uses

Agaves have been used by people for thousands of years for food, fibre and drink. The fermented sap yields pulque; distillation produces mezcal, and tequila is made specifically from the blue agave (Agave tequilana). Sisal comes from Agave sisalana. The sweetener agave syrup (agave nectar) is produced from the sap of several species. Many species are widely grown as ornamental plants in dry gardens.
