Camembert (cheese)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A small round Camembert wheel cut open, showing creamy interior]

Camembert is a moist, soft, creamy cow's-milk cheese with a bloomy white rind, first made in the late 18th century in Camembert, Normandy, in northern France. It is similar in style to Brie but made in smaller, thicker wheels and generally has a deeper, earthier, more intense flavour. Camembert de Normandie made from raw milk holds AOP/PDO protection; most camembert sold worldwide is pasteurised and industrially produced.

History

Tradition credits Marie Harel, a farmer of Camembert village, with creating the cheese in 1791 after a priest from the Brie region, fleeing the Revolution, shared the brie method with her; historians treat details cautiously but the story is central to the cheese's identity. Camembert spread widely after the invention of its little round wooden box in about 1890, which let the soft cheese travel by rail without crushing — and French soldiers' rations in the First World War made it a national symbol.

Production

Milk is set with rennet, the curd ladled into small moulds, drained, salted and inoculated with Penicillium camemberti, which forms the white rind. Over three to five weeks the cheese ripens from a chalky centre to a soft, sometimes almost liquid, interior with aromas of mushroom, butter and cabbage. Raw-milk versions are more complex and are restricted in some countries (the United States requires longer ageing for raw-milk cheeses than camembert allows, so American camembert is pasteurised).

Serving

Camembert is eaten at room temperature with bread, baked whole in its box until molten (often scored and studded with garlic and herbs), or breaded and fried. A ripe wheel should yield to gentle pressure and smell pleasantly mushroomy rather than of ammonia.
