Apostles

The apostles (from Greek apóstolos, "one who is sent off") were the primary disciples of Jesus Christ in the New Testament. According to the Gospels, Jesus chose twelve apostles—often called the Twelve Apostles or simply the Twelve—during his ministry, commissioning them to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and, after his resurrection, to spread his teaching throughout the world. In broader usage, the title "apostle" is also applied to other early Christian missionaries, most notably Paul the Apostle.

The Twelve Apostles chosen by Jesus

The Twelve

The canonical lists of the Twelve (Matthew 10, Mark 3, Luke 6, and Acts 1) name: Simon Peter; his brother Andrew; James and John, the sons of Zebedee; Philip; Bartholomew (often identified with Nathanael); Matthew the tax collector; Thomas; James the son of Alphaeus; Thaddaeus (also called Jude or Lebbaeus); Simon the Zealot (or the Cananaean); and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed Jesus. After Judas's death, the remaining eleven chose Matthias by lot to restore the number twelve (Acts 1).

Mission and martyrdom

The Book of Acts describes the apostles leading the early church in Jerusalem after Pentecost, with Peter and John prominent in its opening chapters and James the Just later leading the Jerusalem community. Christian tradition holds that the apostles dispersed to preach across the Roman world and beyond—Peter and Paul to Rome, Thomas to India, Andrew to Greece and Scythia—and that nearly all of them died as martyrs. Their relics and tombs became major pilgrimage sites, and churches throughout Christendom are dedicated to them.

Paul and other apostles

Paul, though not one of the Twelve, claimed the title of apostle on the basis of his vision of the risen Christ on the road to Damascus and is known as the "Apostle to the Gentiles" for his missions across the eastern Mediterranean and his letters, which form a large part of the New Testament. The New Testament also applies the term to figures such as Barnabas, and a wider circle including Mary Magdalene—honored in tradition as "apostle to the apostles"—played foundational roles. In later Christian usage, missionaries who first evangelized a nation are sometimes called its apostle, as in Saint Boniface, "Apostle of the Germans."
