Gibbons are the supreme brachiators: their long arms, rotating wrists, and hook-like hands let them swing continuously beneath branches.

Brachiation (from Latin brachium, meaning "arm") is a form of arboreal locomotion in which primates swing from tree limb to tree limb using only their arms. During brachiation, the body is alternately supported under each forelimb, and the animal moves in a pendulum-like arc, briefly becoming airborne between handholds at higher speeds. The gibbons and siamangs of Southeast Asia (family Hylobatidae) are the only true specialists, but many other primates can brachiate to some degree, and spider monkeys combine arm-swinging with a prehensile tail that acts as a fifth limb.

Anatomy of brachiators

Specialised brachiators show a suite of adaptations: exceptionally long forelimbs relative to body size (gibbons have an intermembral index around 120–130, among the highest of primates); long, curved fingers forming a permanent hook, with a reduced thumb that does not interfere with the hook grip; a highly mobile shoulder joint and a wrist capable of near-full rotation, allowing the body to pivot under a fixed handhold; a short, stiff lower back; and a broad thorax with the shoulder blades positioned on the back rather than the sides. Gibbon hands are so specialised that the animals habitually walk bipedally on the ground with their arms held aloft for balance.

Mechanics and gaits

Researchers distinguish continuous-contact brachiation, in which one hand always grips a support, from ricochetal brachiation, in which there is an aerial phase between handholds. Mechanically, brachiation resembles an inverted pendulum: kinetic and gravitational potential energy exchange efficiently, making the gait surprisingly economical at speed, with energy recovery comparable to that of human walking or running. Gibbons can cover gaps of many metres and move branch to branch at speeds reported up to about 55 km/h in flight phases, slower sustained speeds being more typical. The risks are real — wild gibbon skeletons commonly show healed fractures from falls.

Evolution

Brachiation was once central to theories of human evolution: the "brachiating hypothesis" held that the upright trunk, mobile shoulders, and long arms of hominoids were inherited from arm-swinging ancestors, preadapting the lineage for bipedalism. Modern interpretations are more nuanced — suspensory behaviour of various kinds is widespread among apes, and true ricochetal brachiation is a specialisation of the small-bodied gibbons — but the shared anatomy of living apes still reflects a strongly arboreal, below-branch heritage. Early hominoids such as Proconsul lacked full brachiating specialisations, which appear to have evolved in parallel in several ape lineages.

Brachiation in other contexts

Children instinctively brachiate on playground "monkey bars," and overhead ladder apparatus is used in gymnastics, obstacle-course racing, and military training to develop upper-body strength. Robotics researchers have built brachiating robots to study energy-efficient locomotion under gravity, and the gait has inspired designs for machines intended to move through canopies or along cables.
