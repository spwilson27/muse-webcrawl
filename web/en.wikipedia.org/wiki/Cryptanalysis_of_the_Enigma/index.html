The cryptanalysis of the Enigma was the effort to break the ciphers produced by the German Enigma rotor machine, used extensively by Nazi Germany before and during the Second World War. Polish mathematicians first broke Enigma in the 1930s; their work, shared with France and Britain in 1939, underpinned the Allied Ultra programme centred at Bletchley Park.

Polish breakthrough

Beginning in 1932, Marian Rejewski of the Polish Cipher Bureau, using permutation theory and material supplied by French intelligence (from the spy Hans-Thilo Schmidt), reconstructed the wiring of the military Enigma. With Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski he developed attacks including the cyclometer, the bomba kryptologiczna, and Zygalski sheets, reading German traffic until changes in procedure and additional rotors outpaced Polish resources. In July 1939, at Pyry near Warsaw, Poland gave its allies replicas and methods.

Bletchley Park

At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, Dilly Knox, and many others industrialised the attack. Turing's Bombe, enhanced by Welchman's diagonal board, tested candidate rotor settings against "cribs" — guessed plaintext such as weather reports and standard phrases. Naval Enigma, with its bigger key space, was harder; capture of key material from U-boats (notably U-110 in May 1941 and U-559 in October 1942) enabled reading of U-boat traffic, vital in the Battle of the Atlantic. A four-rotor naval Enigma ("Shark") blacked out reading for most of 1942 until the U-559 capture.

Impact

Ultra intelligence influenced convoy routing, the Mediterranean campaign, and the Normandy landings. Historians commonly credit it with shortening the war in Europe, possibly by as much as two years. The secret was guarded for decades afterward; public disclosure came only in the 1970s.
