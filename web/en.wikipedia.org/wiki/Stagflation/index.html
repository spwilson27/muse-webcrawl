Stagflation

In economics, stagflation (a portmanteau of stagnation and inflation) is a situation in which the inflation rate is high or increasing, economic growth slows, and unemployment remains steadily high. It presents a dilemma for economic policy, since the measures that lower inflation may worsen unemployment, and vice versa.

US inflation and unemployment rose together during the 1970s stagflation

The 1970s

The term was coined by British politician Iain Macleod in 1965. Stagflation became widespread in the 1970s after the 1973 oil crisis and the 1979 energy shock sharply raised production costs while growth faltered; in the United States, inflation peaked at over 13% in 1980. The experience discredited simple Keynesian Phillips-curve trade-offs and helped spur monetarism and supply-side economics.

Explanations

Economists attribute stagflation mainly to adverse supply shocks (such as oil price rises) combined with accommodative monetary policy and de-anchored inflation expectations. It ended in the early 1980s when central banks under leaders such as Paul Volcker raised interest rates sharply, at the cost of deep recessions.
