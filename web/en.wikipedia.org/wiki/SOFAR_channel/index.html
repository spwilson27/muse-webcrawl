SOFAR channel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The SOFAR channel (short for sound fixing and ranging channel), or deep sound channel, is a horizontal layer of water in the ocean at which depth the speed of sound is at its minimum. It acts as a waveguide for sound: low-frequency waves are trapped within the channel by refraction and can travel thousands of kilometres without interacting with the sea surface or ocean floor, losing very little energy.

Physical basis

Sound speed in seawater increases with temperature and with pressure (depth) but decreases as temperature falls. Near the surface, the temperature term dominates and sound speed falls with depth; below the thermocline, the pressure term dominates and sound speed rises steadily. The result is a minimum — the channel axis — typically at depths of about 600–1,200 metres in low and mid latitudes, rising toward the surface in polar waters. Rays launched near the axis are refracted back toward it from above and below, oscillating around the axis instead of spreading in all directions.

Discovery and applications

The channel was discovered independently in the 1940s by Maurice Ewing and J. Lamar Worzel in the United States and by Leonid Brekhovskikh in the Soviet Union. During the Second World War, Ewing proposed that downed airmen could detonate small explosive charges set to sink to the channel axis; the resulting signals, detected by hydrophone stations, would reveal their position — the origin of the name "sound fixing and ranging".

During the Cold War the United States Navy's SOSUS network of seabed hydrophones exploited the channel to track Soviet submarines across ocean basins. Marine mammals, especially fin and humpback whales, are thought to use the channel to communicate over great distances. The channel is also central to ocean acoustic tomography, which measures travel times of signals to infer ocean temperature; the 1991 Heard Island feasibility test transmitted coded sounds that were detected as far as 18,000 km away.
