The Fama–French five-factor model is an asset pricing model developed by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French to explain average stock returns. It extends their 1993 three-factor model by adding two factors motivated by the dividend discount model: profitability, the difference in returns between firms with robust and weak operating profitability (RMW), and investment, the difference between firms that invest conservatively and those that invest aggressively (CMA). The model was presented in their 2015 paper "A five-factor asset pricing model" in the Journal of Financial Economics.

Specification

The model expresses the excess return on a portfolio as a linear function of five factor portfolios:

Rit − RFt = ai + bi(RMt − RFt) + siSMBt + hiHMLt + riRMWt + ciCMAt + eit

Here RMt − RFt is the market excess return; SMB ("small minus big") is the size factor; HML ("high minus low") is the value factor based on book-to-market equity; RMW ("robust minus weak") is the profitability factor; and CMA ("conservative minus aggressive") is the investment factor. The factors are constructed from sorts of stocks on size combined with book-to-market, profitability, or investment, and Fama and French publish the factor and portfolio returns on Kenneth French's website data library.

Motivation

The dividend discount model implies that, controlling for price and expected cash flows, firms with higher expected profitability should earn higher returns, and firms with higher expected investment (growth in assets) should earn lower returns. Empirically, profitability and investment subsumed several patterns the three-factor model could not explain, such as the Novy-Marx profitability result and the asset-growth anomaly. In tests on U.S. data from 1963 to 2013, the five-factor model captured most of the variation in average returns across portfolios sorted on size, value, profitability, and investment.

Findings and criticism

A notable finding was that the value factor HML became largely redundant in the five-factor model given the profitability and investment factors, though Fama and French retained it for continuity. The model performs poorly for some small, unprofitable, aggressively investing growth stocks, whose returns are lower than the model predicts. Critics note that factor models are vulnerable to data mining and that the number of "factors" in the literature has grown into a "factor zoo"; others ask whether the factors proxy for risk or reflect behavioural mispricing. Variants include international five-factor models (Fama and French, 2017) and the competing q-factor model of Hou, Xue, and Zhang.
