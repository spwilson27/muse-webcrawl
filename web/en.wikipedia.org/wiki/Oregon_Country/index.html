Oregon Country

The Oregon Country was a large region of the Pacific Northwest of North America claimed by both the United States and the United Kingdom. The British generally used the name Columbia District for the fur-trade region administered by the Hudson's Bay Company. The territory comprised the drainage basin of the Columbia River, roughly between 42° and 54°40′ north latitude, west of the Rocky Mountains.

Exploration and the fur trade

American claims rested on Robert Gray's entry into the Columbia River in 1792 and the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806), which wintered at Fort Clatsop near the river's mouth; British claims rested on the explorations of James Cook, George Vancouver, and overland traders David Thompson and Alexander Mackenzie. The Hudson's Bay Company, through its Columbia District and headquarters at Fort Vancouver (founded 1825), effectively governed the region's commerce under chief factor John McLoughlin.

Joint occupation and settlement

The Treaty of 1818 between the United States and Britain provided for joint occupation for ten years, renewed indefinitely in 1827. American missionaries arrived in the 1830s, followed from 1841 by wagon trains of settlers on the Oregon Trail. In 1843 American settlers formed a provisional government at Champoeg. The influx shifted the balance, and the slogan "Fifty-four Forty or Fight" featured in the US election of 1844, asserting a claim to the entire territory.

Resolution

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 settled the boundary at the 49th parallel, extended to the sea through the Strait of Georgia, leaving Vancouver Island wholly British. The United States organized Oregon Territory in 1848; the British portion later became the colonies of Vancouver Island and British Columbia. A residual ambiguity over the San Juan Islands led to the bloodless "Pig War" of 1859, resolved by arbitration in 1872.
