0

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The digit 0 drawn large in a serif typeface]

0 (zero) is a number representing an empty quantity. Adding 0 to any number leaves that number unchanged; multiplying any number by 0 gives 0; and division by 0 is undefined. As a numeral (digit), 0 also serves as a placeholder in positional notation, enabling the representation of large numbers with few symbols.

Zero was a late arrival in mathematics. Babylonian scribes used a placeholder mark by the mid-first millennium BC, but a full number zero — with arithmetic rules — was developed in India: Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 AD) gives rules for computing with zero and negative numbers. The concept travelled through Islamic mathematics (al-Khwarizmi) to Europe, where Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) promoted the 'zephirum'.

Resistance and adoption

Greek mathematics, geometry-centred and suspicious of the void, had no zero (the famous question 'how can nothing be something?'). European adoption was slow — Florence banned the new numerals for bankers in 1299 for fear of forgery — but positional arithmetic's power prevailed by the 16th century.

Philosophically zero pairs with infinity as a productive paradox; in computing it gains further life as the false value, the null reference (Tony Hoare's self-confessed 'billion-dollar mistake'), and the base of 0-indexed arrays — a perennial programmers' quarrel.
