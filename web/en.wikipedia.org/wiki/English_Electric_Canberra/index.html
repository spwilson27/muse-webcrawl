English Electric Canberra

The English Electric Canberra is a British first-generation, jet-powered medium bomber. It was developed by English Electric during the mid-to-late 1940s in response to a 1944 Air Ministry requirement for a successor to the wartime de Havilland Mosquito fast bomber. Amongst the performance requirements for the type was an outstanding high-altitude bombing capability and high speed. These were partly accomplished by making use of newly developed jet propulsion technology. When the Canberra was introduced to service with the Royal Air Force (RAF), the type's first operator, in May 1951, it became the service's first jet-powered bomber.

Development

The origins of the Canberra lay in a requirement issued by the Air Ministry in 1944 for a high-speed, high-altitude bomber to replace the Mosquito. English Electric's design team, led by W. E. W. "Teddy" Petter, produced a design with a large mid-mounted wing, two podded Rolls-Royce Avon turbojets close to the fuselage, and a crew of two in tandem. The prototype VN799 took off for its first flight from Warton on 13 May 1949, piloted by Roland Beamont.

Design

The Canberra was a mid-wing cantilever monoplane with a metal monocoque construction. It had a conventional tail, a tricycle undercarriage, and a large uncluttered bomb bay. The design emphasised speed and altitude over defensive armament, relying on performance to evade interception. Later marks introduced photo-reconnaissance fits, target-towing capability, electronic warfare equipment, and, in the PR.9, a more powerful engine fit and offset fighter-style cockpit.

Operational history

The Canberra served with the RAF from 1951 until its final retirement in 2006 — a 55-year career. It was exported widely and licence-built in the United States as the Martin B-57 Canberra and in Australia by the Government Aircraft Factories. Canberras served in the Suez Crisis, the Vietnam War, the Indo-Pakistani wars, and numerous other conflicts. The type set several altitude and speed records, including a world altitude record of 70,310 ft (21,430 m) in 1957.

Variants

Numerous variants were produced, including the B.2 and B.6 bombers, the PR.3 and PR.7 photographic-reconnaissance aircraft, the T.4 dual-control trainer, the B(I).8 interdictor with an offset canopy, and the high-altitude PR.9. Export and licence production added further marks for overseas operators.

Operators

Operators included the air forces of Argentina, Australia, Chile, Ecuador, Ethiopia, France, India, New Zealand, Peru, Rhodesia, South Africa, Sweden, the United Kingdom, the United States, Venezuela, West Germany, and Zimbabwe.
