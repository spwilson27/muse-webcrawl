Immunoglobulin

Immunoglobulin (Ig) is another name for an antibody: a large, Y-shaped protein produced mainly by plasma cells that is used by the immune system to identify and neutralize foreign objects such as pathogenic bacteria and viruses. Antibodies recognize a unique molecule of the pathogen, called an antigen.

Structure

An immunoglobulin molecule consists of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains joined by disulfide bonds. Each chain has a variable region, which binds antigen, and a constant region, which determines the molecule's class and effector functions. The antigen-binding tips (Fab fragments) and the stem (Fc fragment) can be separated by enzymatic digestion, a classic experiment by Rodney Porter and Gerald Edelman, who shared the 1972 Nobel Prize for work on antibody structure.

Classes

Mammals have five classes of immunoglobulin, named for their heavy chain type: IgG, the most abundant in blood and the only class that crosses the placenta; IgM, the first antibody produced in a response, typically a pentamer; IgA, dominant in mucosal secretions such as saliva, tears and breast milk; IgE, involved in allergy and defence against parasites; and IgD, found mainly as a receptor on naive B cells. Light chains come in two types, kappa (κ) and lambda (λ).

Generation of diversity

Antibody diversity arises from V(D)J recombination of gene segments in developing B cells, junctional diversity, and somatic hypermutation during immune responses, allowing the immune system to generate an enormous repertoire of specificities from a limited set of genes — a mechanism elucidated by Susumu Tonegawa.
