Mind-wandering

Mind-wandering is the experience of thoughts shifting away from the primary task at hand toward internal, self-generated content unrelated to the external environment. It is commonly described as daydreaming, zoning out, or having one's mind "elsewhere." Research using experience-sampling methods suggests that people spend roughly 30–50% of their waking hours engaged in some form of mind-wandering.

Measurement

Psychologists study mind-wandering through probe-caught experience sampling, in which participants performing a task are interrupted at random intervals and asked whether their thoughts were on-task or off-task, and self-caught methods, in which participants report whenever they notice their mind has wandered. Sustained-attention tasks such as the SART (Sustained Attention to Response Task) are standard laboratory paradigms.

Neural basis

Mind-wandering is associated with activity in the default mode network (DMN), a set of brain regions including the medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex that become active during rest and self-referential thought. Executive control regions are also recruited, particularly when the wandering is deliberate or when the person is aware of it.

Costs and benefits

Off-task thought is linked to poorer reading comprehension, reduced driving performance, and lower mood in the moment; a well-known 2010 study by Killingsworth and Gilbert found that a wandering mind is, on average, a less happy mind. However, mind-wandering also supports autobiographical planning, creative incubation, problem solving, and mental simulation of future events. Researchers distinguish between deliberate and spontaneous mind-wandering, and between positive-constructive daydreaming and troubled, ruminative thought, which carry very different consequences. Mind-wandering is related to, but distinct from, attention lapses and to the rumination seen in depression.
