Negev Bedouin

Traditionally pastoral nomads, the Negev Bedouin have undergone rapid sedentarization since the mid-20th century.

The Negev Bedouin (Arabic: بدو النقب, Badū an-Naqab; Hebrew: הבדואים בנגב) are traditionally pastoral nomadic Arab tribes (Bedouin) who live in the Negev desert of southern Israel. Before 1948, an estimated 65,000–100,000 Bedouin lived in the Negev; after the 1948 war, most fled or were expelled, and roughly 11,000 remained. Today their descendants number well over 200,000, forming a significant part of Israel's Arab citizen population.

History

Bedouin tribes have inhabited the Negev for centuries, practicing seasonal migration, herding, and dryland farming. Under Ottoman and British Mandate rule they retained considerable autonomy and customary land practices. After 1948 the remaining population was concentrated by the Israeli military administration into a restricted area northeast of Beersheba known as the Siyaj ("fence"), under military rule until 1966.

Townships and unrecognized villages

From the late 1960s Israel built seven Bedouin townships — including Rahat (now the largest Bedouin city), Tel Sheva, Hura, Lakiya, Kuseife, Ar'arat an-Naqab and Segev Shalom — and encouraged urbanization. A substantial share of the population, however, remained in villages that the state does not officially recognize; these "unrecognized villages" lack connection to national water, electricity and road networks and face demolition and land disputes. A smaller number of villages have been recognized since the 2000s. Land ownership claims based on customary tenure remain a central political and legal dispute, exemplified by the repeated demolitions at al-Araqib and the controversy over the proposed Prawer Plan in 2011–2013, which was shelved after widespread protests.

Society

Negev Bedouin society is undergoing rapid change, with rising educational attainment — especially among women, who now make up the majority of Bedouin university students — alongside high poverty rates and rapid population growth. Bedouin serve voluntarily in the Israel Defense Forces, notably as trackers, though most are not subject to conscription. Cultural institutions such as the Museum of Bedouin Culture near Lahav document the traditional way of life.
