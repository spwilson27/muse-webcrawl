Chicken (game)

The game of chicken, also known as the hawk–dove game or snowdrift game, is a model of conflict for two players in game theory. The principle of the game is that while the ideal outcome is for one player to yield (to avoid the worst outcome if neither yields), individuals try to avoid yielding out of pride, not wanting to look like a "chicken". Each player taunts the other to increase the risk of shame in yielding. However, when neither player yields, the result is the worst possible outcome for both players.

The name "chicken" has its origins in a game in which two drivers drive towards each other on a collision course: one must swerve, or both may die in the crash. If one driver swerves and the other does not, the one who swerved will be called a "chicken", meaning a coward. This terminology is most commonly used in political science and economics. The name "hawk–dove" refers to a situation in which there is a competition for a shared resource and the contestants can choose either conciliation or conflict. This terminology is most commonly used in biology and evolutionary game theory.

In the payoff matrix, if both players swerve, they each receive a neutral payoff. If one swerves and the other goes straight, the swerver loses a little face while the other gains. If both go straight, both suffer the catastrophic outcome. The game has two pure-strategy Nash equilibria: one in which each player swerves while the other goes straight, and vice versa.

Applications

The game of chicken has been used to model brinkmanship in international relations, most famously during the Cuban Missile Crisis, as well as mutual assured destruction in nuclear strategy. In evolutionary biology, the hawk–dove formulation due to John Maynard Smith describes contests over resources between aggressive "hawks" and conciliatory "doves", and has been used to explain the evolution of ritualized, non-lethal competition among animals.
