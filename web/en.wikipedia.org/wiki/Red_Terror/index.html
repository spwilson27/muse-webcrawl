The Red Terror was a campaign of political repression and mass killings carried out by the Bolshevik government, principally through its secret police, the Cheka, during the Russian Civil War (1918–1922). Officially proclaimed in September 1918 after assassination attempts on Bolshevik leaders, it institutionalized the execution of hostages and "class enemies" as an instrument of revolutionary rule.

Origins

Violence against perceived enemies began soon after the October Revolution of 1917, as the Bolsheviks fought to hold power amid civil war. The immediate triggers for formalizing the Terror were the assassination of Petrograd Cheka chief Moisei Uritsky and Fanny Kaplan's attempt on Lenin's life on 30 August 1918. On 5 September the government issued a decree "On Red Terror," authorizing the shooting of hostages and the internment of class enemies in concentration camps.

Course

Under Felix Dzerzhinsky, the Cheka arrested, tortured, and executed former tsarist officials, officers, clergy, merchants, striking workers, rebellious peasants, and members of rival socialist parties. Mass shootings followed anti-Bolshevik uprisings and accompanied the reconquest of territories from the White Armies, notably in Crimea in 1920. Estimates of executions during the Terror vary widely, from tens of thousands in official Cheka statistics to several hundred thousand in later scholarly assessments; millions more perished in the wider civil war from combat, famine, and disease.

Legacy

Bolshevik leaders defended the Terror as a necessary weapon of class war — Leon Trotsky wrote a pamphlet, Terrorism and Communism (1920), justifying it against Karl Kautsky's criticism — while critics, including Rosa Luxemburg before her death, warned against substituting terror for democracy. Historians generally see the Red Terror as laying institutional foundations for the later, far larger repressions of the Stalin era. The term "red terror" has also been applied to analogous campaigns in Hungary (1919), Ethiopia under the Derg, and elsewhere.
