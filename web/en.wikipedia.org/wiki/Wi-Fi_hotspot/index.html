A Wi-Fi hotspot is a physical location where people can obtain Internet access, typically using Wi-Fi technology via a wireless local area network (WLAN) connected to an Internet service provider. Hotspots may be provided free of charge to customers of a business, offered as a paid service, or run by municipalities, libraries, and transport operators for public use.

How hotspots work

A hotspot consists of one or more wireless access points connected to a router with a broadband backhaul link. Many public hotspots present a captive portal: users connecting to the network are redirected to a web page to accept terms, view advertising, enter credentials, or pay before full access is granted. Smartphones and mobile routers can also create personal hotspots (tethering), sharing a cellular data connection with nearby devices over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or USB.

History

Public hotspots proliferated in the early 2000s with companies such as T-Mobile HotSpot and Boingo building networks in cafés, hotels, and airports, and community projects sharing home connections. Coffeehouse chains — most famously Starbucks — made free Wi-Fi a standard amenity. Municipal wireless networks aimed to cover whole cities, though many early projects were scaled back. Today, carrier offload, Passpoint/Hotspot 2.0 automatic authentication, and bundled subscriptions have made hotspot access more seamless.

Security

Open hotspots historically sent traffic unencrypted, exposing users to eavesdropping and "evil twin" attacks in which a rogue access point imitates a legitimate one. Modern protections include WPA2/WPA3 encryption on the local link, HTTPS and VPNs for end-to-end security, and Enhanced Open (OWE). Users are commonly advised to verify network names, disable auto-joining of open networks, and use a VPN on untrusted hotspots.
