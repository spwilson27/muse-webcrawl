Irreligion in Ethiopia — atheism, agnosticism and the absence of organised religious affiliation — is relatively uncommon in Ethiopian society, which is one of the world's most religious by self-reported measures. The majority of the population identifies as Ethiopian Orthodox Christian (about 44% in the 2007 census), with Muslims forming roughly a third, and Protestant Christians and adherents of traditional faiths making up most of the remainder. Official census categories leave little room for the nonreligious, and "no religion" is rarely reported — typically well under 1% of the population.

History

Ethiopia's identity has been closely bound with religion since the conversion of the Aksumite Empire to Christianity in the 4th century CE, and Islam has been present since the 7th century. The socialist Derg regime (1974–1991) adopted an officially secular, Marxist ideology, nationalised church land, curtailed religious institutions and promoted state atheism in official circles — yet mass religious practice continued, and the regime is widely thought to have inadvertently strengthened Pentecostal and other movements by suppressing the established church.

The 1995 Constitution of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia guarantees freedom of religion and formally separates state and religion. In practice, religious identity remains central to personal and communal life, and expressing irreligion can carry significant social stigma, particularly outside Addis Ababa and other large cities.

Contemporary situation

Surveys by Pew Research and WIN/Gallup International have consistently placed Ethiopia among countries with very low self-reported atheism. Urban, university-educated young people form the bulk of the small visible irreligious population; online communities provide a discreet space for discussion. Accurate measurement is difficult because census forms historically listed religion with limited options for non-belief, and because social desirability pressures discourage declaring irreligion.
