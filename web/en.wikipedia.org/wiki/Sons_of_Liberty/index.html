Sons of Liberty

Sons of Liberty
Founded: 1765, Boston, Massachusetts
Type: Political organisation
Purpose: Resistance to British taxation and imperial policy
Notable members: Samuel Adams, John Hancock, Paul Revere, Joseph Warren

The Sons of Liberty was a loosely organised, clandestine group of American colonists created in 1765 to protest British imperial policies — above all the Stamp Act — and to advance the rights of the colonists in the years leading to the American Revolution.

Origins and actions

The organisation grew out of Boston groups such as the Loyal Nine. Sons of Liberty chapters appeared in ports and towns across the Thirteen Colonies, coordinating boycotts of British goods, intimidating stamp distributors into resigning, and staging demonstrations under "liberty trees" and "liberty poles". Their protests helped force repeal of the Stamp Act in 1766.

Role in the Revolution

The Sons remained active through the Townshend Acts, the Boston Massacre (1770) and the Tea Act; Boston members, some disguised as Mohawk Indians, carried out the Boston Tea Party of 16 December 1773. Networks of Sons fed into the Committees of Correspondence and the emerging Patriot movement.

Legacy

The name, drawn from a phrase used in Parliament by Isaac Barré sympathetic to the colonists, has been reused by later groups, and the Sons of Liberty remain a symbol of American resistance to perceived tyranny.
