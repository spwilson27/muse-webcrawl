Teratoma

SpecialtyOncology, pathology
TypeGerm cell tumour
Common sitesOvary, testicle, sacrococcygeal region, mediastinum
ClassificationMature (usually benign) or immature (potentially malignant)

A teratoma is a tumour made up of several different types of tissue, such as hair, muscle, teeth, or bone, derived from more than one embryonic germ layer. Teratomas are a type of germ cell tumour and typically form in the ovaries or testicles, though they also occur along the body's midline — at the base of the spine (sacrococcygeal teratoma, the most common tumour of newborn infants), in the mediastinum, and occasionally in the brain. The name derives from the Greek teras, "monster", reflecting the tumours' bizarre mixture of mature structures.

Pathology

Teratomas arise from pluripotent germ cells that differentiate chaotically into tissues of the ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. They are classified as mature or immature. Mature teratomas consist of well-differentiated, adult-type tissues and are generally benign; the commonest example is the mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst) of the ovary, which accounts for a large share of benign ovarian tumours in women of reproductive age and may contain skin, hair, sebum, and fully formed teeth. Immature teratomas contain embryonic-type tissue, especially immature neuroepithelium, and are graded by its amount; they can behave malignantly and metastasise.

Presentation and treatment

Small teratomas may be incidental findings; larger ones cause pain, swelling, or torsion of the ovary. Rarely, a mature teratoma undergoes malignant transformation, most often into squamous cell carcinoma. Tumour markers such as alpha-fetoprotein and β-hCG are typically normal in pure mature teratomas, helping distinguish them from other germ cell tumours.

Treatment is surgical removal, ideally preserving the affected ovary in benign cases. Immature teratomas may additionally require chemotherapy. Sacrococcygeal teratomas in newborns are removed together with the coccyx, usually in the first days of life, since the risk of malignancy rises with delayed excision.
