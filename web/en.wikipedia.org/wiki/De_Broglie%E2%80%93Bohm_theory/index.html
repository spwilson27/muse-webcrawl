De Broglie–Bohm theory

Proposed1927 (de Broglie); 1952 (Bohm)
TypeInterpretation of quantum mechanics
Key conceptPilot wave guiding particles on definite trajectories

The de Broglie–Bohm theory, also known as pilot-wave theory or Bohmian mechanics, is an interpretation of quantum mechanics in which particles have definite positions at all times and are guided by a wave described by the Schrödinger equation. It is a deterministic, explicitly nonlocal hidden-variable theory.

Overview

In the theory, a system consists of a wavefunction evolving according to the Schrödinger equation and an actual configuration of particles whose motion is determined by a guidance equation derived from the wavefunction. Statistical predictions agree with standard quantum mechanics when particle distributions follow the "quantum equilibrium" hypothesis.

History

Louis de Broglie presented an early pilot-wave model at the 1927 Solvay Conference but abandoned it after criticism. David Bohm independently developed the theory in two papers in 1952, showing that it reproduced all non-relativistic quantum predictions.

Criticism and development

Critics object to the theory's manifest nonlocality and the difficulty of extending it to relativistic quantum field theory, while supporters argue it resolves the measurement problem without observers or collapse. John Stewart Bell championed the theory and cited it in motivating his work on Bell's theorem.
