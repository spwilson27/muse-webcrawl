Sociology of knowledge

The sociology of knowledge studies the relationship between thought and the social conditions in which it arises.

The sociology of knowledge is a branch of sociology that studies the relationship between human thought — beliefs, ideas, ideologies, and systems of knowledge — and the social context in which that thought arises and is maintained. Its guiding premise is that knowledge is not produced in a vacuum: what counts as true, obvious, or thinkable is influenced by the knower's social position, historical period, institutions, and interests.

History

The field was founded in the 1920s by the Hungarian sociologist Karl Mannheim, whose Ideology and Utopia (1929) argued that political thought is shaped by the social location of its bearers, generalizing Marx's concept of ideology into a broader "sociology of knowledge." Precursors include Marx's theory of ideology, Durkheim and Mauss's work on primitive classification, and Max Scheler, who coined the term Wissenssoziologie. In the 1960s, Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann's The Social Construction of Reality (1966) gave the field a phenomenological foundation, analysing how everyday knowledge is produced, institutionalized, and legitimated.

Extensions

The sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK), emerging in the 1970s with David Bloor's "strong programme" and laboratory ethnographies by Bruno Latour and others, extended the approach to science itself, treating successful and failed scientific claims symmetrically and examining how facts are constructed in practice. Related developments include Michel Foucault's analyses of power/knowledge, the sociology of intellectuals, and studies of how information technologies reshape knowledge production. Critics have sometimes charged the field with relativism; its defenders reply that explaining belief socially is compatible with evaluating its truth.
