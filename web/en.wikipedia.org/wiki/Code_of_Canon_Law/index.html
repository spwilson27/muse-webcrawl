Code of Canon Law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Code of Canon Law (Latin: Codex Iuris Canonici) is the codified body of law governing the Roman Catholic Church's Latin Church. Promulgated by Pope John Paul II in 1983 (in force from the first Sunday of Advent 1983), replacing the first code of 1917, it organises 1,752 canons in seven books: general norms; the people of God (the hierarchy, religious institutes, the laity); the teaching office; the sanctifying office (sacraments, including marriage law); temporal goods; sanctions; and processes (including marriage nullity trials and penal procedures). A parallel code for the Eastern Catholic Churches (CCEO) followed in 1990.

Canon law is the oldest continuously operating legal system in the West, built from Scripture, conciliar decrees, papal decretals and Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140), a founding text of the Western legal tradition itself (Berman's thesis). It operates through diocesan tribunals and the Roman Rota, and interacts with secular law in marriage annulments, church property, employment and — notoriously in abuse crises — the handling of clerical offenders, where reforms under Popes Benedict XVI and Francis (e.g., Vos estis lux mundi, 2019) tightened accountability norms.
