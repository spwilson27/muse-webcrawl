The National Wilderness Preservation System (NWPS) is a network of federally protected wilderness areas in the United States, created by the Wilderness Act of 1964 to secure for present and future generations "an enduring resource of wilderness".

Designation

Wilderness areas are designated by Congress on lands managed by four agencies: the Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, Fish and Wildlife Service, and National Park Service. Within designated wilderness, permanent roads, motorised vehicles, mechanised transport and commercial enterprise are generally prohibited, though hiking, camping, fishing and horseback riding are permitted.

Scale

The system began in 1964 with 54 areas covering 9.1 million acres and has grown to more than 800 areas encompassing over 111 million acres — about 4.5 percent of the area of the United States. More than half of the total lies in Alaska, including the largest unit, the Wrangell–Saint Elias Wilderness.

History

The system built on earlier administrative protections such as the Gila Wilderness of 1924, the world's first designated wilderness area. Conservationists including Aldo Leopold, Bob Marshall and Howard Zahniser campaigned for decades for statutory protection; Zahniser drafted the Wilderness Act itself.
