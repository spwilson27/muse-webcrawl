The Tsodilo Hills are a group of quartzite hills rising abruptly from the flat Kalahari in north-western Botswana, near the Okavango Panhandle. They contain one of the highest concentrations of rock art in the world — more than 4,500 individual paintings at some 400 sites — and were inscribed as Botswana's first UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001, sometimes called the "Louvre of the Desert".

The hills

Local tradition personifies the four main hills as a family: the "Male" (the highest, and Botswana's highest point at about 1,489 metres), the "Female", the "Child", and an unnamed grandchild knoll. Archaeology shows the area has been occupied intermittently for as much as 100,000 years, with evidence of mining of specularite (a glittering hematite used as pigment and cosmetic), fishing, trade goods such as glass beads and cowrie shells, and very early ritual activity — including, at Rhino Cave, what has been argued to be one of the world's oldest ritual sites, associated with a carved python-like rock.

Rock art

The paintings, executed mostly in red pigment (with some white), depict wild and domestic animals — eland, rhino, giraffe, cattle — as well as geometric motifs, handprints and human figures. They are attributed to San (Bushman) artists, with some panels credited to later Bantu-speaking pastoralists. The hills remain sacred to the local Hambukushu and San communities, who regard them as a resting place of ancestral spirits and the site of creation.
