Radio-frequency identification

A passive RFID tag showing its antenna coil and chip.

Radio-frequency identification (RFID) uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. An RFID system consists of a tiny radio transponder called an RFID tag, a radio receiver, and a transmitter. When triggered by an electromagnetic interrogation pulse from a nearby RFID reader device, the tag transmits digital data — usually an identifying inventory number — back to the reader. This number can be used to track inventory goods.

Unlike a barcode, the tag does not need to be within the line of sight of the reader, so it may be embedded in the tracked object. Passive tags are powered by energy from the reader's radio waves, while active tags have a local power source (such as a battery) and may operate hundreds of meters from the reader.

Uses

RFID is used in logistics and inventory management, retail theft prevention and checkout, livestock and pet identification (implanted microchips), access control badges, electronic toll collection, passports, library systems, and contactless smart cards. Implantable RFID chips in humans have also been marketed, raising privacy debates. Standards are developed by ISO and by GS1 (EPCglobal), including the widely used EPC Gen2 (ISO 18000-63) UHF standard.

Privacy and security

Because tags can be read silently at a distance, RFID has raised concerns about tracking of people and goods without consent. Countermeasures include kill commands, blocker tags, shielding (Faraday cages in passport covers), and cryptographic authentication in newer chip designs.
