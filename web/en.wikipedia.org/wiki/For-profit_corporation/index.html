For-profit corporation

A for-profit corporation is a corporation whose primary goal is to earn income and distribute earnings to the corporation's shareholders in the form of dividends or retained value. It stands in contrast to a nonprofit corporation, which is organized for purposes other than generating profit and is prohibited from distributing earnings to its members or directors.

Characteristics

For-profit corporations issue stock to shareholders, who elect a board of directors to govern the enterprise. The corporation is a separate legal entity from its owners, providing limited liability: shareholders are generally not personally liable for the corporation's debts or obligations beyond their investment. Profits may be distributed as dividends, reinvested in the business, or held as retained earnings.

Types

In the United States, for-profit corporations include the C corporation, taxed separately from its owners, and the S corporation, a pass-through entity meeting specific eligibility requirements. Related forms include the limited liability company (LLC), which combines limited liability with pass-through taxation, and the benefit corporation, a form adopted in many states that permits directors to consider social and environmental goals alongside profit.
