Boxcar

A boxcar is the North American (AAR) and South Australian Railways term for a railroad car that is enclosed and generally used to carry freight. The boxcar, while not the simplest freight car design, is considered one of the most versatile because it can carry most loads. Boxcars have side sliding doors of varying size and operation, and some include end doors and adjustable bulkheads.

Similar covered freight cars outside North America are covered goods wagons and, depending on the region, are called goods vans, Habbins, covered goods coaches, or tankers.

Development

In North America, the boxcar was one of the earliest freight car designs. Open and closed freight cars both evolved from the wooden barrels and crates used for shipping goods, and early railroads used simple four-wheel cars. During the 19th century, the all-wood boxcar was progressively replaced by steel designs. Most boxcars measure 40 to 50 feet (12 to 15 m) long, though in modern freight service the 50-foot boxcar predominates.

Uses

Boxcars can carry most kinds of freight, including packaged goods, paper products, canned food, and building materials. Because they offer complete weather protection and security, they have long been the general-service freight car. Refrigerated boxcars, often called reefers, carry perishable goods, while insulated boxcars carry goods that need temperature moderation but not active refrigeration.
