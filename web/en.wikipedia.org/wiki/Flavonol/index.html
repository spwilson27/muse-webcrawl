Flavonol

Flavonols are a class of flavonoids that have the 3-hydroxyflavone backbone (IUPAC name: 3-hydroxy-2-phenylchromen-4-one). Their diversity stems from the different positions of the phenolic –OH groups. They should not be confused with flavanols (with an "a"), another class of flavonoids that includes catechins.

Flavonols are present in a wide variety of fruits and vegetables. In Western populations, estimated daily intake is in the range of 20–50 mg per day. Individual intake varies depending on the type of diet consumed.

Occurrence

The best-known flavonols are quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin, and isorhamnetin. Rich food sources include onions, kale, broccoli, apples, berries, leeks, capers, and tea. In plants, flavonols accumulate in leaves and skins, where they absorb ultraviolet radiation and act as a sunscreen, and they contribute to the color of flowers. Most flavonols in foods occur as glycosides — sugar-bound forms such as rutin (quercetin-3-O-rutinoside).

Biosynthesis

Flavonols are synthesized in plants through the phenylpropanoid pathway. The enzyme flavonol synthase converts dihydroflavonols to flavonols; chalcone synthase and chalcone isomerase act earlier in the pathway to produce the flavanone precursors shared with other flavonoid classes.

Health research

Flavonols are potent antioxidants in vitro, but their bioavailability after ingestion is limited, and they are extensively metabolized. Epidemiological and clinical research has examined associations between flavonol intake and cardiovascular health, cognition, and other outcomes, but evidence remains preliminary and health claims are not generally authorized.
