Poison ivy — "leaves of three, let it be" — owes its rash to urushiol.

Urushiol is an oily mixture of organic compounds with allergenic properties found in plants of the family Anacardiaceae, especially poison ivy, poison oak and poison sumac, as well as in the sap of the lacquer tree, cashew nut shells, mango skin and ginkgo fruit pulp. It is responsible for the itching, blistering rash known as urushiol-induced contact dermatitis.

Chemistry

Urushiols are 1,2-dihydroxybenzenes (catechols) bearing a long hydrocarbon side chain of 15 or 17 carbon atoms, saturated or with one to three double bonds; the longer and more unsaturated the chain, the stronger the allergen. On exposure to air, urushiol oxidises and polymerises into a black lacquer — the very property exploited for millennia in Japanese and Chinese urushi lacquerware, from which the compound takes its name.

Mechanism and symptoms

Urushiol penetrates the skin and oxidises to a quinone that binds covalently to skin proteins; the immune system treats the modified proteins as foreign, provoking a delayed (type IV) T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity in sensitised people — an estimated 50–75% of adults. Symptoms begin 12–72 hours after contact: redness, swelling, intensely itchy blisters in streaks where the plant brushed the skin. The fluid in blisters does not spread the rash; only unwashed oil on skin, clothing, tools or pet fur does.

Treatment

Washing promptly with soap and cool water (or a solvent cleanser) can remove unbound oil. Once the rash appears, treatment is symptomatic: calamine, antihistamines and topical or, in severe cases, oral corticosteroids. Most cases resolve in one to three weeks.
