Tungusic peoples are a group of ethnic groups native to Siberia, the Russian Far East and Manchuria in Northeast Asia who speak Tungusic languages. Historically, Tungusic-speaking peoples ranged across a vast territory from the Yenisei River in the west to the Pacific coast, and from the Arctic Ocean south into what is now northeastern China.

History

The ancestors of the Tungusic peoples are associated with Neolithic cultures of the Lake Baikal and Amur River regions. In recorded history, Tungusic groups founded several states that ruled parts of northern China: the Jurchen Jin dynasty (1115–1234) and, later, the Manchu-led Qing dynasty (1644–1912), which ruled all of China. The Manchu language, once the language of the Qing court, is today critically endangered as most ethnic Manchu speak Mandarin.

Peoples

The largest Tungusic groups by population are the Manchu of China. The Evenki — historically called "Tungus", the origin of the name Tungusic — are spread across an enormous area of Siberia and are traditionally reindeer herders, hunters and fishers. Other groups include the Evens of the Russian northeast, the Nanai (Hezhen) of the lower Amur, and the Udege, Oroch, Ulch and Negidal peoples of the Russian Far East. Many of these languages and cultures are now endangered.

Culture

Shamanism was central to traditional Tungusic religion; the English word "shaman" itself derives from the Evenki word šamān. Traditional livelihoods centred on hunting, fishing and reindeer herding, with dwellings such as the conical chum tent. Tungusic peoples figure prominently in the history of Northeast Asia, from the Khitan and Jurchen states to the Manchu conquest of China.
