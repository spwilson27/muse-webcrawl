Propaganda in North Korea

Propaganda in North Korea is pervasive and centrally controlled by the state. Much of the propaganda produced by the Democratic People's Republic of Korea is centred on the ruling Kim family, the ideology of Juche (self-reliance), and hostility toward the United States and South Korea. Propaganda is produced and disseminated by the Propaganda and Agitation Department of the Workers' Party of Korea and permeates education, media, art, and public space.

A propaganda mural in Pyongyang

Themes

Cult of personality

The dominant theme is the veneration of Kim Il-sung, Kim Jong-il, and Kim Jong-un. The leaders are portrayed as infallible, benevolent, and semi-divine: officially sanctioned biographies attribute supernatural circumstances to their births and credit them with extraordinary feats of learning, athletics, and statecraft. Portraits of the first two leaders are mandatory in homes, offices, schools, and public buildings, and citizens wear badges bearing their images.

Anti-Americanism and militarism

The United States is depicted as an imperialist aggressor responsible for the division of Korea and the devastation of the Korean War. The songun ("military-first") policy elevated the armed forces in propaganda, presenting the army as the guardian of the nation. South Korea is portrayed alternately as a puppet state of the United States and as a poorer, colonised half of the nation awaiting liberation.

Purity and self-reliance

North Korean propaganda stresses the racial and cultural purity of the Korean people (the ideology associated with the concept of the nation as a single bloodline) and the virtue of self-reliance under Juche, framing economic hardship as a noble struggle against hostile foreign forces.

Media and methods

All media are state-owned: newspapers such as Rodong Sinmun, the Korean Central News Agency, state television (KCTV), and radio broadcast a single authorised narrative. Radios and televisions sold in the country are fixed to domestic frequencies. Posters, monuments, mosaics, and mass games — synchronised spectacles involving tens of thousands of performers — reinforce the official message visually. Propaganda aimed abroad includes broadcasts across the Demilitarised Zone, leaflet campaigns, the film industry centred on Shin Sang-ok after his controversial 1978 arrival in the North, and, in the internet era, state-run websites and social media accounts.

Assessment

Defectors frequently report that many citizens privately discount official claims, especially since the famine of the 1990s and the growth of smuggled foreign media, yet the propaganda apparatus remains a central instrument of social control and regime legitimacy.
