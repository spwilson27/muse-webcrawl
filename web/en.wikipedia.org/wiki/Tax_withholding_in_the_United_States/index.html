Tax withholding in the United States

Federal, state and payroll taxes withheld from an employee's wages

Tax withholding in the United States is the system by which employers and other payers deduct federal, state and local taxes from payments — principally wages — and remit the amounts to the government on the recipient's behalf. Withheld amounts are credited against the individual's final tax liability when they file an annual return; excess withholding is refunded, and under-withholding may result in a balance due and penalties. Withholding is the mechanism by which most US income tax is collected on a pay-as-you-earn basis.

History

Although withholding was briefly used during the Civil War era and again in 1913–1916, the modern system dates from the Current Tax Payment Act of 1943, enacted during the Second World War as the income tax was expanded to most workers. The economist Milton Friedman, then working at the Treasury, was among those who helped design the system. Withholding transformed the income tax into a mass tax by collecting it gradually from each paycheck.

Federal withholding on wages

Employers withhold federal income tax based on the employee's Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate) and IRS withholding tables. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act prompted a redesigned Form W-4 from 2020, which eliminated withholding allowances in favour of adjustments for multiple jobs, dependents and other income. Employers also withhold Social Security and Medicare taxes under FICA — 6.2% for Social Security up to an annual wage base and 1.45% for Medicare, with an additional 0.9% Medicare tax on high earners — and pay matching employer shares. Withheld taxes are deposited with the Treasury on schedules depending on employer size, and reported annually on Form W-2 to employees and the Social Security Administration.

Other withholding

Withholding also applies to other payments: pension and annuity payments, gambling winnings, and certain payments to foreign persons (generally at 30% unless reduced by treaty). "Backup withholding" (currently 24%) applies when a payee fails to furnish a correct taxpayer identification number or has underreported interest and dividends. Non-employees and those with significant non-wage income may instead pay estimated taxes quarterly using Form 1040-ES.

State and local withholding

Most states with an income tax operate parallel withholding systems with their own forms, and some cities (such as New York City) impose local income tax withholding as well.
