Giving-up density

The giving-up density (GUD) is a concept in behavioral ecology and optimal foraging theory that measures the density of resources remaining in a patch at the moment a forager decides to leave it. Introduced by Joel S. Brown in 1988, the giving-up density provides an indirect way to quantify the forager's perceived costs and benefits of foraging in a patch, including energetic costs, predation risk, and missed opportunities elsewhere.

Theory

According to Brown's patch-use model, a forager should remain in a depleting patch while its harvest rate exceeds the sum of its metabolic cost (C), predation risk cost (P), and missed opportunity cost (MOC). When the harvest rate drops to the point where it just balances these costs, the forager quits the patch. The resource density remaining at that point is the giving-up density:

H = C + P + MOC

A higher GUD indicates that the forager perceived the patch as costly — for example because of greater predation risk — while a lower GUD indicates the forager was willing to exploit the patch more thoroughly.

Experimental use

GUDs are commonly measured experimentally using artificial food patches, such as trays filled with a non-edible substrate (e.g., sand or gravel) mixed with a known initial density of seeds or other food items. After a period of foraging, the remaining food is collected and weighed; the amount remaining is the giving-up density. Researchers manipulate conditions — such as adding predator cues, altering illumination (moonlight), or varying substrate — to test how each factor affects perceived foraging costs.

Studies using GUDs have been conducted on rodents such as deer mice, kangaroo rats and gerbils, as well as birds and other taxa. The approach has been widely applied in research on the ecology of fear and non-consumptive predator effects.
