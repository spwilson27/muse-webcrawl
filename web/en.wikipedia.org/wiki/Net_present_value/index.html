Net present value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, the net present value (NPV) or net present worth (NPW) applies to a series of cash flows occurring at different times. The present value of a cash flow depends on the interval of time between now and the cash flow. It also depends on the annual effective discount rate. NPV accounts for the time value of money: a unit of currency today is worth more than the same unit in the future because it can be invested.

Formula

For a series of cash flows Ct occurring at times t = 0, 1, 2, ..., n, with discount rate r:

NPV = Σt=0..n Ct / (1 + r)t

Cash inflows are positive and outflows (such as the initial investment at t = 0) negative.

Decision rule

For an independent project, the standard rule is: accept if NPV > 0 (the project is expected to add value), reject if NPV < 0, and be indifferent at NPV = 0. For mutually exclusive projects, the one with the highest positive NPV is preferred. NPV is widely regarded by financial economists as theoretically superior to measures such as the internal rate of return (IRR) because it directly measures value added in currency units and assumes reinvestment at the discount rate rather than at the IRR itself.

Interpretation and limitations

NPV is highly sensitive to the chosen discount rate, which should reflect the opportunity cost of capital and the risk of the cash flows. It does not directly account for flexibility (real options), may favour large projects over small ones in absolute terms, and relies on forecasts that may be uncertain. It is nonetheless the central tool of capital budgeting and discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation.
