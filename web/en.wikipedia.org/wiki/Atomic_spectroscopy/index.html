Atomic spectroscopy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atomic spectroscopy is the study of the electromagnetic radiation absorbed and emitted by atoms. Because each chemical element has a characteristic spectrum of spectral lines — determined by the allowed transitions of its electrons between energy levels — atomic spectroscopy is widely used for determining the elemental composition of a sample, in both qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Principles

When energy is supplied to a sample, its atoms are promoted to excited states; as they return to lower states they emit light at discrete wavelengths. Conversely, ground-state atoms absorb light at the same characteristic wavelengths. Before measurement, the sample is usually atomized — converted into free atoms — using a flame, a graphite furnace, or an inductively coupled plasma.

Techniques

  • Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS): measures the absorption of light by ground-state atoms, typically using a hollow-cathode lamp emitting the element's characteristic lines and a flame or graphite furnace atomizer.
  • Atomic emission spectroscopy (AES): measures light emitted by excited atoms; modern instruments often use an inductively coupled plasma (ICP-AES, also called ICP-OES), which can determine many elements simultaneously.
  • Atomic fluorescence spectroscopy (AFS): measures fluorescence emitted after atoms are excited by a light source.
  • X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and related X-ray methods probe inner-shell transitions.

Related mass-spectrometric methods, especially inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), are often grouped with atomic spectroscopy in elemental analysis laboratories.

Applications

Atomic spectroscopy is used in environmental monitoring (trace metals in water and soil), clinical and food analysis, metallurgy and mining, petrochemicals, and pharmaceutical quality control. Detection limits can reach parts per billion or lower, depending on the technique.

History

The field grew from the nineteenth-century spectral studies of Kirchhoff and Bunsen, who showed that elements emit characteristic line spectra — work that led to the discovery of new elements such as caesium and rubidium and laid foundations for quantum theory through the analysis of the hydrogen spectrum.
