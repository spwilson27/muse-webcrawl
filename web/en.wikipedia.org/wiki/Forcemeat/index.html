Forcemeat

Forcemeat (derived from the French farce, "stuffing") is a mixture of ground, lean meat mixed with fat by grinding, sieving, or puréeing the ingredients. The result may either be smooth or coarsely textured. Forcemeats are used in the production of numerous items found in charcuterie, including quenelles, sausages, pâtés, terrines, roulades, and galantines. Forcemeats are usually produced from raw meat, except in the case of a gratin forcemeat. Meats commonly used include pork, fish (pike, trout, or salmon), seafood, game meats (venison, boar, or rabbit), poultry, veal, and pork fatback. Pork fat is preferable because it has a neutral flavor.

Types

Classical French charcuterie recognizes several types of forcemeat. Straight forcemeat combines ground pork and pork fat with a dominant meat in equal parts. Country-style forcemeat is coarser, containing pork liver and garnishes. Gratin forcemeat includes a portion of browned or cooked meat, and mousseline forcemeat is the lightest, incorporating cream and producing a delicate texture suited to quenelles.

Preparation

The ingredients must be kept chilled throughout preparation so the emulsion of meat and fat does not break. Meat is ground progressively finer, seasoned, and blended with fat, ice or cream, and sometimes a panada (a starch paste) as a binder. Eggs and egg whites may be added to stabilize quenelles and mousselines.
