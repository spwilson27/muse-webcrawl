Gemini Planet Imager

[Image placeholder: GPI instrument]

The Gemini Planet Imager (GPI) is a high-contrast imaging instrument built for the Gemini South Telescope in Chile. It is designed specifically for directly imaging and spectroscopically characterising extrasolar planets — a difficult task because a planet is roughly a million to a billion times fainter than its host star and sits very close to it on the sky.

GPI saw first light in November 2013 and began its main science campaign, the Gemini Planet Imager Exoplanet Survey (GPIES), in 2014. The instrument was constructed by a consortium of institutions in the United States and Canada, including the Kavli Institute, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, and the Herzberg Institute of Astrophysics.

Design

GPI combines several technologies. An extreme adaptive optics system, using a micro-electro-mechanical-systems deformable mirror with roughly 4,000 actuators and a computationally powerful real-time controller, corrects atmospheric turbulence thousands of times per second. A coronagraph masks the star's light using an apodised pupil Lyot design, and an integral field spectrograph records a spectrum at every point in the image, allowing astronomers to distinguish planets from background stars and to measure their atmospheric composition. A precision infrared wavefront sensor tracks residual errors.

Results

During GPIES, the instrument surveyed hundreds of young, nearby stars. Its notable results include the discovery of 51 Eridani b, a young Jupiter-like exoplanet notable for strong methane absorption, as well as detailed imaging of debris disks and the HR 8799 planetary system. GPI's successor concept, GPI 2.0, has been planned for installation at Gemini North in Hawaii, with upgraded adaptive optics for improved performance.
