Adjusted gross income

In the United States income tax system, adjusted gross income (AGI) is an individual's total gross income minus specific deductions. It is used to calculate taxable income, which is AGI minus allowances for personal exemptions and itemized deductions. AGI is a key quantity because many deductions, credits, and eligibility thresholds in the tax code — and in programs outside taxation altogether — are defined by reference to it.

Calculation

Gross income includes wages, salaries, tips, business income, capital gains, dividends, interest, rents, royalties, alimony received (under older rules), pensions, and most other income. From this total, taxpayers subtract "above-the-line" adjustments specified in section 62 of the Internal Revenue Code, which have included:

  • contributions to traditional IRAs and certain retirement plans (subject to limits);
  • student loan interest (up to a capped amount);
  • health savings account contributions;
  • one-half of self-employment tax, and self-employed health insurance premiums;
  • educator expenses, moving expenses for members of the armed forces, and alimony paid under pre-2019 agreements;
  • certain business expenses of reservists, performing artists, and fee-basis government officials.

Role in the tax system

AGI determines or limits numerous provisions: the deductibility of medical expenses (only above a percentage-of-AGI floor), charitable contribution ceilings, passive activity loss rules, and the phase-outs of many credits such as the child tax credit and education credits. A related concept, modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), adds back certain excluded items (such as foreign earned income) and is used for eligibility for programs including Medicare premium tiers, premium tax credits under the Affordable Care Act, and Roth IRA contribution limits. State governments and financial aid formulas also frequently key amounts to federal AGI.
