Shetland

Shetland, also called the Shetland Islands, is an archipelago in Scotland lying between Orkney, the Faroe Islands, and Norway. It is the northernmost region of the United Kingdom. The islands lie about 80 km (50 mi) to the northeast of Orkney, 170 km (110 mi) from the Scottish mainland, and 300 km (190 mi) west of Norway. They form part of the border between the Atlantic Ocean to the west and the North Sea to the east. The total area is 1,466 km2 (566 sq mi), and the population totalled 22,986 in 2022. The islands comprise the Shetland Islands Council local authority area, administered by Shetland Islands Council, with its administrative centre and only burgh in Lerwick.

Geography

The archipelago consists of more than 100 islands, of which 16 are inhabited. The largest, known simply as Mainland, is the third-largest Scottish island. Other large islands include Yell, Unst, Whalsay, Bressay, Fetlar, and Fair Isle, which lies roughly halfway between Shetland and Orkney. The landscape is rugged and largely treeless, shaped by glaciation, with a deeply indented coastline of voes (inlets) and geos (narrow clefts in cliffs). The climate is oceanic, mild for its latitude owing to the Gulf Stream, but windy; Shetland holds the British record for the strongest wind gust recorded at low altitude.

History

Humans have lived in Shetland since the Mesolithic period. The islands are rich in prehistoric remains, including the exceptionally preserved Iron Age brochs such as Mousa Broch and the settlement of Jarlshof, occupied from the Bronze Age through the Viking era. Norse settlers arrived in the late 8th and 9th centuries, and Shetland became part of the Norse earldom of Orkney under Norway. In 1469 the islands were pledged by Christian I of Denmark and Norway to Scotland as security for the dowry of his daughter Margaret, betrothed to James III of Scotland; the dowry was never paid, and Shetland was formally annexed to the Scottish crown in 1472. Norse influence survives in place names, the former Norn language, and events such as the Up Helly Aa fire festivals.

Economy and culture

Fishing has long been central to the Shetland economy, and the islands land more fish than England, Wales, and Northern Ireland combined by some measures. The discovery of North Sea oil in the 1970s brought the Sullom Voe oil terminal, then one of the largest in Europe, and a substantial sovereign-wealth-like charitable trust funded by oil revenues. Crofting, salmon and mussel aquaculture, knitwear (including Fair Isle patterns), and tourism are also significant. Shetland has a distinctive culture with strong Norse heritage; the dialect derives from Scots with heavy Norse substrate influence, and the Shetland pony and Shetland sheepdog are famous native breeds.
