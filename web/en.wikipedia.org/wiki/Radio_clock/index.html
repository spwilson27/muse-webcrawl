Radio clock

A radio clock or radio-controlled clock is a clock or watch that automatically synchronises its display to a time standard by receiving radio time signals broadcast by dedicated transmitters, or in some designs by signals from GPS satellites. Such clocks keep essentially perfect time without manual setting and automatically adjust for daylight saving time.

Terrestrial time signals

Several longwave and shortwave services broadcast coded time signals: WWVB in the United States (from Fort Collins, Colorado), DCF77 in Germany (near Frankfurt), MSF in the United Kingdom, and JJY in Japan. A typical signal encodes the current date and time once per minute using amplitude or phase modulation of the carrier. Receivers in clocks decode the signal — usually overnight, when reception is best — and correct the quartz movement.

History

Radio time signals date to the early 20th century, when stations such as the Eiffel Tower transmitter broadcast time for navigators and jewellers. Consumer radio-controlled clocks became common in the 1980s and 1990s as receiver chips became cheap; Junghans produced the first commercially successful radio-controlled wristwatch in 1990.

GPS clocks

Clocks and watches that synchronise using GPS signals obtain time from the satellite constellation, which carries atomic-clock time, allowing them to work anywhere in the world rather than only within range of a terrestrial transmitter.
