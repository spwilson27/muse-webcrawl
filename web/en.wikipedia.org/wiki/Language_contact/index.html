Language contact

Language contact occurs when speakers of different languages interact and their languages influence each other. Contact is one of the principal engines of language change: it produces loanwords, calques, structural borrowing, code-switching, bilingualism, and in extreme cases entirely new languages such as pidgins, creoles, and mixed languages. The modern field is often traced to Uriel Weinreich's Languages in Contact (1953) and was systematised by Sarah Thomason and Terrence Kaufman's Language Contact, Creolization, and Genetic Linguistics (1988).

Mechanisms of influence

The most common contact effect is lexical borrowing: English absorbed thousands of French words after the Norman Conquest, and languages worldwide now borrow English technical and popular vocabulary. Beyond words, languages can borrow sounds (the click consonants of some Bantu languages entered from Khoisan), grammatical patterns (the Balkan sprachbund, where Greek, Albanian, Romanian, and South Slavic languages converged structurally), and discourse habits. Thomason and Kaufman proposed a borrowing scale: casual contact transfers only words, while intense, prolonged contact with widespread bilingualism can transfer structure — and under language shift, speakers may impose features of their first language on the target language, producing substrate effects such as those of Irish on Irish English.

Contact languages

When groups without a common language need to communicate — historically in trade or on plantations — a simplified pidgin may arise, as with Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea or the Mediterranean Lingua Franca. If children grow up speaking a pidgin, it expands into a full creole language. Rare mixed languages, such as Michif (French nouns with Cree verbs) or Media Lengua, combine the grammars and vocabularies of two languages in systematic ways. Koines arise when mutually intelligible dialects level their differences in new settlements.

Shift, maintenance, and death

Contact also drives language shift, in which a community abandons its language over generations, often under economic or political pressure; most of the world's endangered languages are being lost this way. Outcomes are determined more by social factors — prestige, demography, intermarriage, state policy — than by the structures of the languages involved, a central claim of modern contact linguistics.
