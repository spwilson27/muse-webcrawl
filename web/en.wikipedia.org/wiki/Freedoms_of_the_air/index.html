Freedoms of the air

Freedoms of the air
OriginChicago Convention, 1944
NumberNine recognised freedoms
Administered viaBilateral and multilateral air service agreements

The freedoms of the air, also called the nine freedoms, are a set of commercial aviation rights granting a country's airlines the privilege to enter and land in another country's airspace and territory. They were formulated at the Chicago Convention on International Civil Aviation of 1944 and are exchanged between states through bilateral and multilateral air service agreements, including "open skies" treaties.

The freedoms

#FreedomDescription
1stOverflightFly across a foreign country without landing.
2ndTechnical stopLand in a foreign country for non-traffic purposes, e.g. refuelling or maintenance.
3rdSet-downCarry passengers or cargo from the home country to a foreign country.
4thPick-upCarry passengers or cargo from a foreign country back to the home country.
5thBeyond rightsCarry traffic between two foreign countries on a flight originating or ending in the home country.
6thConnecting via homeCarry traffic between two foreign countries via the home country (a combination of the 3rd and 4th).
7thStandalone foreign serviceCarry traffic between two foreign countries without the flight touching the home country.
8thConsecutive cabotageCarry domestic traffic within a foreign country on a flight that began or will end in the home country.
9thStandalone cabotageCarry domestic traffic wholly within a foreign country.

Application

The first two freedoms — transit rights — are granted to most signatories under the International Air Services Transit Agreement. Traffic rights (third through ninth) must usually be negotiated. Cabotage freedoms are rarely granted; a notable exception is within the European Union, where airlines of member states enjoy full cabotage under the single aviation market. Fifth-freedom routes are common in long-haul networks — for example, airlines carrying passengers between two foreign cities as one leg of a journey to or from their home base. The sixth freedom underpins the hub-and-spoke models of Gulf and other connecting-hub carriers.
