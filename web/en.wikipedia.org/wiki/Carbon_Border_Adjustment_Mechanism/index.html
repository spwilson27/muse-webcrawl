The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is a policy instrument of the European Union designed to put a fair price on the carbon emitted during the production of carbon-intensive goods entering the EU. It forms part of the European Green Deal and the "Fit for 55" package, and is intended to prevent carbon leakage — the relocation of production to countries with weaker climate policies — while encouraging cleaner industrial production outside the EU.

Under the mechanism, EU importers of covered goods must purchase CBAM certificates corresponding to the embedded greenhouse gas emissions of their imports. The certificate price is linked to the weekly average auction price of allowances under the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS). Importers may deduct any carbon price already paid in the country of origin.

Background

As the EU raised its own climate ambition and the price of EU ETS allowances rose, concerns grew that European producers would be undercut by imports from jurisdictions without equivalent carbon pricing. CBAM was proposed by the European Commission in July 2021 and adopted in 2023 after negotiations between the European Parliament and the Council.

Implementation

A transitional phase began on 1 October 2023, during which importers are required only to report the embedded emissions of covered goods, without financial adjustment. The definitive regime, in which certificates must be surrendered, begins on 1 January 2026, phased in as the free allocation of EU ETS allowances to domestic industry is phased out.

Reactions

Several trading partners have criticised CBAM as protectionist or as shifting the burden of decarbonisation onto developing economies, while others have been prompted to develop their own carbon pricing systems. The United Kingdom has announced plans for its own CBAM.
