Triiodothyronine

Triiodothyronine (T3)
FormulaC15H12I3NO4
ClassThyroid hormone
SourceThyroid gland; peripheral conversion from thyroxine (T4)
Drug formLiothyronine

Triiodothyronine, also known as T3, is a thyroid hormone. It affects almost every physiological process in the body, including growth and development, metabolism, body temperature, and heart rate. T3 is the more biologically active of the two principal thyroid hormones; its production in the body is controlled by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which is released by the anterior pituitary gland, itself regulated by thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus.


Triiodothyronine (T3)

Production and metabolism

The thyroid gland secretes mainly thyroxine (T4), a prohormone containing four iodine atoms. Most circulating T3 is produced outside the thyroid by deiodinase enzymes in the liver, kidneys and other tissues, which remove one iodine atom from T4. T3 circulates bound to plasma proteins such as thyroxine-binding globulin, with only a small free fraction active. It acts by binding to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, which regulate gene transcription.

Clinical significance

Measurement of free T3, together with T4 and TSH, is used in diagnosing hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. In a minority of hyperthyroid patients only T3 is elevated ("T3 toxicosis"). As a drug, T3 is sold as liothyronine and is used in some cases of hypothyroidism, usually in combination with levothyroxine; its shorter half-life makes dosing less stable than T4 alone. Reverse T3, an inactive isomer, is produced from T4 in illness and fasting.
