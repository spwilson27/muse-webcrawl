Patina

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Patina (Italian for "film" or "tinge") is a thin layer that variously forms on the surface of copper, brass, bronze and similar metals (tarnish produced by oxidation or other chemical processes), or certain stones, and wooden furniture (sheen produced by age, wear and polishing), or any similar acquired change of a surface through age and exposure.

Chemistry

On metal, patina is a coating of various chemical compounds such as oxides, carbonates, sulfides or sulfates formed on the surface during exposure to atmospheric elements. The best-known example is the green verdigris that forms on copper and bronze: copper first oxidises to cuprite and then, in the presence of carbon dioxide and water, to basic copper carbonates such as malachite; sulfur compounds in polluted air produce dark brochantite. The green surface of the Statue of Liberty is a patina of this kind that formed within a few decades of its dedication in 1886.

Applications

Artists and metalworkers often deliberately create patinas with chemical treatments — liver of sulfur, ferric nitrate, ammonia fumes and other reagents produce greens, blues, browns and blacks on bronze sculpture. A stable patina protects the metal beneath from further corrosion, and in art and antiques it is usually valued as evidence of age; removing it by polishing can drastically reduce an object's value. The term is used metaphorically for any accretion of character with time, as in "a patina of respectability".
