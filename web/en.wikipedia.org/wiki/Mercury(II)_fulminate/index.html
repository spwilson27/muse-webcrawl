Mercury(II) fulminate, or fulminate of mercury, is a primary explosive — a compound too sensitive to be used as a main charge but ideal as an initiator. It is the mercury salt of fulminic acid, with the chemical formula Hg(CNO)₂. Mercury fulminate is highly sensitive to friction, heat and shock, and is mainly used as a trigger for other, more stable explosives in blasting caps and percussion caps.

History

Fulminating compounds were described by the alchemist Cornelius Drebbel and others in the 17th century, and Edward Howard's investigations around 1800 established the properties of mercury fulminate. In 1807 the Scottish minister Alexander John Forsyth patented the use of fulminates to ignite gunpowder, leading to the percussion lock and, within a few decades, the self-contained percussion cap and the metallic cartridge. Alfred Nobel's blasting cap (1865) used mercury fulminate to detonate dynamite reliably.

Preparation and properties

It is prepared by dissolving mercury in nitric acid and adding the solution to ethanol. The product forms grey-to-white crystals. Because of its toxicity and the environmental damage caused by mercury, and because it degrades in storage, mercury fulminate has been largely replaced in detonators by lead azide, lead styphnate and diazodinitrophenol (DDNP).

In popular culture

Mercury fulminate was famously depicted in the television series Breaking Bad, in which crystals of it are thrown to the floor to produce an explosion; the portrayal exaggerated the power of a small quantity.
