Aspiration pneumonia is a type of pneumonia caused by the entry of food, saliva, liquid or vomit from the mouth or stomach into the airways and lungs, carrying bacteria with it. It is related to, but distinct from, aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson's syndrome), the chemical lung injury caused by aspirating sterile acidic gastric contents.

Risk factors and causes

Healthy people frequently micro-aspirate without harm, protected by coughing and normal immunity. Risk rises when swallowing is impaired (dysphagia after stroke, in Parkinson's disease or dementia), consciousness is reduced (sedation, alcohol, seizures, anaesthesia), or reflux and vomiting occur. Poor oral hygiene increases the bacterial load of aspirated secretions. The right main bronchus is wider and more vertical than the left, so aspirated material most often reaches the right lower lobe or, in recumbent patients, the posterior segments of the upper lobes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Symptoms include cough, fever, breathlessness and purulent sputum developing over days, sometimes in frailer patients with few classic signs. Chest imaging shows infiltrates in dependent lung segments, and the diagnosis rests on a compatible history of aspiration risk. Treatment involves antibiotics covering oral anaerobes and Gram-negative organisms, supportive care, nil-by-mouth assessment, speech-and-language swallow evaluation, oral hygiene and positioning measures to prevent recurrence.

Prognosis

Aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of death in elderly people and in those with advanced neurological disease, and it accounts for a substantial share of community- and hospital-acquired pneumonias.
