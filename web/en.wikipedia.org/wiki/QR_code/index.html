A QR code (short for quick-response code) is a type of two-dimensional matrix barcode invented in 1994 by the Japanese company Denso Wave for labelling automobile parts. It features black squares on a white background with fiducial markers, and can be read by imaging devices such as smartphone cameras. Reed–Solomon error correction allows codes to be scanned even when partially damaged or obscured.

Design

A QR code consists of black squares arranged in a grid on a white background, which can be read in both directions (hence "quick response"). Three large squares in the corners serve as finder patterns, with a smaller alignment pattern adjusting for perspective. Data is encoded in four standard modes: numeric, alphanumeric, byte/binary, and kanji. The largest version (Version 40) has 177 × 177 modules and can hold 7,089 numeric characters.

History and adoption

Denso Wave, a Toyota subsidiary, developed the code to track vehicles and parts during manufacturing; it chose not to exercise its patent rights, aiding widespread adoption. The QR code became an ISO standard in 2000. Initially popular in Japan, it spread worldwide with smartphones, and exploded in China through mobile payment platforms such as WeChat Pay and Alipay. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated its use for contactless menus, check-ins, and vaccination records. QR codes now appear in payments, ticketing, product packaging, authentication, and advertising.

Security

Because a QR code is opaque to the human eye, malicious codes can direct users to phishing sites or trigger unwanted actions, a practice sometimes called "quishing." Users are advised to preview URLs before opening them.
