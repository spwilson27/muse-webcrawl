Krzysztof Eugeniusz Penderecki (23 November 1933 – 29 March 2020) was a Polish composer and conductor. His best-known works include the Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima, the St Luke Passion, the Polish Requiem, and his eight symphonies. Hailed as Poland's greatest living composer in his lifetime, he was one of the most decorated musicians of his era.

Early life and avant-garde period

Penderecki was born in Dębica and studied composition at the Academy of Music in Kraków. He rose to prominence at the 1959 Warsaw Autumn festival, winning all three prizes in a national composition competition. His breakthrough came with the Threnody (1960), written for 52 string instruments and exploiting tone clusters, quarter tones, and unconventional playing techniques — slapping strings, playing behind the bridge — that defined the "sonorist" style of the Polish avant-garde. Works of this period include Anaklasis, Polymorphia, and Fluorescences; several were later used in film soundtracks, including The Exorcist and The Shining.

Later style

With the St Luke Passion (1966), written for the 700th anniversary of Münster Cathedral, Penderecki turned toward sacred music and more traditional forms. From the 1970s his style shifted toward neo-romanticism — longer melodic lines, tonal centres, and genres such as the symphony, concerto, and requiem — a move that divided critics but widened his audience. The Polish Requiem (1984, expanded 1993 and 2005) commemorated key figures and events of Polish history. He also composed operas, including The Devils of Loudun and Paradise Lost.

Conducting and legacy

Penderecki conducted major orchestras worldwide and taught at Yale and in Kraków. He founded the European Centre for Music in Lusławice, near his manor, where he also tended a large arboretum. He won four Grammy Awards and numerous state honours, and died in Kraków on 29 March 2020, aged 86.
