Siddha

In Indian religions, a siddha (Sanskrit: सिद्ध, "accomplished one") is one who has attained siddhi, a state of spiritual perfection and supernatural powers, or in Jainism specifically a liberated soul who has destroyed all karma and attained moksha. The term is used across Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism, with somewhat different meanings in each tradition.

A siddha: a perfected being in Indian religious traditions.

In Jainism

In Jainism, siddha has a precise meaning: a soul that has completely destroyed all karmic matter and attained liberation, residing forever in bliss at the top of the universe in the Siddhashila. Siddhas are formless and are worshipped as the highest exemplars in the Namokar Mantra, where the salutation to the siddhas follows that to the arhats. Having shed all eight types of karma, the siddha possesses infinite knowledge, perception, energy, and bliss.

In Hinduism

In Hindu traditions, a siddha is a perfected master or yogin who has realized the Self and often is credited with siddhis — paranormal powers such as levitation, clairvoyance, or the ability to become minute or immense. The classical list of eight great siddhis (aṣṭa-siddhi) appears in Patañjali's Yoga Sutras tradition. South India's Tamil Siddhar tradition, associated with figures such as Tirumular, combined yoga, alchemy, and medicine; the Siddha system of medicine is named after these masters. In the tantric traditions of the early medieval period, the eighty-four mahasiddhas — eccentric, often antinomian adepts such as Saraha, Tilopa, and Naropa — became central figures in both Hindu tantra and Vajrayana Buddhism.

In Buddhism

In Vajrayana Buddhism, the mahasiddhas are revered as tantric adepts who attained realization outside monastic convention; their songs of realization (doha) and life stories shaped Tibetan Buddhist lineages. The Indian mahasiddha tradition flourished roughly between the 8th and 12th centuries CE.
