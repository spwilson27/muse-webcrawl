Advanced sleep phase disorder
SpecialtySleep medicine, neurology
SymptomsVery early sleep onset and awakening

Advanced sleep phase disorder (ASPD), also known as advanced sleep phase syndrome, is a circadian rhythm sleep disorder in which the major sleep episode is advanced in relation to the desired clock time. People with ASPD fall asleep in the early evening (for example between 6 and 9 p.m.) and wake in the early morning hours (between 2 and 5 a.m.), and find it very difficult to stay awake later or to sleep in, even when they wish to.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Sleep itself is typically normal in quality and duration; the complaint is one of timing — evening sleepiness that interferes with social life, and early-morning awakening that cannot be shifted. Diagnosis is based on history, sleep diaries and sometimes actigraphy; it must be distinguished from depression-related early awakening and from ordinary "morningness" (being a lark) that causes no distress. ASPD becomes more common with age.

Familial advanced sleep phase

A rare inherited form, familial advanced sleep phase syndrome, runs in families as an autosomal dominant trait. It was the first human circadian disorder linked to specific gene mutations: a point mutation in the clock gene PER2 (hPER2 S662G) described in 2001, and later a mutation in casein kinase I delta (CSNK1D). Affected families show a shortened circadian period of about 23.3 hours.

Treatment

Management includes bright-light exposure in the early evening to delay the clock, avoidance of bright light early in the morning, carefully timed low-dose melatonin in the morning (used cautiously), and maintaining consistent schedules.
