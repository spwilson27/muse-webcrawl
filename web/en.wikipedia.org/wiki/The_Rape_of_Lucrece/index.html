The Rape of Lucrece is a narrative poem by William Shakespeare about the legendary Roman noblewoman Lucretia. Published in 1594 and dedicated to Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton, it follows Shakespeare's earlier narrative poem Venus and Adonis (1593). Here "rape" carries its older sense of abduction and violation, following the account in Livy and Ovid.

Summary

Sextus Tarquinius, son of the Roman king, inflamed by Collatine's praise of his wife Lucrece's chastity, visits her house at Collatia and rapes her at knifepoint. Lucrece summons her husband and father, exacts an oath of vengeance, and kills herself. Her death prompts the revolt led by Brutus that overthrows the Tarquin monarchy and founds the Roman Republic.

Style and themes

The poem comprises 265 stanzas of rhyme royal (1,855 lines) and is notable for its extended rhetorical set pieces, in which Lucrece debates Night, Opportunity and Time, and contemplates a painting of the fall of Troy. It explores themes of honour, shame, lust, tyranny and political resistance.
