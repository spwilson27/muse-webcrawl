Historically informed performance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Historically informed performance (also known as period performance, or the authentic performance movement) is an approach to the performance of classical music, which aims to be faithful to the approach, manner and style of the musical era in which a work was originally conceived. It is based on two key aspects: the application of the stylistic and technical aspects of performance, known as performance practice, which were known in the time of composition; and the use of period instruments, which may be reproductions of historical instruments that were in use at the time of the original composition, or sometimes originals.

History

The movement has roots in the early music revival of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including the work of Arnold Dolmetsch, who built reproductions of early instruments and wrote a treatise on seventeenth- and eighteenth-century performance practice (1915), and Wanda Landowska, who championed the harpsichord. After the Second World War the movement gathered momentum, particularly with figures such as Nikolaus Harnoncourt, who founded the Concentus Musicus Wien in 1953, and Gustav Leonhardt in the Netherlands.

During the 1970s and 1980s, specialist period-instrument ensembles proliferated: Christopher Hogwood's Academy of Ancient Music (founded 1973), Trevor Pinnock's English Concert, John Eliot Gardiner's English Baroque Soloists and Monteverdi Choir, and Roger Norrington's London Classical Players, whose recordings of the Beethoven symphonies on period instruments attracted wide attention. The movement's centre of gravity expanded from Renaissance and Baroque music to the Classical and early Romantic repertory, and eventually to composers as late as Brahms, Wagner and Mahler.

Practices

Historically informed performers draw on treatises, tutors, iconography and surviving instruments to reconstruct aspects of historical performance including:

  • Use of period instruments or reproductions: gut strings, natural horns and trumpets, wooden flutes, valveless brass, harpsichords and fortepianos.
  • Historical tuning systems and pitch standards, such as A = 415 Hz for Baroque music rather than the modern A = 440–442 Hz.
  • Temperaments other than equal temperament, such as meantone and well temperaments.
  • Performance conventions: improvised ornamentation, figured-bass realisation (continuo), flexible tempo, and restrained use of vibrato in string playing.
  • Smaller ensembles and choral forces, including one-voice-per-part singing in some repertories.

Criticism and debate

The claims of "authenticity" associated with the movement have been vigorously debated. The musicologist Richard Taruskin argued that what was marketed as historical reconstruction was in fact a distinctly modernist aesthetic, shaped by twentieth-century tastes for clarity, speed and detachment. Others have noted that historical evidence is often ambiguous, that notation underdetermines performance, and that no performance can recover the ears of a past audience. Many performers consequently prefer the term "historically informed" to "authentic", presenting scholarship as a stimulus to fresh interpretation rather than a guarantee of correctness. The movement has also influenced mainstream orchestras, which now commonly adopt lighter articulation, reduced vibrato and smaller string sections in Baroque and Classical repertory even when using modern instruments.
