Muslim conquest of Spain

The Muslim conquest of Spain, also known as the Umayyad conquest of Hispania, was the expansion of the Umayyad Caliphate into the Iberian Peninsula in the early 8th century. Between 711 and 718, Muslim forces—largely Berbers led by Arab commanders—overthrew the Visigothic Kingdom and established the territory known in Arabic as al-Andalus, which at its greatest extent covered most of the peninsula and Septimania in southern France.

Background

The Visigothic Kingdom of Toledo was weakened by dynastic disputes, including a succession struggle between King Roderic and the sons of the previous king, Wittiza. Across the Strait of Gibraltar, the governor of Ifriqiya, Musa ibn Nusayr, had completed the conquest of North Africa.

The conquest

In 711, Tariq ibn Ziyad, a Berber commander serving Musa, crossed the strait with some 7,000–12,000 men and landed near Gibraltar (from Arabic Jabal Ṭāriq, "mountain of Tariq"). On 19 July 711 his army destroyed Roderic's forces at the Battle of Guadalete, where Roderic was killed. Tariq advanced rapidly, taking Córdoba and Toledo. Musa followed in 712 with a larger army, capturing Seville and Mérida. By about 718, organized Visigothic resistance had collapsed, except in the far north, where the Battle of Covadonga (c. 718–722), won by Pelagius of Asturias, later became the symbolic start of the Christian Reconquista.

Settlement and society

The conquerors established treaties with local elites, such as the pact with Theodemir of Murcia (713), allowing many communities to keep their property and religion in exchange for tribute. Al-Andalus was governed as a province of the Umayyad Caliphate until 756, when the Umayyad refugee Abd al-Rahman I founded the independent Emirate of Córdoba—later the Caliphate of Córdoba (929–1031), one of the most prosperous states of medieval Europe.
