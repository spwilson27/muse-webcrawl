Seismic retrofit

Seismic retrofitting is the modification of existing structures to make them more resistant to seismic activity, ground motion, or soil failure caused by earthquakes. It is applied to buildings, bridges, and other structures that were built before modern seismic codes, or to standards later found inadequate. Retrofitting aims primarily to protect life safety and limit damage; it is generally more economical than demolishing and rebuilding.

External steel bracing is a common retrofit for deficient frames.

Techniques

  • Adding lateral systems: new shear walls, steel braced frames, or moment frames to resist horizontal forces.
  • Strengthening diaphragms and connections: tying floors and roofs to walls, bolting wood-frame houses to their foundations, and adding anchors so parts of the building act together.
  • Column jacketing: wrapping concrete or masonry columns in steel, concrete, or fiber-reinforced polymer to improve confinement and ductility.
  • Base isolation: inserting bearings that decouple the building from ground motion, used on important structures such as hospitals and historic buildings.
  • Supplemental damping: installing dampers that dissipate seismic energy.
  • Masonry strengthening: bracing parapets, anchoring unreinforced masonry walls, and adding reinforced overlays; soft-story buildings are often retrofitted with new frames or walls at the weak level.

Policy

Devastating urban earthquakes — San Fernando (1971), Loma Prieta (1989), Northridge (1994) in California; Kobe (1995) in Japan; and others — prompted mandatory retrofit ordinances for vulnerable classes such as unreinforced masonry buildings, non-ductile concrete frames, and soft-story apartments. Programs in California, Japan, New Zealand, Turkey, and Italy combine engineering standards (such as ASCE 41 and FEMA guidance) with deadlines, financing, and disclosure requirements. Bridges have been extensively retrofitted with cable restrainers, seat extenders, and column jackets.
