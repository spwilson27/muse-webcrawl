The Mi'kmaq (also spelled Mi'kmaw or Micmac; singular Mi'kmaw) are a First Nations people of the Northeastern Woodlands, indigenous to the areas now known as Canada's Atlantic Provinces, the Gaspé Peninsula of Quebec, and the northeastern region of Maine. Their traditional territory, Mi'kma'ki, was traditionally divided into seven districts.

History

The Mi'kmaq were among the first Indigenous peoples in North America to encounter Europeans, and they developed early trading and alliance relationships with the French, formalized in the Concordat with the Vatican tradition and a series of treaties with the British Crown in the eighteenth century. The Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1725–1779 remain legally significant in Canadian Aboriginal law.

Language and culture

The Mi'kmaq language is an Eastern Algonquian language with several dialects; immersion programs and orthography standardization efforts support its revitalization. Traditionally, governance rested with the Santé Mawiómi (Grand Council), led by a Kji-Saqmaw (Grand Chief). Hunting, fishing, and gathering followed seasonal rounds across Mi'kma'ki.

Modern communities

Today Mi'kmaq First Nations in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland, and Quebec administer their own communities, schools, and fisheries, and Mi'kmaq rights — including the moderate livelihood fishery recognized by the Supreme Court of Canada in the Marshall decisions — remain prominent public issues.
