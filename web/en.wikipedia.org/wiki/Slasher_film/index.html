A slasher film is a subgenre of horror films involving a killer or a group of killers stalking and murdering a group of people, usually by use of bladed or sharp tools. Although the term "slasher" may be used more generally for any horror film involving murder, slasher films are defined by a structured cycle of set-piece killings, a usually masked or psychotic antagonist, and a surviving "final girl" who confronts the killer.

History

The genre drew on earlier works including Psycho (1960), Italian giallo films and The Texas Chain Saw Massacre (1974), but crystallised with John Carpenter's Halloween (1978), whose commercial success launched the Golden Age of 1978–1984, including Friday the 13th, Prom Night, My Bloody Valentine and A Nightmare on Elm Street. After a decline, Wes Craven's self-aware Scream (1996) revived the genre and spawned many imitators and remakes.

Conventions

Common elements include an opening murder or past trauma, an isolated setting, victims picked off one by one (often after transgressions such as sex or drug use), subjective "killer's point of view" shots, and the final girl — a resourceful female survivor analysed in Carol Clover's influential 1992 study Men, Women, and Chain Saws.
