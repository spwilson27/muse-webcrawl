Oldowan

The Oldowan (or Oldowan Industrial Complex) is an archaeological industry of stone tools used during the Lower Palaeolithic, from about 2.6 million years ago until at least 1.7 million years ago. It was the first widespread stone tool culture, and it was named by Louis Leakey after Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, where characteristic tools were first recognised in the 1930s.

Tools and manufacture

Oldowan assemblages consist of simple core tools — choppers, scrapers and pounding tools made by striking a cobble or lump of stone with a hammerstone — together with the sharp flakes struck from them, which were probably the primary cutting implements. Raw materials were often carried some distance to sites, indicating planning. Makers included early members of the genus Homo such as Homo habilis, and possibly also Paranthropus.

Distribution and significance

Oldowan sites are found across much of Africa, including Gona and Hadar in Ethiopia and Koobi Fora in Kenya, and later in Eurasia, as at Dmanisi in Georgia. Still older tools, dated to about 3.3 million years ago from Lomekwi in Kenya, have been assigned to a separate "Lomekwian" industry. The Oldowan was succeeded by the Acheulean industry, characterised by handaxes, around 1.7 million years ago, though Oldowan-style tools persisted in some regions.
