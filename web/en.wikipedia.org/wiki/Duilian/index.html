Duilian

A duilian pasted around a doorway during Chinese New Year celebrations.

Duilian (simplified Chinese: 对联; traditional Chinese: 對聯; pinyin: duìlián) is a pair of lines of poetry, written in matching calligraphy and traditionally pasted on either side of a door or hung on pillars. A special form displayed during the Chinese New Year is the chunlian or Spring Festival couplet. Duilian is one of the most characteristic forms of Chinese poetry and a popular branch of Chinese calligraphy.

Rules of composition

A well-formed duilian obeys strict rules of parallelism. The two lines must have exactly the same number of characters; corresponding characters should match in part of speech and be related or contrasted in meaning; and the tonal pattern of one line should mirror the other, with the first line typically ending in an oblique (falling) tone and the second in a level tone. The lines are read from top to bottom, and traditionally the first line is placed on the right-hand side of the door (from the viewer's perspective facing it).

History

The custom is traced to the Later Shu kingdom of the Five Dynasties period, when the ruler Meng Chang is said to have written an auspicious couplet for the New Year around 964 AD. During the Ming and Qing dynasties the art flourished, and couplets came to be used for weddings, birthdays, funerals, shop openings and temple inscriptions as well as for the New Year. A horizontal scroll bearing a short motto is often placed above the door between the two vertical lines.
