Spanish colonization of the Americas

The Spanish colonization of the Americas began in 1493 on the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, after the initial arrival of Christopher Columbus, sponsored by the Catholic Monarchs of Spain, in 1492. It was the first phase in the European colonization of the Americas, and it established the largest colonial empire of the early modern period. Spanish territories in the Americas were incorporated into the Spanish Empire and ruled from Madrid through the Council of the Indies and, on the ground, viceroyalties such as New Spain (1535) and Peru (1542).

Conquest

Small expeditions of conquistadors, acting under royal licences, overthrew the Aztec Empire under Hernán Cortés (1519–1521) and the Inca Empire under Francisco Pizarro (1532–1533), aided by Indigenous allies, European diseases, steel weapons, and horses. Other expeditions conquered the Maya (piecemeal, until 1697 in Petén), the Muisca, and peoples of the Río de la Plata, Chile, and the North American borderlands. By 1570 Spain claimed most of South America, Central America, Mexico, Florida, and the Caribbean, and later the Philippines were administered through New Spain.

Colonial society and economy

Colonial rule rested on cities founded on a grid plan, the encomienda and later repartimiento labour systems, haciendas, and the mining of silver at Potosí and Zacatecas, which financed the Spanish monarchy and global trade through the Manila galleon. Roman Catholic missions, orders such as the Franciscans, Dominicans, and Jesuits, and the Spanish Inquisition sought to convert Indigenous peoples. Old World diseases caused catastrophic population decline among Native Americans, and the resulting labour shortage drove the importation of enslaved Africans. A stratified society of peninsulares, creoles, mestizos, Indigenous peoples, and Africans emerged, and the Columbian exchange transformed diets and environments on both sides of the Atlantic.

Independence

Triggered in part by the Napoleonic invasion of Spain, wars of independence from 1810 to the 1820s, led by figures such as Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín, ended Spanish rule on the mainland; Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish until 1898, when they were lost in the Spanish–American War.

Legacy

The colonization spread the Spanish language, Catholicism, and European legal and urban institutions across Latin America, while debates continue over its violence — the "Black Legend" — and over the interpretation of figures such as Bartolomé de las Casas, who denounced abuses against Indigenous peoples.
