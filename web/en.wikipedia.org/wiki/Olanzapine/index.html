Olanzapine, sold under the brand name Zyprexa among others, is an atypical antipsychotic medication primarily used to treat schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. It was developed by Eli Lilly and approved in the United States in 1996. It is available as tablets, orally disintegrating tablets, short-acting injection, and a long-acting depot (olanzapine pamoate), and in a fixed combination with fluoxetine (Symbyax) for treatment-resistant depression and bipolar depression.

Medical uses and efficacy

Olanzapine is effective for the positive and, to a degree, negative symptoms of schizophrenia and for acute manic or mixed episodes. In the landmark CATIE trial it showed somewhat longer time to discontinuation than several comparators, offset by worse metabolic effects. Guidelines generally reserve it where its efficacy advantage justifies its side-effect burden.

Adverse effects

The most important adverse effects are metabolic: substantial weight gain, increased appetite, hyperglycaemia and new-onset type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidaemia — among the most pronounced of any antipsychotic. Sedation and dizziness are common; extrapyramidal symptoms are less frequent than with first-generation agents but can occur, and there is a small risk of neuroleptic malignant syndrome and tardive dyskinesia. The long-acting injection carries a rare post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome requiring observation after each dose. Like other antipsychotics, it carries a warning of increased mortality when used for dementia-related psychosis in older adults.

Pharmacology

Olanzapine, a thienobenzodiazepine structurally related to clozapine, antagonises dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, with significant affinity also for histamine H1, muscarinic and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors — a profile that explains much of its sedative and metabolic profile. It is metabolised mainly by CYP1A2 and glucuronidation; smoking induces its clearance.
