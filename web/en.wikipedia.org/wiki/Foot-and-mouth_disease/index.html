Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a highly contagious viral disease of cloven-hoofed animals, including cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and many wild species. It is characterised by fever and painful blister-like sores on the mouth, tongue, feet and teats, leading to lameness, loss of condition and sharp falls in milk yield. Although adult animals usually recover, the disease causes severe economic losses through lost production and trade restrictions, and it can kill young stock by attacking the heart muscle.

Cause and spread

The disease is caused by foot-and-mouth disease virus, a member of the genus Aphthovirus in the family Picornaviridae, which exists in seven immunologically distinct serotypes (O, A, C, Asia 1 and SAT 1–3). The virus spreads by direct contact, in droplets and aerosols — sometimes over long distances across water — and on contaminated vehicles, feed, clothing and equipment. Infected animals excrete virus before clinical signs appear, making control difficult.

Control

Countries free of the disease protect their status by import controls and, in outbreaks, by movement restrictions, biosecurity and the slaughter and disposal of infected and exposed animals ("stamping out"). Vaccination is used in endemic regions and sometimes as emergency ring vaccination, but because immunity is serotype-specific and vaccinated animals cannot easily be distinguished from infected ones, vaccination policies have major trade implications. The 2001 outbreak in the United Kingdom led to the slaughter of more than six million animals and losses estimated in the billions of pounds, and it reshaped British and European contingency planning.

Humans

FMD is not considered a human health risk; it is distinct from hand, foot and mouth disease, a mild human illness caused by unrelated enteroviruses. Human infection with the animal virus is exceedingly rare and mild.
