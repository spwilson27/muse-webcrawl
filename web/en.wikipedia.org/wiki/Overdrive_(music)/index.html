Overdrive (music)

In music, overdrive is a type of distorted electric-guitar sound produced when an amplifier, or the gain stages driving it, are pushed beyond their clean headroom so the signal begins to clip. It is the mildest and most "transparent" family of guitar distortion effects — softer and more dynamically responsive than the harder clipping of distortion or fuzz — retaining much of the instrument's natural tone and reacting strongly to picking strength and the guitar's volume control.

Origin

Overdrive began as an accident of small, low-powered tube amplifiers turned up loud in the 1940s and 1950s: blues and rock-and-roll guitarists such as Chuck Berry and Link Wray embraced the warm breakup of an overdriven amp. The classic rock sound of the 1960s — cranked British valve amplifiers — was built on it.

Overdrive pedals

Because stage and home volumes make a fully cranked amplifier impractical, overdrive pedals simulate the effect at any volume by clipping the signal in a controlled way, often with a tone control and a midrange emphasis that helps the guitar sit in a band mix. Landmark designs include the Ibanez Tube Screamer (late 1970s), the Boss OD-1, and the Klon Centaur (1990s). Guitarists also use a lightly set overdrive pedal to "push" a tube amp's input harder, combining pedal and amplifier breakup.

Overdrive vs. distortion and fuzz

The three terms describe points on a continuum of clipping: overdrive (soft, amp-like clipping, moderate gain), distortion (harder clipping, higher gain, typical of hard rock and metal), and fuzz (extreme, raspy clipping derived from early transistor circuits). The boundaries are matters of convention rather than physics.
