Nucleus accumbens

The nucleus accumbens (NAc or NAcc; also known as the accumbens nucleus) is a region in the basal forebrain rostral to the preoptic area of the hypothalamus. Together with the olfactory tubercle it forms the ventral striatum. The nucleus accumbens is a central component of the brain's reward system and plays an important role in motivation, pleasure, reinforcement learning, and addiction.

Structure

Each hemisphere has its own nucleus accumbens, divided into a central core, resembling the dorsal striatum, and an outer shell with connections to the extended amygdala and hypothalamus. The great majority of its neurons are GABAergic medium spiny neurons, divided roughly equally between those expressing D1 and D2 dopamine receptors.

Function

The accumbens receives dopaminergic input from the ventral tegmental area via the mesolimbic pathway, and glutamatergic input from the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus. It integrates motivational and contextual information to guide goal-directed behaviour, and is implicated in reward prediction, the experience of pleasure (in concert with "hedonic hotspots"), and the reinforcing effects of food, sex, music, and drugs of abuse. Nearly all addictive drugs increase dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens shell, a fact central to theories of addiction.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the accumbens is implicated in depression (especially anhedonia), schizophrenia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and addiction. It has been a target for experimental deep brain stimulation in severe, treatment-resistant cases of these conditions.
