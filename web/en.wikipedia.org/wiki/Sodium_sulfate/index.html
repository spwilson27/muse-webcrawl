Sodium sulfate

Sodium sulfate (also known as sodium sulphate or sulfate of soda) is the inorganic compound with formula Na2SO4 as well as several related hydrates. All forms are white solids that are highly soluble in water. The decahydrate, Na2SO4·10H2O, is known as Glauber's salt, named after the Dutch/German chemist and apothecary Johann Rudolf Glauber, who discovered it in Austrian spring water in the 17th century.

Production and occurrence

With an annual production of about six million tonnes, sodium sulfate is a major commodity chemical. It is produced by mining the minerals mirabilite and thenardite, and as a by-product of hydrochloric acid manufacture (the Mannheim and Hargreaves processes) and other chemical industries.

Applications

The main use is in the kraft process for manufacturing wood pulp, where it supplies sodium and sulfur. It is also a filler in powdered home laundry detergents, an ingredient in glass making (as a fining agent to remove small air bubbles), and is used in textile dyeing, in freeze-protection and dehumidifying mixtures, and in the laboratory as an inert drying agent for organic solutions. Glauber's salt was historically used as a laxative.

Properties

Sodium sulfate has an unusually steep solubility curve: solubility rises rapidly to a maximum at 32.4 °C (the transition point of the decahydrate) and then falls slightly, which makes it useful for thermal energy storage research.
