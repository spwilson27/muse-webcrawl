The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is a church in the Christian Quarter of Jerusalem's Old City, venerated by most Christians as the site of Jesus's crucifixion (Calvary/Golgotha), burial and resurrection. First built under Emperor Constantine and consecrated in 335 AD on a site identified by his mother Helena, it encloses the last stations of the Via Dolorosa and the empty tomb (the Aedicule), and has been a supreme goal of pilgrimage for seventeen centuries.

History and the Status Quo

The church was destroyed by the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim in 1009, rebuilt with Byzantine help, and taken over and enlarged by the Crusaders (the present building is substantially theirs, rededicated 1149). Six Christian communities share it under a fragile 'Status Quo' codified in Ottoman decrees (1757, 1852s): rights over every stone, lamp and cleaning duty are specified to the centimetre — a ladder left on a ledge since the 18th century, the famous 'immovable ladder', symbolises the arrangement. Two Muslim families have held the key and opened the door daily since the time of Saladin, by tradition.
