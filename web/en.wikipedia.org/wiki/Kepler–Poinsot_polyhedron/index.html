Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron

The small stellated dodecahedron, one of the four Kepler–Poinsot solids

In geometry, a Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron is one of four regular star polyhedra. They may be obtained by stellating the regular convex dodecahedron and icosahedron, and differ from them in having regular pentagrammic faces or vertex figures. Together with the five convex Platonic solids, they complete the set of regular polyhedra: all their faces are congruent regular polygons and the same number of faces meet at each vertex — with the star polygons (pentagrams) admitted.

History

Johannes Kepler described the two stellated dodecahedra in his Harmonices Mundi (1619), having earlier encountered a mosaic depiction of the small stellated dodecahedron by Paolo Uccello in St Mark's Basilica, Venice. Almost two centuries later, Louis Poinsot rediscovered Kepler's solids and added the great dodecahedron and great icosahedron (1809). Augustin Cauchy proved in 1813 that the list of regular star polyhedra is complete, and Bertrand and Cayley later gave the solids their current names and systematic treatment.

Properties

The Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra are self-intersecting: their surfaces pass through themselves, so notions of volume and density require care — each can be assigned a "density" counting how many times the surface winds around its centre. They form two dual pairs: the small stellated dodecahedron is dual to the great dodecahedron, and the great stellated dodecahedron to the great icosahedron. They appear in art, notably in M. C. Escher's works, and as models in crystallography and design.
