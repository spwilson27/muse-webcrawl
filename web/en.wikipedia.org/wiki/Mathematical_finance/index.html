Mathematical finance, also known as quantitative finance and financial mathematics, is a field of applied mathematics concerned with mathematical modelling of financial markets. The field treats the prices of financial assets and derivatives as given, and seeks to model them — particularly derivatives such as options — using stochastic processes, in order to value contracts, construct hedges, and manage risk.

History

The discipline begins with Louis Bachelier's 1900 thesis Théorie de la spéculation, which modelled stock prices with what is now called Brownian motion. Modern portfolio theory followed with Harry Markowitz (1952) and the capital asset pricing model. The decisive breakthrough was the Black–Scholes–Merton model (1973), which derived a partial differential equation for the price of a European option under geometric Brownian motion and showed that a continuously rebalanced hedge could eliminate risk — the foundation of risk-neutral pricing. Robert Merton, Fischer Black and Myron Scholes were recognised with the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics.

Core concepts

Central ideas include arbitrage and the fundamental theorem of asset pricing (no-arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of a risk-neutral probability measure), martingales under the risk-neutral measure, Itô's lemma and stochastic differential equations, replication and hedging, and numerical methods such as Monte Carlo simulation, binomial trees, and finite-difference schemes. Interest-rate modelling, credit risk, volatility modelling (including stochastic volatility models such as Heston's), and model risk are major branches.

Criticism

The 2008 financial crisis prompted criticism that Gaussian-copula and related models underestimated tail risk and correlation in extreme events; practitioners and theorists continue to debate the limits of model-based pricing.
