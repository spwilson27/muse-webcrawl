Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary

Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary (Vietnamese: từ Hán-Việt, literally "words of Chinese-Vietnamese origin") is the stratum of the Vietnamese language borrowed from Chinese. Estimates commonly put the proportion of Sino-Vietnamese words in Vietnamese at around 30 to 60 percent of the lexicon, with the share rising sharply in formal, literary, political, and academic registers. The borrowings reflect a millennium of Chinese rule over northern Vietnam (111 BC – AD 939) and many subsequent centuries of cultural exchange.

Layers of borrowing

Linguists distinguish several layers. Old Sino-Vietnamese entered the language in the earliest centuries of contact. The systematic Sino-Vietnamese readings (âm Hán-Việt) were established during the Tang dynasty period, when Vietnamese scholars adopted regular pronunciations for Chinese characters based on Middle Chinese; these readings, preserved in dictionaries, are remarkably consistent and are still used to read chữ Hán texts. Later Vietnamised or colloquial borrowings (âm Hán-Việt biến âm), such as bàn "table" from 盤, diverged from the literary readings through everyday use.

Characteristics

Sino-Vietnamese morphemes are monosyllabic and are typically combined into two-syllable compounds, much like Sino-Korean and Sino-Japanese vocabulary, to which the system is closely analogous. Because a single Chinese syllable often corresponds to many homophonous characters, Sino-Vietnamese words in the modern Latin-based script (chữ Quốc ngữ) can be highly ambiguous in isolation. Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary dominates abstract and technical terms: for example độc lập (獨立, "independence"), lịch sử (歷史, "history"), and khoa học (科學, "science"). Some terms were coined in China or Japan during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and then adopted into Vietnamese, including words for modern concepts such as "telephone" (điện thoại, 電話) and "economy" (kinh tế, 經濟).

Usage

Native Vietnamese (thuần Việt) words tend to dominate everyday speech and basic vocabulary, while Sino-Vietnamese compounds predominate in writing and formal speech. The interplay of the two strata gives Vietnamese much of its stylistic range, similar to the interplay of Germanic and Latinate vocabulary in English.
