Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act

The Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act (CAPTA, Public Law 93-247) is a United States federal law providing federal funding and guidance to states in support of prevention, assessment, investigation, prosecution, and treatment activities related to child abuse and neglect. Originally enacted on January 31, 1974, it has been amended and reauthorised numerous times, and it forms the legislative foundation of the modern American child protective services system.

Background

The law followed growing public and professional attention to child maltreatment in the 1960s, including the influential 1962 paper "The Battered-Child Syndrome" by C. Henry Kempe and colleagues. Senator Walter Mondale was the principal sponsor; Congress sought to create a federal role that would support state systems without displacing state responsibility for child protection.

Provisions

  • Established the National Center on Child Abuse and Neglect (NCCAN) to collect and disseminate information, compile research, and coordinate federal activities.
  • Provides formula grants to states that meet eligibility requirements, including maintaining laws for mandatory reporting of known or suspected child abuse and neglect, prompt investigation of reports, confidentiality of records, and appointment of guardians ad litem for children in court proceedings.
  • Sets a federal floor definition of child abuse and neglect: any recent act or failure to act by a parent or caretaker resulting in death, serious physical or emotional harm, sexual abuse or exploitation, or an act presenting an imminent risk of serious harm.
  • Authorises discretionary grants for research, demonstration projects, training and technical assistance, and funds the National Child Abuse and Neglect Data System (NCANDS) and community-based child abuse prevention (CBCAP) grants.

Amendments

CAPTA was substantially amended in 1978, 1984, 1988, 1992, 1996, 2003 (the Keeping Children and Families Safe Act), and reauthorised by the CAPTA Reauthorization Act of 2010. Later legislation attached new requirements, including provisions addressing infants born affected by substance exposure (through the Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery Act of 2016) and the trafficking of children.

Impact

Although federal funding under CAPTA is modest relative to total state child-welfare spending, its state-plan requirements have shaped reporting laws, investigative procedures, and data collection in every state.
