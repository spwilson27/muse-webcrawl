Stomatal conductance

Stomatal conductance (usually denoted gs or gsw when expressed for water vapour) is a measure of the rate at which carbon dioxide enters, or water vapour exits, through the stomata of a leaf. It is effectively the inverse of stomatal resistance and is widely used in plant physiology, agronomy, meteorology and land-surface modelling to link photosynthesis, transpiration and the exchange of gases between vegetation and the atmosphere.

Stomatal conductance
Symbol: gs, gsw
SI unit: mol m−2 s−1
Also reported in: mmol m−2 s−1, m s−1
Inverse: stomatal resistance

Definition

Stomata are small pores, usually on the underside of a leaf, flanked by a pair of guard cells whose turgor controls the aperture. Because nearly all water lost by a plant and nearly all carbon gained in photosynthesis pass through these pores, the conductance of the stomatal pathway is a central control on plant water relations and growth. Conductance is commonly estimated with a porometer, which measures the humidity change in a chamber clamped to a leaf, or inferred from gas-exchange measurements of photosynthesis and transpiration.

Typical maximum stomatal conductances for water vapour range from about 100 to over 1,000 mmol m−2 s−1, depending on species and conditions. Conductance to CO2 is lower than to water vapour, in the approximate ratio of their diffusivities in air (water vapour diffuses about 1.6 times faster than CO2).

Environmental controls

Stomata respond to light (especially blue light), intercellular CO2 concentration, humidity and vapour pressure deficit, leaf water status, temperature and the plant hormone abscisic acid (ABA). High vapour pressure deficit, drought and elevated ABA typically close stomata, reducing conductance and conserving water at the cost of carbon uptake. Plants balance these competing demands; the ratio of assimilation to conductance defines intrinsic water-use efficiency.

Modelling

Empirical models such as the Ball–Woodrow–Berry model relate stomatal conductance to photosynthesis, relative humidity and CO2 at the leaf surface. Later optimal-stomata models, including the unified stomatal optimisation theory of Medlyn and co-workers, predict conductance from the marginal water cost of carbon gain. These formulations are embedded in terrestrial biosphere models and weather forecast systems, where stomatal behaviour influences evapotranspiration, surface temperature and even regional rainfall.

Applications

Stomatal conductance is used to schedule irrigation, screen crop cultivars for drought tolerance, interpret satellite observations of vegetation stress, and estimate canopy-scale fluxes via upscaling approaches such as the Penman–Monteith equation, in which a surface or canopy conductance term aggregates stomatal behaviour across a whole canopy.
