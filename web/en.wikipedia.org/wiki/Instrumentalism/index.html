Instrumentalism

Instrumentalism is a pragmatic philosophical approach that regards scientific theories and concepts not as descriptions of reality that are true or false, but as instruments or tools for predicting phenomena and solving problems. On this view, the value of an idea lies in its usefulness for guiding action and inquiry rather than in its correspondence to an underlying reality. Instrumentalism is associated especially with the American pragmatist John Dewey, and in the philosophy of science it is closely related to anti-realist positions such as constructive empiricism and to Pierre Duhem's treatment of physical theory.

Origins and development

The outlook grew out of the pragmatism of Charles Sanders Peirce and William James, for whom the meaning and worth of a belief were tied to its practical consequences. Dewey developed this into a general theory of inquiry, which he sometimes called instrumentalism: ideas are plans of action devised to resolve problematic situations, and they are warranted insofar as they succeed. Earlier anticipations include the astronomers' traditional willingness to "save the phenomena" with models, such as epicycles, that were not claimed to describe real motions.

In philosophy of science

As a doctrine about science, instrumentalism holds that theories are devices for systematising observations and generating predictions. Terms referring to unobservable entities — electrons, fields, wave functions — need not be taken as referring to real things; they are useful fictions or calculational aids. This avoids metaphysical commitments and sidesteps questions about the truth of theories that have historically been replaced. Critics, notably scientific realists, object that instrumentalism cannot explain the novel predictive success of theories (the "no miracles" argument) or why some tools work so reliably if they do not latch onto real structure.

Related positions

Instrumentalism overlaps with operationalism, which defines concepts by the operations used to measure them; with logical positivism's suspicion of metaphysics; and with Milton Friedman's methodological argument that the assumptions of economic models should be judged only by their predictions. More recent variants appear in discussions of quantum mechanics, where interpretations are sometimes compared purely by their empirical adequacy.
