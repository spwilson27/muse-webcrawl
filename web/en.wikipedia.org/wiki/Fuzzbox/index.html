Fuzzbox

A fuzzbox (or fuzz box) is a type of effects unit for electric guitar that produces a distorted, "fuzzy" sound through deliberate clipping of the audio signal. Fuzz was one of the first guitar effects to be produced as a standalone pedal, and its harsh, saturated tone became a hallmark of 1960s rock, psychedelic music, garage rock and later stoner and alternative rock.

History

Distorted guitar tones were first obtained accidentally from damaged or overloaded amplifiers — notably the faulty mixing console channel heard on Marty Robbins's "Don't Worry" (1961). The first purpose-built fuzz unit was the Maestro Fuzz-Tone FZ-1, released by Gibson in 1962; it achieved fame when Keith Richards used it for the riff of the Rolling Stones' "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" (1965). In Britain, the Tone Bender series followed, and in 1966 the Dallas-Arbiter Fuzz Face appeared with its distinctive round enclosure.

Technology

A fuzzbox amplifies the guitar signal until its waveform is severely clipped, approaching a square wave rich in harmonics. Early designs used germanium transistors, whose temperature-sensitive, "leaky" behaviour gives a warm, spongy distortion; later and more stable designs use silicon transistors for a brighter, more aggressive fuzz. The circuit typically provides controls for fuzz (gain) and volume, and the sound cleans up noticeably when the guitar's own volume control is rolled back — a characteristic exploited by players such as Jimi Hendrix.

Notable models and users

  • Maestro Fuzz-Tone — the first commercial fuzz; used by Keith Richards.
  • Fuzz Face — associated with Jimi Hendrix, David Gilmour and Eric Johnson.
  • Tone Bender — used by Jimmy Page and Mick Ronson.
  • Electro-Harmonix Big Muff Pi — a later, sustaining fuzz/distortion used by David Gilmour, Jack White and many alternative rock bands.
