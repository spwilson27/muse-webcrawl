Audio mixing (recorded music)

In sound recording and reproduction, audio mixing is the process of combining multitrack recordings into a final mono, stereo or surround sound product. In the process, the relative volume levels, frequency ranges and dynamic character of the individual sources are adjusted and balanced, and effects such as reverb may be added. The result — the "mix" — is then passed to mastering before distribution. This article covers mixing in music production; for mixing in other contexts see audio mixing.

History

Early commercial recordings were made with a single microphone and balanced acoustically, by positioning performers around the recording horn or microphone. The advent of multitrack tape recorders in the 1950s and 1960s separated recording from mixing, allowing engineers to capture performances on discrete tracks and balance them afterwards. Consoles grew from a handful of channels to the large-format analogue desks of the 1970s and 1980s (such as those by Neve and SSL), and from the 1990s mixing increasingly moved "in the box" to digital audio workstations such as Pro Tools, Logic Pro and Cubase. Modern mixers work with dozens or hundreds of tracks and extensive automation of every parameter.

Process

Mixing typically begins with organising and editing the tracks, correcting timing and tuning, and removing noise. The engineer then establishes a static balance of levels, uses equalisation to give each element its own frequency space, applies compression and other dynamics processing, places sources in the stereo image by panning, and adds time-based effects (reverb, delay, chorus) to suggest space and depth. Automation is used to ride levels and effects through the song — for example, lifting a vocal in the chorus. The finished mix is printed to a stereo (or surround) master and checked on different loudspeakers and headphones.

The mixing engineer

Mixing is usually carried out by a mixing engineer, sometimes the recording engineer and sometimes a specialist brought in after tracking. Prominent mixing engineers are credited in their own right, and the mix is widely regarded as a creative contribution comparable to production and arrangement.
