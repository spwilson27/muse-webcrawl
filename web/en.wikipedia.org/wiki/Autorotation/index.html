Autorotation

Autorotation is a state of flight in which the main rotor of a rotorcraft is driven solely by aerodynamic forces, with no power from the engine. The term may also refer to the corresponding condition in fixed-wing aircraft. Autorotation is the principle that allows an autogyro to fly and enables a helicopter to land safely after engine failure.

Helicopters

In a helicopter, autorotation is entered following engine failure or deliberate training: the pilot immediately lowers the collective pitch, reducing drag so that the upward flow of air through the rotor, generated by the aircraft's descent, keeps the rotor turning. The rotor disc is divided into a driven region near the hub, where aerodynamic forces push the blades forward, and a driving (propeller) region toward the tips. During the descent, kinetic energy is stored in the spinning rotor; just before touchdown the pilot raises the collective ("flares" and pulls pitch) to cushion the landing. This manoeuvre is a standard, regularly practised emergency procedure, and its feasibility depends on altitude and airspeed, as shown in the helicopter's height–velocity diagram (the "dead man's curve").

Autogyros

In an autogyro, autorotation is the normal state of flight rather than an emergency condition: forward thrust from a propeller drives air up through the free-spinning rotor, which produces lift. Juan de la Cierva developed the autogyro after observing that a rotor allowed to spin freely could not stall in the manner of a fixed wing.

Fixed-wing aircraft and seeds

In fixed-wing aviation, autorotation describes the self-induced rotation of a stalled wing, which can lead to a spin. In nature, the autorotation of winged seeds such as those of the maple (samaras) slows their descent and aids wind dispersal — a phenomenon that inspired early rotorcraft research.
