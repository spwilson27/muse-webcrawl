Algorithmic trading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algorithmic trading is the use of computer programs following defined instructions — algorithms — to place and manage trades in financial markets, varying such parameters as price, timing and quantity faster and more systematically than human traders can. It is used by investment banks, pension funds, hedge funds and proprietary trading firms, and by the mid-2010s accounted for the majority of trading volume on major developed markets.

History

Computerised order routing began with the New York Stock Exchange's "designated order turnaround" system in the 1970s and programme trading of index baskets in the 1980s, which figured in debates over the 1987 crash. Decimalisation, electronic exchanges and falling computing costs in the 2000s spread algorithms from execution tools to full strategies; the speed-focused subset known as high-frequency trading (HFT) grew after 2005, competing on latency measured in microseconds and co-locating servers at exchange data centres.

Strategies

Common families include: execution algorithms that slice large orders to minimise market impact (VWAP, TWAP, implementation shortfall); statistical arbitrage and pairs trading; market making, quoting both sides to earn the spread; index and cross-asset arbitrage; trend-following and mean-reversion signals; and event-driven trading on news and data releases, increasingly assisted by machine learning in signal discovery, execution and risk control.

Controversy and regulation

Algorithms have been scrutinised after market disruptions, above all the "Flash Crash" of 6 May 2010, when U.S. indices plunged and largely recovered within minutes, and the 2012 Knight Capital incident, in which a software deployment error lost the firm some $440 million in under an hour. Regulators responded with circuit breakers, market-access rules in the U.S. (SEC Rule 15c3-5), and the MiFID II regime in Europe imposing testing, record-keeping and kill-switch requirements on algorithmic traders. Supporters credit algorithms with narrower spreads and deeper liquidity; critics cite fragility, arms races in speed and manipulative tactics such as spoofing, which is expressly prohibited.
