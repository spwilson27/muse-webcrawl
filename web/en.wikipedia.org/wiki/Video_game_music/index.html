Video game music

Video game music (VGM) is the soundtrack that accompanies video games, from the simple synthesized beeps of early arcade machines to fully orchestrated scores recorded with symphony orchestras. It is both a functional component of game design—guiding emotion, signalling events, and filling silence—and a musical culture in its own right, with concerts, albums, and fan remixes forming part of video game culture.

An orchestra performing game music; concert series such as Video Games Live popularized the repertoire.

History

Early arcade and console hardware produced sound through programmable sound generators, obliging composers to work within a handful of channels. Composers such as Koji Kondo (Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda) and Nobuo Uematsu (Final Fantasy) created enduring melodies within these constraints, defining the chiptune aesthetic. The compact disc era of the 1990s allowed recorded audio and licensed music, while modern games employ adaptive scores that change dynamically with gameplay, and middleware such as Wwise and FMOD to manage interactive music.

Notable composers and works

Uematsu's Final Fantasy scores, Kondo's Nintendo themes, and Yasunori Mitsuda's Chrono Trigger are frequently cited landmarks. Western composers including Jeremy Soule (The Elder Scrolls), Austin Wintory (Journey, the first game score nominated for a Grammy Award), and Mick Gordon (Doom) expanded the field's profile. In 2023 the Grammy Awards introduced a dedicated category for video game and interactive media scores.

Cultural impact

Game music is performed in concert halls worldwide by tours such as Video Games Live and Distant Worlds. Fan communities produce remixes and arrangements through sites like OverClocked ReMix, and chiptune artists compose with original vintage hardware. Soundtracks are sold and streamed independently of their games, with some charting commercially.
