Emil Adolf von Behring (15 March 1854 – 31 March 1917) was a German physiologist who received the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901, for his work on serum therapy and the development of an antitoxin against diphtheria.

Work on diphtheria and tetanus

Working in Berlin at the Institute for Infectious Diseases alongside Robert Koch and in collaboration with Shibasaburo Kitasato, Behring showed in 1890 that the blood serum of animals immunised against diphtheria or tetanus contained substances — antitoxins — that could protect and even cure other animals when transferred to them. The first successful treatment of a child with diphtheria antitoxin followed in 1891, and large-scale production in horses made the therapy widely available, dramatically reducing child mortality from the disease.

Later life

Behring became professor at Halle and then Marburg, where he founded the Behringwerke company to produce sera and vaccines. Ennobled in 1901, he also researched tuberculosis, proposing immunisation approaches for cattle and infants, though with less success than his diphtheria work. He died in Marburg in 1917. He is often called the "saviour of children" for the impact of diphtheria serum therapy.
