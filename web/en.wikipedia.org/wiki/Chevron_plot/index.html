A chevron plot is a method of representing the folding and unfolding kinetics of a protein as a plot of the logarithm of the observed rate constant (log kobs) against the concentration of denaturant, such as urea or guanidinium chloride. The plot takes its name from its characteristic V shape, resembling a chevron insignia, produced when both folding and unfolding arms are measured over a shared range of conditions.

Interpretation

At high denaturant concentrations the protein unfolds, and the observed rate rises with denaturant — this forms the upward-sloping unfolding arm. At low denaturant concentrations the protein refolds, and the observed rate falls as denaturant increases — the folding arm. Extrapolating both arms to zero denaturant yields the folding and unfolding rate constants in water, from which the equilibrium stability of the protein (ΔG) can be calculated. The slopes of the arms, called m-values, report on how much surface area is buried or exposed in the transition state relative to the folded and unfolded states.

Rollover and intermediates

Ideal two-state folders give straight arms meeting at a sharp minimum. Deviations — "rollover", in which an arm curves — are evidence of folding intermediates, parallel pathways, or a transition state that moves along the reaction coordinate as conditions change (Hammond behaviour). Chevron analysis is therefore a standard diagnostic tool in protein folding studies, often combined with phi-value analysis and stopped-flow spectroscopy.
