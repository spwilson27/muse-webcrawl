Kyudo

Kyūdō (弓道, "way of the bow") is the Japanese martial art of archery. Kyūdō is based on kyūjutsu ("art of archery"), which originated with the samurai class of feudal Japan. In 1919, the name of kyūjutsu was officially changed to kyūdō, following the example set by judo. Kyūdō is practised by thousands of people worldwide as a budo discipline that blends physical, moral, and spiritual development; the All Nippon Kyudo Federation was founded in 1949 and the International Kyudo Federation in 2006.

Equipment and form

The kyūdō bow (yumi) is exceptionally tall — over two metres — and traditionally made of bamboo and wood, gripped in the lower third. The archer wears a stiff glove (yugake) on the drawing hand and shoots at targets 28 metres away in the standard sharei procedure, or 60 metres in enteki. Shooting follows an eight-step form, the shahō-hassetsu: ashibumi (footing), dōzukuri (body formation), yugamae (bow readying), uchiokoshi (raising), hikiwake (drawing apart), kai (full draw), hanare (release), and zanshin (remaining form/spirit).

Philosophy

Unlike most Western archery, kyūdō treats hitting the target as secondary to correct form and spirit. Its ideals are expressed as shin–zen–bi (truth, goodness, beauty), and Zen influence — popularised in the West by Eugen Herrigel's Zen in the Art of Archery (1948) — is strong in many schools, though the degree of religious content varies. Ranks are awarded as in other budo, with dan examinations emphasising form and etiquette.
