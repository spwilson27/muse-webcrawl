Seymour Martin Lipset (18 March 1922 – 31 December 2006) was an American sociologist and political scientist, one of the founders of modern political sociology. His work addressed the conditions of democracy, social stratification, political parties and trade unions, and American exceptionalism.

Career

Lipset, the son of Russian Jewish immigrants, was active in socialist youth politics before earning his doctorate at Columbia University in 1949. His dissertation became Agrarian Socialism (1950), a study of the Saskatchewan Co-operative Commonwealth Federation. With Robert Michels's "iron law of oligarchy" as its foil, his Union Democracy (1956) examined the exceptional internal democracy of the International Typographical Union. He taught at Columbia, Berkeley, Harvard and Stanford, and served as president of both the American Political Science Association and the American Sociological Association — the only person to head both.

Contributions

Lipset's essay "Some Social Requisites of Democracy" (1959) and his book Political Man (1960) argued that economic development, education and a large middle class create the social foundations of stable democracy — a thesis, often called modernisation theory, that has been debated and refined ever since. With Stein Rokkan he developed the influential theory that Western party systems were "frozen" around the cleavage structures — centre–periphery, church–state, land–industry and owners–workers — formed at the time of mass enfranchisement; the "freezing hypothesis" of 1967 remains a reference point in party-system research, including for Sartori's classifications.

Lipset also coined the phrase "end of ideology" debates of the era, studied student politics, and wrote extensively on American exceptionalism in American Exceptionalism: A Double-Edged Sword (1996).
