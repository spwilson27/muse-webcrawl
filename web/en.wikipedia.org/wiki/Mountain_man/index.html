Mountain man

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

A mountain man was an explorer, trapper and frontiersman who lived in the North American Rocky Mountains and other western ranges, principally in the first half of the 19th century, roughly 1810 to the 1880s, with the peak era in 1820–1840. Mountain men were instrumental in opening the various emigrant trails — later travelled by pioneers in wagons — that allowed Americans to settle the new territories of the American West by organised wagon trains.

The fur trade

Most mountain men worked in the fur trade, trapping beaver whose pelts supplied the fashion for felt hats. Trade was organised in several ways: some men were employees of companies such as William Ashley's Rocky Mountain Fur Company or the American Fur Company; others were "free trappers" who sold to whoever paid best. From 1825 Ashley instituted the rendezvous system: instead of permanent trading posts, a caravan of supplies met the trappers each summer at an agreed valley, where a season's furs were exchanged in a boisterous week-long fair.

Life and people

The work was exceptionally dangerous: grizzly bears, harsh winters, disease and conflicts with Native American nations killed many. Famous mountain men include Jedediah Smith, remembered for his explorations of the Great Basin and California; Jim Bridger, later an army scout and founder of Fort Bridger; Kit Carson; John Colter, of "Colter's Run"; and Hugh Glass, whose survival after a bear mauling became legend. Many men married into Native nations, learned indigenous languages and skills, and served as guides and interpreters for the emigration that followed.

Decline and legacy

By the early 1840s changing fashion (silk hats replaced beaver felt) and over-trapping had largely collapsed the trade. Many mountain men became army scouts, wagon-train guides, or settlers. The romantic image of the mountain man — self-reliant, dressed in fringed buckskin, fluent in sign language — became a durable fixture of American folklore, and modern enthusiasts re-enact the rendezvous tradition.
