The George C. Page Museum is a natural history museum in Los Angeles, California, dedicated to the study and display of fossils from the La Brea Tar Pits. Located in Hancock Park on Wilshire Boulevard's Museum Row, the museum presents the plants and animals that lived in the Los Angeles Basin between roughly 50,000 and 11,000 years ago, during the last Ice Age, and whose remains were preserved in the area's natural asphalt deposits. It is administered by the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County and is now branded, together with the tar pits, as La Brea Tar Pits and Museum.

History

The tar pits at Rancho La Brea had yielded fossils to scientific excavation since the early 20th century, with the bulk of the collection — over a million specimens — recovered from excavations in 1913–15 and stored at the county museum in Exposition Park. Philanthropist George C. Page, who as a boy from Nebraska had been fascinated by the tar pits when he first visited Los Angeles, funded a museum on site so that the fossils could be displayed where they were found. The Page Museum opened to the public in 1977.

Collections and exhibits

The museum displays skeletons of dire wolves, saber-toothed cats (Smilodon fatalis, the California state fossil), Columbian mammoths, American mastodons, giant ground sloths, bison, and the only fossil human found at the site, the roughly 9,000-year-old "La Brea Woman". A glass-walled fossil laboratory, nicknamed the "fishbowl", lets visitors watch preparators clean and sort bones. Outside, visitors can view active excavation sites — including "Project 23", a set of fossil deposits salvaged in wooden crates during construction of a neighbouring parking structure — and the bubbling Lake Pit, a remnant of early asphalt mining.
