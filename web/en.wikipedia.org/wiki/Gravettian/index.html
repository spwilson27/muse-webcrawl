Gravettian

A Venus figurine of the Gravettian period.

The Gravettian was an archaeological industry of the European Upper Paleolithic that succeeded the Aurignacian circa 33,000 years ago. It lasted until about 21,000 years ago, spanning the approach to the Last Glacial Maximum. It is named for the type site of La Gravette in the Dordogne region of France.

Culture

The Gravettian is known for its distinctive stone tool kit, including small pointed blades with blunt backs used in hunting, and for some of the earliest known fired ceramic objects — the figurines from Dolní Věstonice in the present-day Czech Republic, dated to roughly 29,000–25,000 years ago. The culture is especially famous for its "Venus figurines", small sculptures of women with exaggerated features carved from ivory, stone or clay, found from France to Siberia.

Gravettian hunter-gatherers specialized in hunting large game, particularly mammoths, and constructed semi-permanent camps with dwellings framed in mammoth bone at sites on the East European Plain such as Mezhirich in Ukraine. Burials of the period, such as the triple burial at Dolní Věstonice and the richly adorned interments at Sungir in Russia, indicate elaborate mortuary ritual. Fine personal ornaments, carved pendants and early examples of netting and textile impressions also survive from this period.

Decline

As glacial conditions intensified toward the Last Glacial Maximum, Gravettian populations contracted into southern refugia, and the industry was succeeded in western Europe by the Solutrean and later the Magdalenian, while Epigravettian traditions continued in the east and south.
