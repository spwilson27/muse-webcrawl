Jack Kerouac

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Jack Kerouac leaning against a wall in New York, looking tired and handsome in the 1950s]
Jack Kerouac leaning against a wall in New York, looking tired and handsome in the 1950s

Jean-Louis Lebris de Kérouac (12 March 1922 – 21 October 1969), known as Jack Kerouac, was an American novelist and poet of French-Canadian descent, a leader of the Beat Generation. His novel On the Road (1957) — written in a legendary burst of 'spontaneous prose' — defined the Beat ethos of travel, jazz, spirituality and revolt against postwar conformity, and made him, briefly and uncomfortably, a celebrity.

Life

Kerouac was born in Lowell, Massachusetts, to French-Canadian parents, speaking French before English. A football scholarship to Columbia University collapsed through injury and disputes; wartime service in the merchant marine and Navy framed the road years — hitchhiking and freight-hopping across America and Mexico with Neal Cassady, the 'Dean Moriarty' of the novel — and the friendships with Allen Ginsberg and William S. Burroughs that formed the Beats.

On the Road and the Duluoz Legend

After the conventional The Town and the City (1950), Kerouac typed the novel of his road trips on a 120-foot scroll in three weeks of April 1951; heavily edited, On the Road appeared in 1957 to a famous New York Times rave ('the most beautifully executed, the clearest and the most important utterance' of its generation). He followed it with the sprawling autobiographical sequence he called the Duluoz Legend: The Dharma Bums (1958, on Buddhism, a bestseller), The Subterraneans, Doctor Sax, Big Sur (1962, on his breakdown).

Decline and legacy

Fame curdled: conservative, Catholic and alcoholic, Kerouac disowned the hippies who claimed him, and died of cirrhosis at 47 in Florida. Posthumous publication of the unexpurgated scroll (2007) and steady scholarly attention have secured his standing as a major American stylist whose influence runs from rock lyrics to travel writing.
