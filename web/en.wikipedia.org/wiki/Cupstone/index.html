Cupstone

Cupstones, also known as anvil stones, pitted cobbles, or nutting stones, are prehistoric stone artefacts bearing one or more cup-shaped depressions pecked or ground into their surface. They are found on archaeological sites in many parts of the world and are especially common in North America, where they are associated with Native American food processing.

Description

A cupstone is typically a river cobble or slab of sandstone, granite, or other hard rock with a shallow, roughly circular pit on one or both faces. The pits are usually a few centimetres across and deep enough to hold small objects steady while they are worked.

Interpretations

The most widely accepted interpretation is that the cups served as sockets for cracking hard-shelled nuts such as hickory nuts and acorns: the nut was placed in the depression and struck with a hammerstone. Other proposed functions include holding material steady while drilling fire, grinding pigment or medicine, and use as small anvils for working bone or stone tools. Some cupules in rock art panels may instead have had ritual purposes, and large boulders with many cups are sometimes called "cup-and-ring" stones in Europe.

Distribution

Cupstones occur on sites ranging in age from the Archaic period to historic times in North America, and similar pitted stones are reported from every inhabited continent. Because a simple pitted stone can be produced by many activities, archaeologists rely on context, wear patterns, and residues to interpret individual specimens.
