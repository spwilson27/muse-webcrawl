The martini is a cocktail made with gin and dry vermouth, garnished with an olive or a lemon twist. One of the best-known mixed drinks, it became an emblem of mid-twentieth-century sophistication and spawned countless variations. The classic preparation combines gin and dry vermouth in proportions that have grown progressively "drier" (less vermouth) over time, served ice-cold in a stemmed cocktail glass.

Preparation

The International Bartenders Association (IBA) recipe specifies 6 cl (2 oz) gin and 1 cl (1/3 oz) dry vermouth, stirred with ice and strained, garnished with an olive or lemon peel. Purists generally stir a gin martini to keep it clear and silky; the famous "shaken, not stirred" instruction is associated with James Bond, whose preference in Ian Fleming's novels was largely for the vodka martini. A dry martini uses less vermouth, a wet one more; a dirty martini adds olive brine; a perfect martini splits the vermouth between sweet and dry; a Gibson is garnished with a cocktail onion instead of an olive.

Variations

  • Vodka martini — vodka replaces gin; popularised after the 1950s and by the Bond films.
  • Vesper — Fleming's original Bond drink: gin, vodka and Kina Lillet (now usually Lillet Blanc), shaken.
  • Gibson — garnished with a pickled onion.
  • "Martini" drinks of the 1980s–2000s — sweet drinks served in cocktail glasses, such as the appletini and espresso martini, which share only the glass with the original.

History

The martini evolved in the late 19th century from earlier gin-and-vermouth drinks such as the Martinez (itself possibly derived from the Manhattan). Recipes under the name "Martini" appear in American bartending guides of the 1880s–1900s, originally sweeter, using sweet vermouth and Old Tom gin; the dry style with London dry gin and French vermouth became standard by the early 20th century. The drink rose to iconic status during and after Prohibition, and was associated with figures from Franklin D. Roosevelt to the fictional James Bond. H. L. Mencken called it "the only American invention as perfect as the sonnet."
