Injera

Injera (Amharic: እንጀራ, ənǧära; Tigrinya: ቃንጫ, q̱anč̣a) is a sour fermented pancake-like flatbread with a slightly spongy texture, traditionally made of teff flour. It is the national dish of Ethiopia and Eritrea. In making injera, teff flour is mixed with water and allowed to ferment for several days, as with sourdough starter. The resulting batter is poured onto a large circular griddle known as a mitad and cooked briefly on one side only; steam creates the bread's characteristic "eyes" (small holes) on the upper surface.

Preparation and serving

Where teff is scarce or expensive, wheat, barley, corn or rice flour may be substituted in whole or in part, producing breads of different colour and texture; in Ethiopia pure teff injera is prized. A large piece of injera is laid on a platter and stews — wat, made from vegetables, legumes or meats — are ladled on top. Diners tear off pieces of additional injera with the right hand and use them to scoop up the food, so the bread serves simultaneously as plate, utensil and part of the meal. The injera beneath the stew, soaked in its juices, is eaten at the end.

Nutrition

Teff is naturally gluten-free and relatively high in iron, calcium and protein for a grain; fermentation further improves mineral availability and gives injera its distinctive tang. Because teff yields in Ethiopia are limited and the grain is costly abroad, diaspora communities often blend teff with other flours.

Cultural role

Injera is eaten daily by most Ethiopians and Eritreans and anchors communal eating. In the ritual of gursha, one diner places a rolled morsel of injera and stew into another's mouth as a gesture of affection and respect. Outside the Horn of Africa, injera is the centrepiece of Ethiopian and Eritrean restaurants worldwide.
