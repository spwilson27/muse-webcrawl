Dirichlet L-function

In mathematics, a Dirichlet L-series is a function of the form L(s, χ) = Σ χ(n) / ns over all positive integers n, where χ is a Dirichlet character and s is a complex variable with real part greater than 1. By analytic continuation, this function can be extended to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane, and is then called a Dirichlet L-function.

History and purpose

These functions were introduced in 1837 by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet to prove his theorem on prime numbers in arithmetic progressions — the first use of complex analysis in number theory. The proof requires showing L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for non-principal characters.

Properties

Dirichlet L-functions have Euler products over primes, satisfy functional equations relating L(s, χ) and L(1−s, χ̄), and for the principal character reduce essentially to the Riemann zeta function, which has a pole at s = 1. Their non-trivial zeros are conjectured by the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis to all lie on the critical line Re(s) = ½, a claim with deep consequences for the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions.

Special values

Values at integers connect to class numbers of number fields through the class number formula, and L-values appear throughout modern analytic and algebraic number theory, including in the theory of automorphic L-functions.
