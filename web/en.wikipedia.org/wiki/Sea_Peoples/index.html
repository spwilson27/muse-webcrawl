Sea Peoples

The Sea Peoples are a hypothesised confederation of seafaring raiders and migrants who attacked ancient Egypt and other regions of the eastern Mediterranean in the late Bronze Age, around 1200 BC. Egyptian inscriptions of the New Kingdom — above all those of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu — record battles against groups with names such as Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen, and Weshesh.

Sources and battles

The Sea Peoples first appear in Egyptian records during the reign of Merneptah, who defeated a coalition including the Sherden, Lukka, and Ekwesh allied with Libyans in 1207 BC. In his eighth regnal year (c. 1175 BC) Ramesses III claimed a great victory over a seaborne invasion in the Nile Delta, depicted in detail at Medinet Habu, and a land battle in Djahy (Canaan). Earlier, groups such as the Sherden had served as mercenaries in Egyptian armies.

Identity and origins

The origins of the Sea Peoples remain debated. Proposed homelands include the Aegean, Anatolia, Sicily, Sardinia, and the wider western Mediterranean; the Shekelesh have been linked to Sicily, the Sherden to Sardinia, and the Peleset — widely accepted as the biblical Philistines — to the Aegean world, a connection supported by the Aegean-style material culture of early Philistine cities and by ancient DNA evidence for European-related ancestry in early Iron Age Ashkelon.

Role in the Bronze Age collapse

The Sea Peoples are often cited among the causes of the Late Bronze Age collapse, which saw the destruction of the Hittite Empire, Ugarit, and Mycenaean palace civilisation. Modern scholarship tends to view them as one symptom and accelerator of a systems collapse involving drought, famine, earthquake, and internal revolt, rather than its sole cause.
