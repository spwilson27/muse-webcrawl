The Hussites were a Christian movement in the Kingdom of Bohemia that followed the teachings of the reformer Jan Hus, who was burned at the stake at the Council of Constance in 1415. Hus's death provoked outrage in Bohemia and set in motion decades of religious and political conflict known as the Hussite Wars.

Origins

Hus preached in Prague, notably at the Bethlehem Chapel, emphasising the authority of Scripture, preaching in the vernacular, and criticism of indulgences and clerical corruption. After his execution, his followers demanded communion sub utraque specie — in both kinds, bread and wine — for the laity, symbolised by the chalice.

The Four Articles of Prague

In 1420 the movement agreed on the Four Articles of Prague: free preaching of the word of God; communion in both kinds for all Christians; the exclusion of clergy from secular lordship and wealth; and public punishment of mortal sins. The articles united most Hussite factions, though radicals and moderates disagreed over their scope.

Factions and wars

The movement split between the moderate Utraquists of Prague and the radical Taborites, who founded the fortified town of Tábor and practised a more communal, egalitarian church order. Under commanders such as Jan Žižka and Prokop the Great, Hussite armies using wagon forts (wagenburgs) repeatedly defeated crusades proclaimed against them between 1420 and 1431.

Settlement and legacy

After the radical defeat at the Battle of Lipany (1434), the Compactata of Basel (1436) legally recognised communion in both kinds in Bohemia — an exceptional concession for the period. Utraquist Bohemia persisted until the Battle of White Mountain (1620) brought forced re-Catholicisation. The Hussite tradition later inspired the Moravian Church and remains central to Czech national memory.
