Subterranean fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Subterranean fiction is a subgenre of adventure fiction, science fiction, and fantasy that focuses on underground settings, ranging from natural caves and tunnels to entire civilisations beneath the Earth's surface, sometimes within a Hollow Earth. Such stories frequently involve the discovery of lost worlds, hidden peoples, or prehistoric survivals cut off from the surface.

History

Descents into the underworld are among the oldest narrative motifs, appearing in myths of Hades, Sheol, and katabasis in works such as the Odyssey and the Aeneid. In early modern literature, Athanasius Kircher's Mundus Subterraneus (1665) catalogued the era's speculations about the Earth's interior. The modern subgenre flourished in the nineteenth century with works such as Jules Verne's Journey to the Centre of the Earth (1864), Edward Bulwer-Lytton's The Coming Race (1871), featuring the subterranean Vril-ya, and novels of underground dwellers such as George Sand's and others' tales.

Twentieth century and beyond

Edgar Rice Burroughs' Pellucidar series, beginning with At the Earth's Core (1914), placed adventures inside a hollow Earth lit by a central sun. Subterranean settings remained popular in pulp magazines, comics, films, and role-playing games — the Underdark of Dungeons & Dragons being a well-known example — and continue in contemporary works such as Jeff VanderMeer's and Neil Gaiman's underground fantasies, and Roderick Gordon and Brian Williams' Tunnels series.

Themes

Subterranean fiction often explores themes of descent as psychological or spiritual trial, the survival of archaic worlds into the present, and encounters between surface and underground civilisations, sometimes as satire or utopian/dystopian commentary on contemporary society.
