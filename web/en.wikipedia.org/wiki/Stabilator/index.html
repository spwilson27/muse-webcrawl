Stabilator

A stabilator is a fully movable aircraft horizontal stabilizer. It serves the usual functions of a horizontal stabilizer — longitudinal stability — and also acts as the pitch control surface, combining the roles of stabilizer and elevator; the name is a portmanteau of stabilizer and elevator.

Design

Instead of a fixed stabilizer with a hinged elevator, the entire tailplane pivots about a single axis. Because the whole surface changes angle, a stabilator produces a given pitch force with a smaller deflection than a conventional elevator, and because the surface has no hinge gap it remains effective at high angles. Many stabilators incorporate an anti-servo tab, which increases control forces and provides artificial feel, discouraging over-control — important because the all-moving surface can be highly powerful and easy to overstress. Some designs place the pivot so the surface is mass-balanced to prevent flutter.

Use

Stabilators became essential on transonic and supersonic aircraft: at speeds near Mach 1, shock waves on a fixed stabilizer can render a hinged elevator ineffective, a problem encountered painfully by early jet aircraft in dives. Chuck Yeager's Bell X-1 used an adjustable all-moving tail to maintain pitch control through the transonic regime. Most modern military jets, including the F-16 and F/A-18, use slab stabilators (sometimes differentially deflected for roll augmentation). Among general aviation aircraft, the Piper PA-28 Cherokee family is the best-known stabilator user, with the surface trimmed by an anti-servo tab. Large airliners generally use a trimmable horizontal stabilizer with separate elevators rather than a true stabilator.
