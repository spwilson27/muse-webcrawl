Abhijnanashakuntalam

Abhijñānaśākuntalam

AuthorKālidāsa
LanguageSanskrit (with Prakrit passages)
GenreDrama (nāṭaka), seven acts
Full titleAbhijñānaśākuntalam — "The Recognition of Śakuntalā"
SettingAncient India; hermitage of Kaṇva; court of Duṣyanta

Abhijñānaśākuntalam (Devanagari: अभिज्ञानशाकुन्तलम्; IAST: Abhijñānaśākuntalam), also known as Shakuntala, The Recognition of Shakuntala, The Sign of Shakuntala, and many other variants, is a Sanskrit play by the ancient Indian poet Kālidāsa, dramatising the story of Śakuntalā that is told in the epic Mahābhārata. It is generally considered the finest work of Kālidāsa and one of the masterpieces of classical Sanskrit literature.

Plot

King Duṣyanta, while hunting, arrives at the hermitage of the sage Kaṇva and meets Śakuntalā, the sage's foster-daughter, born of the sage Viśvāmitra and the celestial nymph Menakā. The two fall in love and marry in the gāndharva rite, and the king departs for his capital, leaving a signet ring as a token. Lost in thoughts of him, Śakuntalā neglects to greet the irascible sage Durvāsas, who curses her: the one she dreams of will forget her. The curse is partly softened — the sight of the token will restore his memory — but the ring is lost while she bathes in a river. At court, the king, under the curse, does not recognise the pregnant Śakuntalā, who is taken away by celestial beings. Later, a fisherman finds the ring inside a fish, and the king's memory returns; after a battle fought on behalf of the gods, Duṣyanta finds Śakuntalā and their young son Bharata at the hermitage of another sage and the family is reunited.

Kālidāsa's adaptation

In the Mahābhārata version there is no curse and no ring; the king knowingly rejects Śakuntalā and is rebuked by a divine voice. Kālidāsa's additions of the curse, the ring and the recognition (the abhijñāna of the title) transform the story into a drama of separation and reunion, praised for its lyrical descriptions of nature and its portrayal of love in union and in separation.

Reception and translation

Śakuntalā was the first Indian drama translated into a European language: Sir William Jones's English translation of 1789, rendered into German in 1791, electrified European writers. Goethe famously praised the play in verse, Herder championed it, and it influenced European Romanticism and later composers and choreographers. The play survives in several recensions (Devanāgarī, Bengali and Kashmiri), and its heroine's son, Bharata, is traditionally regarded as the ancestor from whom India takes its name Bhārata.
