Infiltration (hydrology)

Infiltration is the process by which water on the ground surface enters the soil. The infiltration rate in soil science is a measure of the rate at which a particular soil is able to absorb rainfall or irrigation. It is measured in inches per hour or millimeters per hour. The rate decreases as the soil becomes saturated.

Process

Water infiltrates the soil under the influence of gravity and capillary forces. When precipitation exceeds the infiltration capacity, the excess becomes surface runoff. Infiltrated water first replenishes soil moisture; water that percolates below the root zone may recharge groundwater.

Factors affecting infiltration

Soil texture, structure, vegetation cover, antecedent moisture content, surface crusting, slope, and compaction all influence infiltration. Sandy soils generally have high infiltration rates, while clay soils have low rates. Vegetation and organic matter promote infiltration by protecting the surface and maintaining macropores.

Measurement and models

Infiltration is commonly measured with ring infiltrometers. Widely used models include the Horton equation, which describes infiltration capacity decaying exponentially over time; the Green–Ampt model, based on physical soil properties; and the empirical SCS curve number method.
