Ebola virus disease

Ebola virus disease (EVD), commonly known simply as Ebola, is a viral haemorrhagic fever of humans and other primates caused by viruses of the genus Ebolavirus, principally Zaire ebolavirus. Symptoms typically begin 2 to 21 days after infection with fever, sore throat, muscle pain and headache, followed by vomiting, diarrhoea and rash, and in severe cases internal and external bleeding and multi-organ failure. The average case fatality rate is around 50 percent.

Transmission

The disease spreads through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a sick or deceased infected person, and with contaminated surfaces and materials. It is not airborne. Fruit bats are believed to be the natural reservoir; initial human cases follow contact with infected wild animals. Burial practices involving washing or touching the body have amplified transmission in several outbreaks.

Outbreaks

The first recognised outbreaks occurred in 1976 in Nzara (Sudan) and Yambuku (Zaire, now the Democratic Republic of the Congo). The largest epidemic, in West Africa from 2013 to 2016, caused more than 28,600 cases and 11,300 deaths in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone. A major outbreak in the Kivu region of the DRC (2018–2020) recorded nearly 3,500 cases. The World Health Organization declared both emergencies of international concern.

Treatment and prevention

Supportive care — rehydration and treatment of symptoms — improves survival. Two monoclonal antibody treatments (atoltivimab/maftivimab/odesivimab and ansuvimab) are approved for Zaire ebolavirus, and the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine (Ervebo), approved in 2019, is used in ring vaccination of contacts. Survivors may suffer joint pain, fatigue and eye problems, and the virus can persist in immune-privileged sites such as the eyes and semen.
