Cattle drive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Hero image placeholder: cowboys driving a herd along a trail]

A cattle drive is the process of moving a herd of cattle from one place to another, usually by cowboys on horseback. The classic era of the long drive, from the late 1860s to the 1880s, saw millions of Texas longhorns driven north along trails such as the Chisholm Trail to railheads in Kansas, where the animals could be shipped to eastern markets and slaughterhouses.

The trail crew

A drive of a few thousand head employed about a dozen men: a trail boss, cowboys working positions around the herd (point, swing, flank, and the dusty, unenviable "drag" at the rear), a cook with his chuck wagon, and a wrangler who managed the remuda of spare horses. A typical drive covered ten to fifteen miles a day, grazing the herd as it moved so cattle arrived in marketable condition. Stampedes — triggered by storms, noise, or predators — were among the chief hazards, along with river crossings, drought, and rustlers.

Decline

The spread of railroads into Texas, the invention of barbed wire fencing off the open range, quarantine laws against Texas fever, and the catastrophic winters of the mid-1880s ended the long-drive era by the end of the nineteenth century. Shorter local drives continued, and the cattle drive passed into legend as a defining episode of the American West, celebrated in film, television, and song.

Australia

In Australia, drovers moved stock over vast distances along stock routes such as the Canning Stock Route; the drover became an equally romantic national figure, immortalised in Banjo Paterson's verse.
