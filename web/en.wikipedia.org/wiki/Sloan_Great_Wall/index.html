The Sloan Great Wall (SGW) is a cosmic structure formed by a giant wall of galaxies, a galaxy filament first reported in 2003 by J. Richard Gott III, Mario Jurić, and their colleagues from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. At roughly 1.37 billion light-years in length, it is one of the largest known structures in the observable universe.

Discovery and structure

The wall was identified in SDSS redshift data as an exceptionally dense chain of galaxy superclusters, principally the rich superclusters SCl 126, SCl 136, and SCl 82, located about a billion light-years away. It is nearly three times longer than the CfA2 Great Wall discovered in 1989, the previous record holder.

Significance

The Sloan Great Wall is not a single gravitationally bound object but an alignment of superclusters; it will disperse rather than collapse as the universe expands. Structures of this scale test cosmological models: ΛCDM simulations predict a statistical upper limit to large-scale structure, and the SGW and still-larger candidates (such as the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, whose reality is debated) are used to probe whether the cosmological principle of homogeneity holds at the largest scales.
