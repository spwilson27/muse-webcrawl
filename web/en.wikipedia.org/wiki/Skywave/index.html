Skywave

Skywave propagation
Also called: Ionospheric reflection, "skip"
Bands affected: MF and HF (approx. 300 kHz – 30 MHz)
Contrast: Ground wave, line-of-sight

Skywave propagation is a method of radio wave propagation in which waves are refracted by the ionosphere back toward the Earth, allowing them to travel far beyond the horizon — often thousands of kilometres. Skywave is the principal means of long-distance communication in the medium-wave and high-frequency (shortwave) bands, and its behaviour varies strongly with time of day, season, and the solar cycle.

Mechanism

The ionosphere's ionised layers bend radio waves progressively; above a certain angle of incidence, and below the maximum usable frequency (MUF), the wave is returned to Earth. Signals may make several "hops" between ionosphere and ground, circling the globe. The lowest usable frequency (LUF) is set by absorption, chiefly in the daytime D layer, which largely disappears at night — which is why medium-wave broadcast stations can be heard from far away after dark.

Effects and variability

Skywave signals are subject to fading as multiple paths interfere, and to sudden ionospheric disturbances from solar flares. The skip zone is an annular region between the limit of ground-wave coverage and the first skywave return where a station cannot be heard. Near-vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) uses steep angles for reliable regional coverage in the lower HF band.

Uses

Skywave supports international shortwave broadcasting, amateur radio DXing, long-range maritime and aeronautical communication, and over-the-horizon radar. In the AM broadcast band, nighttime skywave forces regulators to impose reduced power or directional patterns on many stations to limit interference.
