Mosul

Mosul (Arabic: الموصل, romanized al-Mawṣil) is a major city in northern Iraq, serving as the capital of Nineveh Governorate. Located on the west bank of the Tigris River opposite the ancient Assyrian city of Nineveh, it is one of Iraq's largest cities, second only to Baghdad. Mosul is a historically diverse city, home to Arabs, Kurds, Assyrians, Turkmens, and other communities.

History

The area of Mosul has been inhabited for millennia, lying close to Nineveh, the last capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire. Mosul grew in importance under Islamic rule, flourishing as a trading center linking Mesopotamia with Anatolia and Persia. The fine cotton fabric "muslin" takes its name from the city. Mosul passed through the hands of various dynasties, including the Hamdanids, Seljuks, Zengids — under whom Nur al-Din fortified it — and eventually the Ottoman Empire, to which it belonged for centuries.

After the First World War, the "Mosul Question" — whether the oil-rich Mosul vilayet would belong to Turkey or to British-mandate Iraq — was settled by the League of Nations in 1925 in Iraq's favor.

Recent history

In June 2014, Mosul was captured by the Islamic State (ISIL), whose leader Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi proclaimed a "caliphate" from the Great Mosque of al-Nuri. Iraqi forces, supported by an international coalition, retook the city after a nine-month battle ending in July 2017. The fighting devastated the Old City, destroyed the al-Nuri Mosque and its leaning al-Hadba minaret, and displaced hundreds of thousands of residents. Reconstruction efforts, including the UNESCO-led "Revive the Spirit of Mosul" initiative, have been under way since.

Geography and economy

Mosul sits about 400 km northwest of Baghdad. The Mosul Dam, Iraq's largest, lies upstream on the Tigris. The city's economy historically rested on trade, agriculture, textiles, and oil from nearby fields.
