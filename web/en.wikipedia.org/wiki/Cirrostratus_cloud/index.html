Cirrostratus cloud

Cirrostratus cloud
AbbreviationCs
GenusCirro- (curl) + stratus (layer)
Altitude6,000–13,000 m (20,000–43,000 ft)
AppearanceThin, whitish veil; sheet-like
PrecipitationNone reaching the ground

Cirrostratus is a high-level, thin, generally uniform cloud type composed of ice crystals. It appears as a transparent, whitish veil that can cover the entire sky, and it is the cloud most associated with producing halo phenomena around the Sun or Moon — a bright ring caused by refraction of light through its hexagonal ice crystals.

Characteristics

Cirrostratus forms at altitudes above about 6,000 metres when a broad layer of air is lifted slowly to saturation, often ahead of a warm front or a large storm system. It is thin enough that the sun remains visible and shadows are still cast on the ground, but its thickening over hours can herald approaching precipitation. The two principal species are fibratus, which shows fibrous structure, and nebulosus, which is featureless and haze-like. When very thin and uniform, cirrostratus can be nearly invisible except for the halo it produces.

Forecasting significance

A sequence of cirrus thickening into cirrostratus and then lowering into altostratus is the classic signature of an advancing warm front, typically bringing rain or snow within 12–24 hours. The old mariner's saying about a ring around the Moon predicting rain reflects this association.
