Hormonal and physiological changes across the menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is a series of natural changes in hormone production and the structures of the uterus and ovaries of the female reproductive system that makes pregnancy possible. The ovarian cycle controls the production and release of eggs and the cyclic release of oestrogen and progesterone. The uterine cycle governs the preparation and maintenance of the lining of the uterus (endometrium) to receive an embryo.

The menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. The average length is 28 days, though cycles between about 23 and 35 days are common in adults. The first period, called menarche, usually begins between the ages of twelve and thirteen. Cycles continue until menopause, when they cease permanently.

Phases

Menstrual phase

The menstrual phase, also called menstruation or a period, is the shedding of the endometrial lining together with blood through the vagina. It typically lasts three to seven days. The first day of bleeding is counted as day one of the cycle.

Follicular phase

During the follicular phase, the pituitary gland releases follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), which stimulates the growth of ovarian follicles. One follicle usually becomes dominant and matures an egg. Rising oestrogen from the developing follicle causes the endometrium to thicken again.

Ovulation

Ovulation occurs around the middle of the cycle, roughly day 14 in a 28-day cycle. A surge of luteinising hormone (LH) triggers the release of the mature egg from the ovary. The egg travels along the fallopian tube and remains viable for about 24 hours; this is the most fertile period of the cycle.

Luteal phase

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone (and some oestrogen). Progesterone prepares the endometrium for possible implantation. If fertilisation does not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates, hormone levels fall, and the endometrium is shed, beginning a new cycle. If pregnancy occurs, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) maintains the corpus luteum.

Hormonal control

The cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses, stimulating the pituitary to release FSH and LH, which act on the ovaries; ovarian hormones in turn provide feedback to the brain and pituitary.

Disorders

Common menstrual disorders include dysmenorrhoea (painful periods), amenorrhoea (absent periods), menorrhagia (heavy bleeding), and premenstrual syndrome (PMS). Conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome and endometriosis can disrupt the cycle.
