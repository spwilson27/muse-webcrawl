Ball screw

A ball screw (also known as a ballscrew) is a mechanical linear actuator that translates rotational motion to linear motion with little friction. It consists of a threaded shaft and a nut, between which a series of ball bearings roll in helical grooves; the balls are recirculated through the nut so that motion can continue indefinitely. Because the contact between screw and nut is rolling rather than sliding, ball screws have much lower friction than conventional lead screws.

Operation and characteristics

Efficiencies of ball screws are typically above 90%, compared with roughly 20–40% for a sliding (Acme) lead screw. This high efficiency makes most ball screws back-drivable: a linear force on the nut can rotate the screw, so applications that must hold a load when power is removed require a brake or counterweight. Ball screws also exhibit low wear, predictable service life, and can be preloaded—by offsetting the raceways or using a double-nut arrangement—to eliminate backlash, a critical requirement for precision positioning.

Applications

Ball screws are used wherever precise, efficient linear motion is needed: CNC machine tools and industrial robots, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, aircraft flight controls and landing-gear actuators, automotive power steering, and medical devices. Historically, they were also used in mechanical computers and in the steering of large vehicles before the widespread use of hydraulic power steering.

History

Although rolling-element screw mechanisms were described in patents in the late 19th century, practical ball screws became common in the mid-20th century, notably in aircraft and automotive steering—the latter popularised by the recirculating-ball steering box—and then in numerically controlled machine tools, where their accuracy and efficiency displaced sliding screws.
