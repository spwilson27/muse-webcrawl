Seismic base isolation

Base isolators decouple a building from ground motion

Seismic base isolation, also known simply as base isolation, is a collection of structural engineering techniques for earthquake engineering that detaches a building or other structure from the ground so that it moves much less during an earthquake, greatly reducing the forces transmitted into the superstructure and protecting both the structure and its contents.

Principles

An isolation system interposes flexible bearings between the structure and its foundation. This lengthens the structure's fundamental period of vibration — typically to two seconds or more — away from the energetic period range of earthquake ground motion, and adds damping that dissipates energy. The building then rides out the shaking as a nearly rigid body on its bearings, with large displacements concentrated at the isolation layer rather than distributed as damaging drift through the storeys.

Types of isolator

The most common devices are elastomeric bearings — stacks of rubber layers reinforced with steel plates — including lead-rubber bearings, in which a central lead core yields to provide damping. Friction pendulum bearings instead support the building on curved sliding surfaces, so that the structure swings like a pendulum during shaking and re-centres afterwards. Systems are often supplemented by viscous dampers.

Applications and performance

Base isolation is widely used in Japan, New Zealand, the United States, Chile, Turkey and elsewhere for hospitals, emergency facilities, bridges and heritage buildings that must remain functional after earthquakes. Isolated buildings performed notably well in events such as the 1995 Kobe and 2011 Christchurch earthquakes compared with conventional fixed-base construction, and the technique has become a standard option in seismic design codes.
