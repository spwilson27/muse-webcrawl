Swan-Ganz catheter

[Image placeholder: Swan-Ganz catheter]

The Swan-Ganz catheter, also known as a pulmonary artery catheter (PAC), is a balloon-tipped catheter that is inserted into a large central vein and advanced through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary artery. It is used to measure pressures in the heart and pulmonary circulation and to estimate cardiac output, providing haemodynamic information that helps guide the management of critically ill patients.

History

The catheter was developed in the late 1960s by cardiologists H. Jeremy Swan and William Ganz at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles. Ganz conceived of using a balloon at the tip so that blood flow itself would carry the catheter into the pulmonary artery, making bedside insertion possible without fluoroscopy. Swan and Ganz published their description of the technique in 1970, and the device rapidly transformed critical care and anaesthetic monitoring.

Technique

The catheter is typically introduced through the internal jugular or subclavian vein using the Seldinger technique. As it is advanced, characteristic pressure waveforms are observed as the tip passes through the right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, and finally the "wedged" position, where the inflated balloon occludes a branch of the pulmonary artery. The pulmonary artery wedge pressure approximates left atrial pressure and, indirectly, left ventricular filling pressure.

Measurements

A Swan-Ganz catheter can measure central venous pressure, right atrial and right ventricular pressures, pulmonary artery pressure, and pulmonary artery wedge pressure. Cardiac output is measured by thermodilution: a known volume of cold saline is injected and the temperature change is detected downstream. Derived values include systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance and stroke volume.

Controversy

Studies in the 1990s and 2000s, including the PAC-Man and ESCAPE trials, found no mortality benefit from routine pulmonary artery catheterisation in many critically ill populations, and use of the device has declined substantially. It remains in use in selected settings such as cardiac surgery, management of pulmonary hypertension, and complex shock states.
