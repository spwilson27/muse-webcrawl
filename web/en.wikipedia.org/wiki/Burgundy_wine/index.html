Burgundy wine (French: vin de Bourgogne) is wine made in the Burgundy region of eastern France. Reds are almost entirely Pinot noir and whites almost entirely Chardonnay; Burgundy is renowned for expressing terroir — the character of individual vineyard sites — more than perhaps any other wine region.

Geography and classification

The heartland is the Côte d'Or, a limestone escarpment divided into the Côte de Nuits (great reds such as Gevrey-Chambertin and Vosne-Romanée, home of Romanée-Conti) and the Côte de Beaune (reds and whites such as Meursault and Puligny-Montrachet), the home of Beaune. North lies Chablis, famed for lean Chardonnay; south lie the Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais. Vineyards are graded regional, village, premier cru and, at the summit, grand cru — 33 grand cru sites — under the AOC system established in 1936.

History

Viticulture dates to Roman times; Cistercian monks in the Middle Ages meticulously delineated vineyard plots (clos), and the dukes of Burgundy promoted the wine. The fragmented pattern of ownership today stems from Napoleonic inheritance laws. Burgundy's climats were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015. Top Burgundies, such as those served in beef bourguignon, are among the most expensive wines in the world.
