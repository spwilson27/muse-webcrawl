Russian farm-fox experiment

The Russian farm-fox experiment is a long-running selective-breeding study begun in 1959 in Novosibirsk, Siberia, by the Soviet geneticist Dmitry Belyayev and continued by Lyudmila Trut. By breeding silver foxes (Vulpes vulpes) solely for tameness, the experiment produced foxes that behaved much like domestic dogs within a few dozen generations.

Design and results

Starting from farm foxes bred for fur, researchers selected only the animals that showed the least fear and aggression toward humans. The tamest line soon displayed dog-like traits: tail-wagging, whining, licking, and eagerness for human contact. Remarkably, the foxes also changed physically — floppy ears, curled tails, shortened snouts, piebald coats, and altered reproductive timing — a suite of correlated traits known as "domestication syndrome." A control line bred for aggression showed the opposite behaviour.

Significance

The experiment is a leading demonstration that selection on behaviour alone can drive rapid domestication and linked morphological change, shedding light on how wolves may have become dogs. Later critics have noted that the founding foxes came from Canadian fur farms and were not fully wild, a caveat the research team has partly disputed, but the study remains a landmark in evolutionary biology.
