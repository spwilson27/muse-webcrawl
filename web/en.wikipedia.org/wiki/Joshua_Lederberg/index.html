Joshua Lederberg

Joshua Lederberg (May 23, 1925 – February 2, 2008) was an American molecular biologist who shared the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his discoveries of genetic recombination and transduction in bacteria. His work laid foundational groundwork for modern genetics and genetic engineering.

Early life and education

Lederberg was born in Montclair, New Jersey, to a Jewish family. A gifted student, he attended Stuyvesant High School in New York City and then Columbia University, receiving his bachelor's degree in zoology in 1944. During the Second World War he served in the U.S. Naval Reserve while continuing his studies.

Scientific career

At Yale University, working with Edward Tatum, Lederberg demonstrated in 1946 that bacteria could exchange genetic material through a process analogous to sexual reproduction, called conjugation. In 1952, with Norton Zinder, he discovered genetic transduction, showing that bacteriophages could carry genes from one bacterium to another. With his wife Esther Lederberg, he developed replica plating, a technique for isolating bacterial mutants.

Later work

Lederberg joined the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1947, moved to Stanford University in 1959, and served as president of Rockefeller University from 1978 to 1990. He was an early advocate for the field of exobiology, advising NASA on the search for life on Mars, and he coined the term "microbiome" in its modern sense. He also worked on artificial intelligence in chemistry through the DENDRAL project.

Legacy

Lederberg died in New York City in 2008. His papers are held at the National Library of Medicine, and he is remembered as one of the central figures of twentieth-century genetics.
