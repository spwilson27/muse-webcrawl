A harpsichord is a musical instrument played by means of a keyboard. This activates a row of levers that turn a trigger mechanism that plucks one or more strings with a small plectrum made from quill or plastic. The strings are under tension on a soundboard in a wooden case; the soundboard amplifies the vibrations from the strings so that the listeners can hear it. Like a pipe organ, a harpsichord may have more than one keyboard manual, and even a pedal board. Harpsichords may also have stop buttons which add or remove additional octaves of strings. Most commonly, harpsichords have two sets of strings that are tuned at the same pitch but produce slightly different tonal qualities.

The harpsichord was widely used in Renaissance and Baroque music, both as an accompaniment instrument and as a soloing instrument. During the Baroque era, the harpsichord was a standard part of the continuo group. The basso continuo part acted as the foundation for many musical pieces in this era. During the late 18th century, with the development of the piano, the harpsichord gradually disappeared from the musical scene (except in opera, where it continued to be used to accompany recitative). In the 20th century, it made a resurgence, being used in historically informed performances of older music, in new compositions, and, in rare cases, in certain styles of popular music (e.g., Baroque pop).

History

The harpsichord may have been invented in the late Middle Ages. By the 16th century, harpsichord makers in Italy were making lightweight instruments with low string tension. A different approach was adopted in Flanders from about 1580 by the Ruckers family, whose instruments became the most famous of all harpsichords and were copied and modified by makers throughout Europe. France, Germany, and Britain all developed their own traditions; the large double-manual instruments of makers such as Pascal Taskin in Paris and Michael Mietke in Berlin represent the final flowering of the craft before the piano displaced it.

Composers who wrote extensively for the harpsichord include William Byrd, Girolamo Frescobaldi, François Couperin, Jean-Philippe Rameau, Domenico Scarlatti — who wrote more than 550 keyboard sonatas — Johann Sebastian Bach, and George Frideric Handel. Bach's Goldberg Variations and The Well-Tempered Clavier were written for the instrument, and Handel's harpsichord suites were among the most popular keyboard works of the eighteenth century.

Mechanism

When a key is pressed, a vertical wooden strip called a jack rises; a small plectrum, mounted on a tongue pivoting in the jack, plucks the string. When the key is released, the jack falls back, the tongue pivots so the plectrum passes the string silently, and a felt damper silences the string. Because the pluck is essentially the same however hard the key is struck, the harpsichord cannot vary dynamics by touch; loudness and colour are changed instead by registration — engaging or disengaging sets of strings (called stops or registers), including 8-foot (normal pitch), 4-foot (one octave higher), and occasionally 16-foot ranks.

Varieties

Virginals

The virginals (or virginal) is a smaller, rectangular form of harpsichord popular in northern Europe in the late Renaissance and early Baroque. Despite the name, it was not exclusively played by young women, though it was strongly associated with domestic music-making.

Spinet

The spinet is a small harpsichord with a triangular or wing-shaped case and strings set at an angle to the keyboard, designed to save space.

Clavicytherium

The clavicytherium is an upright harpsichord, with the strings vertical, saving floor space and projecting the sound directly toward the listener.

Revival

The twentieth century saw a major revival of the harpsichord, led by performers such as Wanda Landowska, who commissioned modern instruments from Pleyel and championed the music of Bach and Scarlatti in concert and on record. Ralph Kirkpatrick, Gustav Leonhardt, and others drove a movement toward historically informed performance using copies of antique instruments. Modern builders largely construct instruments modelled on historical examples rather than the heavy iron-framed concert harpsichords of the early revival.
