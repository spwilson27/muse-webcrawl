Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412 – 323 BC) was a Greek philosopher and archetypal Cynic, who made a virtue of poverty and shamelessness. Exiled from Sinope for defacing the currency, he lived in Athens and Corinth in a large ceramic jar (a pithos, often rendered 'tub'), owned only a cloak, staff and wooden bowl — the last discarded on seeing a child drink from cupped hands — and performed his philosophy in public: searching with a lantern in daylight for an honest man, and, when Alexander the Great offered him any favour, asking him to stop blocking the sun.

Legacy

Diogenes' anecdotes, preserved by Diogenes Laertius, made him philosophy's great performance artist: his life was his argument that civilisation's conventions are mostly fraud and that happiness needs nothing. The Cynics influenced Stoicism (Zeno studied with Diogenes' pupil Crates) and, in modern usage, inverted: 'cynic' now means a sneering unbeliever in motives, nearly reversing his idealistic scorn.
