Field emission, also known as field electron emission or cold emission, is the emission of electrons from the surface of a material under the influence of a strong electric field. Unlike thermionic emission, no heating is required: the applied field narrows the surface potential barrier so that electrons escape by quantum tunnelling.

History

Field emission was reported experimentally in the late 19th and early 20th centuries but could not be explained classically. In 1928 Ralph Fowler and Lothar Nordheim showed that it follows naturally from the then-new quantum mechanics, deriving the Fowler–Nordheim equation. Erwin Müller used the phenomenon in his field emission microscope (1936) and field ion microscope (1951), the first instruments to image individual atoms.

Theory

An electron inside a metal is confined by a barrier related to the material's work function φ. A strong external field (on the order of volts per nanometre) deforms the barrier into a thin triangular shape a few nanometres wide. The tunnelling current density depends exponentially on both the field strength and φ3/2, as captured by the Fowler–Nordheim equation. Because macroscopic surfaces rarely sustain such fields uniformly, emitters use sharp tips or nanoscale protrusions that locally enhance the field by a factor β.

Field emitters

Traditional emitters are etched tungsten needles. Modern research uses molybdenum microtip arrays (Spindt cathodes), carbon nanotubes, silicon nanowires, and graphene edges, which combine low turn-on fields with robustness for vacuum nanoelectronics.

Applications

  • Microscopy: field emission guns provide the bright, coherent electron sources used in scanning and transmission electron microscopes.
  • Displays: field-emission displays were developed as flat-panel rivals to LCDs.
  • Vacuum electronics: microwave amplifiers, miniature X-ray tubes, and electric propulsion (field-emission electric propulsion for spacecraft) exploit cold cathodes.
  • Surface science: field ion microscopy and atom probe tomography descend directly from Müller's instruments.
