Anglo-German naval arms race

Period1898–1914
PartiesUnited Kingdom and German Empire
Key figuresAlfred von Tirpitz, John Fisher, Winston Churchill

The Anglo-German naval arms race was a competition in warship construction between the United Kingdom and the German Empire in the years before the First World War. Together with the colonial rivalries of the period, it was a major cause of the deterioration in relations between the two countries and of Britain's alignment with France and Russia.

Origins

Under Kaiser Wilhelm II and his Secretary of State for the Navy, Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, Germany passed Navy Laws in 1898 and 1900 authorising a battle fleet of 38 battleships. Tirpitz's "risk theory" held that a fleet strong enough to inflict unacceptable damage on the Royal Navy would deter Britain from war and force it to concede Germany's colonial ambitions.

The Dreadnought race

Britain responded by concentrating its fleet in home waters, reforming under Admiral John "Jackie" Fisher, and in 1906 launching HMS Dreadnought, whose all-big-gun armament and turbine propulsion made all existing battleships obsolete and restarted the race on new terms. A public scare in 1909 ("We want eight and we won't wait") pushed British building further ahead. By 1912 Germany, constrained by cost and the needs of its army, effectively conceded the race, and Britain retained a clear margin in dreadnoughts by 1914 — but the naval rivalry had already poisoned Anglo-German relations and helped drive Britain into the Entente.
