Fronde

The Fronde (French: [fʁɔ̃d]) was a series of civil wars in France between 1648 and 1653, during the minority of King Louis XIV. The name derives from the fronde, a sling with which Parisian crowds supposedly threatened the windows of Cardinal Mazarin's supporters. The revolts — first of the parlements, then of the great princes — were the last serious challenge to royal authority before the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV's personal rule.

Causes and the Fronde of the Parlement

Heavy taxation for the war against Spain and resentment of Mazarin's fiscal expedients (and of new offices sold and then taxed) provoked the Parlement of Paris to demand reforms in 1648. When the regent Anne of Austria arrested the popular councillor Pierre Broussel in August 1648, Paris rose in the Day of the Barricades. The court fled the capital in January 1649 and blockaded it; the Peace of Rueil (March 1649) ended the first phase.

The Fronde of the Princes

Ambitions of the great nobles, above all Louis II de Bourbon, Prince of Condé — the victor of Rocroi — turned the movement into aristocratic civil war. Condé was arrested in 1650, released in 1651, and then openly rebelled, seizing Paris and later entering Spanish service. Royal armies under Turenne defeated the rebels, notably at the Battle of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine (July 1652), where the Grande Mademoiselle (Anne Marie Louise d'Orléans) famously ordered the Bastille's cannon fired to let Condé's troops into Paris. Mazarin twice withdrew into tactical exile but returned in triumph in February 1653.

Consequences

The Fronde's failure discredited resistance to the crown: parlements were forbidden to interfere in state affairs, and the nobility's military power waned. Louis XIV, whose childhood memories of the court fleeing Paris shaped his distrust of the capital and the high aristocracy, resolved never to repeat the experience — a determination embodied in his personal rule after 1661 and in the palace of Versailles.
