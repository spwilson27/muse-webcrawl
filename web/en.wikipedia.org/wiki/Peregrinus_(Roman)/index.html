Peregrinus (Roman)

In the early Roman Empire, a peregrinus (Latin for "foreigner", plural peregrini) was a free provincial subject who was not a Roman citizen. Peregrini made up the great majority of the empire's population in the 1st and 2nd centuries AD, as citizenship was initially restricted to Italy and a few favoured communities and individuals elsewhere. The term is the origin of the word "pilgrim" in English, via the later Latin sense of a traveller or wanderer.

Legal status

Peregrini were governed in most private matters by the laws of their own communities (civitates peregrinae), while Roman magistrates and governors exercised jurisdiction in criminal and inter-community cases. They could not contract a Roman legal marriage, could not make a will under Roman law, and were liable to certain taxes from which citizens were exempt, such as the tributum. In Roman law, the flexible body of rules called the ius gentium ("law of nations") developed partly to handle cases involving peregrini, and a special magistrate, the praetor peregrinus, was created around 242 BC to handle litigation between foreigners, or between foreigners and citizens, at Rome.

Pathways to citizenship

Citizenship could be granted to whole communities, to individuals for distinguished service, and — most systematically — to auxiliary soldiers on honourable discharge after 25 years of service, a grant recorded on bronze military diplomas. Latin rights (ius Latii) formed an intermediate status in which local magistrates of a Latin community acquired citizenship. Over time the proportion of citizens in the provinces steadily grew.

Abolition of the status

In AD 212 the emperor Caracalla issued the Constitutio Antoniniana (Edict of Caracalla), which granted Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. The legal distinction between citizen and peregrinus thereby largely disappeared, though the edict's fiscal motives — expanding the tax base — were noted already by the historian Cassius Dio.
