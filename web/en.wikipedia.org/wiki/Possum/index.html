Possum

Possums are marsupials of the suborder Phalangeriformes, native to Australia, New Guinea, and Sulawesi, and introduced to New Zealand. There are about 70 species, including the brushtail possums, ringtail possums, gliders, and the honey possum. The name derives from the Algonquian word for the North American Virginia opossum, to which possums are only distantly related — in strict usage, "opossums" are the American marsupials of the order Didelphimorphia, while "possums" are their Australasian counterparts.

Characteristics

Possums are generally nocturnal, arboreal marsupials with prehensile tails and opposable thumbs on the hind feet for climbing. The common brushtail possum (Trichosurus vulpecula) is the largest and most familiar species, about the size of a domestic cat, and has adapted readily to urban environments. Sugar gliders and other petaurids glide between trees using a membrane between the limbs. The tiny honey possum feeds exclusively on nectar and pollen and is an important pollinator.

In New Zealand

Brushtail possums were introduced to New Zealand in 1837 to establish a fur trade. Without natural predators their numbers exploded — peaking at an estimated 70 million — and they became a devastating pest, defoliating native forests, preying on native birds' eggs and chicks, and spreading bovine tuberculosis. They are a principal target of the Predator Free 2050 programme.

Terminology

The confusion between "possum" and "opossum" is old: Captain Cook's naturalists applied the American term to the Australian animals because of their superficial resemblance. "Playing possum" refers to the opossum's death-feigning behavior, not the Australian animals.
