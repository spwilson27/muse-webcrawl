Bullion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Stacked gold bullion bars in a vault]
Gold bullion bars stored in a vault

Bullion is non-ferrous metal that has been refined to a high standard of elemental purity, traded on commodity markets in the form of bars, ingots, and coins. The word "bullion" is normally applied to the bulk metal — chiefly gold, silver, and platinum-group metals — used in the production of coins and held as reserves by governments and central banks. Its value is determined by its mass and purity rather than by any face value, which distinguishes bullion from currency.

Forms and standards

Bullion bars are stamped with their weight, fineness, refiner, and usually a serial number. The London bullion market's "Good Delivery" standard for gold specifies bars of roughly 400 troy ounces (about 12.4 kg) with a minimum fineness of 995 parts per thousand. Silver Good Delivery bars are much larger, around 1,000 troy ounces. Smaller bars of one troy ounce, 100 grams, or one kilogram are produced for private investors. Bullion purity is expressed in parts per thousand or in karats for gold (24 karat being pure).

Markets

The largest centre of bullion trading is London, where the London Bullion Market Association sets standards and a twice-daily benchmark price (the LBMA Gold Price) is published. Other major markets include New York (COMEX futures), Zurich, Hong Kong, and Shanghai. Most bullion trading is over-the-counter between banks and dealers; investors may also gain exposure through exchange-traded funds backed by allocated metal.

Reserves and investment

Central banks hold gold bullion as part of their foreign-exchange reserves, valuing it as an asset without counterparty risk; the United States holds the largest official stockpile, much of it at Fort Knox and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Private investors buy bullion as a hedge against inflation and financial instability, accepting storage and insurance costs in exchange for direct ownership of the metal. Coins struck for investors, such as the American Gold Eagle and the Canadian Maple Leaf, are treated as bullion because their value tracks the metal content rather than numismatic rarity.
