Sympathy

Sympathy is the perception, understanding, and reaction to the distress or need of another life form. It involves feelings of concern and sorrow for someone else's misfortune, without necessarily sharing their emotional state — a distinction from empathy.

Etymology

The word derives from Greek sympatheia, from syn (together) and pathos (feeling, suffering). It entered English via Latin in the late 16th century.

Philosophy

David Hume and Adam Smith placed sympathy at the centre of moral philosophy. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), Smith argued sympathy underlies moral judgement. Later philosophers distinguished sympathy from compassion and altruism.

Psychology

Sympathy involves cognitive appraisal and affective concern. It can motivate helping behaviour, though it may also produce personal distress. Related concepts include pity, empathic concern, and perspective-taking. See mirror neurons.

Expressions

Sympathy is commonly expressed through condolence messages, cards, flowers, and offers of support following bereavement or illness.
