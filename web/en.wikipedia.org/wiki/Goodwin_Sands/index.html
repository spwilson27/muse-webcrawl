Goodwin Sands

The Goodwin Sands is a 10-mile-long (16 km) sandbank at the southern end of the North Sea lying 6 miles (10 km) off the Deal coast in Kent, England. The area consists of a layer of fine sand approximately 82 ft (25 m) deep lying on an Upper Chalk platform belonging to the same geological feature that incorporates the White Cliffs of Dover. The banks lie between 0 ft 6 in (0.15 m) and 10 ft (3 m) beneath the surface, depending on the state of the tide. Tides and currents are constantly shifting the shoals.

More than 2,000 ships are believed to have been wrecked upon the Goodwin Sands because they lie in the high-traffic shipping lanes through the Straits of Dover, the busiest shipping lane in the world.

History

Legend holds that the Sands were once the fertile low-lying island of Lomea, part of the estates of Godwin, Earl of Wessex, in the 11th century. According to the legend, the island's sea walls were neglected while resources were diverted to building the church tower at Tenterden, and the land was lost to the sea in a great storm in 1099. Modern geological evidence, however, suggests the sands were never a settled island in historical times.

Notable wrecks

The Great Storm of 1703 caused devastation on the Sands: thirteen men-of-war were wrecked with the loss of around 2,000 men, an event described by Daniel Defoe. Other famous wrecks include the Dutch East Indiaman Rooswijk (1740), the Admiral van Tromp, and the passenger steamer SS Montrose. During the Second World War the Sands were mined and used for target practice.

The wreck of the American Liberty ship Richard Montgomery, which sank in the Thames Estuary nearby in 1944 with a cargo of explosives, is often confused with the Goodwin wrecks, though it lies some distance away near Sheerness.

Navigation and lightships

The danger of the Sands is marked by three lightships (now largely replaced by buoys) and the lightvessels East Goodwin, South Goodwin and North Goodwin stations. The wreck of the South Goodwin lightship, which broke free in a storm in November 1954 with the loss of all but one of her crew, is a well-known local tragedy; the sole survivor was rescued from the wreck's tower.

Sport

At spring low tides, parts of the Sands dry out and become firm enough to walk on. A cricket match was famously played on the Sands in 1824, and the tradition has been revived occasionally by local teams who sail out at low water. The Goodwin Sands are also a popular, if hazardous, spot for kitesurfing and for watching the local seal colony.

Proposed developments

Schemes have periodically been proposed to build an airport or a deep-water port on the Sands, most notably in the 1970s and again in the 2000s, but none has been carried out, partly because of the area's importance as a marine habitat and war grave site.
