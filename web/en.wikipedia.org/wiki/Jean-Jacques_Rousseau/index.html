Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) was a Genevan philosopher, writer and composer whose thought transformed politics, education and literature. His Discourse on Inequality blamed civilisation and property for corrupting natural human goodness and pity; The Social Contract (1762) opened 'Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains' and grounded legitimacy in the general will; Émile revolutionised educational thought; and his Confessions invented modern autobiography.

Influence and turmoil

Persecuted for Émile's religious profession, hounded across Europe, increasingly paranoid, Rousseau quarrelled with Hume and the philosophes who had been his friends. The French Revolution canonised him (his remains moved to the Panthéon in 1794); his legacy splits between democratic theory and critics, from Burke to Popper, who read totalitarian seeds in the general will.
