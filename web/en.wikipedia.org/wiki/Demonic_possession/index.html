Demonic possession

Demonic possession

Demonic possession is the belief that a person can be inhabited or controlled by a malevolent supernatural entity, such as a demon or evil spirit. The concept appears in many religions and cultures, from the exorcisms described in the New Testament to spirit possession traditions in Islam (jinn), Judaism (dybbuk), Hinduism, Buddhism and African and Afro-diasporic religions. The ritual removal of the possessing entity is known as exorcism.

Characteristics

Signs traditionally attributed to possession include speaking in languages unknown to the person (xenoglossy), extraordinary physical strength, aversion to sacred objects, knowledge of hidden things, violent convulsions, blasphemous speech and dramatic changes of voice or personality. The Roman Catholic Church's rite of exorcism, revised in 1999 (De exorcismis et supplicationibus quibusdam), requires authorised exorcists to rule out mental illness before concluding that possession is present, and formal criteria were set out in earlier manuals such as the Rituale Romanum of 1614.

History

Belief in possession dates to antiquity: Babylonian, Egyptian and Greek sources describe illnesses caused by spirits and their ritual expulsion. In the Gospels, Jesus is repeatedly depicted casting out demons, most famously the "Legion" of the Gerasene demoniac. Possession panics recurred in the early modern period, including the Loudun possessions (1632–1638) in France and episodes associated with the Salem witch trials. Famous modern cases include that of Anneliese Michel, a German woman who died in 1976 after months of exorcisms, whose story inspired films including The Exorcism of Emily Rose; William Friedkin's 1973 film The Exorcist, based on a 1949 case in the United States, shaped popular imagery of possession.

Medical and psychological interpretations

Modern medicine and psychology generally explain historical and contemporary reports of possession in terms of dissociative identity disorder, schizophrenia and other psychoses, epilepsy, Tourette syndrome, mania, and culturally shaped trance and possession states; the ICD-11 recognises "possession trance disorder" as a dissociative disorder when involuntary and distressing. Anthropologists distinguish pathological possession beliefs from voluntary, ritual spirit possession practised in traditions such as Vodou, Umbanda and zar cults, in which possession is a valued religious experience rather than an affliction. Surveys suggest belief in demonic possession remains widespread, and demand for exorcism has risen in some countries in recent decades.
