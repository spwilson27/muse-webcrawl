A harquebus (also spelled arquebus) is a long gun that appeared in Europe and the Ottoman Empire during the 15th century. An infantryman armed with one was called a harquebusier. The term derives from the German Hakenbüchse, "hook gun", referring to an early form with a hook used to brace the weapon against walls.

History

Early harquebuses were heavy hand cannons fitted with a matchlock mechanism, in which a lever holding a slow-burning match applied it to the priming pan. They were often fired from a forked rest. Through the 16th century, improved designs were widely adopted by Spanish, French, and other European armies, and the weapon spread to Asia, where it was used extensively in Japan as the tanegashima.

Use in battle

Harquebusiers typically fought in massed formations alongside pikemen, delivering volleys of fire. Although slow to reload and inaccurate at long range, the harquebus could penetrate armour and required far less training than the longbow. Over time, lighter and more powerful versions evolved into the musket, which replaced it by the end of the 16th century.
