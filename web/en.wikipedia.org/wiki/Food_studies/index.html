Food studies
An interdisciplinary field examining food's relationships with culture, history, politics, economics and health.

Food studies is an interdisciplinary field of inquiry concerned with food and its contexts — the examination of the relationships between food and the human experience. It involves fields such as history, anthropology, sociology, geography, literary studies, economics, public health, and philosophy, and it is distinct from food science and nutrition in focusing on food's social, cultural, political, and historical dimensions rather than its chemical or biological properties.

Scope

Food studies scholars examine how food shapes and is shaped by identity (national, ethnic, religious, class, and gender), memory, and place; how foodways move with migration and diaspora; the history of cuisines, cookbooks, restaurants, and domestic cooking; the politics of hunger, aid, trade, and agricultural policy; and the meanings of eating and abstaining, from feasts to fasts. Foundational works include anthropologist Sidney Mintz's Sweetness and Power (1985), which traced sugar's place in modern capitalism; Mary Douglas's structuralist analyses of meals; and Claude Lévi-Strauss's writings on cooking as a cultural system ("the raw and the cooked").

Academic development

The field coalesced in the late 20th century. The Association for the Study of Food and Society was founded in 1985, and journals such as Food and Foodways, Gastronomica (founded 2001), and Food, Culture & Society gave the field dedicated venues. University programs followed — notably the food studies programme established at New York University in 1996, the first of its kind in the United States, and Boston University's gastronomy programme founded earlier with Julia Child and Jacques Pépin's involvement — alongside degree programs at universities in Britain, Italy (including the Slow Food-founded University of Gastronomic Sciences at Pollenzo, 2004), Australia, and elsewhere.

Themes

  • Cuisine and identity: national dishes, culinary heritage, and UNESCO's inscription of food traditions (such as the Mediterranean diet and washoku) as intangible cultural heritage
  • Globalization: the Columbian exchange, fast food, fusion, and the localization of global brands
  • Domestic labour: gender, cooking, and the home; the professionalization of chefs
  • The body: eating disorders, obesity discourse, taste, and disgust
  • Activism: Slow Food, local food movements, food justice, and food sovereignty (promoted by La Vía Campesina)
