Mexica

The Mexica (Nahuatl: Mēxihcah, singular Mēxihcatl) were a Nahuatl-speaking people of the Valley of Mexico who founded the city of Mexico-Tenochtitlan and, through the Aztec Triple Alliance, became the rulers of the Aztec Empire. The word "Aztec" — popularised by Alexander von Humboldt — refers more broadly to the peoples of the empire, whereas "Mexica" properly denotes the inhabitants of Tenochtitlan and its sister city Tlatelolco. The modern country of Mexico takes its name from them.

Migration and foundation

According to Mexica tradition, the people left their mythical homeland of Aztlan under the guidance of their god Huitzilopochtli, wandering for generations before reaching the Valley of Mexico. There, on an island in Lake Texcoco, they saw the prophesied sign — an eagle perched on a prickly-pear cactus — and founded Tenochtitlan, traditionally in 1325. Subordinate at first to the Tepanec city of Azcapotzalco, the Mexica under Itzcoatl allied with Texcoco and Tlacopan in 1428 to form the Triple Alliance, the core of the Aztec Empire.

Society

Mexica society was organised into calpolli (wards), each with its own temple, school (telpochcalli for commoners, calmecac for nobles and priests), and lands. Society was stratified between the pipiltin (nobility), macehualtin (commoners), and enslaved persons (tlacotin), with long-distance merchants (pochteca) forming a privileged guild-like class. Farming on chinampas — raised beds in the lake — supplied the great city's food, and tribute from subject city-states brought luxuries such as cacao, feathers, jade and gold.

Religion and ritual

The Great Temple (Huey Teocalli) dominated Tenochtitlan, with twin shrines to Huitzilopochtli and the rain god Tlaloc. The Mexica practised human sacrifice on a large scale, believing the sun required nourishment to continue its course; the New Fire ceremony every 52 years marked the renewal of the calendar cycle.

Conquest

The empire fell to the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés and his Indigenous allies, above all Tlaxcala, after the siege of Tenochtitlan ended on 13 August 1521. The Mexica population was devastated by war and Old World epidemics, especially smallpox, but Nahua people and the Nahuatl language survive in Mexico today, with well over a million speakers.
