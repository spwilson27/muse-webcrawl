Dhow

A dhow with its characteristic triangular lateen sail.

Dhow (Arabic: داو) is the generic name of a number of traditional sailing vessels with one or more masts, with settee or sometimes lateen sails, used in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean region. Typically sporting long thin hulls, dhows are trading vessels primarily used to carry heavy items, such as fruit, fresh water, or other bulky merchandise, along the coasts of Eastern Arabia, East Africa, Yemen, and coastal South Asia (Pakistan, India, Bangladesh).

History

The exact origins of the dhow are debated, but Arab and Indian sailors developed these vessels over many centuries for the monsoon trade of the Indian Ocean. Historically, dhows were built with sewn-plank construction, using coir rope instead of nails — a technique described by Marco Polo and other travellers. Ibn Battuta and other medieval sources document their widespread use from East Africa to India.

Types

Many named types exist, varying by region and size: the large ocean-going baghlah and boom, the jahazi and bedeni of the East African coast, the sambuk, and the smaller jalibut. Common features include a raked stem, a long keel, and the settee (short lateen) rig.

Modern use

Motorised dhows remain important for regional trade across the Arabian Sea and along the East African coast, and traditional wooden dhows are still built in yards in Oman, India (such as Beypore), and elsewhere. Dhow racing is a popular heritage sport in the Gulf states.
