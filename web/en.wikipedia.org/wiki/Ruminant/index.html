Ruminants are herbivorous mammals of the suborder Ruminantia that are able to acquire nutrients from plant-based food by fermenting it in a specialized stomach prior to digestion, principally through microbial actions. The process, called rumination, involves regurgitating the partially digested food (the "cud"), chewing it again to further break down plant matter, and re-swallowing it — behaviour known as "chewing the cud".

The four-chambered stomach

The ruminant stomach has four compartments: the rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. Swallowed forage enters the rumen and reticulum, where a dense community of bacteria, protozoa, and fungi ferments cellulose and other plant material, producing volatile fatty acids that the animal absorbs as its main energy source. The partially fermented material is regurgitated as cud and rechewed to reduce particle size. The omasum absorbs water and minerals, while the abomasum — the "true stomach" — secretes acid and enzymes much like the stomach of a monogastric animal. Microbes washed through with the digesta are themselves digested, supplying the animal with high-quality protein.

Diversity and importance

There are roughly 200 species of ruminants, including cattle, sheep, goats, deer, giraffes, antelope, bison, buffalo, and the small mouse-deer or chevrotains, which have a simpler three-chambered stomach. Wild ruminants occupy nearly every terrestrial biome, and domesticated species are central to human agriculture, providing milk, meat, leather, wool, and draught power.

Environmental aspects

Methanogenic archaea in the rumen produce methane as a by-product of fermentation, which the animal releases mainly by belching. Enteric fermentation from livestock is therefore a significant source of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions, an active area of research in feed additives and breeding.
