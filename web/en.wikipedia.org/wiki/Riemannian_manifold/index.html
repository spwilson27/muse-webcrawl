In differential geometry, a Riemannian manifold or Riemannian space (M, g), so named after the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, is a real, smooth manifold M equipped with a positive-definite inner product gp on the tangent space TpM at each point p. The family gp varies smoothly in p and is called a Riemannian metric (tensor). These terms are named after Bernhard Riemann, who introduced the ideas in his 1854 habilitation lecture Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen ("On the hypotheses which lie at the bases of geometry").

Introduction

A Riemannian metric makes it possible to measure geometric quantities on the manifold: the length of a curve (by integrating the norm of its velocity), angles between tangent vectors, areas and volumes, and distances between points, defined as the infimum of lengths of curves joining them. Every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric, and every surface embedded in Euclidean space inherits one from the ambient inner product. Familiar examples include Euclidean space itself (with the flat metric), the sphere with its round metric, and hyperbolic space.

Geodesics and connections

The metric determines a unique torsion-free connection compatible with it, the Levi-Civita connection, from which one defines parallel transport, the exponential map, and geodesics — curves that locally minimise length and generalise straight lines. The theory of geodesics, including completeness (the Hopf–Rinow theorem) and the variation of their length, is a central part of the subject.

Curvature

Curvature measures how a Riemannian manifold deviates from being flat. The Riemann curvature tensor, built from the Levi-Civita connection, encodes this information; its contractions give the Ricci curvature and scalar curvature, while in dimension two it reduces to the Gaussian curvature. Comparison theorems (such as those of Rauch, Toponogov, and Bishop–Gromov) relate bounds on curvature to the manifold's topology and geometry. Spaces of constant sectional curvature — spheres, Euclidean spaces, and hyperbolic spaces — are the basic models, and their study culminated in results such as the Poincaré conjecture, proved by Grigori Perelman using Ricci flow on Riemannian manifolds.

Applications

Riemannian geometry provides the mathematical language of general relativity, where spacetime is modelled by a Lorentzian (pseudo-Riemannian) generalisation of these ideas. Riemannian manifolds also underpin modern work in geometric analysis, topology, data science (manifold learning), computer graphics, and robotics.
