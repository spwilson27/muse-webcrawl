Subroutine

In computer programming, a subroutine (also called a procedure, function, routine or method) is a named sequence of instructions that performs a specific task, packaged so that it can be invoked ("called") from multiple places in a program. Subroutines are a fundamental tool for abstraction, code reuse and modular design.

History

The concept dates to the earliest stored-program computers: David Wheeler developed the subroutine library for the EDSAC in the late 1940s, and the idea of closed subroutines with a standard calling convention — including the "Wheeler jump" — was described in the 1951 book The Preparation of Programs for an Electronic Digital Computer. Maurice Wilkes, Wheeler and Stanley Gill are credited with the invention.

Mechanism

A call typically pushes a return address and any arguments onto a call stack, transfers control to the subroutine, and resumes the caller when a return instruction restores the saved state. Subroutines may accept parameters and return values; those that can call themselves are recursive. Compilers may inline small subroutines to reduce call overhead.
