Comping (jazz)

In jazz, comping (an abbreviation of accompanying, or possibly from the verb to complement) is the chords, rhythms, and countermelodies that keyboard players (piano or Hammond organ), guitar players, or drummers use to support a musician's improvised solo or melody lines. It is also the action of accompanying, and the left-hand part of a solo pianist.

Role in the rhythm section

The comping instruments form part of the rhythm section, together with the bassist, who typically plays a walking bass line outlining the chord progression, and the drummer, who maintains the beat. Rather than playing chords on every beat, a comping pianist or guitarist interjects chord voicings at rhythmically varied, syncopated points — often using anticipations, Charleston rhythms and "kicks" that answer the soloist's phrases. Good comping is interactive: the accompanist listens and responds, supports the harmony without cluttering it, and shapes the dynamics of the performance.

Techniques

Pianists use rootless voicings, shell voicings and quartal harmony to leave space and let the bass carry the roots. Guitarists in the style of Freddie Green of the Count Basie Orchestra play steady four-to-the-bar chords, while others, such as Jim Hall, favour sparse, conversational accompaniment. Drummers also "comp" on the snare and bass drum behind soloists while keeping time on the ride cymbal and hi-hat. During a piano or guitar solo, the roles shift as the other chordal instrument takes over the comping.
