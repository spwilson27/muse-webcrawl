The Paris Peace Accords, officially the Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet Nam, was a peace agreement signed on January 27, 1973, to establish peace in Vietnam and end the Vietnam War. The agreement was signed by the governments of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North Vietnam), the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam), and the United States, as well as the Provisional Revolutionary Government (PRG), representing the Viet Cong.

Terms

The accords provided for a ceasefire throughout Vietnam, the complete withdrawal of U.S. and allied forces within 60 days, the return of prisoners of war, and the recognition of the PRG as a party to the future political settlement. Crucially, North Vietnamese troops were allowed to remain in place in the South, while the question of Vietnam's reunification was deferred to future negotiations between North and South.

Negotiations

Talks had begun in Paris in 1968, but secret negotiations between U.S. National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger and North Vietnamese politburo member Lê Đức Thọ proved decisive. A breakthrough came in October 1972, though the final signing was delayed after South Vietnamese President Nguyễn Văn Thiệu objected to the terms and the U.S. launched the "Christmas bombings" (Operation Linebacker II) in December 1972. Kissinger and Lê Đức Thọ were jointly awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize, which Lê declined.

Aftermath

The last U.S. combat troops left Vietnam on 29 March 1973. Fighting between North and South resumed almost immediately, however, and the ceasefire effectively collapsed. In April 1975, North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon, ending the war and leading to the reunification of Vietnam under communist rule in 1976.
