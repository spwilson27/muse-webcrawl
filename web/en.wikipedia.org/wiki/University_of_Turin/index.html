The University of Turin (Italian: Università degli Studi di Torino, UNITO) is a public research university in the city of Turin, in the Piedmont region of north-western Italy. Founded in 1404, it is one of the oldest universities in Europe and continues to play an important role in training and research, enrolling roughly 81,000 students.

History

The Studium Generale was established in 1404 under Prince Louis of Achaea, with a bull from the Avignon pope Benedict XIII, at a time when Turin was the capital of the Duchy of Savoy. Erasmus of Rotterdam studied there in 1506. The university was repeatedly reformed by the Savoy dukes and, after the unification of Italy, became a centre of Italian intellectual life; figures associated with it include the Nobel laureates Rita Levi-Montalcini, Renato Dulbecco, and Salvador Luria, all of whom studied medicine at Turin, as well as Cesare Pavese, Primo Levi, Norberto Bobbio, and Umberto Eco, who graduated in philosophy there in 1954.

Organisation

The university is organised into departments spanning agriculture, chemistry, computer science, economics, law, medicine, pharmacy, physics, psychology, veterinary medicine, philosophy, literature, and foreign languages, among others. Its main seat is the Palazzo del Rettorato in central Turin, and in recent years many faculties have moved to the Luigi Einaudi Campus on the banks of the Dora Riparia. UNITO operates major specialised centres, hospitals, the Egyptian Museum collaboration, and the Botanic Gardens of Turin.
