Thomas Crombie Schelling (14 April 1921 – 13 December 2016) was an American economist and professor of foreign policy, national security, nuclear strategy, and arms control. He shared the 2005 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Robert Aumann "for having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through game-theory analysis."

Career

Schelling earned his doctorate in economics from Harvard in 1951, worked on the Marshall Plan and in the Truman administration, and taught at Yale and Harvard before joining the University of Maryland. Much of his influential work was done at the RAND Corporation, where he applied game theory to the central strategic problem of the age: how thermonuclear war could be avoided.

Contributions

His book The Strategy of Conflict (1960) recast bargaining as a mixed-motive game, introducing the idea of focal points — salient options that people coordinate on without communication — and analysing threats, promises, commitments, and brinkmanship, "the threat that leaves something to chance." Arms and Influence (1966) developed the theory of coercive violence, distinguishing deterrence from compellence. In Micromotives and Macrobehavior (1978), his checkerboard model showed how mild individual preferences can produce stark patterns of residential segregation nobody intended. He later wrote influentially on addiction as self-command problems and on the economics of climate change.
