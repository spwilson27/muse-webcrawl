Galvanometer

A galvanometer is a type of sensitive electromechanical instrument for detecting and measuring electric current. Named after Luigi Galvani, it works by deflecting a pointer (or a beam of light) in response to current flowing through a coil in a magnetic field. Galvanometers were the first instruments to detect and measure small amounts of electric current, and they formed the basis of later ammeters and voltmeters.

History

Hans Christian Ørsted's discovery in 1820 that a current deflects a compass needle led Johann Schweigger to build the first galvanometer the same year by coiling wire around a compass to multiply the effect. André-Marie Ampère developed similar instruments, and Leopoldo Nobili's astatic galvanometer (1825) greatly improved sensitivity. In the 1880s Jacques-Arsène d'Arsonval and Edward Weston developed the moving-coil, permanent-magnet design — the D'Arsonval movement — that became standard.

Types and operation

In a moving-coil galvanometer, current through a coil suspended between the poles of a magnet produces a torque proportional to the current; a spring (or torsion fibre) balances it, so deflection is proportional to current. Mirror galvanometers, developed by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) for transatlantic telegraphy, reflect a light beam from a tiny mirror on the suspension, giving extreme sensitivity for detecting minute currents. Ballistic galvanometers measure total charge from a transient pulse. With added series or shunt resistors, the basic movement becomes a voltmeter or ammeter.

Uses

Historically, galvanometers were essential in telegraphy, electrical laboratories, and bridge measurements (as null detectors in Wheatstone bridges). Analog panel meters based on the moving-coil movement remain in use, though digital multimeters have replaced galvanometers for most purposes; the mechanism also survives in laser-scanning "galvo" mirrors.
