Yodeling

Stylistic origins: Alpine herding calls, folk song
Cultural origins: Central Europe, especially the Alps
Related forms: Alpine folk music, country music yodel, kulning

Yodeling (also spelled jodeling) is a form of singing that involves repeated and rapid changes of pitch between the low chest voice and the high head voice or falsetto. The technique is most strongly associated with the mountainous regions of Central Europe — Switzerland, Austria, and southern Germany — where it developed from the calls used by herders to communicate across Alpine valleys and summon livestock.

Origins and Alpine tradition

Herders' calls, cow-bells, and the alphorn formed a family of mountain sound practices. The wordless natural yodel (Juchzer) and song yodels became central to Alpine folk music in the 19th century, when travelling Tyrolean singing families popularised the style across Europe and North America. In Switzerland, organised yodel choirs and festivals, overseen by associations founded in the early 20th century, remain an important part of folk culture.

Global spread

Yodel-like techniques appear in many cultures, including Central African Pygmy singing, Scandinavian kulning, and Hawaiian falsetto. In the United States, yodeling entered country music through performers such as Riley Puckett and, most famously, Jimmie Rodgers, whose "blue yodels" of the late 1920s fused blues with Alpine technique. Singers from Hank Williams to Taylor Ware carried the style into later country, western, and popular music.

Technique

The yodel depends on a deliberate register break: the singer alternates rapidly between modal ("chest") phonation and falsetto ("head") phonation, often on nonsense syllables such as "yodel-ay-ee". Skilled yodellers use wide interval leaps, and the form is celebrated in competitions and record attempts; the world's longest continuous yodels have lasted many hours.
