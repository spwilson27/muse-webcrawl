A supersonic aircraft is an aircraft capable of supersonic flight — flight faster than the speed of sound (Mach 1). Most supersonic aircraft have been military fighters, bombers and reconnaissance types, together with experimental research aircraft. Only two supersonic airliners have entered passenger service: the Anglo-French Concorde and the Soviet Tupolev Tu-144, both now retired.

History

On 14 October 1947 the rocket-powered Bell X-1, piloted by Captain Charles "Chuck" Yeager, became the first crewed aircraft to exceed Mach 1 in level flight, reaching about Mach 1.06 high over the Mojave Desert. Swept wings, thin airfoils, area-ruled fuselages and powerful afterburning jet engines quickly made supersonic speed routine for fighters: the North American F-100 Super Sabre (1954) was the first production fighter capable of supersonic level flight, and the Lockheed F-104 Starfighter (1958) reached Mach 2. The extreme of the type is represented by the Mach 3.2 Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird reconnaissance aircraft.

Design considerations

Supersonic aircraft face sharply increased wave drag near Mach 1, shifting centres of pressure, high skin temperatures from aerodynamic heating, and sonic booms that restrict overland supersonic flight in many countries. Their designs use thin, swept or delta wings, slender fuselages shaped according to the area rule, powerful engines with variable-geometry intakes, and flight control systems able to cope with changing stability across the speed range.

Civil supersonic flight

Concorde entered service in 1976, cruising at Mach 2 and about 60,000 ft, halving transatlantic flight times; the Tu-144 flew briefly in Soviet service. High operating costs, noise restrictions, limited range and the 2000 Concorde crash led to retirement in 2003. In the 21st century, companies such as Boom Supersonic and research programmes such as NASA's X-59 quiet-supersonic demonstrator aim to revive civil supersonic travel with greatly reduced sonic booms.
