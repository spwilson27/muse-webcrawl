Sleight of hand

Sleight of hand
Also called: Prestidigitation, legerdemain
Used in: Close-up magic, card magic, cheating
French: prestidigitation

Sleight of hand (also prestidigitation or legerdemain) is the set of fine motor skills and deceptive techniques used by entertainers — usually magicians — to secretly manipulate objects such as cards and coins. Combined with misdirection, naturalness of movement, and patter, sleight of hand creates the impression of impossible feats while the method remains hidden from the audience.

Techniques

Fundamental moves include the palm (secretly holding an object in the hand), the false transfer (appearing to pass an object from one hand to the other while retaining it), the switch, the false shuffle and cut in card work, the pass (secretly transposing parts of the deck), and the force (making a spectator's "free" choice predetermined). Coin magic adds vanishes, productions, and the classic French drop.

History

Sleight of hand is ancient: the cups and balls routine dates to antiquity and appears in Egyptian tomb imagery and Roman accounts. The 1584 English work The Discoverie of Witchcraft exposed sleights to refute claims of sorcery. In the 19th and 20th centuries, performers such as Dai Vernon ("the Professor") elevated card and coin sleight to an art characterised by subtlety and natural handling.

Other uses

The same skills are used dishonestly by card cheats and con artists in games such as three-card monte, and — more benignly — by pickpockets performing in entertainment acts, and by bartenders and flair performers.
