Noble cause corruption

Noble cause corruption is corruption committed in the name of good ends — misconduct by police or other officials who believe that the desirable result of their actions (such as convicting a person they believe to be guilty) justifies the illegal or unethical means used to achieve it. Examples include planting evidence, falsifying testimony, coercing confessions, and conducting unlawful searches.

Concept

The concept was developed in police ethics literature, notably by John Kleinig in The Ethics of Policing (1996) and by John Crank and Michael Caldero in Police Ethics: The Corruption of Noble Cause (2000). Crank and Caldero argue that the "noble cause" — the commitment to protect society and punish the guilty — can itself become a corrupting ideal when officers come to value outcomes over lawful process.

Relation to consequentialism

Noble cause corruption is an example of ends-oriented (consequentialist) reasoning applied within institutions normally bound by deontological constraints such as due process. Commentators note the similarity to the "Dirty Harry problem," named after the film character, in which an officer faces the choice of breaking rules to stop someone he believes is a dangerous criminal.

Consequences

Beyond wrongful convictions, noble cause corruption undermines public trust and the legitimacy of the justice system. Cases exposed by DNA exonerations have repeatedly revealed officers or prosecutors who cut corners because they were convinced of a suspect's guilt.
