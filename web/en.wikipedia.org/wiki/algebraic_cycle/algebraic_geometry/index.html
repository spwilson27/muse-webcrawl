Algebraic cycle

In mathematics, an algebraic cycle of an algebraic variety is, roughly speaking, a formal linear combination of subvarieties of that variety. Algebraic cycles play a central role in algebraic geometry, generalizing the notions of curves on a surface and divisors, and uniting geometry with the study of cohomology theories.

Definition

Let X be a smooth projective variety over a field. A cycle of codimension p (or dimension d) on X is a formal finite sum Σ ni [Vi], where the Vi are irreducible subvarieties of the given codimension and the coefficients ni are integers (or, in variants of the theory, rational numbers). The free abelian group generated by such cycles is denoted Zp(X).

Equivalence relations

Cycles are studied modulo various equivalence relations. Two cycles are rationally equivalent if their difference is the divisor of a rational function on a subvariety one dimension higher; quotienting by rational equivalence yields the Chow groups of X. Coarser relations include algebraic equivalence, homological equivalence (with respect to a chosen cohomology theory), and numerical equivalence. The standard conjectures of Grothendieck concern the relationships between these notions.

Intersection theory and applications

Cycles can be intersected, and a well-developed intersection theory allows one to "count" geometric objects, as in classical enumerative geometry — for instance, the famous count of 27 lines on a cubic surface or 3264 conics tangent to five given conics. Cycles also appear in the formulation of the Hodge conjecture, one of the Millennium Prize Problems, which asks whether certain cohomology classes on a smooth projective complex variety come from algebraic cycles.
