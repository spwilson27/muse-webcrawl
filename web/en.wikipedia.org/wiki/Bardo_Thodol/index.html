Bardo Thodol

The Bardo Thodol (Tibetan: བར་དོ་ཐོས་གྲོལ, "Liberation Through Hearing During the Intermediate State"), commonly known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead, is a funerary text of the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism. It describes the experiences of consciousness during the bardo—the intermediate state between death and rebirth—and is read aloud to the dying and the recently dead to guide them toward liberation or a favorable rebirth.

Origins

Tradition attributes the text to Padmasambhava, the eighth-century master who brought Buddhism to Tibet, who is said to have concealed it as a terma (treasure text) to be revealed later. It was discovered by the treasure-revealer Karma Lingpa in the fourteenth century as part of a larger cycle of teachings. The text forms part of a broader literature on the six bardos, including the bardo of dying, the bardo of dharmata, and the bardo of becoming.

Contents

The work guides the deceased through the visions that arise after death: first the clear light of reality, then the dazzling appearances of peaceful deities, then wrathful deities—all to be recognized as projections of one's own mind. If liberation is not achieved at these stages, the instructions continue through the judgment and the approach toward rebirth in one of the six realms. In Tibetan practice, a lama reads the instructions into the ear of the corpse over a period of up to 49 days, the maximum duration of the intermediate state.

Western reception

The English name was coined by editor W. Y. Evans-Wentz for his 1927 translation, made with Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, echoing the Egyptian Book of the Dead. Carl Jung wrote a psychological commentary for a later edition, and the text influenced Timothy Leary, Ralph Metzner, and Richard Alpert's 1964 manual The Psychedelic Experience. Numerous scholarly translations have since appeared, including a complete translation of Karma Lingpa's cycle by Gyurme Dorje (2005).
