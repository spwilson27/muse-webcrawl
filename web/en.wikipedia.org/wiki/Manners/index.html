Manners

Manners are the unenforced standards of conduct that demonstrate a person to be proper, polite and refined within a society. Manners are a subset of social norms governing interpersonal behaviour — covering greetings, conversation, dress, dining and deference — and they vary widely across cultures and historical periods while tending to promote social harmony, signal respect and ease interaction between strangers.

Relationship to etiquette and morals

Manners are often distinguished from etiquette, the codified rules of specific settings (court protocol, formal dining, business), and from morals and ethics, which concern right and wrong in a deeper sense. A person may know etiquette without possessing good manners if the rules are applied without consideration for others' comfort; conversely, genuine politeness may show through even where formal rules are unknown. Sociologists such as Norbert Elias, in The Civilizing Process (1939), traced the historical development of European manners — covering bodily functions, table behaviour and speech — as part of the formation of state societies and self-restraint.

History of conduct literature

Instruction in manners has a long literary tradition, from Ptahhotep's maxims in ancient Egypt, through medieval courtesy books and Erasmus's De Civilitate (1530), to Emily Post's Etiquette (1922) and modern advice columns. Such works both reflected and shaped the expectations of their societies.

Cultural variation

Behaviours counted as polite in one culture may be rude in another: examples include greeting customs (bowing, handshakes, cheek kissing), rules about shoes indoors, eating with hands versus utensils, directness in speech, and the acceptability of lateness. Travellers and diplomats are traditionally advised to study local conventions of politeness.
