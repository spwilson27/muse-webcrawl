California genocide

The California genocide refers to the killing of thousands of Native Americans in California by agents of the United States government and by settlers, principally during the mid-19th century following the California Gold Rush. Between 1846 and 1873, the Indigenous population of California fell from roughly 150,000 to about 30,000. Historians have documented hundreds of massacres, as well as deaths through disease, starvation, displacement, enslavement and the destruction of food sources, many occurring with the funding, authorisation or participation of state and federal authorities.

Background and course

Indigenous Californians had already suffered catastrophic decline under the Spanish mission system and Mexican rule. After the United States seized California in the Mexican–American War (1846–48) and gold was discovered in 1848, a flood of settlers entered the state. The first governor, Peter Hardeman Burnett, declared in 1851 that “a war of extermination will continue to be waged between the races until the Indian race becomes extinct”. The state legislature passed the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians, which in practice legalised the indenture and enslavement of Native people, and funded militia expeditions against Native communities; the federal government later reimbursed much of that expenditure. Massacres documented by historians include the Bloody Island massacre of Pomo people (1850), the Yontocket and Achulet massacres of Tolowa people (1853), the Indian Island massacre of Wiyot people (1860), and the Round Valley campaigns against the Yuki, among many others.

Recognition

Scholars such as Benjamin Madley have argued that the events meet the definition of genocide under the UN Convention, a characterisation now reflected in official recognition: in June 2019, Governor Gavin Newsom issued a formal apology to California Native Americans, describing the state's actions as genocide, and established a Truth and Healing Council. The University of California and other institutions have likewise acknowledged their historical roles. Debate continues over terminology, periodisation and the relative weights of killing, policy and disease, but the scale of the catastrophe — among the most severe demographic collapses of Indigenous peoples in North America — is broadly accepted.
