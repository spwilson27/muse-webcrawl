Reverse osmosis

Reverse osmosis (RO) is a water purification process that uses a partially permeable membrane to separate water molecules from other dissolved and suspended substances. In natural osmosis, solvent moves through a membrane from a region of low solute concentration to one of high solute concentration. Reverse osmosis applies external pressure greater than the osmotic pressure to the more concentrated side, forcing water molecules through the membrane in the opposite direction while dissolved salts, organics, bacteria and pyrogens are largely retained and discharged in a concentrated reject stream (brine).

History

The phenomenon of osmosis through semipermeable membranes was observed in the eighteenth century by Jean-Antoine Nollet. Practical reverse osmosis became possible with the development of synthetic membranes in the 1950s; the Loeb–Sourirajan cellulose acetate membrane (c. 1959–1960) at UCLA made the process commercially promising. The first commercial RO plant began operation in Coalinga, California, in 1965. Thin-film composite polyamide membranes, developed in the 1970s, remain the industry standard.

Process

Feed water is pressurised by a high-pressure pump and passed across the membrane surface in cross-flow. Purified water (permeate) passes through the membrane; the remainder (concentrate) carries away rejected contaminants, preventing fouling. Pretreatment—filtration, softening or antiscalant dosing, and chlorination control—protects the membrane. Seawater systems recover energy from the high-pressure brine using isobaric pressure exchangers, cutting energy consumption to roughly 3 kWh per cubic metre of product water.

Applications

  • Desalination — RO is the leading technology for turning seawater and brackish water into drinking water, used from municipal plants in Israel, Singapore and Australia to naval vessels and submarines.
  • Residential purification — under-sink units remove lead, nitrates, arsenic, fluoride and many other contaminants.
  • Industrial use — boiler feed water, pharmaceutical and semiconductor ultrapure water, and wastewater reclamation.
  • Food and beverage — concentration of juices and milk, dealcoholisation of beer, and production of maple syrup (removing water from sap).

Limitations

RO wastes a portion of feed water as brine, removes beneficial minerals along with contaminants, and cannot remove some small, uncharged molecules (such as certain pesticides and dissolved gases) without additional treatment. Membranes are subject to fouling, scaling and chemical degradation by chlorine.
