Antiochus Kantemir

Prince Antiochus Dmitrievich Kantemir (Russian: Антиох Дмитриевич Кантемир; 21 September 1708 – 11 April 1744) was a Moldavian-born Russian statesman, diplomat and poet, regarded as one of the founders of modern Russian literature. He was the youngest son of Dimitrie Cantemir, the Moldavian prince and scholar.

Life and career

Kantemir was born in Constantinople (Istanbul) and came to Russia after his father's failed campaign against the Ottomans in 1711. Educated in St Petersburg, he served as Russia's resident minister in London (1732–1736) and ambassador in Paris (1738–1744), where he moved in Enlightenment circles and corresponded with Montesquieu.

Literary work

Kantemir's nine verse Satires (written from 1729) attacked ignorance, corruption and the opponents of Peter the Great's reforms, and are considered the starting point of Russian secular poetry. He also wrote a philosophical treatise, Letters on Nature and Man, translated Fontenelle and Horace, and composed fables and epigrams. His works, written in a syllabic verse later superseded by the reforms of Trediakovsky and Lomonosov, were published posthumously in 1762.
