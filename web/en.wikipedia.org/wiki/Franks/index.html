Franks

The Franks (Latin: Franci) were a group of related Germanic peoples who first appear in Roman sources in the 3rd century AD, living near the Lower Rhine. From their heartland in what is now the Netherlands, Belgium, and the German Rhineland, they expanded into Roman Gaul and ultimately founded the Frankish kingdoms that became the core of medieval France and Germany.

Origins

The Franks comprised several tribes, principally the Salian Franks and the Ripuarian Franks. They raided Roman territory during the crisis of the 3rd century and later served Rome as federates (foederati), guarding the Rhine frontier. As Roman authority collapsed in the 5th century, Frankish kings filled the vacuum.

Merovingian kingdom

Clovis I (reigned 481–511), of the Merovingian dynasty, united the Frankish tribes, defeated the last Roman ruler in Gaul at Soissons (486), conquered the Visigothic kingdom at Vouillé (507), and converted to Catholic Christianity — a decision that aligned his kingdom with the Gallo-Roman population and the Church. His descendants partitioned the realm among their heirs, a practice that repeatedly fragmented Frankish power.

Carolingian empire

Power shifted to the mayors of the palace, culminating in Charles Martel's victory near Tours in 732. His son Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian in 751 and founded the Carolingian dynasty. Pepin's son Charlemagne conquered much of western and central Europe and was crowned Emperor by the Pope in 800. The Treaty of Verdun (843) divided the empire among Charlemagne's grandsons into West, Middle, and East Francia — forerunners of France and Germany.

Society and legacy

The Franks gave their name to France and to the region of Franconia. Their legal tradition, the Salic law, and their fusion of Germanic and Roman institutions shaped medieval Europe. In the Levant and eastern Mediterranean, "Frank" became a generic term for Western European Christians, especially Crusaders.
