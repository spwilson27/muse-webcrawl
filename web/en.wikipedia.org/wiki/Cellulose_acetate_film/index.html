Cellulose acetate film, or safety film, is a photographic and motion-picture film base made from cellulose acetate, developed as a non-flammable replacement for the dangerously combustible cellulose nitrate film stock used in the early cinema industry.

History

Cellulose acetate was first prepared in 1865 and produced commercially as fibre and lacquer in the early 20th century. Kodak introduced cellulose diacetate film for amateur use in 1909 and for the 16 mm format in 1923, marketing acetate stock as safety film because it burned slowly and self-extinguished compared with nitrate. Professional 35 mm production continued on nitrate until the late 1940s, when improved cellulose triacetate bases became strong and stable enough; Kodak ceased nitrate manufacture in 1951, and acetate became the industry standard until polyester (PET) bases gained ground late in the century.

Vinegar syndrome

Acetate film is subject to a characteristic decay known as vinegar syndrome: hydrolysis of the acetate base releases acetic acid (smelling of vinegar), which autocatalytically accelerates further breakdown, causing shrinkage, brittleness, buckling and eventual destruction of the image. Archivists monitor degradation with A-D strips and store acetate film in cold, dry conditions to slow the reaction; affected films are prioritised for duplication and digitisation.
