Rolling (metalworking)

In metalworking, rolling is a metal forming process in which metal stock is passed through one or more pairs of rolls to reduce the thickness and to make the thickness uniform, and/or to impart a desired mechanical property. The concept is similar to the rolling of dough. Rolling is classified according to the temperature of the metal rolled: if the temperature is above its recrystallization temperature, the process is known as hot rolling; if below, it is cold rolling.

Hot rolling of a steel slab in a rolling mill

Hot rolling

Hot rolling is performed above the recrystallization temperature, so the workpiece deforms easily and does not strain-harden during the process. It is used to produce most bulk steel products — I-beams, rails, plate, sheet, and bar — from cast slabs, blooms, and billets. Because the metal is scaled and the final dimensions are less precise, hot-rolled products are typically used where exact tolerances are not required. The blue-grey mill scale on hot-rolled steel is a layer of iron oxides formed at high temperature.

Cold rolling

Cold rolling increases the strength of the metal by strain hardening (by up to about 20%) and produces a smoother surface and tighter dimensional tolerances. Cold-rolled sheet, strip, and foil are used for automobile body panels, appliances, and cans. Subsequent annealing may be used to restore ductility.

Rolling mills

A rolling mill consists of one or more roll stands, each holding a pair (or cluster) of work rolls supported by larger backup rolls. Configurations include two-high, three-high, four-high, and cluster (Sendzimir) mills for very thin foil. Shaped products are made with grooved rolls that progressively form the profile across successive stands.

Defects

Characteristic rolling defects include edge cracking, alligatoring, wavy edges, and centre buckling, arising from uneven deformation, friction effects, and roll deflection across the width of the strip.
