Galactic plane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The galactic plane is the plane on which the majority of a disc-shaped galaxy's mass lies. The directions perpendicular to the galactic plane point to the galactic poles. In actual usage, the terms galactic plane and galactic poles usually refer specifically to the plane and poles of the Milky Way, in which planet Earth is located.

Some galaxies are irregular and do not have a well-defined disc. Even in the case of a barred spiral galaxy like the Milky Way, defining the galactic plane is slightly imprecise and arbitrary since the stars are not perfectly coplanar. In 1959 the IAU defined the position of the Milky Way's galactic north pole as exactly RA = 12h 49m, Dec = 27° 24′ in the then-used B1950 epoch; in the currently used J2000 epoch, after precession is accounted for, its position is RA 12h 51m 26.282s, Dec 27° 07′ 42.01″.

Galactic coordinate system

The galactic plane underlies the galactic coordinate system, in which positions are expressed as galactic longitude (l) and galactic latitude (b) measured from the plane. The zero point of longitude is set toward the Galactic Center in Sagittarius. Along the plane lies the zone of avoidance — the band of sky obscured at visible wavelengths by interstellar dust concentrated in the plane.

Formation

Disc galaxies acquire their planar structure as gas cools and collapses while conserving angular momentum: material perpendicular to the net rotation axis settles into a thin, rotating disc. Most stars, gas, and dust of a spiral galaxy reside in or near this plane, while globular clusters and dark-matter halo populations are distributed more spherically.
