Dutch Waterline

The Dutch Waterline (Dutch: Hollandsche Waterlinie) was a series of water-based defences in the Netherlands.

The Dutch Waterline (Dutch: Hollandsche Waterlinie) was a series of water-based defences conceived in the early 17th century by Maurice of Nassau. Combined with natural bodies of water, the line could be used to transform the economic heartland of the Dutch Republic, almost all of it below sea level, into an island that was almost impossible to reach. It consisted of a system of locks, sluices, forts and inundation areas stretching from the Zuiderzee in the north to the Biesbosch in the south.

History

The idea of using water defences dated back to the Eighty Years' War, when dikes were breached to relieve the Siege of Leiden in 1574 and flood the surrounding polders. A formal line was developed under Prince Maurice of Orange and his half-brother Frederick Henry. The line first filled its important defensive role during the Rampjaar of 1672, when troops under Louis XIV invaded the Dutch Republic and were stopped before Amsterdam.

The line was tested again during the French Revolutionary Wars in 1794–95, when a severe frost froze the water and French forces simply walked across the ice. Improvements led to the construction of the New Dutch Waterline in the 19th century, slightly further east, with forts built to defend the locks and dikes that controlled the inundations. The line was mobilised in 1870, 1914 and 1939, but was rendered obsolete by air power; in May 1940 German forces largely bypassed it.

Description

The line protected the provinces of Holland, and later the cities of Amsterdam and Utrecht. By opening sluices and cutting dikes, the land could be flooded to a depth of about 30 centimetres (12 inches) — too deep for infantry to wade through comfortably and too shallow for boats. Roads crossing the line were guarded by forts, and higher ground that could not be inundated was defended by additional works.

Legacy

Today many of the forts survive, and the New Dutch Waterline was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2021 as an extension of the Defence Line of Amsterdam site. The recreated line now forms part of hiking and cycling routes.
