Battle of Carillon

The Battle of Carillon, also known as the Battle of Ticonderoga (1758), was fought on 8 July 1758 during the French and Indian War (the North American theater of the Seven Years' War). A British army of some 16,000 men under General James Abercrombie assaulted the entrenched French position at Fort Carillon (later Fort Ticonderoga) on Lake Champlain, defended by about 3,600 French and Canadian troops under the Marquis de Montcalm. The frontal attacks were repulsed with very heavy losses in one of the bloodiest battles of the war in America.

Background

For the 1758 campaign Britain mounted a huge effort against New France. Abercrombie's army — the largest yet assembled in North America, including the 42nd (Black Watch) Highlanders — advanced up Lake George toward Fort Carillon. The landing was marred on 6 July by a confused skirmish in which Brigadier George Howe, the army's most admired officer, was killed. Montcalm, expecting attack, built a formidable defensive line on the heights before the fort: a thick breastwork fronted by a tangle of felled trees (abatis) that channeled attackers into fields of fire.

Battle

On 8 July, misled by an engineer's report that the works could be rushed, Abercrombie ordered repeated frontal assaults without waiting for his artillery. For hours British and provincial regiments advanced into the abatis and were shot down by French musketry from the breastwork; the Black Watch famously fought its way to the wall itself. By evening the attacks had failed with roughly 2,000 British casualties against a few hundred French, and Abercrombie withdrew down the lake.

Aftermath

Carillon was France's most lopsided victory of the war, celebrated in Canada and France, but it could not change the strategic balance. The following year a British army under Jeffery Amherst advanced in strength, and the French blew up and abandoned the fort in July 1759.
