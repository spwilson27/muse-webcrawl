Contingency (philosophy)

In philosophy and logic, contingency is the status of propositions and entities that are neither necessarily true nor necessarily false. A contingent proposition is true in some possible worlds and false in others: it could have been otherwise. The concept is central to modal logic and metaphysics, where it contrasts with necessity and impossibility.

FieldModal logic, metaphysics
Contrasts withNecessity, impossibility
Key thinkersAristotle, Leibniz, Aquinas, Kripke

Contingent propositions and beings

A statement such as "the cat is on the mat" is contingent: it is in fact true or false, but might have been the reverse. By contrast, mathematical truths are often held to be necessary, and contradictions impossible. In medieval and early modern philosophy, a contingent being is one that depends on something else for its existence and could fail to exist; cosmological arguments, such as those of Thomas Aquinas and Leibniz, reason from the existence of contingent beings to a necessary being. Leibniz also held that contingent truths, unlike necessary truths, require infinite analysis and are known through God's choice of the best possible world.

Modal logic

In modern modal logic, contingency is expressed using the possibility (◇) and necessity (□) operators: a proposition p is contingent when ◇p ∧ ◇¬p — it is possible and its negation is also possible. Saul Kripke's possible-worlds semantics gave this framework a rigorous model theory, and sparked debate over whether necessities of identity and origin qualify the line between the contingent and the necessary.
