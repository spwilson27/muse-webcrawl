Agricultural subsidy

An agricultural subsidy is a government incentive paid to agribusinesses, agricultural organisations, and farmers to supplement their income, manage the supply of agricultural commodities, and influence the cost and supply of such commodities. Subsidies take many forms—direct payments, price supports, subsidised crop insurance and credit, tax concessions, and payments for environmental services—and are justified by supporters on grounds of food security, farm income stability, and rural development, while critics point to market distortion, environmental harm, and unfair effects on farmers in developing countries.

Types of support

  • Direct payments to farmers, historically linked to production of specific crops ("coupled") or paid per hectare regardless of what is grown ("decoupled").
  • Price supports and intervention buying, in which governments buy surpluses or guarantee minimum prices.
  • Subsidised insurance and credit, including premium support for crop insurance and concessional loans.
  • Input subsidies on fertiliser, seed, fuel, or irrigation water, common in developing countries.
  • Export subsidies, largely eliminated under the WTO's 2015 Nairobi decision.
  • Agri-environmental payments for practices such as habitat creation, soil conservation, and carbon sequestration.

Major programmes

The European Union's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), established in 1962, has long been the EU's largest budget item, now emphasising decoupled direct payments and "greening" conditions. In the United States, farm support is legislated through periodic farm bills, covering commodity programmes (such as Price Loss Coverage), subsidised crop insurance, and conservation programmes; the first agricultural subsidies date to the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933. China and India are also among the world's largest providers of agricultural support; the OECD estimates total support to agriculture across 54 countries at roughly US$850 billion per year in recent estimates.

Effects and criticism

Economists criticise subsidies for encouraging overproduction, depressing world prices to the detriment of unsubsidised producers (a central issue in WTO Doha Round disputes, e.g., over cotton), benefiting large farms disproportionately, and incentivising environmentally damaging practices such as excessive fertiliser use. Reforms have trended toward decoupled payments and environmental conditionality, though support levels remain politically durable because of concentrated farm lobbies and dispersed taxpayer costs.
