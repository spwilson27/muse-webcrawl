Muwashshah


Manuscript of Andalusi strophic poetry.

The muwashshah (Arabic: مُوَشَّح, literally "girdled"; plural muwashshahat) is a genre of secular Arabic poetry and music that developed in al-Andalus (Muslim Spain) from the 9th–10th centuries. Unlike the classical monorhymed qasida, the muwashshah is strophic, built from alternating refrains and verses with changing rhymes.

Form

A typical muwashshah consists of five stanzas, each combining lines in classical Arabic with a final refrain called the kharja ("exit"), which is often written in colloquial Andalusi Arabic or in Mozarabic (Romance) — some of the earliest recorded lyric verse in a Romance language. Themes were commonly love, wine and praise of patrons.

Legacy

The form is credited to poets such as Muqaddam ibn Mu'afa of Cabra and was perfected by figures like Ibn Quzman (who wrote in the related zajal form). Muwashshahat remain a living sung repertoire in the Arab world — in Aleppo's wasla suites, Moroccan Andalusi music (al-āla), and Maghrebi traditions — and scholars have debated their influence on the troubadour lyric of medieval Europe.
