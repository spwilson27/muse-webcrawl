Urea-formaldehyde

Urea-formaldehyde (UF) is a non-transparent thermosetting resin or polymer produced from urea and formaldehyde. It is among the most widely used amino resins, valued for its low cost, fast curing, high hardness, and good adhesion, and is used principally as an adhesive for wood products and as a moulding compound.

Chemistry and production

UF resins form by the condensation reaction of urea with formaldehyde, first producing methylolureas under alkaline conditions and then cross-linking into a three-dimensional network when cured under acidic conditions, often with heat. The molar ratio of formaldehyde to urea strongly affects reactivity and emissions; modern formulations are engineered to minimise free formaldehyde.

Uses

  • Adhesives for particleboard, medium-density fibreboard (MDF), plywood, and other engineered wood
  • Moulded articles such as electrical fittings, buttons, and bottle caps
  • Laminates and surface coatings
  • Textile finishes for wrinkle resistance
  • Urea-formaldehyde foam insulation (UFFI), used in building cavities in the 1970s
  • Slow-release nitrogen fertiliser in controlled-release forms

Health and environmental concerns

UF-bonded products can emit formaldehyde gas, especially when new or exposed to heat and humidity, and formaldehyde is classified as a human carcinogen. UFFI was banned in Canada in 1980 and was the subject of widespread concern in the United States. Regulations in many jurisdictions now set emission standards for composite wood products, spurring lower-emitting resins and alternative binders.
