Anti-satellite weapon

Anti-satellite weapons (ASAT) are space weapons designed to incapacitate or destroy satellites for strategic or tactical purposes. Several nations possess operational ASAT systems, and although no ASAT system has yet been used in warfare, some countries have successfully shot down their own satellites to demonstrate their capability.

Types

ASAT systems include direct-ascent kinetic kill vehicles launched from the ground, sea, or air; co-orbital systems that manoeuvre close to a target satellite and attack it; and directed-energy or electronic methods intended to disable a satellite without physically destroying it. Kinetic destruction at orbital velocities creates large clouds of space debris, which can threaten other spacecraft for years.

History

The United States and the Soviet Union both developed ASAT programmes during the Cold War. In 1985 a US F-15 launched an ASM-135 missile that destroyed the Solwind satellite. China destroyed its FengYun-1C weather satellite in 2007, producing the largest debris cloud ever tracked. The United States destroyed the malfunctioning USA-193 satellite in 2008 (Operation Burnt Frost). In 2019 India conducted Mission Shakti, destroying a satellite in low Earth orbit and becoming the fourth country to demonstrate the capability. In November 2021 Russia destroyed its Kosmos 1408 satellite, generating a debris field that forced crews aboard the International Space Station to take shelter.

Debris and policy concerns

Destructive tests have drawn international criticism because of the debris hazard, and in 2022 the United States announced a unilateral moratorium on destructive direct-ascent ASAT missile testing, a commitment later endorsed by a UN General Assembly resolution and joined by other states. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 prohibits weapons of mass destruction in orbit but does not ban conventional ASAT systems.
