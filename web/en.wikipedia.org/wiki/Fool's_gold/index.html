Fool's gold

Fool's gold is a nickname for the mineral pyrite (iron pyrite, iron disulfide, FeS2), whose metallic lustre and pale brass-yellow hue have caused it to be mistaken for gold by inexperienced prospectors — hence the name. In fact, pyrite can be distinguished from gold in several simple ways, and, unlike gold, it is brittle, much harder, and far lighter.

Pyrite

Pyrite is the most abundant sulfide mineral. It crystallizes in the isometric system, commonly forming cubes, octahedra, and striated pyritohedra, and occurs in sedimentary rocks, metamorphic rocks, coal beds, and hydrothermal veins, frequently in association with true gold deposits. Its name derives from the Greek pyr (fire), because it strikes sparks when hit with steel — a property once used in wheel-lock firearms and fire-starting.

Distinguishing pyrite from gold

  • Hardness: pyrite (Mohs 6–6.5) cannot be scratched by a steel knife and shatters when hammered; gold (2.5–3) is soft, malleable, and dents
  • Colour and tarnish: gold keeps a rich yellow colour even in shadow; pyrite is paler, brassy, and may tarnish brown
  • Streak: pyrite leaves a greenish-black streak on unglazed porcelain; gold leaves a yellow streak
  • Form: pyrite occurs in defined crystals and angular fragments; native gold occurs in rounded nuggets and wires
  • Density: gold (specific gravity ~19.3) is far heavier than pyrite (~5.0)

Other "fool's golds"

Two other minerals are regularly mistaken for gold and sometimes called fool's gold: chalcopyrite, a copper iron sulfide with a deeper yellow colour that tarnishes to iridescent blues and purples; and flakes of golden mica (especially weathered biotite or phlogopite), which glitter in sediments and streams but are paper-light and flexible.

In history and culture

The phrase entered the language in the age of exploration and gold rushes: expeditions such as Martin Frobisher's voyages to Canada in the 1570s famously shipped home tons of worthless black ore (hornblende-bearing rock containing pyrite) believed to contain gold. In placer mining camps of the California and Klondike gold rushes, "fool's gold" became the emblem of the greenhorn's disappointment. Pyrite itself is genuinely useful: historically a source of sulfur and sulfuric acid, a minor ore of iron, and today studied for applications in photovoltaics; it is also prized by collectors for its perfect natural cubes, such as those from Navajún, Spain.

Ironically, pyrite can contain invisible "refractory" gold within its crystal structure — in some deposits, notably Carlin-type ores in Nevada, the fool's gold literally has gold in it, recoverable only by roasting or pressure oxidation.
