The philosophy of technology examines what technology is, how it shapes human life and society, and how we should judge and govern it. Although reflection on technē (craft) is as old as Plato and Aristotle, the philosophy of technology emerged as a distinct field in the twentieth century, when industrial and then digital systems made technology's power — and the question of whether we control it or it controls us — impossible to ignore.

Classical approaches

Martin Heidegger's essay "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954) argued that technology's essence is not a collection of tools but a way of revealing the world: Gestell ("enframing"), in which nature and eventually human beings themselves appear as "standing-reserve", stock to be optimised — a diagnosis that made Heidegger, despite his politics, foundational for the field. Jacques Ellul's The Technological Society (1954) described "technique" as an autonomous, self-augmenting system following its own logic of efficiency. Lewis Mumford distinguished democratic from authoritarian technics and warned of the "megamachine". Ernst Kapp, who coined the field's name in 1877, had earlier seen tools as organ projections.

Empirical and critical turns

From the 1980s the field took an "empirical turn" (Don Ihde's postphenomenology of human–technology relations; Peter-Paul Verbeek's mediation theory), studying concrete artefacts and how they co-shape perception and morality, rather than Technology in the abstract. Langdon Winner asked "Do artifacts have politics?" (1980), answering that designs can embody political arrangements. Andrew Feenberg's critical theory of technology holds that technology is neither neutral nor autonomous but "ambivalent" — shaped by social values and open to democratic redesign. Bruno Latour and actor–network theory further dissolved the boundary between the social and the technical.

Contemporary questions

Current work concentrates on the ethics and politics of artificial intelligence, algorithms and platforms (bias, opacity, surveillance), biotechnology and human enhancement, environmental technology and climate responsibility, and the philosophy of information (Luciano Floridi). Recurring debates persist across all of them: instrumentalism (tools are neutral) versus substantivism (technology carries values), technological determinism versus social construction, and whether "technology" in the singular is even a coherent object of thought.
