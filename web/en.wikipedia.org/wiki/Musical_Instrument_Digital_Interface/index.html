Musical Instrument Digital Interface

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is a technical standard that describes a communication protocol, digital interface, and electrical connectors that connect a wide variety of electronic musical instruments, computers, and related audio devices for playing, editing, and recording music. The specification originates in a paper by Dave Smith and Chet Wood presented in 1981; the MIDI standard was first demonstrated publicly in 1983, when a Sequential Circuits Prophet-600 was connected to a Roland Jupiter-6.

How it works

MIDI does not transmit audio. Instead it transmits event messages — data about a performance, such as which note was pressed, its velocity, pitch bend, and control changes such as volume, pan and modulation. A single MIDI link carries up to sixteen channels of information, each typically assigned to a different instrument or part. Messages are transmitted serially at 31.25 kbit/s over the original 5-pin DIN connector, and today more commonly over USB, Bluetooth, or networks. Instruments called sequencers record these events so performances can be edited note by note and played back through synthesisers or software instruments.

General MIDI and files

The General MIDI standard (1991) defined a fixed set of 128 instrument sounds ("patches") arranged in a standard order, so a MIDI file would sound broadly similar on any compliant device. Standard MIDI Files (.mid) became a common format for distributing music for games, karaoke, ringtones and education. Extensions such as GS (Roland) and XG (Yamaha) expanded the sound sets.

MIDI 2.0

MIDI 2.0, adopted in 2020, greatly expands the original specification: it uses 32-bit resolution for controllers and velocity, supports per-note controllers and articulation, and adds two-way communication in which devices negotiate capabilities and exchange property data. MIDI 2.0 remains backward compatible with MIDI 1.0 devices through MIDI Capability Inquiry (MIDI-CI). The standard is maintained by the MIDI Association (formerly the MIDI Manufacturers Association).

Applications

MIDI underlies modern music production in digital audio workstations, live keyboard rigs, electronic drums, DJ controllers and guitar systems. It is also used beyond music, for example in theatrical lighting control (MIDI Show Control), stage automation, and interactive art. In 2016 the MIDI Manufacturers Association launched the MIDI Association to promote the standard globally.
