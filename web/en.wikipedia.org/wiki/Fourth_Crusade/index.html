Fourth Crusade

The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) was a Latin Christian armed expedition called by Pope Innocent III. Intended to reconquer Muslim-controlled Jerusalem by invading Egypt, the crusaders instead, diverted by Venetian interests and Byzantine dynastic intrigue, sacked the Christian cities of Zara and then Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, in April 1204. The crusaders established the Latin Empire in the conquered territories.

Course of events

The crusaders contracted with Venice for transport but could not pay the full price; in lieu, they helped Venice capture the rebellious city of Zara, despite papal prohibition and excommunication. En route, the exiled Byzantine prince Alexios Angelos promised money, troops and submission of the Orthodox Church to Rome if the crusaders restored his father, Isaac II. After a first intervention in 1203 and the subsequent murder of Alexios IV, the crusaders stormed Constantinople on 13 April 1204 and looted it for three days, destroying or carrying off immense treasures, including relics and classical artworks such as the bronze horses of St Mark's.

Aftermath

The Byzantine Empire was partitioned (the Partitio terrarum imperii Romaniae) among the crusaders and Venice; Baldwin of Flanders became the first Latin Emperor. Byzantine successor states at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond preserved imperial claims, and Michael VIII Palaiologos of Nicaea retook Constantinople in 1261. The sack permanently embittered relations between the Catholic and Orthodox churches.
