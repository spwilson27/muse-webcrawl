Faking good

Faking good, also known as positive impression management or defensive responding, is a pattern of responding to psychological tests and questionnaires in which the respondent deliberately presents themselves in an excessively favourable light — minimising faults and symptoms while exaggerating virtues — in order to influence the outcome of an evaluation. It is the mirror image of faking bad, in which a respondent exaggerates symptoms or problems.

Contexts

Faking good is a concern wherever self-report instruments carry high stakes. In personnel selection, job applicants may answer personality inventories in ways they believe employers want. In forensic and family-court settings, parents in custody disputes may under-report psychopathology and present unusually virtuous profiles on instruments such as the MMPI-2. In clinical settings, patients may minimise symptoms to obtain discharge, avoid stigma, or secure privileges, and members of some professions (such as pilots or police officers) may under-report psychological difficulties to remain certified for duty.

Detection

Major instruments incorporate validity scales designed to detect faking good. The MMPI's L ("Lie") scale contains items describing minor faults that almost everyone has but that a defensive respondent denies; the K ("Correction") scale measures more subtle defensiveness and is used to adjust clinical scale scores. Other tools include the Marlowe–Crowne Social Desirability Scale, Paulhus's Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding (which separates impression management from self-deceptive enhancement), "infrequency" and virtue items on the Personality Assessment Inventory (PIM scale), and forced-choice item formats that equate alternatives for social desirability.

Research findings

Laboratory studies show that instructed respondents can substantially shift their profiles in a favourable direction, but that validity scales detect such instructed faking reasonably well. In real applicant settings, the degree of distortion is typically smaller and its effect on hiring outcomes debated; critics note that high social-desirability scores can reflect genuine adjustment rather than deception, particularly in cultures or professions that value restraint. Distinguishing deliberate deception from self-deception and from honest virtue remains a methodological difficulty, and test manuals caution against treating elevation on a single validity scale as proof of lying.
