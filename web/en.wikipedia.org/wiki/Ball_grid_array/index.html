Ball grid array


The underside of a BGA package, showing the grid of solder balls.

A ball grid array (BGA) is a type of surface-mount packaging used for integrated circuits. BGA packages are permanently mounted to a printed circuit board using an array of small solder balls arranged in a grid on the underside of the package. During assembly the board and package are heated so the balls melt (reflow) and form the electrical and mechanical connections.

BGAs derive their advantages from the high density of connections they allow: unlike dual in-line or quad flat packages, which route connections only around the perimeter, a BGA uses the whole underside of the package, so packages with many hundreds of pins can remain compact. The short path from the chip to the balls also gives low inductance, good electrical performance at high speeds, and the solder balls conduct heat into the board.

Variants

  • PBGA – plastic ball grid array, the common low-cost form.
  • CBGA / CCGA – ceramic BGA and ceramic column grid array, used in high-reliability applications.
  • FBGA – fine-pitch BGA, with smaller ball pitch, common for memory.
  • µBGA / CSP – micro BGA and chip-scale packages, barely larger than the die itself.
  • LGA – land grid array, a related package using flat pads rather than balls; the mating socket or board provides the contacts.

Advantages and disadvantages

The main disadvantage of BGAs is inspection and rework: once soldered, the joints are hidden beneath the package, so defects such as voids, bridging, or "head-in-pillow" joints must be detected by X-ray inspection. Repair requires specialised hot-air or infrared rework stations to remove and reball the device. Because package and board expand at different rates with temperature, thermal cycling can fatigue the solder joints, a problem addressed with underfill adhesives in portable devices.

BGA technology was developed in the late 1980s and 1990s, with Motorola and IBM among its pioneers, and became standard for microprocessors, chipsets, GPUs, FPGAs, and memory as pin counts rose beyond the practical limits of peripheral packages.
