A Garratt is a type of articulated steam locomotive invented by the British engineer Herbert William Garratt and built mainly by Beyer, Peacock and Company of Manchester, who marketed it as the Beyer-Garratt. In the Garratt arrangement, the boiler, firebox and cab are carried on a central frame slung between two separate engine units, each with its own cylinders, driving wheels, and coal and water supplies.

Design

Garratt patented the concept in 1907. By hanging the boiler unit between two power bogies, the design freed the boiler from the size limits imposed by the driving wheels, allowing a large, free-steaming boiler and a wide, deep firebox, while the total weight was spread over many axles. Garratts could thus combine high power with a low axle load and could negotiate sharp curves, making them ideal for lightly laid colonial railways in Africa, Asia, Australia and South America.

Development and use

The first Garratts, the K class of 1909, were built for the North East Dundas Tramway in Tasmania. The type was subsequently built in very large numbers and sizes, culminating in giants such as the South African Railways Class GL and the East African Railways 59 class "Mount Kilimanjaro" engines — among the largest and most powerful steam locomotives ever built for metre gauge. In Britain, the LMS built the LNER-designed U1 banker and a class of freight Garratts. Garratts were built under licence in several countries, and examples remained in regular service in southern Africa into the 21st century, notably on tourist and industrial lines in Zimbabwe and South Africa.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages over the rival Mallet design include equal running quality in both directions, better weight distribution and a boiler unrestricted in diameter by the frames. Drawbacks included the long steam pipes to the two engine units, the variation in adhesive weight as water and coal were consumed, and higher construction and maintenance costs.
