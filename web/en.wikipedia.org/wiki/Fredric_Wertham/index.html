Fredric Wertham

Fredric Wertham (born Friedrich Ignatz Wertheimer; March 20, 1895 – November 18, 1981) was a German-American psychiatrist and campaigning author. He is best known for his 1954 book Seduction of the Innocent, which argued that comic books corrupted children and led to juvenile delinquency — a claim that triggered US Senate hearings and the industry's self-censoring Comics Code Authority.

Born: March 20, 1895, Nuremberg, Germany
Died: November 18, 1981, Kempton, Pennsylvania, U.S.
Field: Psychiatry
Known for: Seduction of the Innocent (1954); anti-comics campaign

Career

Wertham studied medicine in Munich, Erlangen, London and Vienna, working with Emil Kraepelin and Sigmund Freud, before emigrating to the United States in 1922. He held senior posts at Johns Hopkins and Bellevue Hospital and became director of psychiatric clinics for the New York courts and hospitals. In 1946 he founded the Lafargue Clinic in Harlem, one of the first psychiatric clinics for African Americans, staffed by volunteers including Ralph Ellison. His expert evidence on the psychological harm of segregation contributed to cases leading toward Brown v. Board of Education, and his clinical work with offenders appeared in books such as Dark Legend (1941) and The Show of Violence (1949).

Seduction of the Innocent

From the late 1940s Wertham campaigned against crime and horror comics, arguing in articles and then in Seduction of the Innocent (1954) that they caused illiteracy, violence and sexual deviance; he notoriously discussed Batman and Robin as a supposed homosexual pairing and Wonder Woman's alleged lesbian themes. Testifying before Senator Estes Kefauver's Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency in 1954, he helped precipitate a moral panic: publishers adopted the restrictive Comics Code, EC Comics' famous horror and crime lines were destroyed, and hundreds of titles folded. Later scholarship, notably Carol Tilley's archival research published in 2012, showed that Wertham manipulated, overstated and fabricated parts of his evidence.

Later life

Wertham later turned his attention to television violence (The World of Fanzines, 1973, treated comics fandom unexpectedly sympathetically) and retired to Pennsylvania, where he died in 1981. His papers are held by the Library of Congress.
