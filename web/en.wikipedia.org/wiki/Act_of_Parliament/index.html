Act of Parliament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Act of Parliament is a law enacted by a national or subnational parliament, the primary form of legislation in Westminster-derived systems. In the United Kingdom an Act is a bill that has passed both Houses of Parliament and received royal assent; since the Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949 the House of Commons can ultimately legislate without the Lords. Acts are cited by short title and year (e.g., the Human Rights Act 1998) or by chapter number, published by the King's Printer, and (since 2011 for most) available in revised form as amended.

Equivalent instruments elsewhere: Acts of Congress in the United States (bills passed by both Houses and signed, or repassed over veto), Acts of the Scottish Parliament and Senedd Cymru under devolution, and the statute law of Commonwealth parliaments. In the hierarchy of norms an Act (primary legislation) overrides secondary legislation and common law, and in the UK tradition of Parliamentary sovereignty cannot be struck down by courts — subject now to interpretive duties under human-rights law and, historically, the disapplication required by EU law (1973–2020). Drafting is by specialist parliamentary counsel to conventions of form (sections, schedules, extent and commencement clauses) that interpreters later parse.
