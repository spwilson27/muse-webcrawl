Kingdom (biology)

In biology, a kingdom is the second-highest taxonomic rank, just below domain. Kingdoms are divided into smaller groups called phyla. The classification of living things into kingdoms was introduced by Carl Linnaeus, who in 1735 recognised two kingdoms — animals and plants — and the number and definition of kingdoms has been repeatedly revised since.

Historical systems

  • Two kingdoms (Linnaeus, 1735): Animalia and Plantae (Vegetabilia).
  • Three kingdoms (Haeckel, 1866): Protista added for microorganisms.
  • Four kingdoms (Copeland, 1938): Monera separated bacteria and cyanobacteria.
  • Five kingdoms (Whittaker, 1969): Monera, Protista, Plantae, Fungi and Animalia — long the standard textbook scheme, based on cell type and mode of nutrition.
  • Six kingdoms (Woese and others): split Monera into Eubacteria and Archaebacteria.

Domains and modern views

In 1990 Carl Woese proposed three domains — Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya — above the kingdom level, based on ribosomal RNA phylogeny. Within the eukaryotes, molecular evidence has reorganised the old protist kingdoms into supergroups such as Opisthokonta (which includes both animals and fungi), Archaeplastida and SAR. Many biologists now regard "kingdom" as a convenient grade rather than a natural rank, and systems such as those of Thomas Cavalier-Smith recognise six or seven kingdoms, including Chromista. Nonetheless, the animal, plant and fungi kingdoms remain in universal use.
