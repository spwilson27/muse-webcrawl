Full-frame digital SLR

A full-frame digital SLR (single-lens reflex) camera is a digital SLR whose image sensor is the same size as a frame of 35 mm film, approximately 36 × 24 mm. The format preserves the field of view of lenses designed for film cameras and offers advantages in low-light performance and depth-of-field control compared with smaller "crop" sensors such as APS-C.

History

Early digital SLRs used sensors much smaller than 35 mm film because large sensors were prohibitively expensive. The first full-frame models appeared in the early 2000s, including the Contax N Digital and Kodak DCS Pro 14n, followed by Canon's EOS-1Ds series and, from 2007, Nikon's D3, which brought full-frame sensors to a broader professional market. Canon's EOS 5D (2005) and especially the 5D Mark II (2008)—whose video capability transformed filmmaking—made full frame mainstream among enthusiasts.

Characteristics

Because of their larger photosites and total light-gathering area, full-frame sensors generally produce lower noise at high ISO settings and shallower depth of field at a given aperture and field of view. The cameras and lenses are typically larger and costlier than cropped-sensor equivalents. Since the 2010s full-frame sensors have also become standard in high-end mirrorless cameras, while the DSLR form itself has declined.
