A facial recognition system is a technology capable of identifying or verifying a person from a digital image or a video frame from a video source. There are multiple methods in which facial recognition systems work, but in general, they work by comparing selected facial features from a given image with faces within a database.

It is also described as a biometric artificial intelligence based application that can uniquely identify a person by analysing patterns based on the person's facial textures and shape.

History

Early work on automated facial recognition began in the 1960s, when researchers including Woody Bledsoe, Helen Chan Wolf, and Charles Bisson developed semi-automated systems that required an operator to locate features such as eyes, ears, nose, and mouth on photographs. In the 1970s, Goldstein, Harmon, and Lesk used 21 specific subjective markers such as hair colour and lip thickness to automate recognition.

In 1991, Matthew Turk and Alex Pentland demonstrated eigenface-based recognition, showing that residual error could be used for face detection in video. The development of deep learning methods in the 2010s, particularly the DeepFace system announced by Facebook researchers in 2014 and Google's FaceNet in 2015, dramatically improved accuracy rates on benchmark datasets.

How facial recognition works

Face detection

The process begins with detecting a face in an image or video frame. Algorithms such as the Viola–Jones framework (2001) made real-time detection practical.

Face capture and feature extraction

Once detected, the face is normalised (aligned, scaled) and converted into a compact numerical representation — a "faceprint" or embedding — encoding the geometry and texture of facial landmarks such as the distance between the eyes, the shape of the cheekbones, and the contours of the lips, nose and chin.

Matching

The embedding is compared against a database of stored templates. A similarity score determines whether the face belongs to a known individual (identification, 1:N) or matches a claimed identity (verification, 1:1).

Applications

  • Unlocking devices — systems such as Apple's Face ID (2017) use infrared depth mapping.
  • Law enforcement — police agencies compare probe images against mugshot databases.
  • Border control — automated passport gates match travellers to passport photos.
  • Social media tagging — platforms suggest tags for people in uploaded photos.
  • Retail and banking — identity verification and payment authorisation.

Effectiveness and limitations

Benchmark testing by NIST (the Face Recognition Vendor Test) has shown top algorithms exceeding 99% accuracy under ideal conditions, but accuracy varies with image quality, pose, ageing, and demographic group. A 2019 NIST study found that many algorithms exhibited higher false-positive rates for African American and Asian faces than for Caucasian faces, raising concerns about bias.

Privacy and regulation

Facial recognition has attracted significant controversy over mass surveillance, consent, and civil liberties. Several US cities, including San Francisco (2019), Boston, and Portland, have banned government use of the technology. The Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (2008) requires informed consent before collecting face geometry. In the European Union, the GDPR treats biometric data as a special category, and the EU AI Act (2024) restricts real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces. Companies such as Clearview AI, which scraped billions of images from the web, have faced fines from European regulators.
