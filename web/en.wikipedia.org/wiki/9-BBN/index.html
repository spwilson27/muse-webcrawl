9-BBN

9-BBN is the common abbreviation for 9-borabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane, an organoborane compound with the formula C8H15B. It is one of the most widely used reagents for hydroboration, valued for its high regio- and stereoselectivity with alkenes and alkynes. The reagent was developed in the laboratory of Herbert C. Brown, whose work on hydroboration earned the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1979.

Preparation and structure

9-BBN is prepared by the hydroboration of 1,5-cyclooctadiene with borane (diborane or borane complexes such as BH3·THF). In the solid state and in concentrated solution it exists largely as a hydride-bridged dimer, which dissociates to the reactive monomer in donor solvents. Commercially it is most often supplied as a 0.5 M solution in tetrahydrofuran.

Reactivity

The reagent adds across carbon–carbon double bonds in a concerted, syn fashion, placing boron at the less substituted carbon (anti-Markovnikov orientation). Because of the steric bulk of the bicyclic framework, 9-BBN discriminates strongly between alkenes of different substitution patterns, reacting much faster with terminal and less hindered alkenes. Oxidation of the resulting organoborane (typically with alkaline hydrogen peroxide) gives the alcohol with overall anti-Markovnikov selectivity — the basis of the hydroboration–oxidation reaction.

With alkynes, 9-BBN performs a single hydroboration to give vinylboranes, which are valuable intermediates, for example in the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling, in which the vinyl-9-BBN couples with organic halides under palladium catalysis with retention of alkene geometry. The reagent also reduces certain functional groups and cleaves ethers under forcing conditions.

Handling

9-BBN solutions are air- and moisture-sensitive and are handled under inert atmosphere. The solid dimer is a mild fire hazard and reacts with water to release hydrogen.
