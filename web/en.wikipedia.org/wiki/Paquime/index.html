Paquime

Paquimé, also known as Casas Grandes, is a prehistoric archaeological site and UNESCO World Heritage Site in northwestern Chihuahua, Mexico, near the modern town of Nuevo Casas Grandes. It was the largest and most complex settlement of the Casas Grandes culture, a regional centre that flourished between about 1130 and 1450 CE and mediated trade between Mesoamerica and the American Southwest.

The city

At its height, Paquimé contained multi-storey adobe apartment blocks with hundreds of rooms housing several thousand people, built of puddled adobe with T-shaped doorways, a feature shared with Ancestral Pueblo architecture to the north. The site includes large ceremonial mounds, plazas, ballcourts in the Mesoamerican style, sophisticated water-management works (a reservoir and canals fed by springs), and pens for scarlet macaws, whose feathers were traded widely. Residents produced distinctive polychrome pottery — Ramos, Babícora, and other wares — and imported turquoise, shell, copper bells, and macaws from distant regions.

History and decline

Paquimé grew rapidly in its Medio period (c. 1200–1450) into a regional capital dominating the Casas Grandes valley. Around 1450 the city was abandoned — evidence of burning and unburied dead has led some archaeologists to suggest attack, though drought and social change are also proposed. The nearby on-site Museo de las Culturas del Norte displays artifacts from the excavations led by Charles C. Di Peso in the late 1950s and early 1960s.
