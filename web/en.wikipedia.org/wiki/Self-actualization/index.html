Self-actualization

Self-actualization is the realization or fulfilment of one's talents and potentialities, regarded within humanistic psychology as a fundamental human drive. The term was coined by the neurologist Kurt Goldstein for the organism's tendency to actualize its capacities, and was developed most famously by Abraham Maslow.

Maslow's account

In Maslow's hierarchy of needs, self-actualization sits at the top of the pyramid, above physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, and esteem. Maslow described it as the desire "to become everything one is capable of becoming": growth motivated rather than deficiency motivated, pursued only once the lower "deficiency needs" are reasonably satisfied. Later in life Maslow placed self-transcendence beyond self-actualization as a further motivational level.

Studying people he took to be self-actualized — including figures such as Abraham Lincoln and Eleanor Roosevelt — Maslow listed characteristic traits: efficient perception of reality, acceptance of self and others, spontaneity, problem-centred rather than ego-centred orientation, autonomy, continued freshness of appreciation, and frequent "peak experiences" of intense joy and absorption.

Other perspectives and criticism

Carl Rogers used a related concept, the "fully functioning person", driven by an actualizing tendency toward openness, creativity, and living in the present. Humanistic ideas of self-actualization influenced counselling, education, management theory, and the broader self-help culture. Critics have argued that the concept is vague and difficult to test empirically, that Maslow's sample was small and culturally narrow, and that the hierarchy's ordering is not universally supported by research. Nevertheless, renewed work in positive psychology has revisited self-actualization as a measurable dimension of well-being, with modern scales attempting to operationalize Maslow's trait list.
