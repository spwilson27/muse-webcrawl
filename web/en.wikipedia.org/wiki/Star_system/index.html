Star system

A multiple star system: several stars bound together by gravity orbit a common centre of mass.

A star system or stellar system is a small number of stars that orbit each other, bound by gravitational attraction. A star system with two stars is a binary star; systems with three, four or more are called multiple star systems. Star systems are not to be confused with planetary systems, which include planets and similar bodies orbiting a star, nor with very large groupings such as star clusters or galaxies.

Multiple star systems

Roughly a third or more of the stellar systems in the Milky Way are multiple. Well-known examples include Alpha Centauri, a triple system and the nearest to the Sun; Castor, a sextuple system; and Mizar in the Big Dipper, a famous visual double.

Hierarchical structure

Most multiple systems are organised hierarchically: close pairs orbit each other, and these pairs in turn orbit more distant companions, which keeps the system gravitationally stable over long timescales. In a triple system, for instance, two stars usually form a tight binary while the third orbits at a much greater distance. Components are conventionally labelled A, B, C, with subscripts for members of close pairs (Aa, Ab, and so on).

Discovery

Multiple stars are discovered as visual doubles through telescopes, as spectroscopic binaries through Doppler shifts in their spectra, and as eclipsing binaries through periodic dimming. Modern surveys, including the Gaia mission, have greatly expanded the census of wide binaries.
