Lichun (Chinese: 立春; pinyin: lìchūn), known in Japanese as Risshun, in Korean as Ipchun and in Vietnamese as Lập xuân, is the first of the 24 solar terms (jieqi) of the traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar. It begins when the Sun reaches the celestial longitude of 315° and ends when it reaches 330°, falling most often on 4 February in the Gregorian calendar. Lichun signifies the beginning of spring in East Asian tradition.

Significance

Although the Chinese New Year (Spring Festival) is the most famous spring celebration, Lichun has independent agricultural importance: it marked the official start of the ploughing season. In imperial China, the emperor and officials performed rites on Lichun to pray for a good harvest, including ceremonially ploughing a field and "welcoming spring" (迎春) outside the eastern gate of the capital. A well-known folk rite was "beating the spring ox" (打春牛): a clay or paper ox was symbolically whipped to drive out winter's laziness, and its scattered fragments were scrambled for as lucky charms.

Customs and pentads

Traditional customs include eating spring rolls and fresh spring vegetables ("biting the spring", 咬春), posting the character 春 ("spring") calligraphy, and divining the year's weather by the day's conditions. The three five-day pentads of Lichun are described as: "east wind thaws the frozen earth" (東風解凍); "hibernating insects begin to stir" (蟄蟲始振); and "fish rise bearing ice on their backs" (魚陟負冰). In Japan, Risshun is preceded by Setsubun, and the day remains a seasonal reference in haiku and everyday seasonal greetings.
