Frobenius theorem (real division algebras)

The Frobenius theorem, proved by Ferdinand Georg Frobenius in 1877, is a fundamental classification theorem in algebra. It states that the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the real numbers, up to isomorphism, are:

  • the real numbers R themselves;
  • the complex numbers C, a two-dimensional algebra over R;
  • the quaternions H, a four-dimensional noncommutative algebra over R.

Significance

The theorem explains why attempts to construct number systems beyond the quaternions while preserving associativity and division must fail in finite dimensions over the reals. If associativity is dropped, the octonions O provide an eight-dimensional normed division algebra; the later Hurwitz theorem showed that R, C, H and O exhaust the normed division algebras over the reals. If instead finiteness is dropped, infinite-dimensional examples abound, such as fields of rational functions.

Proof idea

Classical proofs proceed by studying the elements of the algebra whose square is negative. Every element outside the real axis generates a copy of the complex numbers; if the algebra is larger than C, one finds an element anticommuting appropriately with the complex unit, generating the quaternions, and then shows any further independent element would force a zero divisor or violate associativity. Modern proofs often use algebraic topology or the theory of Clifford algebras.
