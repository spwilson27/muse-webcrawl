Shot reverse shot

Shot reverse shot (also stylized as shot/reverse shot) is a film technique used for dialogue sequences and other exchanges between two characters, in which the camera alternates between a shot of one figure and a reverse-angle shot of the other. It is one of the fundamental tools of continuity editing, and is most often associated with coverage of conversation scenes, confrontations, and telephone calls.

Technique

In a typical shot reverse shot pattern, the scene is first established with a wider master shot that shows both characters in the same space. The camera then cuts to a closer shot of the first character, usually framed over the shoulder of the second, and then to the corresponding shot of the second character shown over the shoulder of the first. The two shots are taken from roughly opposite sides of the axis of action, but both are filmed from the same side of the imaginary 180-degree line that runs between the characters, so that screen directions remain consistent: character A always appears to look right and character B to look left, or vice versa.

Because the eyelines match across the cut, viewers perceive the characters as looking directly at one another even though the two halves of the exchange may have been filmed at different times, with the other actor reading lines off camera or even absent. Close-ups and singles may be derived from the same setup at increasing sizes, producing the familiar pattern of master, two-shots, over-the-shoulders, and close-ups.

In classical Hollywood cinema

The technique became a cornerstone of the classical Hollywood style, in which continuity editing aimed to make cuts invisible and subordinate technique to narrative clarity. Directors such as John Ford and Howard Hawks used shot reverse shot structures to stage long dialogue scenes efficiently, while Alfred Hitchcock exploited the pattern's expectations in suspense sequences, withholding the reverse angle to deny the audience information about what a character is looking at.

Variations and criticism

Variations include the "dirty single," which includes part of the other actor's shoulder or head in the frame, and the "clean single," which does not. Filmmakers may deliberately break the pattern by crossing the line, re-establishing the geography with a new master shot, or staging a conversation in a single moving take instead. Some critics and filmmakers, notably Jean-Luc Godard and the directors of the French New Wave, treated the routine use of shot reverse shot as a symptom of formulaic filmmaking and sought alternatives in long takes, deep staging, and jump cuts.
