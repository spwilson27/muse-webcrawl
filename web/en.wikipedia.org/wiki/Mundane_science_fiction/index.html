Mundane science fiction

Mundane science fiction is a subgenre of science fiction that favours realistic, plausible depictions of the near future on or near Earth, using technology consistent with established science. It rejects or avoids such genre staples as faster-than-light travel, contact with extraterrestrials, time travel, parallel universes and telepathy, holding that credible stories can be told within the limits of known physics.

History

The term was coined by writer Geoff Ryman and others, building on an earlier essay by Geoffrey A. Landis. A "Mundane Manifesto" circulated in 2004 argued that the familiar furniture of science fiction amounted to escapism at a moment when Earth faces real crises of climate, resources and population, and that the most pressing futures to imagine are those unfolding here. Ryman's novel Air (2005) is frequently cited as an exemplar.

Reception

The movement provoked debate: supporters praised its rigour and relevance, while critics objected to prescriptive manifestos and noted that much earlier hard science fiction and dystopian fiction already worked within mundane constraints. Authors associated with the approach include Ryman, Maureen F. McHugh and Kim Stanley Robinson, whose near-future works emphasise climate change and social transformation.
