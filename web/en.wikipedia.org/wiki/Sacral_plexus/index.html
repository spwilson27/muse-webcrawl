Sacral plexus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The sacral plexus is a nerve plexus which provides motor and sensory nerves for the posterior thigh, most of the lower leg and foot, and part of the pelvis. It is formed by the ventral rami of the spinal nerves L4 and L5 and the sacral nerves S1 to S4, converging on the surface of the piriformis muscle in the pelvis. It is part of the larger lumbosacral plexus.

Branches

The plexus gives off several branches, including the superior and inferior gluteal nerves, the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, perforating cutaneous nerve, and muscular branches to the pelvic floor and external hip rotators. Its largest terminal branch is the sciatic nerve (L4–S3), the largest nerve in the body, which leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen and supplies the posterior compartment of the thigh before dividing into the tibial and common fibular (peroneal) nerves. The pudendal nerve (S2–S4) supplies the perineum and external genitalia and is the principal nerve of the pelvic floor.

Clinical significance

Injury to the sacral plexus may result from pelvic fractures, tumours, childbirth or radiation, producing pain, weakness and sensory loss in the buttock, leg and perineum. Compression of the sciatic nerve or its roots is the classic cause of sciatica, while pudendal nerve entrapment can cause chronic perineal pain.
