The Mac transition to Apple silicon was the process of switching the central processing units (CPUs) of Apple's Mac line of personal computers from Intel's x86-64 processors to Apple-designed systems on a chip (SoCs) using the ARM64 architecture, branded as Apple silicon. CEO Tim Cook announced the transition at WWDC on June 22, 2020, describing a roughly two-year plan. It was the third architecture change in Mac history, following the switch from Motorola 68000 to PowerPC in 1994 and from PowerPC to Intel in 2005–2006.

Background

Apple had designed its own ARM-based chips for the iPhone and iPad since the A4 in 2010, and their performance per watt steadily surpassed Intel's offerings, while Intel's processor roadmap delays frustrated Apple. Rumours of an ARM Mac circulated for years before the announcement. To ease the transition, Apple provided Rosetta 2, a binary translator that runs x86-64 applications on Apple silicon; Universal 2 binaries that bundle both architectures; and the ability to run iPhone and iPad apps natively on the Mac. Developers could rent a Developer Transition Kit — a Mac mini enclosure containing an A12Z Bionic chip.

Rollout

The first Apple silicon Macs, released in November 2020 with the M1 chip — a MacBook Air, a 13-inch MacBook Pro, and a Mac mini — won wide praise for performance and battery life. The M1 Pro and M1 Max followed in 2021 in the redesigned 14- and 16-inch MacBook Pros, and the M1 Ultra (fusing two M1 Max dies) powered the Mac Studio in 2022. The M2 generation began in June 2022, and the transition completed in June 2023 when the Mac Pro moved to the M2 Ultra, ending sales of Intel Macs. The M3 family (the first built on a 3 nm process) arrived in October 2023, followed by the M4 in 2024.

Reception and impact

The transition was widely judged a success: Apple silicon Macs offered industry-leading performance per watt, prompting renewed competition from Intel and Qualcomm. Some capabilities were lost — Boot Camp support for running Windows natively disappeared, and eGPU support ended — but virtualization filled most gaps. Intel Macs continued receiving macOS updates for several years, with support gradually phased out in later releases.
