Battle of Dungeness

The Battle of Dungeness (also called the Battle of Dungeness Head) was a naval engagement of the First Anglo-Dutch War fought on 30 November 1652 (Old Style) off the headland of Dungeness, on the coast of Kent in southern England. A Dutch fleet under Maarten Tromp defeated an English squadron commanded by Robert Blake, who was forced to withdraw up the Thames.

Background

By late November 1652 the English fleet had been weakened by the dispatch of squadrons to the Mediterranean and elsewhere, while the main English fleet under Blake had also been reduced by storm damage and desertion. Tromp, returning to sea with a large convoy of merchantmen, sought out Blake in the Downs and attacked his outnumbered squadron off Dungeness.

The battle

Tromp's fleet of roughly ninety ships overwhelmed Blake's smaller force. The English fought a delaying action in the falling darkness; two English ships were lost when their magazines exploded, and others were captured or badly damaged. Blake managed to save the greater part of his fleet by withdrawing into the Thames.

Aftermath

The victory gave the Dutch temporary control of the English Channel and restored Dutch naval prestige after earlier setbacks. Tromp is said, in the famous legend, to have hoisted a broom to the masthead of his flagship to signify that he had swept the seas clear of the English — an anecdote historians regard as apocryphal. The English responded by reforming their naval administration and fighting instructions, and the war continued into 1653 with the battles of Portland, the Gabbard and Scheveningen.
