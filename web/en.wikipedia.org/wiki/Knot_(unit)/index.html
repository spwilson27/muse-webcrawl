The knot is a unit of speed equal to one nautical mile per hour, exactly 1.852 km/h (approximately 1.151 mph or 0.514 m/s). The ISO standard symbol for the knot is kn; the symbol kt is also common, especially in aviation. The knot is not an SI unit but is accepted for use with the SI, and it is the standard unit of speed in maritime and air navigation worldwide, where distances are measured in nautical miles because of their direct relation to the Earth's geometry (one nautical mile ≈ one minute of latitude).

Terminology

Speeds in knots are properly stated as, for example, "twenty knots" — the expression "knots per hour" is redundant and incorrect, since the knot already incorporates the hour. Although the unit is not used with SI prefixes, speeds of vessels, aircraft, wind, ocean currents, and even air-pollution transport are routinely reported in knots.

Origin

The unit derives from the chip log, the traditional method of measuring a ship's speed. A wooden chip (the log) attached to a line knotted at regular intervals was thrown overboard; a sailor counted the number of knots that ran out through his hands in a fixed time (measured by a sandglass), and that count was the ship's speed in "knots". With a 28-second glass, the knots were spaced about 47 feet 3 inches apart so that the count corresponded to nautical miles per hour. The method was in use by the seventeenth century and survived into the era of patent logs and, ultimately, electronic speed measurement.

Modern use

The knot remains the standard speed unit of the International Maritime Organization and of aviation (ICAO), where indicated airspeed is displayed in knots. Weather services report wind speeds in knots in marine forecasts (and in aviation products internationally), while the Beaufort scale's wind-force ranges are conventionally tabulated in knots. High-speed craft, submarines, and torpedoes are likewise rated in knots.
