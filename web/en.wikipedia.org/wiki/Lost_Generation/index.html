Lost Generation

Lost Generation

Definition: Social generational cohort

Born: c. 1883–1900

Defined by: Coming of age during World War I

The Lost Generation is the social generational cohort that was in early adulthood during World War I, and emerged immediately after. "Lost" in this context refers to the "disoriented, wandering, directionless" spirit of many of the war's survivors in the early postwar period. The term is also particularly used to refer to a group of American expatriate writers living in Paris during the 1920s.

Origin of the term

The phrase is attributed to writer Gertrude Stein, who is said to have taken it from a French garage owner who complained that the young men who had served in the war were une génération perdue — a lost generation — because they could no longer do ordinary work properly. Ernest Hemingway popularised the phrase by using it as an epigraph to his 1926 novel The Sun Also Rises, crediting Stein.

Writers associated with the term

Writers commonly associated with the Lost Generation include Hemingway, F. Scott Fitzgerald, Ezra Pound, T. S. Eliot, John Dos Passos, and Stein herself. Their works explored disillusionment with the war, the hollowness of postwar prosperity, and expatriate life in Paris and elsewhere in Europe.
