Guerrilla theatre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Street performance intended to startle a public audience into political awareness

Guerrilla theatre (or guerrilla theater) is a form of protest and political performance, first prominent in the 1960s, in which theatrical events are staged — often unannounced — in public places to confront unsuspecting audiences with a political message. The term adapts "guerrilla warfare" to the stage: small, mobile, surprise actions outside the institutions of commercial theatre.

History

The form is usually traced to the San Francisco Mime Troupe, founded in 1959 by R. G. Davis, whose free performances in public parks turned to direct agitation against the Vietnam War; Davis published the essay "Guerrilla Theatre" in the Tulane Drama Review in 1966. Influential groups included El Teatro Campesino, founded by Luis Valdez in 1965 to support the United Farm Workers' strike with short comic actos performed on picket lines, and the French and American street-theatre movements around the events of May 1968.

Practice and legacy

Guerrilla theatre typically uses minimal props and costumes, direct address, humour, and audience participation or confrontation. Contemporary descendants include the actions of the Yippies, ACT UP die-ins, the Billionaires for Bush, flash mobs, and culture-jamming performance collectives such as the Yes Men. The form influenced Augusto Boal's "invisible theatre" within the Theatre of the Oppressed, in which actors stage scripted scenes in daily life without revealing that a performance is occurring.
