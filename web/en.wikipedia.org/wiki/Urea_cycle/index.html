The cycle converts two amino groups and one bicarbonate into urea.

The urea cycle (also known as the ornithine cycle) is a cycle of biochemical reactions that produces urea from ammonia. It is the principal means by which terrestrial vertebrates, including humans, dispose of excess nitrogen from the breakdown of amino acids, ammonia being highly toxic — especially to the brain.

Reactions

The cycle takes place in the liver, spanning the mitochondrial matrix and the cytosol. Ammonia and bicarbonate are first combined to form carbamoyl phosphate (enzyme: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, the rate-limiting step, activated by N-acetylglutamate). Carbamoyl phosphate enters the cycle, reacting with ornithine to give citrulline; citrulline then combines with aspartate (donating the second nitrogen) to form argininosuccinate, which is cleaved to arginine and fumarate. Finally, arginase hydrolyses arginine to release urea and regenerate ornithine. The overall process consumes the equivalent of four ATP. The fumarate produced links the cycle to the citric acid cycle — Krebs called the arrangement the "Krebs bicycle".

Regulation and disorders

Flux through the cycle rises with high protein intake and starvation. Inherited defects in its enzymes cause urea cycle disorders, in which ammonia accumulates (hyperammonemia), producing vomiting, lethargy and potentially fatal encephalopathy, often in newborns. The commonest is ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, an X-linked condition. Treatment combines dietary protein restriction with nitrogen-scavenging drugs such as sodium benzoate and glycerol phenylbutyrate.

History

The cycle was discovered in 1932 by Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit — the first metabolic cycle to be elucidated, five years before Krebs described the citric acid cycle.
