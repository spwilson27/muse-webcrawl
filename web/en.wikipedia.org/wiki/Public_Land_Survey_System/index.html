Public Land Survey System

The Public Land Survey System (PLSS) is the surveying method developed and used in the United States to plat, or divide, real property for sale and settling. Established by the Land Ordinance of 1785, it is also known as the rectangular survey system.

Structure

The system is based on initial points where a principal meridian (north–south) and a baseline (east–west) intersect. Land is divided into townships, nominally six miles square, identified by township (rows north or south of the baseline) and range (columns east or west of the meridian). Each township is subdivided into 36 sections of one square mile (640 acres), which can be further divided into quarter sections, quarter-quarter sections, and smaller aliquot parts.

History

Proposed by Thomas Jefferson and adopted after the American Revolutionary War, the system replaced the irregular metes-and-bounds descriptions of the original colonies with a standardized grid, facilitating orderly land sales and clear titles. It was applied across most of the United States west of the Appalachians — about 30 states — beginning with the Seven Ranges of Ohio.

Modern use

The PLSS remains the legal basis for land descriptions across much of the country and is administered today by the Bureau of Land Management. Its grid visibly shapes road networks, field patterns, and county boundaries in the Midwest and West.
