Administrative divisions of China

The administrative divisions of China form a multi-tiered hierarchy through which the People's Republic of China (PRC) governs its territory. Under the constitution, administration is formally divided into three levels — provincial, county, and township — but in practice a prefectural tier is interposed between the provincial and county levels, giving the system five de facto tiers when the central government itself is counted.

Provincial level

At the highest subnational tier, China is divided into four types of provincial-level unit:

  • Provinces (shěng) — the standard unit, such as Guangdong, Sichuan, and Shandong. The PRC claims Taiwan as its 23rd province, although Taiwan is administered by the Republic of China.
  • Autonomous regions (zìzhìqū) — five regions associated with particular ethnic minority groups: Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang (Uyghur), Tibet (Xizang), Guangxi (Zhuang), and Ningxia (Hui).
  • Municipalities (zhíxiáshì) — four large cities governed directly by the central government: Beijing (the capital), Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing. Chongqing, created in 1997, is by area and population closer to a small province.
  • Special administrative regions (tèbié xíngzhèngqū) — Hong Kong (transferred from the United Kingdom in 1997) and Macau (transferred from Portugal in 1999), which retain distinct legal and economic systems under the "one country, two systems" framework.

Prefecture level

Below the provinces and autonomous regions, most territory is organized into prefecture-level units. The most common type is the prefecture-level city (dìjíshì), a city that administers surrounding rural counties as well as its urban core; examples include Suzhou, Chengdu's neighbouring prefectures, and most provincial capitals' peers. Other types include prefectures (dìqū), now rare; autonomous prefectures (zìzhìzhōu) in minority areas; and leagues (méng) in Inner Mongolia, a survival of the Mongolian banner system. Direct-controlled county-level cities and districts bypass this tier in some provinces.

County level

The county level is the oldest continuously used tier of Chinese administration, dating back more than two thousand years. Its units include counties (xiàn), autonomous counties, county-level cities (xiànjíshì), and urban districts (qū) — subdivisions of larger cities that function as counties. Special districts and forestry districts occur in a few places, and banners (qí) in Inner Mongolia are the county-level equivalents of counties.

Township level and below

Township-level units comprise towns (zhèn), townships (xiāng), ethnic townships, and urban subdistricts (jiēdào). Below them, villages (cūn) and urban residential communities (shèqū) serve as grassroots self-governing organisations; they are not formally state organs, but village committees and residents' committees perform many local administrative functions.

History

The layered system descends from the commandery–county structure established under the Qin and Han dynasties, refined in Tang and Song times through circuits and prefectures, and reshaped under the Yuan dynasty, which created the provinces in recognisably modern form. The Republic of China retained provinces subdivided into counties; the People's Republic added autonomous regions, direct-controlled municipalities, and later the special administrative regions, while converting most prefectures into prefecture-level cities from the 1980s onwards.
