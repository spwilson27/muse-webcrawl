Chastity

Chastity is a virtue related to temperance and continence. In traditional moral theology, chastity means the successful integration of sexuality within the person and thus the inner unity of man in his bodily and spiritual being; its opposite is lust. The virtue is ranked among the seven heavenly virtues, as the counterpart to the seven deadly sins of lust.

Religious views

In Christianity, chastity is required of all persons according to their state of life: consecrated virginity or celibacy for priests and religious, continence for the unmarried, and conjugal chastity for the married. Catholic teaching, articulated in the Catechism of the Catholic Church, treats chastity as a moral virtue and a gift of the Holy Spirit that calls for apprenticeship in self-mastery. The Protestant Reformers retained chastity as an ideal while rejecting mandatory clerical celibacy.

In Islam, haya (modesty) and guarding one's chastity (iffa) are central ethical commands, enjoined for both men and women in the Qur'an (e.g. 24:30–31). Buddhism presents chastity as part of the moral discipline (sila) leading to enlightenment, with complete celibacy required of monastics.

Philosophical views

Ancient philosophers, notably the Stoics, treated chastity as an aspect of the self-control central to the good life. Modern secular ethics tends to frame the underlying concerns in terms of sexual ethics, consent and self-respect rather than as a religiously imposed virtue, and the concept has been the subject of considerable feminist and historical critique — notably Michel Foucault's analysis of the "repressive hypothesis" in The History of Sexuality.

Symbols

Allegorical depictions of chastity in art — for example in Cesare Ripa's Iconologia (1593) — commonly show a veiled woman, sometimes with a unicorn, an animal once thought to be tameable only by a virgin.
