Black hole information paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a black hole forming from a collapsing star and evaporating into radiation, with a question mark over the information]
Does information vanish when a black hole evaporates?

The black hole information paradox is a conflict between quantum mechanics and general relativity: if a black hole forms from a pure quantum state and then evaporates completely by Hawking radiation, and if that radiation is exactly thermal (featureless), the information about the initial state is destroyed — violating the unitarity (information conservation) at quantum theory's core.

Stephen Hawking posed the paradox in 1976 and for decades defended information loss; John Preskill and Kip Thorne bet against him. The AdS/CFT correspondence (1997) strongly implied unitarity (the boundary theory never loses information), and Hawking conceded the bet in 2004. A sharper formulation by Almheiri, Marolf, Polchinski and Sully (2012) — the 'firewall paradox' — showed that unitarity, the equivalence principle and effective field theory outside the hole cannot all hold naively. In 2019, computations using quantum extremal surfaces and 'islands' reproduced the Page curve (the entropy profile unitarity requires) within semiclassical gravity itself — the current, still-debated resolution: information escapes, encoded in the radiation's subtle correlations, with the black hole interior partially reconstructible from it.

Why it matters

The paradox is the most productive crisis in fundamental physics: it forces any theory of Quantum gravity to explain how spacetime and information interlock, and it drove the holographic revolution, ER = EPR, and the quantum-information reframing of gravity. Its resolution is not settled consensus, but the centre of gravity of expert opinion has moved decisively toward information conservation.
