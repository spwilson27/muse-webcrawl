Memnon of Rhodes (Greek: Μέμνων ὁ Ῥόδιος; c. 380 – 333 BC) was a prominent Greek commander and mercenary general who served the Persian Achaemenid Empire against Alexander the Great. Widely regarded as Alexander's most capable opponent in the opening phase of the conquest of Persia, he advocated a scorched-earth and naval strategy that might have posed a serious threat had he lived.

Early career

Memnon and his elder brother Mentor entered Persian service in western Asia Minor; Mentor rose to command the Persian forces on the Asian coast and, after his death around 340 BC, Memnon inherited much of his position and married his widow, Barsine. Memnon had spent time at the Macedonian court of Philip II as a hostage-exile, giving him first-hand knowledge of Macedonian methods.

War with Alexander

When Alexander invaded Asia in 334 BC, Memnon counselled the Persian satraps to avoid pitched battle, destroy supplies, and carry the war into Greece by sea. The advice was rejected, and the Persian army was defeated at the Battle of the Granicus, where Memnon and his sons fought. He then organised the defence of Miletus and of Halicarnassus, whose prolonged siege slowed Alexander's advance.

Darius III appointed Memnon commander of the Persian fleet and of the Aegean coast. In 333 BC he began a promising counter-offensive, retaking Chios and most of Lesbos and threatening to bring the war to Macedonia itself — but while besieging Mytilene he fell ill and died. His death removed the ablest commander on the Persian side; Alexander captured his widow Barsine at Damascus after the Battle of Issus.

Assessment

Ancient sources such as Diodorus Siculus and Arrian treat Memnon as a commander of exceptional ability, and historians have often speculated that a longer-lived Memnon, executing his naval strategy, could have changed the course of Alexander's campaign.
