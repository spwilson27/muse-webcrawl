Present value

In economics and finance, present value (PV), also known as present discounted value, is the value of an expected income stream determined as of the date of valuation. The present value is usually less than the future value because money has interest-earning potential — a characteristic referred to as the time value of money — except during times of negative interest rates, when the present value will be greater than the future value.

Calculation

The present value of a single future amount FV to be received after n periods, discounted at an interest rate r per period, is:

PV = FV / (1 + r)n

For a series of cash flows CFt received at times t = 1 … n, the present value is the sum of the discounted flows:

PV = Σt CFt / (1 + r)t

With continuous compounding, the discount factor becomes e−rt. The periodic rate used is generally the opportunity cost of capital or a risk-adjusted discount rate.

Annuities and perpetuities

For an annuity paying a constant amount C each period for n periods, the formula simplifies to PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r. As n grows without bound, a perpetuity has present value PV = C / r, an elegant result underpinning the valuation of consols and the dividend discount model of stock valuation.

Applications

  • Net present value (NPV): subtracting an investment's cost from the present value of its expected cash inflows gives its NPV; a positive NPV indicates a value-creating project.
  • Bond valuation: a bond's price equals the present value of its coupon payments plus the present value of its principal.
  • Loan amortisation: mortgage and loan payments are computed so that the present value of the payments equals the principal.
  • Valuation and damages: present value methods underlie discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis and the calculation of lump-sum awards for lost future earnings.

Choice of discount rate

The calculated present value is highly sensitive to the discount rate, which should reflect both the time value of money and the riskiness of the cash flows. Debates in public economics — for example over the social discount rate used to evaluate climate-change policy, as in the Stern Review — turn largely on this choice.
