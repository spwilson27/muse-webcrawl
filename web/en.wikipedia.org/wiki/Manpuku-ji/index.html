Manpuku-ji (Japanese: 萬福寺, "Temple of Full Fortune") is a Buddhist temple in Uji, Kyoto, founded in 1661 by the Chinese monk Ingen Ryūki (known in Chinese as Yinyuan Longqi, 1592–1673). It is the head temple of the Ōbaku school of Zen, the third of Japan's Zen schools alongside Rinzai and Sōtō.

History

Ingen arrived in Nagasaki in 1654 at the invitation of Chinese merchant communities, bringing a more Ming-dynasty form of Chan Buddhism than the Zen then practised in Japan. Granted land by the Tokugawa shogunate, he built Manpuku-ji in continental Chinese style: its halls, inscriptions, and layout follow Ming models, and the temple's first thirteen chief priests were Chinese-born. Construction of the main buildings continued into the 1670s; the Daiyūhōden (main hall) was completed in 1668.

Architecture and treasures

The temple preserves one of Japan's finest ensembles of Chinese-style religious architecture, including the Tenno-den, Daiyūhōden, and Hatto halls, many designated Important Cultural Properties. Its treasures include a celebrated wooden fish drum (gyoban) carved in classic Ming fashion and the complete woodblocks of the Ōbaku edition of the Buddhist canon (the Tetsugen/Dai Nippon Zokuzōkyō print project), some 60,000 blocks still stored in the temple's scripture house.

Ōbaku culture

Ingen is also credited with introducing several foods to Japan, most famously kidney beans — still called ingen-mame after him — and the temple is known for fucha ryōri, Chinese-style vegetarian banquet cuisine served communally, an ancestor of modern Japanese shōjin cooking variants.
