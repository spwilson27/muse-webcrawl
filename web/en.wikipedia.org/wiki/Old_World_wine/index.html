Old World wine

Terraced vineyards of the Old World, where winemaking traditions span millennia.

Old World wine refers to wine made in the traditional wine-growing regions of Europe and the Mediterranean basin, where viticulture originated and where winemaking traditions extend back thousands of years — to Phoenician, Greek, and Roman antiquity. The term is used in contrast to New World wine, produced in regions colonized by Europeans after the Age of Exploration, such as the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa.

Philosophy and regulation

Old World winemaking is strongly associated with the concept of terroir — the idea that a wine expresses the soil, climate, and tradition of a specific place — and with legal appellation systems that regulate grape varieties, yields, and methods. France's Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC), Italy's DOC/DOCG, Spain's DO, and Germany's Prädikat system codify centuries of local practice. Old World wines are typically labelled by place (Bordeaux, Chianti, Rioja, Mosel) rather than by grape variety, in contrast to New World practice.

Style

Because of cooler climates and traditions of restraint, Old World wines are often described as more earthy, structured, higher in acidity, and lower in alcohol and overt fruit than their New World counterparts — though the contrast has blurred as styles have converged and climates warmed. The famous 1976 "Judgment of Paris" tasting, in which Californian wines outscored top Bordeaux and Burgundies, marked a turning point in the relationship between the two worlds of wine.
