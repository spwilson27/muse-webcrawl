Union shop

A union shop is a workplace in which the employer may hire either union or non-union workers, but non-union workers must join the labour union within a specified period (for example, 30 days) or lose their job. The union shop is a form of union security agreement designed to solve the "free rider" problem — workers benefiting from a union-negotiated contract without contributing to the union that represents them.

Workers at a union shop must join the union shortly after hiring.

Comparison with other arrangements

  • Closed shop: only union members may be hired. (Illegal for most employment in the United States since the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947.)
  • Union shop: non-union workers may be hired but must join the union within a set time.
  • Agency shop: workers need not join the union but must pay agency fees for representation services.
  • Open shop: union membership or fees are entirely voluntary.

Legal status

In the United States, union shops are permitted in the private sector under the National Labor Relations Act, except in states with "right-to-work" laws, which prohibit requiring union membership or fees as a condition of employment. In the public sector, the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Janus v. AFSCME (2018) barred agency fees for public employees on First Amendment grounds. Rules vary considerably in other countries.
