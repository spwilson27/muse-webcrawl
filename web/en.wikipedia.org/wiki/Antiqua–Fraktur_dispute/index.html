Antiqua–Fraktur dispute

The Antiqua–Fraktur dispute (German: Antiqua-Fraktur-Streit) was a long-running typographic and political controversy in Germany over whether German texts should be printed in blackletter typefaces (Fraktur and related broken scripts) or in roman type (Antiqua), the Latin-style lettering used in most of Europe.

Background

From the sixteenth century, German printing developed a dual practice: Fraktur for German-language texts and Antiqua for foreign languages and academic works. In the nineteenth century the choice became entangled with national identity — defenders of Fraktur saw it as authentically German, while reformers argued Antiqua was more legible, modern, and internationally compatible. The dispute repeatedly reached the Reichstag, which in 1911 narrowly rejected a proposal to make Antiqua the official script.

Resolution

Paradoxically, the Nazi regime — which had earlier glorified blackletter as Germanic — abruptly ended the dispute in 1941, when Martin Bormann's Normalschrifterlass declared Fraktur to be "Schwabacher Judenlettern" ("Jewish letters") and mandated Antiqua as the standard typeface, partly for practical communication in occupied Europe. After the war Antiqua remained standard, and Fraktur survived only in decorative uses such as newspaper nameplates, signage, and certificates.
