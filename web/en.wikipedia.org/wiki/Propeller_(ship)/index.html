Propeller (ship)

A propeller is a device with a rotating hub and radiating blades that are set at a pitch to form a helical spiral that, when rotated, exerts linear thrust upon a working fluid such as water. A marine propeller — traditionally called a screw propeller — is the most common propulsor on ships and boats.

History

The principle of the screw derives from Archimedes' screw, used for lifting water since antiquity. In the 1830s and 1840s, practical marine screw propellers were developed by Francis Pettit Smith in England and John Ericsson, whose designs were demonstrated on vessels such as SS Archimedes (1838). The Royal Navy's 1845 "tug of war" between the screw-driven HMS Rattler and the paddle steamer HMS Alecto settled the question in favour of the screw, and propellers rapidly displaced paddle wheels.

Design

Propeller blades are shaped as hydrofoil sections; as the propeller turns, the blades generate lift directed forward, accelerating water astern and producing thrust. Key parameters include diameter, pitch (the theoretical distance advanced in one revolution), blade number, blade area ratio, skew, and rake. Most ship propellers are cast from bronze alloys, particularly nickel-aluminium bronze, for strength and corrosion resistance.

Propeller efficiency is limited by cavitation — the formation and collapse of vapour bubbles on the blade surfaces at high loading, which causes noise, vibration, and erosion. Modern designs include fixed-pitch and controllable-pitch propellers, ducted propellers (Kort nozzles), contra-rotating propellers, and azimuthing pods.
