The Gardens of Versailles (French: Jardins du château de Versailles) occupy part of the grounds of the Palace of Versailles in France. Designed principally by André Le Nôtre for Louis XIV from 1661, they are the supreme example of the French formal garden (jardin à la française): a vast geometric landscape of parterres, bosquets, canals, fountains and statuary expressing royal power over nature.

Layout and features

West of the palace, the Water Parterre and Latona Fountain open onto the Royal Avenue (the "green carpet") leading to the Fountain of Apollo and the mile-long Grand Canal, where the king's gondolas and miniature warships once sailed. Flanking groves (bosquets) conceal fountains, ballrooms and colonnades, including the Enceladus Fountain and the Colonnade Grove. The gardens originally held some 1,400 fountains fed by an elaborate hydraulic system; the Machine de Marly pumped water from the Seine to supply them. The Orangery, below the palace's south terrace, shelters citrus trees in winter.

History

Construction occupied tens of thousands of workers over four decades; Louis XIV wrote a guide to touring the gardens himself. The gardens were replanted roughly every hundred years, most recently after storms felled thousands of trees in 1999. Together with the palace they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
