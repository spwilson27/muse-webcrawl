Convicts in Australia

Between 1788 and 1868, more than 162,000 convicts were transported by the British government to Australian penal colonies. Britain, which had previously sent convicts to its American colonies, turned to Australia after American independence closed that destination. The First Fleet of 11 ships, carrying about 736 convicts, arrived at Sydney Cove in January 1788 and founded the colony of New South Wales.

The transportation system

Convicts were sentenced to terms of transportation — commonly seven years, fourteen years, or life — for offences that ranged from theft and burglary to political rebellion. On arrival, most were assigned to work for the government or for private settlers ("assignment"), building roads, bridges and public buildings. Well-behaved convicts could earn a ticket of leave, permitting them to work for wages within a district, and eventually a conditional or absolute pardon.

Penal stations and secondary punishment

Convicts who offended again in the colonies could be sent to harsh secondary penal stations such as Norfolk Island, Port Arthur in Van Diemen's Land, Moreton Bay, and Macquarie Harbour, which gained grim reputations for discipline and flogging. Female convicts were often sent to "female factories" such as Parramatta, where they worked at spinning and laundry and were assigned out as domestic servants.

End of transportation

Transportation to New South Wales ended in 1840, to Van Diemen's Land in 1853, and the last convict ship, the Hougoumont, reached Western Australia in 1868. Opposition from free settlers, the availability of free migrant labour, and changing British penal policy all contributed to the system's decline. Emancipated convicts ("emancipists") became farmers, traders and landowners, and convict ancestry — once a source of shame called the "convict stain" — is now widely acknowledged in Australian family history. Eleven Australian Convict Sites are inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
