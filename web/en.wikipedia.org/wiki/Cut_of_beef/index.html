Cut of beef

During butchering, beef is first divided into primal cuts, pieces of meat initially separated from the carcass. Different countries and cultures have different ways of making the cuts, so a primal cut in one country may be a different cut in another. The same raw material may be cut, sold, named, and cooked differently from place to place.

American primal cuts

In the United States, the main primal cuts from a side of beef are:

  • Chuck — from the shoulder; used for roasts and ground beef.
  • Rib — rib roasts, rib-eye steaks, and short ribs.
  • Loin — subdivided into short loin (T-bone and porterhouse steaks) and sirloin.
  • Round — lean hindquarter cuts, rump and round roasts.
  • Brisket — from the breast; tough, suited to slow cooking and barbecue.
  • Plate — short ribs and skirt steak.
  • Flank — flank steak, from the abdominal muscles.
  • Shank — from the leg; very tough, used in soups and stews such as osso buco-style dishes.

British and European cuts

British butchery names a larger set of primals, including silverside, topside, thick flank, and clod. French butchery traditions differ again, dividing the carcass along different muscle seams. Kosher and halal butchery impose further restrictions on which parts of the animal may be consumed.

Cooking implications

Cuts from muscles that do more work contain more connective tissue and are tougher, requiring slow moist cooking, while cuts from less-worked muscles (such as the tenderloin/fillet) are tender and suited to fast dry-heat cooking.
