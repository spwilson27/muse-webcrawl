Yaldabaoth

Yaldabaoth, also called Jaldabaoth or Ialdabaoth, is a malevolent god and demiurge figure in Gnostic cosmologies — principally those of the Sethian and Ophite schools preserved in texts from the Nag Hammadi library such as the Apocryphon of John, the Hypostasis of the Archons, and On the Origin of the World. He is typically depicted as a lion-faced serpent, an image matching the lion-headed figures on so-called "Abrasax gems."

Mythology

In the Sethian myth, Yaldabaoth is the offspring of Sophia (Wisdom), a divine aeon who, acting without her consort or the approval of the higher God, produces a flawed, ignorant being. Ashamed, she hides him in a cloud outside the Pleroma. Yaldabaoth, ignorant of the higher realms, steals power from his mother, creates the material cosmos and a retinue of archons, and proclaims — echoing the God of the Hebrew Bible — "I am God and there is no other God beside me," thereby revealing his ignorance. He fashions Adam's body but unwittingly breathes into it the divine power taken from Sophia; in many texts, his jealousy of humanity and his role as the biblical creator are central to the Gnostic reinterpretation of Genesis, including the casting of the serpent of Eden as a benefactor against his commands.

Name and other roles

The name's etymology is debated — often interpreted as Aramaic for "child of chaos" or "begetter of the heavens" — and the figure also appears under the names Samael ("blind god") and Saklas ("the fool"). In some systems Yaldabaoth has three names/forms: Yaldabaoth, Saklas, and Samael. Though damned as ignorant and arrogant, in some texts his eventual repentance (in Pistis Sophia, where he is condemned for other reasons) and his son Sabaoth's elevation complicate his purely villainous role.
