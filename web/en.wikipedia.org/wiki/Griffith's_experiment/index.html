Griffith's experiment, published in 1928 by British bacteriologist Frederick Griffith, was the first experiment to demonstrate bacterial transformation: the transfer of hereditary information between bacteria, changing one strain into another. Working with Streptococcus pneumoniae in mice, Griffith showed that a heritable "transforming principle" from dead bacteria could permanently alter living ones — a result that set the stage for the identification of DNA as the genetic material.

The experiment

Griffith used two strains of pneumococcus: the virulent smooth (S) strain, whose polysaccharide capsule protected it from the mouse immune system, and the harmless rough (R) strain, which lacked the capsule. Injected alone, live S bacteria killed the mice; live R bacteria did not; and heat-killed S bacteria did not. But when he injected a mixture of live R bacteria and heat-killed S bacteria, the mice died — and their blood contained living S bacteria. The harmless R strain had been transformed into the virulent form, and the change was inherited by its descendants.

Significance

Griffith's "transforming principle" was identified in 1944 by Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty, who showed that it was DNA — a landmark result, confirmed to a sceptical community by the Hershey–Chase experiment of 1952. Griffith himself, a quiet Ministry of Health pathologist, was killed in a London air raid in 1941, before the full significance of his discovery was recognised.
