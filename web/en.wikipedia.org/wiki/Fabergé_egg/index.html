A Fabergé egg is a jewelled egg created by the jewellery firm House of Fabergé, in Saint Petersburg, Russia. As many as 69 were created, of which 57 survive today. Virtually all the eggs were manufactured under the supervision of Peter Carl Fabergé between 1885 and 1917. The most famous are the 52 "Imperial" eggs, 50 of which were delivered, two of which are considered lost, made for the Russian emperors Alexander III and Nicholas II as Easter gifts for their wives and mothers.

History

The House of Fabergé was founded by Gustav Fabergé in 1842 in St. Petersburg. His son Peter Carl Fabergé took over the firm in 1882 and transformed it into Russia's leading jeweller. In 1885, Tsar Alexander III commissioned the first Imperial Easter egg — the Hen Egg — as a gift for his wife, Empress Maria Feodorovna. The Empress was so delighted that the Tsar ordered an egg every year, giving Fabergé complete creative freedom with the single stipulation that each egg be unique and contain a surprise.

After Alexander III's death in 1894, his son Nicholas II continued the tradition, presenting eggs to both his wife, Empress Alexandra Feodorovna, and his mother, the Dowager Empress. Production ceased with the Russian Revolution in 1917, when the Fabergé family fled and the firm's workshops were nationalised.

The Imperial eggs

The Imperial eggs are renowned for their craftsmanship, combining gold, enamel, diamonds, rubies and other precious materials with intricate mechanical surprises. Famous examples include the Diamond Trellis Egg (1892), the Coronation Egg (1897), which contains a miniature replica of the Imperial coronation coach, and the Lilies of the Valley Egg (1898). Of the 50 delivered Imperial eggs, 42 are known to survive in museum and private collections around the world, including the Kremlin Armoury in Moscow, the Fabergé Museum in St. Petersburg, and the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts.

Other eggs

In addition to the Imperial series, Fabergé produced eggs for a small number of private clients, including the Rothschild family and the industrialist Alexander Kelch, for whom seven eggs were made between 1898 and 1905. A further egg, known as the Constellation Egg, was left unfinished in 1917.

Legacy

Fabergé eggs are among the most coveted objects in the decorative arts. In 2007, the Rothschild Egg sold at auction for £8.9 million, then a record for a Fabergé object. In 2014, the long-lost Third Imperial Egg resurfaced at an American flea market, where it had been bought for its scrap gold value. The eggs remain enduring symbols of pre-revolutionary Russian luxury and craftsmanship, and the Peter Carl Fabergé name is still associated with jewelled eggs produced by the revived Fabergé brand.
