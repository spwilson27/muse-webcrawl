Artificial intelligence art is art created with the assistance or agency of artificial intelligence, most prominently through generative models that produce images from text prompts. The field grew out of earlier algorithmic and generative art, but expanded rapidly in the late 2010s and early 2020s as generative adversarial networks (GANs) and then diffusion models made photorealistic and stylistically varied image synthesis widely accessible.

History

Early experiments included Harold Cohen's AARON program, developed from the late 1960s, which autonomously produced drawings and paintings. In 2015, Google's DeepDream popularized the psychedelic transformation of images using neural network feature visualization. GANs, introduced by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues in 2014, enabled systems such as Artbreeder and the portrait Edmond de Belamy, which sold at Christie's in 2018 for $432,500. From 2021, text-to-image systems — OpenAI's DALL-E and CLIP-guided models, followed in 2022 by Midjourney, DALL-E 2, and the open-source Stable Diffusion — brought AI image generation to a mass audience. In 2022, Jason Allen's Midjourney-generated Théâtre D'opéra Spatial won first place in the digital art category at the Colorado State Fair, provoking wide debate.

Techniques

Modern AI art typically relies on models trained on large datasets of image–text pairs. Diffusion models generate images by progressively denoising random noise guided by a text embedding. Artists' practices range from single-prompt generation to elaborate workflows involving image-to-image translation, inpainting, fine-tuning on personal datasets, and post-processing. Related media include AI-generated music, video, and text.

Debate and criticism

AI art has raised disputes over authorship, originality, and copyright — including whether training on copyrighted works is fair use, and whether AI-generated images can themselves be copyrighted; the U.S. Copyright Office has held that purely machine-generated material lacks human authorship. Critics also raise concerns about bias in training data, the displacement of illustrators, and the use of living artists' styles without consent, while proponents describe the tools as a democratizing new medium.
