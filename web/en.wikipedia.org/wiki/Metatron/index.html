Metatron

Metatron (Biblical Hebrew: מֶטָטְרוֹן‎, romanized: Meṭāṭrōn), also spelled Mattatron, is an angel in Judaism and Islam mentioned in a few brief passages in the Talmud and featuring prominently in mystical Jewish texts such as the Book of Enoch. He is often identified with the biblical patriarch Enoch, who was taken up to heaven and transformed into the chief of the archangels, the "scribe of righteousness", chancellor of heaven, and the "lesser YHWH".

Origins

There is no consensus on the etymology of Metatron's name; it may be a purely artificial name, possibly composed to signify the one who "stands beside (God's) throne" (Greek meta + thronos), or derived from the Latin metator, a guide or measurer. The figure probably crystallised during the early centuries CE in the apocalyptic and Merkabah (chariot) mystical traditions.

In the Talmud and Enochic literature

The Babylonian Talmud mentions Metatron three times. In tractate Hagiga, the heretic Elisha ben Abuyah sees Metatron seated in heaven — something angels cannot do — and concludes there are "two powers in heaven", a vision explained by Metatron's unique privilege as heavenly scribe. In the Slavonic 2 Enoch, Enoch is transformed into Metatron before the throne of God. The Hebrew 3 Enoch (the Book of the Palaces) describes Rabbi Ishmael's ascent and names Metatron as the Prince of the Presence, who reveals that he is Enoch, son of Jared, raised to heaven and given a throne like God's own.

Role and titles

Metatron is the highest of the angels in many medieval Jewish hierarchies, the recording angel who keeps the heavenly archives, the guide of human souls, and the conductor of the celestial choir. In some traditions he is associated with the Shekhinah and with the voice of God speaking to the prophets. Because of his exalted status, mainstream rabbinic Judaism generally treated the figure cautiously.

In popular culture

Metatron appears widely in modern fiction: in Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials trilogy as the ruling Regent of heaven; in the television series Supernatural as the Scribe of God; in Neil Gaiman and Terry Pratchett's Good Omens as the Voice of God; and as a character in the Shin Megami Tensei and Bayonetta game series.
