Glucagon


Glucagon, a 29-amino-acid peptide hormone.

Glucagon is a peptide hormone produced by the alpha cells of the pancreas. It raises the concentration of glucose and fatty acids in the bloodstream and is considered the main catabolic hormone of the body. Its effect is opposite to that of insulin, which lowers blood glucose; together the two hormones form the core of the body's glucose homeostasis system.

Structure and synthesis

Human glucagon is a single-chain polypeptide of 29 amino acids with a molecular weight of about 3,485 daltons. It is encoded by the GCG gene and is cleaved from the precursor proglucagon. In the pancreatic alpha cells, prohormone convertase 2 processes proglucagon to release glucagon, whereas in intestinal L cells the same precursor yields the incretin hormones GLP-1 and GLP-2. Glucagon is secreted in response to low blood glucose, to certain amino acids, and to catecholamines; it is suppressed by hyperglycaemia, insulin and somatostatin.

Function

Glucagon acts principally on the liver, binding to the glucagon receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor that activates adenylyl cyclase and raises intracellular cyclic AMP. This cascade stimulates glycogenolysis — the breakdown of stored glycogen to glucose — and gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from amino acids and other substrates, while inhibiting glycogen synthesis and glycolysis. Glucagon also promotes lipolysis and ketogenesis, particularly during fasting, and has effects on the heart, kidneys and gastrointestinal tract.

Medical use

Pharmaceutical glucagon is used as an emergency treatment for severe hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes who are unable to take glucose by mouth; it is supplied as an injection kit and, more recently, as a nasal spray. It is also used to relax smooth muscle during radiological examinations of the gastrointestinal tract and as a treatment for beta-blocker overdose, where it increases heart rate and contractility independently of beta-adrenergic receptors. Excess endogenous glucagon, usually from a pancreatic glucagonoma, causes hyperglycaemia and a characteristic rash (necrolytic migratory erythema) as part of the glucagonoma syndrome.
