Seven Sleepers

The Seven Sleepers, also known as the Sleepers of Ephesus and in Islam as the Companions of the Cave (Aṣḥāb al-Kahf), are a group of Christian youths in a famous legend who hid in a cave during a persecution of Christians, fell into a miraculous sleep, and awoke centuries later after Christianity had triumphed. The story is attested in both Christian tradition and the Qur'an (Sūrat al-Kahf, chapter 18).

The legend

In the common version, during the persecution under the emperor Decius (c. 250 AD), seven young Christians of Ephesus — often named Maximilian, Iamblicus, Martinian, John, Dionysius, Exacustodianus (Constantine), and Antoninus, though lists vary — fled to a cave on Mount Celion, where they were sealed in by the emperor's order. They fell asleep, and when the cave was opened during the reign of Theodosius II, some 300 years later (in some versions 372 or 478 years), they awoke thinking only a day had passed. One of them, going into the city to buy bread, astonished the townspeople with his ancient coins and his confession of the faith; the sleepers were received as living proof of the resurrection of the body, and after testifying they died and were venerated as saints.

Sources and spread

The story is first attested in Syriac sources of the 5th–6th centuries, including a homily by Jacob of Sarug, and was popularised in Latin by Gregory of Tours. It spread throughout Christendom and into Islamic tradition; the Qur'anic version adds the sleepers' faithful dog and leaves the number and duration partly ambiguous, though popular tradition usually mirrors the seven and the 309 years mentioned in the text. Caves identified with the legend are shown at Ephesus in Turkey and near Amman in Jordan, among other sites.

Veneration

The Seven Sleepers are commemorated in the Roman Martyrology and in Eastern Orthodox tradition, with feast days in late July or early August depending on the calendar. In German folk tradition, Seven Sleepers' Day (27 June) is associated with weather prognostication. The tale has been compared to other long-sleep legends, such as Epimenides and Rip Van Winkle.
