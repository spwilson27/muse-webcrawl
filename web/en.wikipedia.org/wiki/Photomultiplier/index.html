Photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) or photomultipliers are extremely sensitive detectors of light in the ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared ranges of the electromagnetic spectrum. They are members of the class of vacuum tubes, more specifically vacuum phototubes. These detectors multiply the current produced by incident light by as much as 100 million times or 108 (i.e., 160 dB), in multiple dynode stages, enabling (for example) individual photons to be detected when the incident flux of light is low.

Construction and principle of operation

A photomultiplier consists of a glass vacuum tube containing a photocathode, a series of electrodes called dynodes, and an anode. When a photon strikes the photocathode, it ejects a photoelectron by the photoelectric effect. A high-voltage supply maintains successive dynodes at increasing potentials (typically 100–200 V steps), so the electron is accelerated from dynode to dynode, knocking out several secondary electrons at each stage. The cascade grows geometrically until a measurable pulse of charge — potentially millions of electrons — is collected at the anode for each initial photon.

Photocathode materials, such as bialkali compounds (potassium–caesium–antimony), determine the spectral response. Common dynode configurations include the venetian blind, box-and-grid, linear-focused, and circular-cage (compact) designs.

Applications

Photomultipliers are used wherever extremely low light levels must be measured: scintillation detectors for gamma rays and particle physics (including large neutrino observatories such as Super-Kamiokande, whose 20-inch PMTs are the largest made), medical imaging (gamma cameras and PET scanners), spectrophotometry, astronomy, confocal microscopy, flow cytometry, and lidar. They compete with solid-state devices such as avalanche photodiodes and silicon photomultipliers, but remain unmatched for large-area, low-noise photon counting.

History

The first photomultiplier was demonstrated in the early 1930s, building on discovery of secondary emission. Vladimir Zworykin and colleagues at RCA developed practical devices during the 1930s, and photomultipliers became standard instruments in wartime and post-war nuclear physics.
