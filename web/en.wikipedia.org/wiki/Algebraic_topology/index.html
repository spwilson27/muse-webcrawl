Algebraic topology is a branch of mathematics that uses tools from abstract algebra to study topological spaces. Its basic goal is to find algebraic invariants that classify topological spaces up to homeomorphism or, more often, up to homotopy equivalence — quantities such as groups that stay unchanged when a space is stretched or bent without tearing.

Main constructions

The fundamental group π₁(X) records the loops in a space X up to deformation; it distinguishes, for example, a circle (π₁ = ℤ) from a disc (π₁ trivial). Homology and cohomology theories assign sequences of abelian groups to a space, capturing the number of "holes" of each dimension, as summarised by the Betti numbers and the Euler characteristic. Higher homotopy groups πₙ(X) generalise the fundamental group using maps from n-dimensional spheres, though they are typically much harder to compute.

Methods

The subject is organised around functors from categories of spaces to categories of algebraic objects, turning geometric problems into algebraic ones. Key computational tools include the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, the Künneth formula, spectral sequences, and classifying spaces. Foundational results include the Brouwer fixed-point theorem, the hairy ball theorem, the Jordan–Brouwer separation theorem, and the Poincaré duality theorem for manifolds.

History

Algebraic topology began with Henri Poincaré's 1895 paper Analysis Situs, which introduced homology and the fundamental group. In the 1920s Emmy Noether's insight that homology should be organised into groups reshaped the field, and Heinz Hopf, Solomon Lefschetz, and others developed it further; the mid-20th century brought cohomology theories, category theory, and applications to geometry and physics.
