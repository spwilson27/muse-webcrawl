The Maurists, or the Congregation of Saint Maur (French: Congrégation de Saint-Maur), were a congregation of French Benedictine monks founded in the early 17th century and named after Saint Maurus, a disciple of Saint Benedict. The congregation became celebrated across Europe for its rigorous scholarship and monumental critical editions of historical and patristic texts.

History

The congregation grew out of the reform movement begun in Lorraine by Didier de la Cour and was approved in France in 1621, with the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés in Paris becoming its centre. Its constitutions emphasised communal discipline combined with organised intellectual labour, with monks assigned to collaborative scholarly projects.

Scholarly achievements

Maurist scholars laid foundations of modern historical method. Jean Mabillon founded the science of diplomatics with his De re diplomatica (1681) and advanced palaeography through his travels and studies. Bernard de Montfaucon produced Palaeographia Graeca (1708) and vast illustrated surveys of antiquity. Other Maurists produced the great editions of the Church Fathers, the Recueil des historiens des Gaules et de la France, the Histoire littéraire de la France, and the treatise L'Art de vérifier les dates, a cornerstone of chronology.

Suppression

The congregation was suppressed during the French Revolution in 1790 and its monasteries confiscated, though its scholarly standards influenced later historical and philological work, and its projects were continued by institutions such as the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres.
