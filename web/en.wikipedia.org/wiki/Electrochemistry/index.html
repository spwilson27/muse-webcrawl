Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry studying the interchange of chemical and electrical energy: how chemical reactions produce electricity (galvanic cells) and how electricity drives reactions (electrolysis).

It explains and designs batteries, fuel cells, electroplating, industrial electrolysis (aluminium, chlorine, caustic soda), corrosion and its prevention, and sensors such as the pH electrode. The field began with Galvani's frogs and Volta's pile (1800) and was quantified by Faraday's laws (1830s) and the Nernst equation (1889).

[Image: A Daniell cell with zinc and copper electrodes and a voltmeter]

Cells

A galvanic (voltaic) cell turns a spontaneous redox reaction into current — oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode — while an electrolytic cell uses current to force a non-spontaneous reaction, as in extracting sodium from molten salt. Rechargeable batteries run both ways.

Potentials

Standard electrode potentials rank half-reactions' tendency to be reduced, letting chemists predict cell voltages and which metals displace which. The standard hydrogen electrode is the zero of the scale.
