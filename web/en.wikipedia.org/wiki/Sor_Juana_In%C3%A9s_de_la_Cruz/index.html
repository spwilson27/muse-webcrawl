Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz
Born: 12 November 1651, San Miguel Nepantla, New Spain
Died: 17 April 1695 (aged 43), Mexico City
Occupation: Nun, writer, philosopher, composer, poet

Juana Inés de Asbaje y Ramírez de Santillana, better known as Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (12 November 1651 – 17 April 1695), was a Mexican writer, philosopher, composer and poet of the Baroque period, and a Hieronymite nun. Her outspoken opinions granted her lifelong names such as "the Tenth Muse", "the Mexican Phoenix", and "the Phoenix of America" by her contemporary critics.

Early life

Juana Inés de Asbaje was born in San Miguel Nepantla near Amecameca. She was the illegitimate daughter of a Spanish captain, Pedro Manuel de Asbaje, and a criollo woman, Isabel Ramírez. A child prodigy, she learned to read at three, wrote a loas at eight, and by adolescence had mastered Latin, Greek logic, and Nahuatl. She taught herself in her grandfather's library at the hacienda of Panoaya.

Life as a nun

Unable to attend university because of her sex, Juana entered the Convent of Santa Paula of the Hieronymite nuns in Mexico City in 1669, where she remained for the rest of her life. The convent gave her a cell she turned into a salon and library of some 4,000 volumes, scientific instruments, and musical instruments. She corresponded with the viceregal court and with leading intellectuals of the Spanish world.

Works

Her most famous work is the Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz (1691), a defence of women's right to education written in reply to the Bishop of Puebla, who had criticized her secular poetry under the pseudonym "Sor Filotea". Her long philosophical poem Primero sueño ("First Dream", 1685) describes the soul's search for knowledge. She also wrote villancicos, carols, plays such as Los empeños de una casa, and sacramental dramas including El divino Narciso.

Death and legacy

In her final years Sor Juana renewed her vows, sold her library and instruments, and devoted herself to charity. She died on 17 April 1695 while nursing her fellow nuns during an epidemic. Today she is considered a national icon of Mexico, a pioneer of women's rights, and one of the great writers of the Spanish Golden Age; her portrait appears on the Mexican 200-peso banknote.
