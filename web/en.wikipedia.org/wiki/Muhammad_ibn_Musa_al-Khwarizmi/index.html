Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c. 780 – c. 850) was a Persian mathematician, astronomer, and geographer of the Islamic Golden Age, working at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad under the Abbasid caliphs. His name gave rise to the word algorithm, and the title of his most famous book gave us algebra.

Algebra

Al-Khwarizmi's Kitāb al-Mukhtaṣar fī Ḥisāb al-Jabr wal-Muqābalah ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing", c. 820) is considered the founding text of algebra as an independent discipline. It systematically classified and solved linear and quadratic equations, using the operations of al-jabr ("completion", restoring negative terms) and al-muqābala ("balancing"), and applied them to practical problems of trade, inheritance, and surveying. Translated into Latin in the 12th century, it shaped European mathematics for centuries.

Numerals and arithmetic

His book on calculation with Hindu numerals (surviving in Latin as Algoritmi de numero Indorum) introduced the Indian decimal positional number system, including zero, to the Islamic world and, through Latin translations, to Europe — hence "algorism" and "algorithm" from the Latinised form of his name, Algoritmi. His astronomical tables (zīj) and his geography, the Book of the Description of the Earth, which revised Ptolemy with some 2,400 coordinates of localities, were likewise influential.

Legacy

Al-Khwarizmi's combination of Indian, Greek, and Persian learning, and his methodical, problem-solving approach, mark him as one of the most influential mathematicians of the medieval world; the crater Al-Khwarizmi on the Moon is named after him.
