Mite

Mites are small arachnids (eight-legged arthropods) belonging, together with the ticks, to the subclass Acari. They are one of the most diverse groups of invertebrates, with over 55,000 described species and an estimated total of half a million to a million species. Mites occupy almost every habitat, including soil, leaf litter, freshwater, the sea, plants, and the bodies of other animals as parasites. Most mites are tiny — many are less than 1 mm long — though some parasitic species that engorge with blood can grow much larger.

Body plan and life cycle

Unlike spiders, mites have an unsegmented body in which the cephalothorax and abdomen are fused, and the mouthparts form a distinct structure called the gnathosoma. Adults have four pairs of legs; the larva has three pairs, gaining the fourth at the first nymphal stage. Mites develop through egg, larva, one or more nymph stages, and adult. Respiration is by tracheae or directly through the body surface. Many mites reproduce rapidly, and some groups, such as the oribatid soil mites, live for years and reproduce slowly.

Ecology

Soil mites are major decomposers, fragmenting leaf litter and feeding fungi and bacteria, which promotes nutrient cycling. Other mites are predators of insects and nematodes, plant feeders (such as the destructive two-spotted spider mite), scavengers in stored food and houses (flour mites, dust mites), or parasites. Dust mites feed on shed skin flakes and their faeces are a common cause of house-dust allergy and asthma. Chiggers (trombiculid larvae) bite vertebrates; scabies mites burrow into human skin; the honey-bee parasite Varroa destructor is a major threat to beekeeping worldwide.

Medical and economic importance

Parasitic mites transmit diseases including scrub typhus (via chiggers) and cause conditions such as scabies and demodicosis. Plant-feeding mites damage crops and ornamentals, while predatory mites such as Phytoseiulus persimilis are used in biological pest control. Acarology is the scientific study of mites and ticks.
