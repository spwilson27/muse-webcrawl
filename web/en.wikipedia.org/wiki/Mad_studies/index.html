Mad studies

Mad studies is an interdisciplinary field of inquiry that centres the perspectives, histories and knowledge of people who identify as Mad, mentally ill, psychiatric survivors, consumers or service users. Emerging from the psychiatric survivor movement and disability studies, it examines how madness is understood, governed and lived, and challenges the dominance of purely biomedical accounts of mental distress.

Origins

The term was popularised in the 2010s, notably through the anthology Mad Matters: A Critical Reader in Canadian Mad Studies (2013). Its roots lie in earlier survivor activism, the work of figures such as Judi Chamberlin, and critical scholarship including Michel Foucault's history of madness and Erving Goffman's study of asylums.

Themes

Scholars in mad studies explore sanism (prejudice against people deemed mad), the history of psychiatric institutions, alternatives to coercive treatment, and the intersection of madness with race, gender, class and colonialism. The field emphasises "nothing about us without us": research and teaching led by Mad people themselves.

Academic presence

Mad studies courses and programmes have been established at universities in Canada, the United Kingdom and elsewhere, and the field sustains conferences, journals and community partnerships.
