Decimal Day in the United Kingdom and Ireland was Monday, 15 February 1971, the day on which both countries decimalised their currencies. The pound sterling was divided into 100 new pence instead of the old system of 20 shillings, each of 12 (old) pence—240 pence to the pound—known as £sd.

Background

The £sd system, inherited from Roman and medieval practice, had been criticized for centuries as cumbersome for calculation and accounting, and most other countries had adopted decimal currency. A committee of inquiry chaired by Lord Halsbury reported in 1963 in favour of decimalisation, and the government announced the change in 1966, choosing the pound (rather than a new ten-shilling unit) as the base to preserve the pound's international standing. The Decimal Currency Board was set up to manage the transition, and the Decimal Currency Acts were passed in 1967 and 1969.

The changeover

New 5p, 10p, and 50p coins—equivalent to the old shilling, florin, and ten-shilling note—had entered circulation from 1968, and ½p, 1p, and 2p coins appeared on the day itself. Banks closed for several days around the weekend before Decimal Day to convert accounts, and an extensive public-information campaign used leaflets, broadcasts, and songs to teach conversions. Old pennies and threepenny bits were withdrawn, though sixpences circulated as 2½p until 1980 and shillings and florins remained in use as 5p and 10p pieces for years.

Effects

The transition was widely judged smooth. Decimalisation simplified pricing and accounting, though some consumers suspected—and studies partly confirmed—small price rises from rounding. Ireland, whose pound was linked to sterling, decimalised the same day; it later replaced the punt with the euro in 2002. The halfpenny was withdrawn in 1984 owing to inflation.
