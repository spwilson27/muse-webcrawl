Mac transition to Intel processors

The Mac transition to Intel processors was the process of switching the central processing units (CPUs) of Apple's line of Macintosh computers from PowerPC processors to Intel x86 processors. The change was announced at the 2005 Worldwide Developers Conference (WWDC) by Apple CEO Steve Jobs, who said the transition would span "the next two years". The first Intel-based Macs — an iMac and the MacBook Pro — were introduced in January 2006, and the transition was completed in August 2006 with the Mac Pro and Intel Xserve, ahead of schedule.

Background

Apple had used PowerPC chips from the AIM alliance (Apple, IBM and Motorola/Freescale) since 1994. By the early 2000s Apple was dissatisfied with the chips' performance-per-watt roadmap: the PowerPC G5 was too hot and power-hungry for laptops, and IBM had little interest in developing low-power versions. Jobs revealed that Apple had secretly maintained Intel-compatible builds of Mac OS X (the "Marklar" project) for years as an insurance policy.

Software compatibility

Developers were offered Universal binaries, which packaged PowerPC and x86 code in a single application, and a US$999 Developer Transition Kit based on a Pentium 4 tower. For end users, the Rosetta dynamic binary translator, developed with Transitive Corporation, allowed most PowerPC applications to run on Intel Macs, albeit with a performance penalty; Classic (Mac OS 9) support was dropped. Apple completed the shift in its own operating system with Mac OS X 10.6 Snow Leopard (2009), which was Intel-only, and Rosetta was removed in Mac OS X Lion (2011).

Consequences and the next transition

The Intel platform allowed Macs to run Windows natively (assisted by Boot Camp), contributed to growth in Mac sales, and defined the platform for 14 years. In June 2020 Apple announced the reverse course: the Mac transition to Apple silicon, completed by 2023 — see macOS Big Sur, the first release to support it.
