Lunar distance

The lunar distance (LD) is a unit of measurement in astronomy, equal to the average distance from the centre of Earth to the centre of the Moon — about 384,400 kilometres (238,900 miles), or roughly 1.28 light-seconds. It is used to express the distances of near-Earth objects such as asteroids passing close to Earth.

Value and variation

The Moon's orbit is elliptical, so its instantaneous distance varies from about 356,500 km at perigee to 406,700 km at apogee. The often-quoted semi-major axis is 384,400 km. Lunar laser ranging experiments, using retroreflectors left by Apollo astronauts and Soviet rovers, measure the distance with millimetre precision.

Tidal recession

Measurements show the Moon is receding from Earth at about 3.8 centimetres per year, as tidal friction transfers angular momentum from Earth's rotation to the Moon's orbit, lengthening both the day and the month. In the distant past the Moon was much closer and appeared larger in the sky.

Uses

Besides asteroid close-approach reporting, the lunar distance was historically important in navigation: the "lunar distance method" let mariners determine Greenwich time — and hence longitude — by measuring the angle between the Moon and a reference star.
