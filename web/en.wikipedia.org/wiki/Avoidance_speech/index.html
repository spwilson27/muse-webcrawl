Avoidance speech

Avoidance speech is a group of sociolinguistic phenomena in which a special restricted speech style is used in the presence of certain relatives or taboo persons. Avoidance speech is found in many Aboriginal Australian languages and Austronesian languages as well as some North American, African and other languages, most famously the so-called mother-in-law languages.

Characteristics

Avoidance styles share the phonology and grammar of the everyday style but use a greatly reduced, more generic vocabulary: one avoidance word may replace many ordinary words. In the Dyirbal language of Queensland, the everyday style (Guwal) contrasts with the mother-in-law style (Jalnguy), which a speaker used in the presence of taboo relatives such as a mother-in-law or father-in-law. Robert M. W. Dixon's description of Dyirbal made avoidance speech famous in linguistics.

In some communities the avoidance is stronger still: certain relatives do not speak to each other at all, or communicate only through a third person, or sign. The phenomenon is related to kinship systems and to respect registers such as those in Javanese and other Austronesian languages.
