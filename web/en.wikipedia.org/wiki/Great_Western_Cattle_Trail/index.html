Great Western Cattle Trail

The Great Western Cattle Trail is the name used for the cattle trail established in the late 19th century for moving beef stock and horses from Texas to the railheads and northern pastures of the Great Plains. It ran west of, and roughly parallel to, the better-known Chisholm Trail, and is also known as the Western Trail, the Dodge City Trail, the Fort Griffin Trail and the Texas Trail.

Route

The trail began in the ranching country of south and central Texas, often traced to the vicinity of Bandera, and ran north through Fort Griffin, crossing the Red River at Doan's Crossing near present-day Vernon, Texas. It then continued through Indian Territory (present Oklahoma) to the railhead at Dodge City, Kansas, and on to destinations such as Ogallala, Nebraska, and ultimately to ranches and reservations in the Dakotas, Wyoming, Montana and even Canada.

History

The trail came into heavy use from about 1874, as quarantine laws and settlement pushed the cattle trade westward away from the Chisholm Trail's eastern route. Captain John T. Lytle is credited with blazing the route in 1874 when he drove a herd from south Texas to Fort Robinson, Nebraska. Between 1874 and the mid-1890s millions of head of cattle and horses passed over the trail; some estimates put the figure at up to seven million cattle, making it arguably the most heavily used of all the cattle trails.

The trail's use declined with the spread of barbed-wire fencing, quarantine "deadlines" against Texas fever, the extension of railroads into Texas, and the disastrous winters of the mid-1880s. The last great drives were made in the 1890s.

Legacy

Today the route is marked in many communities by markers placed during a long-running project, begun in 2003, to set a cement marker every six miles along the trail from Texas to Canada. The trail is commemorated in museums in Bandera, Texas, and elsewhere along its course.
