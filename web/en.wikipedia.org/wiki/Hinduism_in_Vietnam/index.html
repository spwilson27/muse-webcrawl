Hinduism in Vietnam

Hinduism in Vietnam is practised mainly by the Cham people, an Austronesian ethnic group descended from the kingdom of Champa, and by a small Tamil Indian community in Ho Chi Minh City. Hinduism is one of the religions recognised by the Vietnamese government. The Balamon Cham, the Hindu branch of the Cham, number in the tens of thousands and live mainly in the provinces of Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận.

History

Hinduism arrived in the region through trade with India and flourished in the Indianised kingdom of Champa from roughly the 4th century AD. Cham kings built brick temple-towers (kalan) dedicated chiefly to Shiva, often in the form of a royal lingam, at sanctuaries such as Mỹ Sơn — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site — and Po Nagar near Nha Trang. As Champa was gradually absorbed by the Vietnamese state by the 19th century, many Cham converted to Islam (the Bani Cham), while the Balamon retained a localised form of Hinduism.

Practices

Balamon religion is strongly localised: it centres on Shiva but incorporates ancestral and royal deities, is led by a hereditary priestly class, and follows distinctive festivals such as Kate, the major celebration honouring kings and deities held around the seventh month of the Cham calendar. Cremation and clan-based customs distinguish Balamon practice from Indian Hinduism.

Tamil Hinduism

Separately, ethnic Tamils who settled in Saigon during the French colonial period built temples in what is now Ho Chi Minh City, most famously the Mariamman Temple in District 1, which remains an active place of worship and a notable landmark.
