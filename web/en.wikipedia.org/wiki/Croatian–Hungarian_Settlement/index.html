The Croatian–Hungarian Settlement (Croatian: Hrvatsko-ugarska nagodba; Hungarian: Horvát–magyar kiegyezés) of 1868 was a constitutional agreement defining the status of Croatia within the Kingdom of Hungary and the Austro-Hungarian Empire. Concluded a year after the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, it recognised the "Kingdom of Croatia, Slavonia and Dalmatia" as a distinct political entity with autonomy in its internal affairs, while subordinating finance, trade and defence to Budapest.

The Sabor, Croatia's parliament, in Zagreb — seat of the autonomous government under the Settlement.

Terms

The Settlement provided for:

  • A Croatian parliament (the Sabor) and an autonomous government in Zagreb headed by the ban (viceroy), who was appointed by the king on the recommendation of the Hungarian prime minister;
  • Croatian autonomy in internal administration, justice, education and religious affairs, with Croatian as the official language in the autonomous sphere;
  • Common affairs — finance, taxation, commerce, transport and the military — handled jointly with Hungary; Croatia sent deputies to the Hungarian parliament and received a fixed share (initially about 45%) of jointly collected revenue for its autonomous budget, a provision chronically disputed (the "financial nagodba" negotiations recurred roughly every ten years);
  • Difficulties left unresolved: the port of Fiume (Rijeka) was claimed by both sides — a last-minute addition (the "Fiume patch", riječka krpica) attached it provisionally to Hungary — and Dalmatia remained under Austrian administration despite being named part of the Triune Kingdom.

Politics under the Settlement

The Unionist Party, favouring close ties with Hungary, dominated the early decades; its leader Károly Khuen-Héderváry, ban from 1883 to 1903, governed through electoral manipulation and played Croat and Serb parties against each other. Opposition came from the Party of Rights (which demanded greater Croatian statehood) and later the Croat-Serb Coalition, whose 1905 Rijeka and Zadar resolutions demanded the unification of Croatia-Slavonia with Dalmatia and cooperation with other South Slavs. The Settlement remained contested until the collapse of the monarchy in 1918, when the Sabor severed state ties with Austria and Hungary (29 October 1918).

Assessment

Historians debate whether the Settlement made Croatia a genuine partner or, in practice, a subordinate province of Hungary: it granted broader autonomy than any other non-Hungarian land of the Hungarian crown, yet financial dependence and the ban's appointment procedure limited its practical sovereignty.
