Ridesharing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ridesharing, in its contemporary commercial sense, refers to an arrangement in which a passenger books a trip in a driver's personal vehicle through a digital platform, typically a smartphone app operated by a transportation network company (TNC). Major providers include Uber, Lyft, DiDi, Grab, and Bolt. The term is also historically applied to carpooling and vanpooling, in which travellers share journeys and split costs; regulators often prefer "ridesourcing" or "ride-hailing" for the commercial app-based model to distinguish it from true cost-sharing carpools.

History

Informal ride-matching dates to wartime car-sharing clubs in the 1940s, and employer vanpools spread in the 1970s. The modern model emerged after 2009–2012 with the founding of Uber (2009) and the launch of Lyft's peer-to-peer service (2012), exploiting GPS smartphones, electronic payments, and two-sided rating systems. Services expanded to pooled rides, food delivery, and micromobility.

Business model

Platforms match riders and drivers algorithmically, set prices (often with dynamic "surge" pricing), process payments, and take a commission, typically 15–30%. Drivers are generally classified as independent contractors — a status contested in many jurisdictions and partly redefined by measures such as California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019) and Proposition 22 (2020), and a UK Supreme Court ruling (2021) that Uber drivers are "workers".

Effects and criticism

Studies find mixed impacts: ridesharing can complement public transit and reduce drunk driving, but it also adds vehicle miles travelled, contributes to urban congestion, and competes with transit ridership. Debate continues over driver pay, safety, accessibility, and data practices.
