Apert syndrome

Apert syndrome
Synonym: Acrocephalosyndactyly type I
Cause: Mutation in FGFR2 gene
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Frequency: About 1 in 65,000–200,000 births
Named after: Eugène Apert

Apert syndrome is a form of acrocephalosyndactyly, a congenital disorder characterised by premature fusion of the skull sutures (craniosynostosis) and fused fingers and toes (syndactyly). It is named after the French physician Eugène Apert, who described it in 1906.

Signs and symptoms

Premature fusion of the coronal sutures restricts skull growth, producing a high, prominent forehead, a flattened midface, widely spaced and bulging eyes, and an underdeveloped upper jaw. The syndactyly of Apert syndrome typically involves fusion of the index, middle and ring fingers so that the hand resembles a mitten, with a similar pattern in the feet. Intelligence ranges from normal to impaired, partly depending on the degree of cranial involvement.

Genetics

The condition is caused by one of two closely adjacent mutations in the FGFR2 gene, which encodes fibroblast growth factor receptor 2. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, although most cases arise from new mutations, which are associated with increased paternal age.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment is surgical and multidisciplinary. Cranial surgery in infancy releases the fused sutures to allow normal brain and skull growth, midface advancement may be performed later, and staged operations separate the fused digits to improve hand function.
