African American literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stack of classic African American books: Narrative of Frederick Douglass, Invisible Man, Beloved and Their Eyes Were Watching God]
A stack of classic African American books: Narrative of Frederick Douglass, Invisible Man, Beloved and Their Eyes Were Watching God

African American literature is the body of literature produced in the United States by writers of African descent. It begins with the slave narratives of Olaudah Equiano and Frederick Douglass and the poetry of Phillis Wheatley, and encompasses the Harlem Renaissance, the protest novel, and the contemporary ascendancy of Toni Morrison and Ta-Nehisi Coates. Its recurring concerns are slavery and its afterlives, freedom, double consciousness and the search for identity in a nation founded on both liberty and bondage.

Slave narratives and early writing

Wheatley, published in London in 1773, was the first African American poet in print; the slave narrative — Equiano (1789), Douglass (1845), Harriet Jacobs (1861) — became America's foundational Black genre, testimony fused with abolitionist argument. After emancipation, Booker T. Washington's Up from Slavery (1901) and W. E. B. Du Bois's The Souls of Black Folk (1903) framed the era's great debate between accommodation and protest, and Du Bois's 'double consciousness' named its central idea.

Harlem Renaissance to mid-century

The 1920s Harlem Renaissance — Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Jean Toomer, Countee Cullen — asserted Black modernism and folk culture; Hurston's Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937), neglected then, is now canonical. Richard Wright's Native Son (1940), Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man (1952, National Book Award) and James Baldwin's essays and novels (Go Tell It on the Mountain, 1953; The Fire Next Time, 1963) defined mid-century Black letters.

Black Arts and the contemporary era

The Black Arts Movement (Amiri Baraka, Gwendolyn Brooks — the first Black Pulitzer winner in poetry, 1950) demanded art for Black liberation. A women's renaissance followed — Alice Walker (The Color Purple, 1982), Toni Morrison (Nobel Prize 1993), Octavia Butler in science fiction — and the twenty-first century has brought memoir and genre-bending work (Coates, Jesmyn Ward, Colson Whitehead, double Pulitzer winner for The Underground Railroad and The Nickel Boys) to the centre of American literature.
