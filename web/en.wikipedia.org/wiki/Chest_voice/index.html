Chest voice is a term used within vocal music. The use of this term varies widely within vocal pedagogical circles and there is no one opinion among vocal music professionals in regards to this term. Chest voice can be used in relation to the following: a specific part of the vocal range or type of vocal register; a vocal resonance area; or a specific vocal timbre. In classical singing pedagogy it is most commonly associated with the lower, fuller register in which the vocal folds vibrate along their full length and thickness, and in which the singer often feels sympathetic vibration in the chest.

Physiology

In chest voice the thyroarytenoid muscles are dominant, the vocal folds are thick and short, and the closed phase of each vibratory cycle is relatively long, producing a rich spectrum of harmonics. It corresponds broadly to the modal register used in ordinary speech and covers the lower portion of a singer's range, below the passaggio where the voice transitions toward head voice.

Usage

Popular and musical-theatre singing makes extensive use of chest voice at high pitches — "belting" — to convey intensity, while classical technique usually blends chest and head resonance to negotiate the passaggio smoothly. Vocal pedagogues debate whether "chest voice" names a true physiological register or merely a resonance sensation, but the term remains standard in studios and voice science alike.
