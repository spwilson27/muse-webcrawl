Robert K. Merton

Robert King Merton (born Meyer Robert Schkolnick; 4 July 1910 – 23 February 2003) was an American sociologist who is considered a founding father of modern sociology, and a major contributor to the subfield of criminology. He served as the 47th President of the American Sociological Association. He spent most of his career teaching at Columbia University, where he attained the rank of University Professor. In 1994 he was awarded the National Medal of Science for his contributions.

Biography

Merton was born in South Philadelphia to Jewish immigrant parents. He studied at Temple University under George E. Simpson, who introduced him to sociology, and took his PhD at Harvard under Pitirim Sorokin, with Talcott Parsons among his examiners. He taught at Tulane University before joining Columbia in 1941, becoming Giddings Professor in 1963 and University Professor in 1974.

Contributions

Merton advocated "theories of the middle range" — theories between minor hypotheses and grand unified theory — and concepts he introduced include the self-fulfilling prophecy, unintended consequences, reference groups, role strain, and the distinction between manifest and latent functions. His strain theory of deviance (1938) explains crime as a product of the gap between culturally approved goals and legitimate means. In the sociology of science he formulated the Mertonian norms (communalism, universalism, disinterestedness, organised scepticism) and the "Matthew effect" of cumulative advantage. With Paul Lazarsfeld he worked on mass communication, propaganda and the focused interview, the origin of the focus group.

Selected works

Social Theory and Social Structure (1949; enlarged 1957, 1968) collects his major essays. Other works include Science, Technology and Society in Seventeenth Century England (1938), On the Shoulders of Giants (1965), and The Sociology of Science (1973). His son, Robert C. Merton, won the Nobel Prize in Economics.
