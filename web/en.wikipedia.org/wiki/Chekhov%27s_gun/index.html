Chekhov's gun

Chekhov's gun (Chekhov's rifle; Russian: Чеховское ружьё) is a narrative principle attributed to Russian playwright Anton Chekhov that suggests a story should not make false promises: every element introduced should be necessary, and irrelevant elements should be removed. Chekhov expressed the idea in several forms, most famously in a letter of 1889: "Remove everything that has no relevance to the story. If you say in the first chapter that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third chapter it absolutely must go off. If it's not going to be fired, it shouldn't be hanging there."

Interpretation

The principle is commonly understood as a warning against extraneous details and red herrings, and as advice that elements planted early in a narrative should pay off later. It is frequently contrasted with the red herring, a deliberate distraction.

In popular culture

The concept is widely cited in writing guides, screenwriting, and criticism of film, theatre, and video games.
