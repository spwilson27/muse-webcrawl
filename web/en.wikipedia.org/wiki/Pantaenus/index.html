Pantaenus

BornSicily (traditionally)
Diedc. 200 AD, Alexandria
Known forHead of the Catechetical School of Alexandria
Feast7 July (Roman Catholic)

Saint Pantaenus (Greek: Πάνταινος; died c. 200) was a Christian theologian and head of the Catechetical School of Alexandria in Egypt during the late second century. A former Stoic philosopher who converted to Christianity, he is remembered chiefly through the testimony of his famous pupil, Clement of Alexandria, and the church historian Eusebius.

Life

According to Eusebius of Caesarea, Pantaenus was a man of great learning who directed the school at Alexandria where Christian doctrine was taught, a school said to have existed from ancient times. Clement, who succeeded him, called him "the last and greatest" of his teachers and referred to him as "the Sicilian bee," gathering the flowers of the prophetic and apostolic meadow.

Tradition holds that Pantaenus undertook a missionary journey to "India" — a term ancient writers used loosely for regions including southern Arabia or Ethiopia — where he was said to have found Christians already in possession of a Hebrew version of the Gospel of Matthew, attributed to the apostle Bartholomew. Jerome adds that Pantaenus wrote commentaries on Scripture, though none of his works survive except, possibly, a few fragments quoted by later authors.

Legacy

Pantaenus is venerated as a saint in the Roman Catholic Church, with a feast day on 7 July, and in the Coptic tradition. His importance lies in establishing the Alexandrian catechetical tradition that Clement and Origen would make famous, blending Christian teaching with Greek philosophy and scholarship.
