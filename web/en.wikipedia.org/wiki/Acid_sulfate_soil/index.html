Acid sulfate soil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acid sulfate soils are naturally occurring soils, sediments or organic substrates (e.g. peat) that are formed under waterlogged conditions. These soils contain iron sulfide minerals (predominantly pyrite) and/or their oxidation products. In an undisturbed, waterlogged state these soils are relatively harmless, but if the soils are drained, excavated or exposed to air by a lowering of the water table, the sulfides react with oxygen to form sulfuric acid, which can be released into waterways and groundwater, acidifying them and mobilising toxic metals such as aluminium and iron.

Formation

The soil-forming process begins when sulfate in seawater mixes with iron-rich sediments and organic matter under anaerobic conditions, typically in coastal and estuarine environments such as mangrove swamps, salt marshes and back-swamps. Bacteria reduce the sulfate to sulfide, which combines with iron to form pyrite (FeS2). Soils in this reduced state are termed potential acid sulfate soils; once oxidised they become actual acid sulfate soils, often recognisable by pale yellow mottles of the mineral jarosite and by soil pH values below 4.

Environmental impact

Drainage of acid sulfate soils for agriculture, aquaculture and coastal development has caused widespread land and water degradation in countries including Australia, Vietnam, Indonesia, the Netherlands and parts of West Africa. Acid drainage can kill fish and other aquatic life, corrode concrete and steel structures, and cause iron-rich precipitates ("iron floc") to smother riverbeds. Fish kills in Australian estuaries such as the Tweed and Richmond rivers have been attributed to acid runoff following heavy rain after drought.

Management

The preferred management strategy is avoidance — leaving potential acid sulfate soils waterlogged and undisturbed. Where disturbance is unavoidable, techniques include applying finely ground agricultural lime to neutralise the acidity produced, shallow excavation, and hydrological management to keep the sulfidic layer below the water table. Several jurisdictions require assessment and management plans before development on low-lying coastal land.
