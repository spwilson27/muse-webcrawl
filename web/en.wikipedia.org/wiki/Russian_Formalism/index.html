Russian formalism

Russian formalism was an influential school of literary criticism in Russia from the 1910s to the 1930s. It arose from two scholarly groups: the Moscow Linguistic Circle (founded 1915), associated with Roman Jakobson, and the Petrograd-based Society for the Study of Poetic Language (OPOYAZ), whose members included Viktor Shklovsky, Boris Eichenbaum, Yuri Tynyanov, Vladimir Propp and Boris Tomashevsky.

Core ideas

The Formalists argued that literary scholarship should study "literariness" (literaturnost) — the formal devices that make a text a work of literature — rather than the author's biography, psychology or social background. Key concepts include:

  • Defamiliarization (ostranenie), Shklovsky's term for the artistic technique of presenting familiar things in unfamiliar ways so that they are perceived freshly; art, he argued, exists "to make the stone stony".
  • Fabula and syuzhet: the distinction between the raw chronological sequence of events in a story (fabula) and the way those events are arranged and presented in the narrative (syuzhet).
  • The dominant: the focusing component of a work that rules and transforms the other components, developed by Jakobson and Tynyanov.
  • Motivation: the justification a text supplies for its devices and digressions.

Development and decline

Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) applied formalist analysis to narrative structure, identifying 31 functions in Russian fairy tales. In the later 1920s the Formalists increasingly engaged with history and social context, evolving "formal-sociological" approaches. Under mounting political pressure in the Soviet Union, formalism was officially condemned as incompatible with Marxist criticism, and by about 1930 the school had dissolved as an organised movement.

Influence

Russian formalism strongly influenced Prague structuralism (through Jakobson and the Prague Linguistic Circle), narratology, and later Western literary theory including structuralism and New Criticism. Its works became widely known in the West from the 1960s onward through translations and anthologies such as Lemon and Reis's Russian Formalist Criticism (1965).
