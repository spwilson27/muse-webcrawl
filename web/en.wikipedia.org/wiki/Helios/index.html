In ancient Greek religion and mythology, Helios (Greek: Ἥλιος, "Sun") is the Titan god and personification of the Sun. Each day he drives a chariot drawn by four horses across the sky from east to west, and sails back at night in a great golden cup across the world-encircling Ocean. As the all-seeing sun he was invoked as a witness to oaths and hidden deeds.

Mythology

Helios is the son of the Titans Hyperion and Theia and brother of Selene (the Moon) and Eos (the Dawn). Among his many children are Circe, Aeëtes, Pasiphaë and the Heliades. His most famous myth is that of his son Phaethon, who begged to drive the sun chariot for one day, lost control of the horses, scorched the earth and was struck down by Zeus's thunderbolt. On the island of Thrinacia Helios kept sacred cattle, which Odysseus's companions slaughtered despite warnings — a sacrilege Helios avenged by demanding Zeus wreck their ship.

Cult and Rhodes

Helios's principal cult centre was the island of Rhodes, his allotted portion when the gods divided the earth. The Rhodians honoured him with the Halieia festival and, after successfully resisting a siege, built the Colossus of Rhodes (c. 280 BCE), a bronze statue of the god some 33 metres tall and one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. In later antiquity Helios was increasingly identified with Apollo, and under Rome his cult merged with that of Sol Invictus.
