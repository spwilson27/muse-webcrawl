Levée en masse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Levée en masse (French pronunciation: [ləve ɑ̃ mɑs]) is a French term used for a policy of mass national conscription, most famously proclaimed by the National Convention of revolutionary France on 23 August 1793, during the French Revolutionary Wars. It mobilized the entire population for the war effort and is widely regarded as a turning point in military history: the point at which warfare began to shift from the limited armies of kings to the mass armies of nations.

The decree of 23 August 1793

Faced with invasion by the First Coalition, civil war in the Vendée, and federalist revolts, the Convention — on the proposal of Bertrand Barère — decreed that "from this moment until that in which the enemy shall have been driven from the soil of the Republic, all Frenchmen are in permanent requisition for the service of the armies." The decree assigned roles to everyone: young men would fight, married men would forge weapons and transport supplies, women would make tents and clothing and serve in hospitals, children would turn linen into lint, and old men would be carried to public squares to exhort the soldiers. Public buildings became barracks and cellars were requisitioned to extract saltpetre for gunpowder.

Effects

Within a year France fielded armies exceeding three-quarters of a million men, dwarfing the forces of its opponents and enabling the victories of 1794, such as Fleurus, that drove the Coalition from French soil. The initial call fell on unmarried men and widowers without children between 18 and 25, but the administrative machinery built for the levée evolved into permanent conscription, systematized by the Jourdan law of 1798.

Legacy

The levée en masse fused the citizen and the soldier, making military service an obligation — and, in revolutionary ideology, a right — of citizenship. Military historians describe it as ushering in the era of mass warfare that culminated in the enormous conscript armies of the two World Wars. The term is also used generically, and in international humanitarian law it refers to the spontaneous taking up of arms by inhabitants of an unoccupied territory on the approach of an enemy; such participants are recognized as lawful belligerents under the Geneva Conventions provided they carry arms openly and obey the laws of war.
