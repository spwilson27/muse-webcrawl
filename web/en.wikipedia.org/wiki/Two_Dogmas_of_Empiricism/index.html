Two Dogmas of Empiricism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"Two Dogmas of Empiricism" is a paper by analytic philosopher Willard Van Orman Quine published in The Philosophical Review in 1951. It is one of the most influential papers of twentieth-century analytic philosophy. In it, Quine attacks two central tenets — "dogmas" — of logical empiricism.

The two dogmas

The first dogma is the belief in a fundamental distinction between analytic truths (true by virtue of meaning alone, such as "No bachelor is married") and synthetic truths (true by virtue of facts about the world). Quine argues that attempts to define analyticity — through synonymy, definition, interchangeability, or semantical rules — are ultimately circular, and that no principled analytic–synthetic distinction can be drawn.

The second dogma is reductionism: the belief that every meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms referring to immediate experience. Quine argues that this verificationist picture fails because individual statements do not face the tribunal of experience alone.

Holism

In place of the dogmas, Quine proposes a form of confirmational holism: "The totality of our so-called knowledge or beliefs... is a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges." On this view, any statement can in principle be retained in the face of recalcitrant experience if sufficiently radical adjustments are made elsewhere in the system, and conversely no statement is immune to revision — not even the laws of logic.

Influence

The paper undermined the logical positivists' programme, helped revive pragmatism and naturalism in philosophy, and set the agenda for subsequent debates about meaning, ontology, and the philosophy of language. Its closing image of science as a field of force bounded by experience remains widely quoted.
