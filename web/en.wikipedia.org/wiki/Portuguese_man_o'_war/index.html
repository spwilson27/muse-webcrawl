Portuguese man o' war

The Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis) is a marine hydrozoan found in the Atlantic Ocean and the Indian Ocean. Despite its resemblance, it is not a true jellyfish but a siphonophore — a colonial organism made up of specialized individual zooids that function together as a single animal. It is named for its gas-filled float, which resembles an 18th-century Portuguese warship (a man-of-war) under full sail. Its long, venomous tentacles can deliver a painful sting powerful enough to kill fish and, rarely, humans.

Structure

The colony comprises four types of zooids: the pneumatophore (the gas bladder or "sail," filled largely with carbon monoxide and nitrogen), dactylozooids (the long stinging tentacles used to capture prey), gastrozooids (digestive polyps), and gonozooids (reproductive structures). The man o' war cannot swim; it drifts at the mercy of wind and currents, its sail oriented left- or right-handed, which helps disperse populations.

Venom and stings

Tentacles are armed with nematocysts that remain potent even in detached tentacles and beached specimens. Stings cause intense pain, whip-like welts, and in severe cases systemic symptoms. Recommended first aid generally involves rinsing with seawater and removing tentacles; recommendations on vinegar and hot water vary by species and region.

Ecology

The man o' war fish (Nomeus gronovii) lives among the tentacles, partly immune to the venom. Predators include loggerhead turtles and the violet sea snail (Janthina janthina), which floats on a raft of bubbles and feeds on the colony.
