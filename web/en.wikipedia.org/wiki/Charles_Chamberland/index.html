Charles Chamberland

Charles Édouard Chamberland (12 March 1851 – 2 May 1908) was a French microbiologist and a close collaborator of Louis Pasteur. He is best known for inventing the Chamberland filter, a porcelain filter with pores smaller than bacteria, and for his role in the famous 1881 public experiment at Pouilly-le-Fort that demonstrated Pasteur's anthrax vaccine.

Career

Chamberland studied physics at the École Normale Supérieure and joined Pasteur's laboratory in the 1870s, working on the sterilisation of culture media and the germ theory of disease. In 1884 he developed the filter that bears his name: an unglazed porcelain candle whose pores retained bacteria, allowing the production of bacteria-free filtrates. Working with such filtrates, researchers later discovered viruses, which passed through the filter that stopped bacteria.

Chamberland is also credited with an accidental but pivotal contribution to vaccinology: in 1880, chicken cholera cultures left unattended during a holiday were found to have weakened, and chickens inoculated with the old cultures survived later exposure to fresh, virulent bacteria — the observation from which Pasteur developed the principle of attenuation. Chamberland subsequently helped prepare the anthrax vaccine trials and became a deputy director of the Pasteur Institute on its foundation in 1888.

Later life

In later years Chamberland entered politics and served in the French Chamber of Deputies for the Jura department. He died in Paris in 1908.
