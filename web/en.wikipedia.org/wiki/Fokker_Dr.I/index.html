The Fokker Dr.I (designated Dreidecker, "triplane") was a World War I fighter aircraft built by Fokker-Flugzeugwerke. The Dr.I saw widespread service in the spring of 1918. It became renowned as the aircraft in which Manfred von Richthofen gained his last 19 victories, and in which he was killed on 21 April 1918.

Design and development

Designed by Reinhold Platz, the Dr.I was inspired by the British Sopwith Triplane, whose manoeuvrability had impressed German pilots. Fokker's prototype, the V.4, flew in July 1917; pre-production aircraft, designated F.I, were delivered for evaluation in August 1917. Werner Voss scored 10 victories in an F.I before being killed in September 1917.

The Dr.I was a single-seat triplane with thick cantilever wings requiring no external bracing wires between the upper wings, a plywood-covered box fuselage in its rear portion, and a distinctive comma-shaped rudder. It was powered by an Oberursel Ur.II rotary engine of 110 hp (82 kW) — a copy of the French Le Rhône — driving a two-blade propeller, and armed with twin synchronized 7.92 mm LMG 08/15 machine guns. Its maximum speed was about 185 km/h (115 mph), modest by 1918 standards, but it had an exceptional rate of climb and was supremely manoeuvrable, able to turn inside most opponents.

Operational history

Deliveries of the production Dr.I began in October 1917, but after two aircraft suffered wing failures later that month the type was grounded; investigation found poor workmanship and moisture damage in the wing construction, and deliveries resumed with strengthened wings in November. The Dr.I equipped elite Jagdstaffeln (fighter squadrons) but was never produced in large numbers — 320 were built in total.

Richthofen, the "Red Baron," flew several Dr.Is, including the famous all-red F.I 103/17 and Dr.I 425/17, in which he was fatally wounded over the Somme on 21 April 1918. Other aces associated with the type include Werner Voss, Kurt Wolff, Karl Allmenröder, and Lothar von Richthofen. Because of its speed disadvantage and engine reliability problems (wartime castor oil substitutes degraded the rotary engines), the Dr.I was withdrawn from front-line service by mid-1918 in favour of the Fokker D.VII, though it continued in training roles and home defence.

Legacy

Despite its brief career and modest production run, the Dr.I is one of the most recognizable aircraft of the First World War, largely through its association with Richthofen. Numerous replicas fly today, and the type features prominently in film, games, and popular culture depictions of the war in the air.
