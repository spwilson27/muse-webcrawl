Harm reduction

Harm reduction, or harm minimisation, is a range of public health policies and practices designed to lessen the negative social and/or physical consequences associated with various human behaviours, particularly recreational drug use, without requiring the cessation of the behaviour itself. The approach is pragmatic and non-judgemental: it prioritises reducing death, disease, and suffering over enforcing abstinence.

History

The philosophy has roots in 1920s British policy (the Rolleston Report, which permitted maintenance prescribing of opioids) and in the Dutch response to heroin use in the 1970s. It expanded rapidly in the 1980s as a response to HIV transmission among people who inject drugs: needle and syringe exchanges began in Amsterdam in 1984 and spread to the United Kingdom, Australia, and elsewhere, substantially reducing HIV incidence.

Practices

  • Needle and syringe programmes providing sterile injecting equipment.
  • Opioid substitution therapy with methadone or buprenorphine.
  • Supervised consumption facilities, such as Vancouver's Insite (opened 2003) and centres in several European cities.
  • Naloxone distribution to reverse opioid overdoses, and fentanyl test strips / drug checking services.
  • Managed alcohol programmes and safer-smoking supplies.

Debate

Supporters cite evidence that harm-reduction measures reduce infectious disease, overdose deaths, and healthcare costs without increasing drug use. Critics argue that such services enable drug use or conflict with abstinence-based recovery and drug prohibition. Harm-reduction ideas have also been applied beyond drugs — to sex work, tobacco (e-cigarettes and snus), and road safety.
