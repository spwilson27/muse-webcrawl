Mutualism (economic theory)

Mutualism is an anarchist school of thought and economic theory that advocates a socialist society based on free markets and usufructs — occupancy-and-use property norms. It is most closely associated with the French philosopher Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, who introduced the theory in the 1840s.

Theory

Mutualists hold that when labour or its products are traded, they ought to exchange for goods or services embodying "the amount of labour necessary to produce an article of exactly similar and equal utility" — the cost principle associated with Josiah Warren. Profit, rent and interest are regarded as exploitation that persists only because of government-enforced monopolies over land, credit and trade. Mutualism therefore proposes that land be held only by those who occupy and use it, that credit be provided at cost by mutual banks issuing currency against real value, and that exchange be freed of privilege rather than of competition.

History

Proudhon developed mutualism in works such as What Is Property? (1840) and proposed a Bank of the People in 1848–49. In the United States, individualist anarchists including Josiah Warren, Stephen Pearl Andrews and Benjamin Tucker developed kindred ideas, running "time stores" that priced goods by labour time and notes denominated in hours. Later writers, including Kevin Carson, have revived mutualist arguments under labels such as "free-market anti-capitalism."

Relation to other movements

Mutualism is sometimes described as a middle ground between individualist and collectivist anarchism. Many mutualists support labour unions and cooperatives, and the mutualist emphasis on reciprocal exchange influenced the cooperative movement and later market socialist thought.
