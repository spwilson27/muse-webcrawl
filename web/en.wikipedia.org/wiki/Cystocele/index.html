A cystocele, also known as a prolapsed bladder, is a medical condition in which a woman's bladder bulges into her vagina. It occurs when the supportive tissues and muscles between the bladder and the front (anterior) wall of the vagina weaken and stretch, allowing the bladder to descend from its normal position. Cystocele is one form of pelvic organ prolapse.

A cystocele occurs when the bladder bulges into the anterior vaginal wall.

Signs and symptoms

Some women with a mild cystocele have no symptoms. When symptoms occur they may include a feeling of fullness or pressure in the pelvis and vagina, a visible or palpable bulge in the vagina, discomfort during sexual intercourse, difficulty emptying the bladder completely, urinary incontinence, and repeated bladder infections. Symptoms often worsen when standing for long periods and improve when lying down.

Causes

A cystocele is caused by weakening of the pelvic floor muscles and connective tissues (fascia) that support the bladder. Factors that increase pressure on the pelvic floor or damage its support structures include pregnancy and vaginal childbirth, ageing and the hormonal changes of menopause, obesity, chronic coughing, chronic constipation with straining, and repeated heavy lifting. Hysterectomy and other pelvic surgery may also contribute.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made by pelvic examination, during which the clinician observes the anterior vaginal wall bulging, often asking the patient to bear down or cough. The severity of prolapse is graded, commonly from grade 1 (mild descent) to grade 4 (complete protrusion). Urodynamic testing or imaging may be used when urinary symptoms are prominent.

Treatment

Treatment depends on severity and symptoms. Mild cases may be managed with pelvic floor muscle exercises (Kegel exercises), weight management, and avoidance of heavy lifting. A vaginal pessary — a removable device inserted into the vagina to support the bladder — is a common non-surgical option. Estrogen therapy may be recommended after menopause. Surgery, such as anterior colporrhaphy (repair of the anterior vaginal wall), is considered when conservative measures fail or the prolapse is severe.
