Pockels effect

Diagram of a Pockels cell electro-optic modulator

The Pockels effect is an electro-optic effect in which the refractive index of a material changes linearly with an applied electric field. It is named after Friedrich Carl Alwin Pockels, who studied the effect in the 1890s. Because the change in refractive index is directly proportional to the applied field, the Pockels effect is also called the linear electro-optic effect, in contrast to the Kerr effect, in which the change is quadratic in the field.

The effect occurs only in crystals that lack inversion symmetry, such as lithium niobate (LiNbO3), gallium arsenide (GaAs), and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KDP). When a voltage is applied across such a crystal, the induced birefringence retards one polarization component of a light beam relative to the other, allowing the crystal to act as a voltage-controlled wave plate.

Applications

A device exploiting the effect is the Pockels cell, widely used as a fast optical switch and modulator. Pockels cells can switch in nanoseconds and are used for Q-switching lasers, modulating laser beams in telecommunications, picking pulses from mode-locked laser trains, and in electro-optic sampling. The half-wave voltage is the applied voltage needed to induce a phase retardation of half a wavelength.
