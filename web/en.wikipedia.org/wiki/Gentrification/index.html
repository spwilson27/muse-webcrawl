Gentrification is the process of change in the character of a neighbourhood through the influx of more affluent residents ("gentry") and businesses. It frequently shifts a neighbourhood's racial and ethnic composition and average household income by accelerating housing development of the built environment to cater to the tastes of the incoming class, while displacing existing residents.

Etymology and history

British sociologist Ruth Glass coined the term in 1964 to describe the influx of middle-class people displacing working-class residents in London neighbourhoods such as Islington. The process intensified in many Western cities from the 1970s onward as manufacturing declined, professionals returned to inner cities, and disinvested housing stock offered a "rent gap", in Neil Smith's theory, between actual ground rent and potential rent under a "higher" use.

Causes and debates

Explanations divide between production-side accounts (capital flows, rent gaps, state-led regeneration and tax incentives) and consumption-side accounts (changing lifestyles and cultural preferences of the new middle class, in the work of David Ley and Sharon Zukin). Empirical research disagrees about how frequently direct displacement occurs and whether incumbent residents also benefit from improved services, safety and housing values; indirect ("exclusionary") displacement — where departing working-class households are replaced by wealthier ones — is widely documented.

Effects and policy responses

Gentrification has been associated with falling crime and rising amenities alongside unaffordable rents, loss of social networks and cultural displacement. Policy responses include inclusionary zoning, community land trusts, rent regulation, right-to-return rules and protections for legacy businesses. The term is now also applied to rural, tourist and "super-gentrification" contexts in global cities.
