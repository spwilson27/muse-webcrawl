The Albertine Rift is the western branch of the East African Rift, covering parts of Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda, Burundi and Tanzania. It stretches from the northern end of Lake Albert to the southern end of Lake Tanganyika and takes its name from Lake Albert.

Geology and geography

The rift was formed by tectonic forces pulling the African continent apart, beginning in the Miocene. It is flanked by some of Africa's highest mountain ranges, including the Virunga Mountains, the Mitumba Mountains and the Rwenzori Mountains, and contains deep rift lakes such as Lake Tanganyika — the second deepest freshwater lake in the world — as well as Lakes Kivu, Edward and Albert. The region remains geologically active, with volcanoes such as Mount Nyiragongo and Mount Nyamuragira among Africa's most active.

Biodiversity

The Albertine Rift is one of the most biologically rich regions on the African continent. Its montane forests, part of the Albertine Rift montane forests ecoregion, harbor an exceptional concentration of endemic species, including more endemic birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians and plants than any other region of comparable size in Africa. Notable species include the mountain gorilla, eastern chimpanzee, L'Hoest's monkey, the Rwenzori turaco and numerous endemic cichlid fishes in the rift lakes.

Conservation

Protected areas in the region include Virunga National Park, Bwindi Impenetrable National Park, Queen Elizabeth National Park, Kibale National Park and Nyungwe Forest National Park. The region's ecosystems face severe pressure from dense human populations, deforestation, poaching and civil conflict, and the Albertine Rift is recognized as both a global biodiversity hotspot and one of the most threatened ecoregions in Africa.
