Ancient Greek grammar describes the morphology and syntax of the Ancient Greek language, a highly inflected Indo-European language. Nouns, adjectives, and pronouns are declined for five cases, three genders, and three numbers (singular, dual, and plural); verbs are conjugated for person and number and distinguished by tense-aspect stems, three voices (active, middle, passive), and moods including the indicative, subjunctive, optative, and imperative. Word order is relatively free, with information structure rather than fixed position determining emphasis, and meaning is carried largely by inflectional endings.

Nominal morphology

Nouns fall into three declensions. The first declension is mostly feminine (e.g., τιμή "honour"), the second mostly masculine and neuter (λόγος "word", δῶρον "gift"), and the third comprises consonant stems of all genders. The definite article (ὁ, ἡ, τό) is fully declined and is a characteristic feature of Classical Greek absent from Homer. Adjectives agree with their nouns in case, gender, and number; comparatives and superlatives are formed with -τερος/-τατος or -ίων/-ιστος.

Verbal system

The verb is built on aspectual stems: the present stem expresses ongoing action, the aorist stem a simple completed event, and the perfect stem a state resulting from a past action; a future stem marks futurity without aspectual contrast. Past tenses in the indicative take the augment (ἐ-) prefix. The middle voice indicates that the subject acts on or for itself, and many verbs are "deponent," having middle forms with active meaning. Participles, declined like adjectives, and infinitives are used with great flexibility, including the articular infinitive.

Syntax and style

Greek syntax relies heavily on participles and subordinate clauses, enabling the elaborate periodic sentences of authors like Thucydides and Demosthenes. Particles (μέν, δέ, γάρ, οὖν) structure discourse. The optative appears in wishes, potential statements, and subordinate clauses in secondary sequence. Standard reference works include Herbert Weir Smyth's Greek Grammar (1920, revised 1956).
