A Torah scroll; Deuteronomy is the fifth and final book of the Pentateuch.

Deuteronomy (from Greek Δευτερονόμιον, "second law"; Hebrew: דְּבָרִים, Devarim, "Words") is the fifth book of the Torah (the first five books of the Hebrew Bible) and of the Christian Old Testament. It presents itself as a series of speeches delivered by Moses to the Israelites in the plains of Moab, on the eve of their entry into the Promised Land.

Structure

The book consists of three sermons or speeches by Moses. The first (chapters 1–4) recapitulates the wanderings in the wilderness. The second (chapters 5–26), the core of the book, restates the Ten Commandments and sets out the Deuteronomic Code — laws governing worship, civil life, and conduct. The third (chapters 27–34) contains blessings and curses, the appointment of Joshua as Moses' successor, the Song of Moses, and an account of Moses' death on Mount Nebo.

Composition

Traditional Jewish and Christian belief attributes Deuteronomy to Moses. Modern scholars generally view it as a composite work that took shape over centuries, with a core often associated with the reign of King Josiah of Judah (late 7th century BCE), when a "book of the law" was reportedly found in the Temple (2 Kings 22), followed by later editing during and after the Babylonian exile.

Themes

Central themes include covenant loyalty to Yahweh alone, the centralisation of worship at a single chosen place, care for the vulnerable (widows, orphans, and foreigners), and the Shema — "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (6:4) — one of the most important declarations in Judaism.

Influence

Deuteronomy's covenant theology profoundly influenced later biblical books and both Jewish and Christian thought. Its laws on kingship, prophecy, and social justice remain widely studied.
