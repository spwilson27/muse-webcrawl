Vanitas

Vanitas (Latin for 'vanity', in the sense of emptiness or futility) is a genre of still-life painting that flourished in the Netherlands in the early 17th century. A vanitas painting contains collections of objects symbolic of the inevitability of death and the transience and vanity of earthly achievements and pleasures; it exhorts the viewer to consider mortality and to repent.

A typical vanitas: skull, hourglass, guttering candle and faded flowers as emblems of mortality.

Etymology and background

The term derives from the opening words of Ecclesiastes in the Latin Vulgate: Vanitas vanitatum omnia vanitas — "Vanity of vanities; all is vanity." The genre is closely related to the older memento mori tradition in Christian art and to the funerary art of ancient Rome. Vanitas paintings were a specialty of the university city of Leiden, where painters such as David Bailly developed the type around the 1620s.

Symbolism

Vanitas objects carry a widely understood symbolic language:

  • Skulls — the certainty of death.
  • Hourglasses and watches — the brevity of life and the passage of time.
  • Extinguished or smoking candles — life extinguished.
  • Wilting flowers, ripe or rotting fruit — decay and the brevity of beauty.
  • Soap bubbles (homo bulla, "man is a bubble") — fragility of life.
  • Books, instruments, globes, and scientific objects — the vanity of earthly knowledge.
  • Gold vessels, jewellery, coins, playing cards, and musical instruments — the vanity of wealth and pleasure.
  • Crowns, sceptres, and laurel wreaths — the vanity of worldly power and fame.

Artists

Leading practitioners include Pieter Claesz, Willem Claesz Heda, Harmen Steenwyck, Evert Collier, and Jan Davidsz de Heem. The genre's sombre, virtuoso displays of texture let painters demonstrate their skill while delivering an unmistakably moralising message to prosperous Dutch burgher patrons.

Legacy

The vanitas motif persists in later art — from Cézanne's skull still lifes to contemporary works by Damien Hirst and the memento mori imagery of modern photography — whenever artists juxtapose luxury with decay.
