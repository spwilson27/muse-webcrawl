Share repurchase

A share repurchase, commonly known as a stock buyback, is a corporate transaction in which a company buys back its own outstanding shares from the marketplace. Repurchased shares may be retired or held as treasury stock. Buybacks reduce the number of shares outstanding, which raises each remaining share's claim on earnings and assets, and they return cash to shareholders who sell. Alongside dividends, they are one of the two principal methods by which corporations distribute cash to shareholders.

Methods

Companies repurchase shares through several routes:

  • Open market purchases — the company buys shares on the exchange over months or years under an announced authorization; this is by far the most common method in the United States.
  • Fixed-price tender offer — the company offers to buy a stated number of shares at a specified price, usually at a premium, for a limited period.
  • Dutch auction repurchase — shareholders tender within a price range and the company buys at the lowest clearing price.
  • Privately negotiated repurchases — targeted buybacks from a large holder, including "greenmail," a premium buyout to forestall a hostile takeover.
  • Accelerated share repurchases (ASRs) — the company contracts with an investment bank to receive a large block of shares immediately, funded by the bank's market purchases.

Motives and effects

Firms justify buybacks as returning surplus cash, signalling that management believes the shares are undervalued, offsetting dilution from employee stock options, and increasing earnings per share. In the United States buybacks historically enjoyed tax advantages over dividends (capital gains treatment and deferral) and greater flexibility, since authorizations need not be completed. Since 1982, SEC Rule 10b-18 has provided a safe harbour protecting open-market repurchases from manipulation liability, and analysts credit it with the enormous growth of buybacks: US companies have spent trillions of dollars on repurchases, and in peak years S&P 500 buybacks exceeded $800 billion. In 2023 a 1% federal excise tax on net repurchases took effect under the Inflation Reduction Act.

Criticism

Critics argue that buybacks enrich executives (whose pay is often tied to earnings per share), substitute financial engineering for investment in workers and R&D, and can be badly timed — companies bought record amounts near market peaks in 2007 and again before the 2020 crash. Senator- and author-level critics have proposed restrictions or higher taxes, while defenders respond that repurchases simply recycle capital to more productive uses and that shareholders reinvest the proceeds elsewhere in the economy.
