Republicanism in Australia

Republicanism in Australia is a movement to change Australia's system of government from a constitutional monarchy to a republic, replacing the monarch — currently Charles III, represented federally by the governor-general — with an Australian head of state. Although Australia is fully independent, the monarch is shared with the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth realms.

History

Republican sentiment appeared in the nineteenth century, notably in the Bulletin magazine and among Irish-Australian and goldfields radicals, but federation in 1901 kept the Crown. The issue revived in the 1970s and gained momentum under Prime Minister Paul Keating, who established the Republic Advisory Committee in 1993. Prime Minister John Howard, a monarchist, convened a Constitutional Convention in 1998, half elected and half appointed, which recommended a "bi-partisan appointment" model in which the president would be chosen by a two-thirds majority of a joint sitting of Parliament.

The 1999 referendum

On 6 November 1999 Australians voted on becoming a republic with that model of president. The proposal was defeated nationally (about 45% Yes) and in every state, despite opinion polls showing majority support for a republic in principle. Post-mortems blamed the specific model: many republicans, especially supporters of direct election, campaigned for No, splitting the vote. A second question on a constitutional preamble also failed.

Since 1999

The issue receded under later governments, though the Australian Republic Movement continues to campaign, proposing a model with direct election following nominations by parliaments. The Albanese government appointed an Assistant Minister for the Republic in 2022 but did not hold a referendum in its first term, and the position was dropped after the government's 2025 re-election. Polling suggests support for a republic fluctuates with the popularity of the royal family and has often fallen short of a majority in recent years.
