Spoils system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The spoils system is the practice, in US politics, of a winning party rewarding its campaign workers and supporters with government jobs — replacing the previous administration's appointees wholesale. The phrase comes from Senator William L. Marcy's 1832 defence of Andrew Jackson's appointments: "to the victor belong the spoils of the enemy". Jackson systematised rotation in office as democratic doctrine (offices simple enough for any citizen, corruption bred by permanence), and for half a century the federal service ran on partisan turnover, assessments on officeholders' salaries funding campaigns.

Reform and residues

The system's pathologies — incompetence, corruption, campaign machinery staffed by the state — culminated symbolically in President Garfield's assassination (1881) by a disappointed office-seeker, propelling the Pendleton Act (1883) to create competitive-examination merit appointments, initially covering about a tenth of federal jobs and expanding by later statute and executive action until the merit service covered the great majority. Patronage persists at the margins: a few thousand political appointees atop US agencies (an unusually deep layer by comparative standards), state and local remnants (litigated in the Rutan and Elrod line limiting party-based hiring and firing), and judicial clerk and ambassadorial postings as political plums. The reform debate never closed: merit insulation versus democratic responsiveness is re-argued whenever administrations seek to reclassify civil servants as at-will.
