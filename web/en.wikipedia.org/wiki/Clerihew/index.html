Clerihew

A clerihew is a whimsical, four-line biographical poem invented by Edmund Clerihew Bentley. The first line is the name of the poem's subject, usually a famous person, put at the end of the line; the rhyme scheme is AABB; the lines may be of unequal length and irregular metre; and the poem is typically absurd, trivial, or gently satirical rather than informative.

Edmund Clerihew Bentley (1875–1956), who invented the form as a schoolboy.

Origin

Bentley (1875–1956) devised the form as a sixteen-year-old schoolboy at St Paul's School, London; the first example, about the chemist Sir Humphry Davy, begins:

Sir Humphry Davy
Abominated gravy.
He lived in the odium
Of having discovered sodium.

His first collection, Biography for Beginners (1905), illustrated by his friend G. K. Chesterton, popularized the form; further volumes followed, and the clerihew became a staple of light verse, with later practitioners including W. H. Auden. The charm of the form lies in the collision between a dignified proper name and deliberately bathetic rhymes and pseudo-facts — the second line often being a strained rhyme with the name, and the remaining lines offering irrelevant "biographical" detail.
