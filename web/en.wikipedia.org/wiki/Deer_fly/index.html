Deer fly

Family: Tabanidae

Genus: Chrysops and related genera

Deer flies are bloodsucking insects of the horse fly family Tabanidae, principally members of the genus Chrysops. They are smaller than horse flies, typically 6 to 12 mm long, and are recognisable by their brightly patterned eyes and dark bands or spots on the wings.

Biology

Females require a blood meal to develop their eggs and bite mammals, including deer, livestock, and humans, cutting the skin with blade-like mouthparts and lapping the pooled blood. Males feed on nectar and pollen. Females lay egg masses on vegetation overhanging wet ground; the larvae develop in mud and saturated soil at the margins of ponds, marshes, and streams, where they feed on other invertebrates and organic matter.

Medical significance

Deer fly bites are painful and can provoke local swelling and, in sensitized individuals, stronger reactions. Chrysops species are mechanical vectors of several diseases and parasites, notably the bacterium Francisella tularensis (tularaemia, historically called "deer fly fever" in parts of the western United States), the filarial worm Loa loa in West and Central Africa, and anthrax among livestock. Their painful attacks also cause annoyance and lost grazing in cattle.

Control

Protective clothing, repellents containing DEET, and traps exploiting the flies' attraction to dark moving objects and carbon dioxide are the usual defences; landscape-scale control is difficult because breeding sites are widespread wetlands.
