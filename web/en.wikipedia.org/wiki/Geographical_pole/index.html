Geographical pole

A geographical pole or geographic pole is either of the two points on Earth where its axis of rotation intersects its surface. The North Pole lies in the Arctic Ocean, while the South Pole lies in Antarctica.

Geographical pole
Points: North Pole (90°N), South Pole (90°S)
Related: Magnetic pole, geomagnetic pole

Definition

At each pole, all lines of longitude converge, day and night each last (in principle) six months, and the Sun traces a circle around the sky. The poles slowly move relative to the surface — polar motion — mainly due to the Chandler wobble and annual mass redistribution; the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service tracks this movement.

Other poles

Distinct from the geographical poles are the magnetic poles (where the geomagnetic field points vertically), the geomagnetic poles (dipole model intersections), and the poles of inaccessibility (points farthest from any coastline). Other planets and moons likewise have geographical poles defined by their rotation axes, by convention using the system's north.
