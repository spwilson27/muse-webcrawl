Ancient Greek calendars

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ancient Greek calendars were a diverse set of lunisolar calendars used by the Greek city-states. Because each polis kept its own calendar — with its own year-start, month names, and intercalation rules — there was no single "Greek calendar"; the best documented is the Attic calendar of Athens. All shared the basic design: twelve lunar months alternating between 29 ("hollow") and 30 ("full") days, giving a year of about 354 days, corrected to the solar year by periodically inserting a thirteenth, intercalary month.

Structure

The month began with the new moon (noumenia) and was divided into three decades keyed to the phases of the moon: the "waxing" days, the days "over ten", and the "waning" days, counted backwards to the last day, called "old and new". Months were named after festivals held in them: in Athens, for example, the year began with Hekatombaion (roughly July), followed by Metageitnion, Boedromion, Pyanepsion, Maimakterion, Poseideon, Gamelion, Anthesterion, Elaphebolion, Mounichion, Thargelion, and Skirophorion. Intercalation usually repeated Poseideon.

Dating and eras

Historical events were dated by eponymous magistrates — at Athens, the archon of the year — rather than by a continuous count of years, which complicates conversion to modern dates. Greeks reckoned eras from events such as the first Olympic Games (traditionally 776 BC), counted in four-year Olympiads. Later, the Seleucid era (from 312 BC) gave the Hellenistic world a continuous numbering. States also ran parallel calendars: Athens distinguished the festival calendar from a "bouleutic" calendar used for council business, a source of notorious confusion.

Other calendars

The Macedonian calendar, spread by Alexander's conquests, used Babylonian-influenced month names (Dios, Apellaios, Audnaios, and so on) and underlay several calendars of the Hellenistic East. The Spartan, Delian, Delphic, and Aetolian calendars each had distinct month names and year-beginnings, reflecting the political fragmentation of the Greek world.
