Enhanced oil recovery

Enhanced oil recovery (abbreviated EOR), also called tertiary recovery, is the extraction of crude oil from an oil field that cannot be extracted otherwise. Whereas primary recovery relies on the natural pressure of the reservoir and secondary recovery on waterflooding or gas injection to maintain pressure, enhanced recovery alters the properties of the oil or the reservoir rock to mobilise oil left behind. EOR can extract 30% to 60% or more of a reservoir's original oil in place, compared with 20% to 40% using primary and secondary recovery.

Techniques

Gas injection

Gas injection is the most common EOR approach in the United States. Gases such as carbon dioxide, natural gas or nitrogen are pumped into the reservoir, where they expand and mix with the oil, reducing its viscosity and improving its flow. Carbon dioxide EOR is the most prominent method; it also offers the possibility of permanent CO₂ storage in the reservoir, linking the process to carbon capture and storage.

Thermal recovery

Thermal methods, principally steam injection, heat the crude oil in the formation to decrease its viscosity, which is particularly effective for heavy oils and oil sands. Variants include cyclic steam stimulation ("huff and puff"), steam flooding and steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD).

Chemical injection

Chemical flooding uses polymer flooding, surfactant (micellar) flooding and alkaline–surfactant–polymer (ASP) flooding. Surfactants lower the interfacial tension between oil and water so that trapped oil droplets can be released, while polymers thicken the injected water to improve sweep efficiency.

Economics and environment

EOR projects are capital-intensive and are usually viable only when oil prices are sufficiently high; CO₂ EOR additionally depends on supplies of cheap carbon dioxide, traditionally from natural CO₂ reservoirs but increasingly from industrial capture. Environmental concerns include the energy and emissions involved in producing steam, and the fate of injected CO₂, though proponents note that CO₂ EOR reservoirs can retain a substantial fraction of injected gas.
