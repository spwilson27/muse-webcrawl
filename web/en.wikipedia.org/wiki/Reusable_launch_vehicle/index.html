Reusable launch vehicle

A reusable launch vehicle (RLV) has parts that can be recovered and reflown, while carrying payloads from the surface to outer space. Rocket stages are the most common launch vehicle parts to reuse. Smaller parts such as fairings, boosters, or rocket engines can also be reused, in addition to the entire vehicle being reusable as in a spaceplane.

History

Fully reusable vehicles were long a goal of spaceflight advocates, as reuse promises to greatly reduce the cost of access to space. The partially reusable Space Shuttle (1981–2011) recovered and reflew its orbiters and solid rocket boosters but expended its external tank, and its refurbishment costs proved high. The Soviet Buran flew once in 1988.

Modern developments

Since 2015, SpaceX has routinely recovered and reflown the first stage of its Falcon 9 rocket, landing boosters on drone ships and landing pads using propulsive descent; the Falcon Heavy and Falcon 9 also recover their payload fairings. SpaceX's Starship is designed to be fully and rapidly reusable. Blue Origin's suborbital New Shepard and orbital New Glenn also recover their first stages, and Rocket Lab has pursued recovery of the Electron first stage.

Recovery methods

Recovery techniques include parachutes with splashdown or mid-air capture, horizontal winged landing as with the Space Shuttle, and vertical propulsive landing. Trade-offs include the mass penalty of recovery hardware and propellant reserves against refurbishment and fleet-size savings.
