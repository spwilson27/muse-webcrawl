William of Rubruck

Bornc. 1215–1230, Rubrouck, County of Flanders
Diedc. 1270 or later
OccupationFranciscan friar, missionary, explorer
Known forJourney to the Mongol court at Karakorum, 1253–1255

William of Rubruck (c. 1220 – c. 1270), also known as Willem van Ruysbroeck, was a Flemish Franciscan missionary and explorer whose account of his journey across Asia to the court of the Mongol khan Möngke is one of the great travel narratives of the Middle Ages.

Journey to Mongolia

William had accompanied King Louis IX of France on the Seventh Crusade and reached the Holy Land. In 1253, with the king's blessing and letters concerning an alliance and the conversion of the Mongols, he travelled from Constantinople across the Black Sea, through the Crimea and the Central Asian steppe to the camp of Batu Khan on the Volga, and onward to Möngke Khan's court near Karakorum, which he reached at the end of December 1253. There he debated theology with Nestorian Christians, Muslims and Buddhists, ministered to European captives, and observed Mongol customs, diet, felt tents and kumis with ethnographic precision.

The Itinerarium

Forbidden to remain as a missionary, William returned by way of the Caucasus and Anatolia, arriving in Tripoli in August 1255. His report to Louis IX, the Itinerarium, is observational, witty and notably free of the marvels that fill other medieval travel accounts; it records, among much else, the first clear Western evidence that China (Cathay) was the same land as the ancient Seres, and describes paper money, rice wine and the geography of the Caspian Sea — correctly identified as a lake, not an inlet of the ocean. Roger Bacon drew on the account in his Opus Majus.
