Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (27 August 1770 – 14 November 1831) was a German philosopher and a major figure in German idealism. His philosophising encompasses the whole of reality, including history, art, religion, and logic, and is organised as a comprehensive system aiming to grasp the whole in its rational development.

Hegel's main works include The Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), the Science of Logic (1812–16), the Encyclopedia of the Philosophical Sciences (1817), and Elements of the Philosophy of Right (1820). He is known for his dialectical method — often summarised as thesis, antithesis, synthesis — and for concepts such as the master-slave dialectic, the end of history, Geist ("spirit" or "mind"), and Aufhebung (sublation).

Life

Born in Stuttgart, Hegel studied at the Tübinger Stift with Friedrich Hölderlin and Friedrich Schelling. He worked as a tutor, journalist, and schoolmaster in Nuremberg before becoming professor at Heidelberg and then at the University of Berlin, where he taught until his death, probably of cholera, in 1831.

Philosophy

The Phenomenology of Spirit traces the development of consciousness through shapes such as sense-certainty, self-consciousness — including the famous master-slave (lordship and bondage) dialectic — reason, spirit, and absolute knowing. His political philosophy in the Philosophy of Right defends a constitutional state uniting family, civil society and the state, and his philosophy of history treats world history as progress in the consciousness of freedom.

Influence

After Hegel's death, his followers split into Right and Left (Young) Hegelians; Karl Marx's historical materialism developed through a critical transformation of Hegel's dialectic. Hegel deeply influenced existentialism, hermeneutics, critical theory, and contemporary analytic figures such as Robert Brandom and John McDowell.
