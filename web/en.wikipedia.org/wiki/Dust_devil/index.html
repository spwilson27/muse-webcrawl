Dust devil

Dust devil
TypeWhirlwind weather phenomenon
Typical height10–300 m (some exceed 1 km)
Wind speedsUsually below 100 km/h; rarely EF1-equivalent
ConditionsClear skies, intense surface heating, light winds

A dust devil is a strong, well-formed, and relatively short-lived whirlwind that forms when hot air near the surface rises rapidly through cooler air above it, stretching vertical vorticity into a rotating column. Dust devils pick up dust and debris from the ground, making the vortex visible, and are a common sight in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide.

Formation

Dust devils form on clear days when the sun strongly heats bare ground, creating a layer of superheated air at the surface. If horizontal wind shear or small pre-existing eddies give the rising air rotation, the updraft stretches the rotation into a tight vortex, much as an ice skater spins faster when pulling in their arms. They usually occur under light ambient winds and dissipate within minutes.

Intensity and hazards

Most dust devils are small and weak, with winds comparable to a modest gust, but the largest and strongest can reach intensities equivalent to an EF0 or EF1 tornado and have occasionally caused damage, injuries and even deaths. Unlike tornadoes, dust devils form from the ground up under fair weather and are not connected to a parent thunderstorm.

Elsewhere

Dust devils are extremely common on Mars, where orbiting spacecraft and rovers have photographed vortices many times larger than terrestrial ones; their passage scours dust from solar panels of rovers, sometimes beneficially cleaning them. Similar vortices of steam, snow, ash or fire are known as steam devils, snow devils and fire whirls.
