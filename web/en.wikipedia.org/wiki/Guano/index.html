Guano (from Spanish guano, from Quechua wanu) is the accumulated excrement of seabirds, bats or seals. As a manure it is a highly effective fertiliser because of its exceptionally high content of nitrogen, phosphate and potassium — plant nutrients essential for growth — and it was historically also valued as a source of saltpetre for gunpowder.

Seabird guano and the guano age

The richest deposits formed on rainless islands off Peru — above all the Chincha Islands — where vast colonies of guanay cormorants, boobies and pelicans fed on the anchoveta of the Humboldt Current and the dry climate preserved their droppings in layers tens of metres deep. Indigenous Peruvians had used guano for centuries; its value was publicised in Europe after Alexander von Humboldt encountered it in 1802. From the 1840s to the 1870s a "guano age" followed: Peru exported millions of tonnes, chiefly to Britain and the United States, financing the Peruvian state while the deposits were worked in brutal conditions by indentured and convict labour, including Chinese coolies. The United States passed the Guano Islands Act of 1856, allowing its citizens to claim uninhabited guano islands. Competition over guano and nitrate fields in the Atacama was a background cause of the War of the Pacific (1879–1884).

Decline and modern use

The trade declined as the richest deposits were exhausted and synthetic nitrogen fixation (the Haber–Bosch process) arrived in the 20th century. Peru still harvests guano sustainably from protected bird islands on a rotational basis, and it commands a premium as an organic fertiliser. Bat guano is mined from caves in many countries for garden fertiliser.
