Fujian tulou

The Fujian tulou (simplified Chinese: 福建土楼; "Fujian earth buildings") are large, fortified communal residences built by the Hakka and other peoples in the mountainous areas of Fujian province in southeastern China. Constructed mainly between the 12th and 20th centuries, they are renowned for their massive rammed-earth walls and circular or rectangular plans housing whole clans.

Architecture

A typical tulou rises three to five storeys around a central courtyard containing wells, ancestral halls and stages. The thick earthen walls — sometimes nearly two metres thick — are defensive against bandits and insulate the interior. Rooms on the ground floor serve as kitchens, the second floor for storage, and upper floors as living quarters, with all rooms of equal size reflecting the communal, egalitarian organization of the clan.

Recognition

In 2008 UNESCO inscribed 46 tulou buildings, in clusters such as Chuxi, Tianluokeng, Hekeng and the Gaobei complex (including the large Chengqi Lou), as a World Heritage Site, citing them as exceptional examples of a communal building tradition adapted to a defensive mountain environment.
