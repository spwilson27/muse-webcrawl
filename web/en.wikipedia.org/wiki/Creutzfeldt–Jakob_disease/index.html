Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease

CJD causes spongiform degeneration of brain tissue

Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) is a rare, incurable, and invariably fatal degenerative disease of the brain. It is the most common of the human prion diseases, a group of transmissible spongiform encephalopathies caused by misfolded prion proteins. The disease is named after the German neurologists Hans Gerhard Creutzfeldt and Alfons Maria Jakob, who described cases in the 1920s.

Forms

Most cases (about 85%) are sporadic, arising without known cause. About 10–15% are familial, caused by inherited mutations in the PRNP gene; related inherited disorders include Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker syndrome and fatal familial insomnia. Acquired forms include iatrogenic CJD, transmitted through contaminated medical procedures such as corneal or dura mater grafts and human growth hormone, and variant CJD (vCJD), linked to eating beef from cattle with bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, "mad cow disease"), which emerged in the United Kingdom in the 1990s and typically affects younger patients.

Signs and symptoms

The classic presentation is a rapidly progressive dementia accompanied by muscle jerking (myoclonus), loss of coordination, visual disturbance, and personality change. The disease advances over weeks to months to akinetic mutism and death, often from infection; about 90% of patients with sporadic CJD die within a year of symptom onset.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis draws on the clinical picture, characteristic MRI changes (hyperintensity of the basal ganglia and thalamus; the "pulvinar sign" in vCJD), EEG periodic sharp wave complexes, and cerebrospinal fluid tests such as 14-3-3 protein and the highly sensitive RT-QuIC assay. Definitive diagnosis requires brain biopsy or autopsy. There is no treatment that alters the course of the disease; care is supportive.
