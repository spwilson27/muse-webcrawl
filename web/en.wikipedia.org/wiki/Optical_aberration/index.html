Optical aberration

In optics, aberration is a property of optical systems, such as lenses, that causes light to be spread out over some region of space rather than focused to a point. Aberrations cause the image formed by a lens to be blurred or distorted, with the nature of the distortion depending on the type of aberration. Aberration can be defined as a departure of the performance of an optical system from the predictions of paraxial optics.

Overview

With an ideal lens, light from any given point on an object would pass through the lens and come together at a single point in the image plane (the focal point). Real lenses, in contrast, do not perfectly focus light, and even the theoretically perfect combination of lenses bears imperfections. Optical systems are therefore designed with compromises, using corrective elements to reduce aberrations to an acceptable level.

Monochromatic aberrations

Aberrations that occur for a single wavelength (color) of light are called monochromatic aberrations. The five classical Seidel aberrations are:

  • Spherical aberration — rays passing through the edge of a spherical lens focus at a different distance from rays passing near the center.
  • Coma — off-axis point sources appear as comet-shaped blurs.
  • Astigmatism — rays in two perpendicular planes focus at different distances.
  • Field curvature — the image of a flat object lies on a curved surface.
  • Distortion — magnification varies with distance from the axis, producing barrel or pincushion shapes.

Chromatic aberration

Chromatic aberration arises because the refractive index of a lens material varies with wavelength (dispersion). Different colors therefore focus at different points, producing colored fringes. It can be reduced with achromatic doublets that combine two glasses with different dispersion.
