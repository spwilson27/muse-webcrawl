Hybrid zone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hybrid zone is a region where two genetically distinct populations, subspecies, or species meet, mate, and produce hybrid offspring. Hybrid zones are often narrow bands maintained by a balance between dispersal of parental forms into the zone and selection against hybrids — so-called tension zones — or they may track environmental transitions (ecotones), where each parental form is favoured on its own side.

Types

Researchers distinguish several patterns. Mosaic hybrid zones occur as patchworks where habitat varies. Bounded hybrid superiority describes hybrids that are actually fitter than either parent within the zone itself. In moving hybrid zones, one form steadily replaces another, sometimes driven by climate change or by an invading species; such movement can push the displaced form to extinction.

Significance in evolutionary biology

Hybrid zones act as natural laboratories for the study of speciation. Steep clines in genes across a zone indicate strong selection and emerging reproductive isolation, and zones allow biologists to test models of reinforcement, in which selection strengthens mating barriers where hybridization is costly. Famous examples include the zone between the carrion crow and hooded crow in Europe, the Bombina toad zone in central Europe, and the flicker hybrid zone in North America. Hybrid zones can also permit adaptive genes to cross species boundaries through introgression.
