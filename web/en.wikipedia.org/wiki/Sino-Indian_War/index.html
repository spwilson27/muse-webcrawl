The Sino-Indian War, also known as the Himalayan War or, in India, the War of 1962, was a month-long border conflict fought between China and India from October to November 1962. The People's Liberation Army attacked simultaneously in two widely separated Himalayan theatres — Aksai Chin in the west and the North-East Frontier Agency (present-day Arunachal Pradesh) in the east — routed the unprepared Indian forces, and then declared a unilateral ceasefire and withdrew in the east. The war was a national trauma for India and poisoned Sino-Indian relations for decades.

The war was fought at altitudes above 4,000 metres in the Himalayas.

Causes

The war's roots lay in the undefined Himalayan frontier inherited from British India and Qing/Republican China: the McMahon Line in the east, which China rejected, and Aksai Chin in the west, where China built a strategic road linking Xinjiang and Tibet across territory India claimed. Tensions rose after China's annexation of Tibet (1950–51) and the Tibetan uprising of 1959, when India granted asylum to the Dalai Lama. Border clashes in 1959 (Longju and the Kongka Pass) and India's "Forward Policy" of establishing border posts in disputed areas from 1961 steadily escalated the confrontation.

The war and aftermath

On 20 October 1962, overwhelming PLA forces struck in both sectors. Indian units, poorly equipped, supplied and led, collapsed: the eastern offensive drove to the foothills of Assam, and in the west China secured Aksai Chin. On 21 November China declared a unilateral ceasefire, withdrawing in the east to near the McMahon Line while retaining Aksai Chin — the territorial status quo that essentially persists today. The defeat shattered Prime Minister Nehru's health and his policy of Hindi-Chini friendship, forced a massive expansion of the Indian military, and led India to seek closer ties with the Soviet Union and the United States. The border remains disputed, with deadly clashes recurring as recently as the Galwan Valley clash of 2020.
