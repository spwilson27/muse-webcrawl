The saxhorn is a family of valved brass instruments with conical bores and deep cup-shaped mouthpieces, invented by the Belgian instrument maker Adolphe Sax in Paris in the 1840s. Patented in 1845, saxhorns were designed as a homogeneous family spanning a wide range of pitches, so that a military band could have matched timbre from the highest soprano to the lowest bass voice.

The family

The saxhorn family originally comprised instruments in E♭ and B♭, from the small sopranino down to the contrabass. Members familiar today include the flugelhorn (the soprano saxhorn in B♭), the alto/tenor horn in E♭, and the baritone and euphonium in B♭; the tubas of the modern brass band are also descended from the bass saxhorns. Most saxhorns are built with the bell pointing upward, though Sax also produced variants with forward- or backward-facing bells.

History

Sax promoted his instruments through a famous 1845 "battle of the bands" on the Champ de Mars in Paris, after which saxhorns were adopted by the French military. They became standard in British-style brass bands, where the saxhorn-derived alto horn, baritone, and euphonium remain core voices, and in American Civil War-era bands, often in "over-the-shoulder" form.
