The Duchy of the Archipelago (Italian: Ducato dell'Arcipelago; Greek: Δουκάτο του Αρχιπελάγους), also known as the Duchy of Naxos or Duchy of the Aegean, was a maritime Frankish state created after the Fourth Crusade, comprising most of the Cyclades islands in the Aegean Sea. It was founded in 1207 by the Venetian adventurer Marco Sanudo and endured, under Venetian and later Ottoman suzerainty, until 1579—one of the longest-lived of the Crusader states in Greece.

Foundation

After the sack of Constantinople in 1204, the Cyclades were nominally assigned to Venice in the partition of the Byzantine Empire, but the Republic took no action; Venetian nobles therefore conquered them privately. Marco Sanudo, nephew of Doge Enrico Dandolo, fitted out a small fleet and seized Naxos in 1205/1207, then Paros, Milos, Ios, Santorini, and other islands, distributing many as fiefs to his companions (the Ghisi, Dandolo, Barozzi, and other Venetian families), while the Latin Emperor Henry of Flanders confirmed him as hereditary duke in 1210. The capital was the fortified Kastro of Naxos town, and the duchy introduced Western feudalism and Catholic bishoprics to a largely Orthodox Greek population, whose resentment periodically erupted in revolt, supported at times by the Empire of Nicaea and its Byzantine successor.

Later history

The Sanudo dynasty ruled until 1383, when Francesco I Crispo seized power with Venetian backing and founded the Crispo dynasty. From 1537, when the Ottoman admiral Hayreddin Barbarossa ravaged the islands, the dukes paid tribute to the Sultan while retaining internal rule. The last duke, Joseph Nasi—a Portuguese Jewish courtier and financier appointed by Selim II in 1566—never visited his duchy; after his death in 1579 the Ottomans annexed the islands outright, ending the duchy and, with it, the last major Frankish state in the Aegean.

Legacy

The duchy's castles and Catholic churches still mark the Cycladic landscape, several islands retain Catholic communities descended from the Venetian era, and the period survives in local folklore through the memory of piracy, corsairs, and Frankish lords known collectively as the "Franks."
