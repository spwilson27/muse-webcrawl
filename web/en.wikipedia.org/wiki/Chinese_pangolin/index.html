Chinese pangolin

Scientific name: Manis pentadactyla

Conservation status: Critically Endangered (IUCN)

Range: Southern China, Taiwan, northern Southeast and South Asia

The Chinese pangolin (Manis pentadactyla) is a pangolin species native to East and Southeast Asia, including southern China, Taiwan, northern Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, Nepal, Bhutan and northeastern India. Like all pangolins, it is covered in overlapping keratin scales and feeds almost exclusively on ants and termites.

Description

Adults weigh about 2–7 kg and measure roughly 40–58 cm in body length, with a tail of 25–38 cm. The species has large, well-developed foreclaws adapted for digging into termite mounds and excavating burrows, in which it spends the day. It has no teeth; prey is picked up with a long, sticky tongue and ground in a muscular, gizzard-like stomach. When threatened, it rolls into a tight ball.

Behaviour and reproduction

The Chinese pangolin is solitary and primarily nocturnal. Females usually give birth to a single pup, which rides on the mother's tail. In the cooler parts of its range it may reduce activity in winter. Its burrows — feeding burrows only a few metres long, and deeper residential burrows — are an important sign used by researchers to survey populations.

Threats and conservation

The species is listed as Critically Endangered. It is heavily poached for its meat, considered a delicacy, and for its scales, used in traditional medicine, and it suffers from habitat loss. Commercial international trade has been prohibited since pangolins were transferred to CITES Appendix I in 2017. Despite legal protection, illegal trafficking continues to drive declines.
