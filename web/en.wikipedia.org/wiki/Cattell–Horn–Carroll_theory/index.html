The Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory (commonly abbreviated as CHC theory) is a psychological theory on the structure of human cognitive abilities. Based on the work of its three influential creators — Raymond B. Cattell, John L. Horn and John B. Carroll — CHC theory integrates Cattell and Horn's fluid and crystallised intelligence (Gf–Gc) model with Carroll's three-stratum theory of intelligence. It is currently the most comprehensive and empirically supported psychometric theory of cognitive abilities and serves as the framework for major intelligence test batteries such as the Woodcock–Johnson tests.

Structure

CHC theory arranges abilities in three strata. Stratum I comprises roughly 70–90 narrow abilities (such as induction, memory span and perceptual speed). Stratum II consists of broad abilities, including fluid reasoning (Gf), crystallised knowledge (Gc), short-term working memory (Gwm), long-term storage and retrieval (Glr), visual processing (Gv), auditory processing (Ga), processing speed (Gs), decision and reaction speed (Gt), quantitative knowledge (Gq) and reading and writing ability (Grw). Stratum III is general intelligence (g), although Horn denied the existence of a unitary g and Carroll's model retained it; CHC practice often treats g at the apex as a statistical summary.

Development

Cattell first distinguished fluid from crystallised intelligence in the 1940s; his student Horn expanded the model into multiple broad abilities. Carroll's 1993 survey Human Cognitive Abilities reanalysed more than 460 datasets and proposed a three-level hierarchy. Kevin McGrew and others merged the two frameworks in the late 1990s, producing the CHC model that has guided test construction and the assessment of learning disabilities ever since.
