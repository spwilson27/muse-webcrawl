Galatea (mythology)

The name Galatea (Greek: Γαλάτεια, "she who is milk-white") refers to several figures in Greek mythology.

Galatea the Nereid rides the waves in ancient depictions.

The Nereid

Galatea was one of the Nereids, the fifty sea-nymph daughters of Nereus and Doris. In Sicilian tradition, told most famously by Ovid in the Metamorphoses, the Cyclops Polyphemus loved her, but Galatea loved the youth Acis instead. In jealous rage Polyphemus crushed Acis with a rock; Galatea turned the dying youth's blood into the river Acis, a stream that still flows near Mount Etna.

Pygmalion's statue

In later tradition — the name is not used in Ovid's own text — Galatea is the name given to the ivory statue carved by Pygmalion, king of Cyprus, which the goddess Aphrodite brought to life in answer to his prayers. The story inspired many later works, including George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion and the musical My Fair Lady.

Other figures

A Galatea also appears as the wife of Lamprus of Crete in Antoninus Liberalis, and the name was given to a daughter of Eurytius. The name is borne in astronomy by a moon of Neptune and an asteroid.
