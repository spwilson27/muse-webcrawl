Soil-transmitted helminthiasis

Soil-transmitted helminthiasis is a type of helminth infection (helminthiasis) caused by different species of parasitic worms. It is caused specifically by those worms which are transmitted through soil contaminated with faecal matter and are known as soil-transmitted helminths. The main species that infect people are the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides, the whipworm Trichuris trichiura and the hookworms Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale.

Signs and symptoms

Light infections are usually asymptomatic. Heavy burdens cause abdominal pain, diarrhoea, malnutrition, impaired growth and cognitive development in children, and iron-deficiency anaemia — particularly from hookworm blood loss. Massive Ascaris infections can obstruct the intestine or bile duct.

Transmission

Eggs are passed in the faeces of infected people and contaminate soil in areas with inadequate sanitation. Ascaris and Trichuris infect people who ingest infective eggs on food or hands; hookworm larvae mature in soil and actively penetrate the skin of people walking barefoot.

Prevention and control

The World Health Organization recommends periodic preventive chemotherapy (deworming) with albendazole or mebendazole for at-risk groups — preschool and school-age children, women of reproductive age and workers in high-risk occupations — together with improved water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH). Soil-transmitted helminthiases are classified among the neglected tropical diseases.

Epidemiology

More than a billion people in sub-Saharan Africa, Asia and the Americas are estimated to be infected. The diseases thrive in warm, moist climates where sanitation is poor, and disproportionately affect the poorest communities.
