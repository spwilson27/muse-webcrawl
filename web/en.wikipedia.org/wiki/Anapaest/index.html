Anapaest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anapaest (also spelled anapest; from the Greek ἀνάπαιστος, "struck back", because it is the reverse of a dactyl) is a metrical foot used in formal poetry. In quantitative meter, as in classical Greek and Latin, it consists of two short syllables followed by a long one; in accentual-syllabic verse, such as English, it consists of two unstressed syllables followed by one stressed syllable. It may be seen as a reversed dactyl (– ⏑ ⏑).

Use in English verse

The galloping, rolling rhythm of anapaestic metre suits comic, light, and narrative verse. Famous examples include:

  • Lord Byron's "The Destruction of Sennacherib": "The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold" (anapaestic tetrameter).
  • Clement Clarke Moore's "'Twas the Night Before Christmas", written in anapaestic tetrameter couplets.
  • Edward Lear's limericks, which are anapaestic: "There was an Old Man with a beard".
  • Dr. Seuss's books, such as The Cat in the Hat, which rely on bouncy anapaestic rhythms.
  • Lewis Carroll's "The Walrus and the Carpenter" and much of W. S. Gilbert's patter-adjacent lyric verse.

Classical metres

In Greek poetry the anapaest was associated with marching songs (embateria) and with the entrance songs (parodoi) of choruses in tragedy, where systems of anapaestic dimeters accompany processional entrances. Latin poets used anapaests more sparingly, chiefly in drama adapting Greek models. Anapaestic verse permits substitutions — a spondee or dactyl may replace an anapaest — which gives the marching rhythm its flexibility.
