Miscarriage of justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A miscarriage of justice occurs when a legal proceeding produces an unjust outcome — paradigmatically the conviction and punishment of an innocent person, but also the acquittal of the guilty or gross procedural unfairness. Wrongful convictions are the term's core case: in the United States, the National Registry of Exonerations has recorded more than 3,000 exonerations since 1989 (a figure that grows monthly), and DNA testing — beginning with Gary Dotson's exoneration in 1989 — has freed hundreds of the convicted, including death-row prisoners.

Research on causes converges on eyewitness misidentification (present in most DNA exoneration cases), false confessions, flawed forensic science, informant perjury, and official misconduct, often in combination and disproportionately affecting racial minorities. Remedies and safeguards include appellate review and habeas corpus, conviction-integrity units in prosecutors' offices, the UK's Criminal Cases Review Commission (established 1997 after the Guildford Four and Birmingham Six scandals), statutory compensation schemes, and recording of interrogations. The political weight of miscarriages — from Dreyfus to the Central Park jogger case (the Exonerated Five, vacated 2002) — has repeatedly driven reform of police and forensic practice.
