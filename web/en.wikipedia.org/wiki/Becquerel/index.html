Becquerel

The becquerel (symbol: Bq) is the unit of radioactivity in the International System of Units (SI). One becquerel is defined as the activity of a quantity of radioactive material in which, on average, one nucleus decays (or undergoes one disintegration) per second. It therefore has the dimension of reciprocal time, equivalent to the reciprocal second (s−1).

Definition and history

The becquerel was adopted by the 15th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1975 and is named in honour of the French physicist Henri Becquerel, who discovered radioactivity in 1896 and shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Marie and Pierre Curie. The unit replaced the older curie (Ci), defined as the activity of one gram of radium-226 and equal to 3.7 × 1010 Bq.

Usage

Because one becquerel is a very small amount of activity, measured quantities commonly use SI prefixes: kilobecquerels (kBq), megabecquerels (MBq), and gigabecquerels (GBq). A typical human body contains roughly 4,000–5,000 Bq of naturally occurring potassium-40. The becquerel measures the rate of radioactive decay (activity); it should not be confused with units of absorbed dose, such as the gray, or equivalent dose, such as the sievert, which describe the effects of radiation on matter and tissue. Activity per unit mass or volume is expressed in becquerels per kilogram or per litre and is used to characterise the radioactivity of food, water, and environmental samples.
