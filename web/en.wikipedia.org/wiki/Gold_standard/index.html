Gold standard

A gold standard is a monetary system in which the standard economic unit of account is based on a fixed quantity of gold: currency is either made of gold, or is freely convertible into gold at a fixed price, and gold may be imported and exported without restriction. The classical gold standard flourished from the 1870s to 1914; an interwar version collapsed during the Great Depression, and the post-1944 Bretton Woods system of dollar convertibility ended in 1971. No major country uses a gold standard today.

How it worked

Under a gold standard each currency is defined as a weight of gold, which automatically fixes exchange rates between participating currencies. Imbalances settle through gold flows: a deficit country loses gold, its money supply contracts and its prices fall, restoring competitiveness — the price–specie flow mechanism described by David Hume. The system requires governments to subordinate domestic policy to maintaining convertibility.

History

Britain moved onto gold de facto after Isaac Newton's 1717 mint price overvalued gold, and formally in 1821. Germany's adoption in 1871–73, financed by the French indemnity, triggered general adoption across Europe and the Americas in the 1870s; the United States, on a bimetallic and then greenback footing, formally adopted gold in 1900 after the fierce "Free Silver" campaigns of William Jennings Bryan. The system suspended during the First World War. Britain's 1925 return at the pre-war parity, championed by Winston Churchill, overvalued sterling and deepened industrial strife; Britain abandoned gold in 1931, the United States devalued and embargoed gold in 1933–34, and the remaining gold bloc dissolved by 1936.

Bretton Woods and the end

The 1944 Bretton Woods agreement pegged other currencies to the US dollar, itself convertible into gold at $35 per ounce for foreign governments. Mounting US deficits made the promise untenable, and on 15 August 1971 President Nixon suspended convertibility — the "Nixon shock" — after which the world moved to floating fiat currencies.

Arguments

Supporters credit the gold standard with long-run price stability and restrained government finance. Critics, including Barry Eichengreen and other historians of the Depression, argue it transmitted deflation internationally, prevented lender-of-last-resort action, and prolonged slumps; countries that left gold earliest recovered fastest.
