Verdigris

Verdigris is the common name for the blue-green patina that forms on copper, brass and bronze exposed to air or seawater, and for the pigment historically made from that corrosion. The natural patina consists mainly of basic copper carbonates and, near the sea, copper chlorides; the artificial pigment was copper acetate, produced by exposing copper plates to vinegar vapour.

Pigment

Verdigris was one of the most vivid green pigments available to European painters from antiquity through the nineteenth century, used in manuscript illumination, panel painting and maps. It is unstable — it can darken to brown in oil, attack paper, and react with other pigments — so conservators treat verdigris passages with special care. The name comes from Old French verte-grez, "green of Greece."

Patina

On roofs, domes and statues the patina protects the underlying metal from further corrosion once it stabilises. Famous examples include the Statue of Liberty, whose copper skin weathered from a shiny penny colour to its present green within a few decades of its 1886 dedication, and the green copper roofs of many European civic buildings.
