Parent rock (also called parent material in soil science) is the underlying geological material from which soil, sediment or a younger rock is formed. In soil formation, the parent rock is the rock that weathers in place (residuum) or the material transported and deposited — by water, wind, ice or gravity — on which the soil profile develops. In petrology, the original rock before metamorphism is called the protolith, a concept closely related in function.

Role in soil formation

Parent material is one of the five classic soil-forming factors identified by Hans Jenny, alongside climate, organisms, relief and time. Its mineralogy strongly influences soil texture, fertility and chemistry: granite weathers to sandy, acidic soils; basalt gives fine, clay-rich and often fertile soils; limestone produces distinctive alkaline residual clays such as terra rossa; and quartz-rich sandstone yields nutrient-poor, freely draining profiles. Transported parent materials — alluvium, loess, glacial till, volcanic ash — can differ entirely from the bedrock beneath them and often produce the world's most agriculturally productive soils, as in loess belts and river floodplains.

In petrology and geochemistry

For sedimentary rocks, "parent rock" or source rock analysis identifies where sediment grains originated (provenance studies), reconstructing ancient mountain belts and drainage systems. In metamorphic petrology the protolith determines much of the metamorphic rock's bulk chemistry: a basalt protolith typically becomes amphibolite or schist, while a shale protolith becomes slate, phyllite or schist depending on grade. In petroleum geology, an organic-rich source rock is parent to oil and gas deposits, though here the term "source rock" is standard.
