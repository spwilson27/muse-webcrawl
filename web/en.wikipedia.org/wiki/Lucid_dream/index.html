Lucid dream

A lucid dream is a type of dream in which the dreamer becomes aware that they are dreaming while still asleep. The dreamer may gain the ability to control the characters, narrative, and environment of the dream, though this level of control is not required for a dream to be considered "lucid". Lucid dreaming has been studied scientifically, and its existence is well established.

History

References to awareness within dreams appear in ancient texts on yoga and in Aristotle, who noted that a sleeper may perceive that he is asleep. The term lucide droom was coined by Dutch psychiatrist Frederik van Eeden in 1913. Scientific verification came in the 1970s and 1980s when Keith Hearne, and independently Stephen LaBerge at Stanford University, had lucid dreamers signal their awareness during REM sleep using prearranged eye movements recorded on a polygraph.

Scientific basis

Lucid dreams occur during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. Neuroimaging studies associate lucidity with heightened activity in prefrontal regions — areas normally deactivated in REM — and with increased gamma-band (around 40 Hz) coherence, suggesting a partially reawakened reflective, self-aware state within the dream.

Induction techniques

Commonly reported methods include reality testing (habitually checking whether one is awake), the mnemonic induction of lucid dreams (MILD), wake-back-to-bed schedules, and wake-initiated lucid dreams (WILD). Studies suggest a substantial minority of people experience at least one lucid dream in their lifetime, and a smaller proportion have them regularly.

Applications and risks

Lucid dreaming has been explored as a treatment for recurrent nightmares and as a tool for motor-skills rehearsal and creative problem solving. Clinicians note possible downsides for some people, including disturbed sleep quality and, rarely, confusion between dreams and waking life.
