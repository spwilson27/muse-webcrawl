Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), formally classified as invasive breast carcinoma of no special type, is the most common type of breast cancer, accounting for roughly 70–80% of all invasive breast malignancies. It arises from the epithelial cells lining the milk ducts and is defined by cancer cells that have breached the basement membrane and invaded the surrounding breast stroma, giving it the capacity to spread to lymph nodes and distant organs.

Signs and diagnosis

IDC may present as a palpable lump, an abnormality on screening mammography, nipple discharge or skin changes. Diagnosis rests on imaging and core needle biopsy. Pathologists grade the tumour (Nottingham grade, based on tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic count) and test for estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and HER2 status, which determine eligibility for endocrine and targeted therapies; staging uses the TNM system.

Treatment

Management typically combines surgery (breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy, with sentinel lymph node biopsy), radiotherapy, and systemic therapy tailored to the tumour's biology: endocrine therapy for hormone-receptor-positive disease, anti-HER2 agents such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive disease, and chemotherapy where indicated. "Triple-negative" tumours lack ER, PR and HER2 expression and generally have a poorer prognosis, increasingly treated with immunotherapy and antibody–drug conjugates.

Prognosis

Prognosis depends on stage, grade and molecular subtype; localised disease has five-year survival above 99% in some registries, while metastatic disease remains incurable but increasingly manageable. IDC is distinguished from ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), its non-invasive precursor confined within the ducts, and from invasive lobular carcinoma, the second most common invasive type.
