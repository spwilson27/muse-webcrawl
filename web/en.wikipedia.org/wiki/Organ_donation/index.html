Organ donation

Organ donation is the process in which a person allows an organ of their body to be removed and transplanted into another person, legally, either by consent while the donor is alive or after death with the assent of the next of kin. Donation may be for research or, more commonly, healthy organs and tissues may be transplanted into persons with end-stage organ failure. Common transplantations include kidneys, heart, liver, pancreas, lungs, intestines, corneas, skin and bone.

Living and deceased donation

Living donors can provide a kidney or part of a liver, and in rare cases segments of other organs; kidney donation is the most common because humans have two kidneys and can live with one. Deceased donation occurs after brain death or circulatory death, with organs allocated through national systems such as the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) in the United States and Eurotransplant in Europe, using criteria of medical urgency, compatibility and waiting time.

Consent systems

Countries use either opt-in systems, in which people register their willingness to donate, or opt-out ("presumed consent") systems, in which donation is the default unless a person has registered an objection. Spain, with an opt-out system combined with a strong coordinator network, has long had among the highest donation rates in the world. In practice, families are usually consulted and can often block procurement.

Shortages and ethics

Demand greatly exceeds supply: in the United States over 100,000 people are typically on the national waiting list, most awaiting kidneys, and many die waiting. The shortage has fuelled debate over incentives, regulated markets (Iran operates a legal paid kidney donation system), organ trafficking, and alternatives such as xenotransplantation — in 2022 a genetically modified pig heart was transplanted into a human patient — and lab-grown organs. The World Health Organization's Guiding Principles and the Declaration of Istanbul (2008) oppose organ commercialism and transplant tourism.
