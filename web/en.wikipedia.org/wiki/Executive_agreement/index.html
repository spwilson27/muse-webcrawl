Executive agreement
Country: United States
Type: International agreement not ratified as a treaty
Authority: President, alone or with Congress
Key cases: United States v. Belmont (1937); United States v. Pink (1942)
The signing of the Destroyers for Bases Agreement of 1940, a major executive agreement under which the United States transferred fifty destroyers to Britain in exchange for base rights.

An executive agreement is an international agreement entered into by the executive branch of the United States government without the advice and consent of the Senate required for a treaty under Article II of the U.S. Constitution. Executive agreements are binding under international law; within domestic law they have a status broadly comparable to treaties, though they cannot override an inconsistent act of Congress or the Constitution.

Types

Executive agreements fall into three broad categories. Congressional-executive agreements are authorised or approved by a majority of both houses of Congress and account for most major trade agreements, including those establishing U.S. participation in the World Trade Organization. Treaty-based agreements are concluded pursuant to an existing treaty. Sole executive agreements rest on the President's own constitutional authority, notably as Commander in Chief and in the conduct of foreign relations, and are used for matters such as claims settlements and the recognition of foreign governments.

History and case law

Although far fewer treaties have been ratified since 1945, the United States has concluded many thousands of executive agreements, which now greatly outnumber treaties. In United States v. Belmont (1937) and United States v. Pink (1942), the Supreme Court upheld the Litvinov Assignment — a sole executive agreement recognising the Soviet Union — against conflicting state law. In Dames & Moore v. Regan (1981), the Court upheld the claims-settlement agreement that resolved the Iran hostage crisis.

Debate

Critics argue that the executive agreement circumvents the deliberate supermajority check of the Treaty Clause; defenders respond that it is indispensable for the volume and speed of modern diplomacy and that Congress retains control through legislation and appropriations. Since 1972 the Case Act has required the executive to report international agreements to Congress.
