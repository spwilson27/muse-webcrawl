Cooling tower

Cooling tower
TypeHeat rejection device
PrincipleEvaporative cooling of a circulating water stream
UsesPower stations, refineries, chemical plants, HVAC

A cooling tower is a heat rejection device that removes waste heat to the atmosphere by cooling a circulating water stream to a lower temperature, principally through evaporation. Cooling towers are used at power stations, oil refineries, petrochemical and chemical plants, food processing plants, and for large building air-conditioning (HVAC) systems. The large hyperboloid concrete towers associated with power stations are natural-draft wet cooling towers; the visible plume above them is condensed water vapour, not smoke.

Natural-draft hyperboloid towers at a power station; the plume is water vapour.

How it works

Warm water from the industrial process is sprayed over "fill" packing inside the tower, maximising contact with air drawn or forced through the tower. A small fraction of the water (typically around 1–2% of circulation) evaporates, carrying away latent heat and cooling the remainder, which collects in a basin and is recirculated. Key performance terms include the range (inlet–outlet temperature difference) and the approach to the ambient wet-bulb temperature, which sets the theoretical cooling limit.

Types

Towers are classified by how air is moved (natural draft vs mechanical draft with fans), by airflow pattern (crossflow vs counterflow), and by whether the process fluid is exposed to air (open/wet) or kept in closed coils (closed-circuit), with dry cooling towers using air alone where water is scarce. The hyperboloid shape of large natural-draft towers provides structural strength with thin concrete shells and promotes the chimney effect that drives airflow.

History and issues

Cooling towers were developed at the start of the 20th century — Dutch engineer Frederik van Iterson patented the hyperboloid design in 1918 — as engines and power plants outgrew available cooling water. Operational concerns include drift (droplet carry-over), blowdown water treatment, biological growth, and Legionnaires' disease outbreaks traced to poorly maintained building cooling towers, which has led to registration and maintenance regulations in many cities.
