Bioelectricity is the set of electrical potentials and currents produced by or occurring within living organisms. It arises from differences in ion concentration across cell membranes, maintained by ion channels and pumps, and underlies nerve impulses, muscle contraction, and many developmental processes.

History

The field began with Luigi Galvani's experiments in the 1780s showing that frog legs twitch when touched by dissimilar metals, which he attributed to "animal electricity" (his rival Volta argued the electricity came from the metals). In the 19th century, Emil du Bois-Reymond measured injury currents and nerve signals. In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley published their quantitative model of the action potential in the squid giant axon, work that won the Nobel Prize in 1963.

Mechanisms

Cells maintain a resting membrane potential (typically −40 to −90 mV) via the sodium–potassium pump and ion-selective channels. In excitable cells — neurons, muscle and some plant cells — voltage-gated channels generate self-propagating action potentials. Some animals, notably electric eels and electric rays, possess specialised electrocyte organs that sum membrane voltages to deliver powerful discharges for predation and defence, while many fish use weak electric fields for electrolocation.

Applications

Medical diagnostics record bioelectric signals: the electrocardiogram (ECG) from the heart, electroencephalogram (EEG) from the brain and electromyogram (EMG) from muscles. Therapeutic devices — cardiac pacemakers, defibrillators, cochlear implants and deep brain stimulators — intervene in bioelectric systems. Research in developmental bioelectricity studies how voltage patterns guide growth and regeneration.
