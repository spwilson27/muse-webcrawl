Oxidative phosphorylation

Oxidative phosphorylation (UK /ˈɒksɪˌdeɪtɪv/, US /ˈɑːksɪˌdeɪtɪv/) is the metabolic pathway in which cells use enzymes to oxidize nutrients, thereby releasing chemical energy in order to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In eukaryotes, it takes place within mitochondria. It is the final stage of cellular respiration, following glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, and accounts for the great majority of ATP produced by aerobic organisms — on the order of 26–28 of the roughly 30–32 ATP molecules yielded per glucose.

Mechanism

Electrons from NADH and FADH2, generated by earlier stages of respiration, pass along the electron transport chain — complexes I through IV embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane — and are finally transferred to oxygen. The energy released pumps protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (proton motive force). As proposed by Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory (1961; Nobel Prize 1978), protons flow back through ATP synthase (complex V), a rotary molecular machine whose rotation drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP.

Efficiency and regulation

Traditionally quoted P/O ratios are about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2. Uncoupling proteins such as thermogenin (UCP1) in brown adipose tissue can short-circuit the gradient to generate heat instead of ATP — a mechanism important in hibernation and infant thermoregulation — and chemical uncouplers such as 2,4-dinitrophenol are dangerous poisons precisely because they collapse the gradient.

Reactive oxygen species

Leakage of electrons, mainly at complexes I and III, generates superoxide and other reactive oxygen species, implicated in ageing and disease; mitochondria defend themselves with enzymes such as superoxide dismutase.
