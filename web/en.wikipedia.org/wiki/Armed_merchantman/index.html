Armed merchantman

A British armed merchant cruiser of the First World War

An armed merchantman is a merchant ship equipped with weapons, typically for self-defence or for conversion to an auxiliary naval role. Armed merchantmen range from merchantmen carrying a defensive gun crew, through defensively equipped merchant ships (DEMS) of the two world wars, to fully commissioned naval auxiliaries such as armed merchant cruisers, auxiliary cruisers used as commerce raiders, and Q-ships.

History

From the age of sail, merchant vessels routinely carried cannon against pirates and privateers; East Indiamen were armed and painted to resemble warships. With the advent of steam and the Hague Conventions, the conversion of merchantmen to warships was regulated, but both world wars saw mass conversions.

Types

Armed merchant cruisers

Large, fast liners taken up by navies — especially the Royal Navy — fitted with (often obsolescent) guns and commissioned as warships. They were used for blockade patrols, such as the Northern Patrol between Scotland and Iceland in both world wars, and for convoy escort in the early years of the Second World War. Their size and high fuel consumption, and their vulnerability once fighting purpose-built warships, limited their combat value; HMS Rawalpindi and HMS Jervis Bay were famously sunk engaging German warships while defending convoys.

Commerce raiders

Germany made extensive use of converted merchantmen (Hilfskreuzer) as disguised surface raiders in both wars — ships such as Atlantis, Pinguin and Kormoran, which sank or captured substantial Allied tonnage before being hunted down.

Defensively equipped merchant ships

In the Second World War most British and Allied merchantmen carried defensive armament, manned by DEMS gunners, against aircraft and surfaced submarines.

Q-ships

Q-ships were heavily armed vessels disguised as helpless merchantmen, intended to lure U-boats to the surface within range of their hidden guns, a tactic of the First World War.
