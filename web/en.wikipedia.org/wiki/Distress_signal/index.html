Distress signal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A distress signal, also known as a distress call, is an internationally recognised means for obtaining help. Distress signals are communicated by transmitting radio signals, displaying a visually detectable item or illumination, or making a sound audible from a distance. A distress signal indicates that a person, ship, aircraft or other vehicle is threatened by a serious or imminent danger and requires immediate assistance.

Maritime signals

Under the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea and SOLAS conventions, recognised signals include: a gun or explosive signal fired at intervals of about a minute; a continuous sounding of fog-signalling apparatus; red rockets or flares; the Morse code signal SOS ( · · · − − − · · · ) by light, sound or radio; the spoken Mayday call by radiotelephony; the international code flags N over C; a square flag with a ball above or below it; flames on the vessel; an orange smoke signal; slowly raising and lowering outstretched arms; and automatic alerts from Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacons (EPIRBs).

Radio distress calls

Mayday (from the French m'aider, "help me") is the universal voice distress call, repeated three times, given absolute priority over all other transmissions. The older Morse telegraph signal SOS, adopted internationally in 1908, was chosen because it is unmistakable and easy to send; it officially stood for nothing. Aviation uses Mayday on VHF frequencies and emergency transponder code 7700. Modern systems under the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) automate alerting via Digital Selective Calling and satellite beacons.

Misuse

False distress signals waste rescue resources and are a criminal offence in most jurisdictions. Hoax Mayday calls and flare misuse are prosecuted in many countries, and cost-recovery may be sought from offenders.
