Barbary Coast

The Barbary Coast (also the Barbary, Turkish: Berberistan) was the name given by Europeans from the 16th to the 19th century to the coastal regions of North Africa — roughly present-day Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya. The name derives from the Berber peoples who inhabited the region.

Barbary pirates

The coast was notorious for the Barbary pirates (or corsairs), who from bases such as Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli raided European shipping and coastal settlements in the Mediterranean and even into the Atlantic, seizing cargoes and enslaving captives. The semi-autonomous Barbary states operated under loose Ottoman suzerainty, and their corsairing economy forced European powers — and later the young United States — to pay tribute or go to war. The First and Second Barbary Wars (1801–1805 and 1815) were fought between the United States and the Barbary states.

Decline

European naval pressure intensified after the Napoleonic Wars; Admiral Exmouth's bombardment of Algiers in 1816 freed thousands of Christian slaves. Corsairing ended effectively with the French conquest of Algiers in 1830, which began the colonisation of Algeria.
