The Second Balkan War was a conflict that broke out on 29 June 1913, when Bulgaria attacked its erstwhile allies Serbia and Greece, the victors — together with Montenegro — of the First Balkan War against the Ottoman Empire. Within weeks Romania and the Ottoman Empire also joined the war against Bulgaria, which, attacked on all sides, was quickly defeated.

Background

In the First Balkan War (1912–13), the Balkan League of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro had expelled the Ottomans from nearly all of their European territory. The victors then quarrelled over the spoils, above all Macedonia, whose division the League's treaties had left ambiguous. Serbia, denied an outlet to the Adriatic by the creation of Albania, demanded compensation in Bulgarian-claimed parts of Macedonia; Greece and Serbia concluded a defensive alliance against Bulgaria on 1 June 1913.

Course of the war

On the night of 29–30 June 1913, Bulgarian forces under General Savov attacked Serbian and Greek positions — an offensive apparently authorised in ambiguous fashion by Tsar Ferdinand's government. The attack failed. Serbian forces counter-attacked and defeated the Bulgarians at Bregalnica, while the Greeks won at Kilkis-Lachanas and advanced toward the Struma valley.

Romania, seizing its opportunity, declared war on 10 July and marched virtually unopposed toward Sofia, occupying Southern Dobruja. The Ottoman Empire re-entered the war and reoccupied Adrianople (Edirne) on 21 July, retaking Eastern Thrace. Surrounded, Bulgaria requested an armistice.

Settlement and consequences

The Treaty of Bucharest (10 August 1913) stripped Bulgaria of most of its gains: Serbia kept northern and central Macedonia, Greece took southern Macedonia and Kavala, and Romania annexed Southern Dobruja. The Treaty of Constantinople with the Ottomans (29 September 1913) fixed the Thracian frontier roughly along the Maritsa river, leaving Edirne in Ottoman hands.

The war left Bulgaria embittered and revisionist, driving Sofia into the arms of the Central Powers in the First World War. Serbia emerged strengthened and self-confident, raising tensions with Austria-Hungary that culminated in the July Crisis of 1914. The Balkan Wars together killed hundreds of thousands of soldiers and civilians and set patterns of ethnic displacement that marked the region for a century.
