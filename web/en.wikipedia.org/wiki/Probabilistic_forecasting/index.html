Probabilistic forecasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Probabilistic forecasting summarises what is known about, or opinions about, future events in the form of a full probability distribution rather than a single point estimate. Probabilistic forecasts usually arise from statistical models built with input data and quantified uncertainty, and are used for risk management, planning, and decision making under uncertainty.

Methods and representations

A probabilistic forecast can be represented as a probability density or mass function, a cumulative distribution function, a set of quantiles, or an ensemble of scenarios. Common approaches include Bayesian predictive distributions, ensemble forecasting (running a simulation model many times with perturbed initial conditions, standard in weather prediction), quantile regression, and distributional regression techniques such as GAMLSS.

Evaluation

Good probabilistic forecasts are both calibrated (events occur with the frequencies implied by the forecast probabilities) and sharp (concentrated, rather than vague). They are scored using proper scoring rules such as the Brier score for binary events, the logarithmic score, and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS). Calibration can be assessed with reliability diagrams and probability integral transform (PIT) histograms.

Applications

Probabilistic forecasting is standard practice in meteorology (e.g., probability of precipitation), energy load and renewable generation forecasting, economics (fan charts for inflation and GDP growth published by central banks), finance, epidemiology, and election forecasting.
