Universal history

A universal history is a work of history that aims to present the entire history of humankind (or of the universe) as a single, coherent narrative, identifying common patterns, themes, or directions in the development of all societies.

Ancient and medieval efforts

Greek historian Polybius treated Roman expansion as a universal process uniting the Mediterranean world. Medieval Christian chroniclers such as Eusebius and Otto of Freising framed all history within a providential scheme from Creation to the Last Judgment, while Islamic scholars like Ibn Khaldun sought general laws of civilization's rise and decline in the Muqaddimah.

Enlightenment and nineteenth century

Enlightenment thinkers — Voltaire, Kant, and Herder — produced universal histories organized around reason and progress. Kant's 1784 essay "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" proposed that human history develops toward a lawful, cosmopolitan order. Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of history presented world history as the unfolding of Spirit toward freedom; Marx reinterpreted the pattern as class struggle driven by modes of production.

Modern approaches

Twentieth-century universal historians included Oswald Spengler (The Decline of the West), Arnold J. Toynbee (A Study of History), and H. G. Wells (The Outline of History). Critics often charge universal history with Eurocentrism and teleology. In the twenty-first century the enterprise survives as "big history," which extends the narrative back to the Big Bang, as in David Christian's Maps of Time.
