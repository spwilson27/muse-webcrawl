Stoma (medicine)

In anatomy, a stoma (from Greek στόμα, "mouth") is any natural or surgically created opening in the body. In medical practice the term most often refers to an opening created by an ostomy procedure, in which a hollow organ — usually the bowel or bladder — is brought through the abdominal wall so that waste can leave the body through the abdominal surface rather than the normal route. A person with such a stoma is sometimes called an ostomate.

Types

  • Colostomy — a stoma formed from the colon (large intestine); output is usually formed stool.
  • Ileostomy — a stoma formed from the ileum (small intestine); output is liquid and rich in enzymes.
  • Urostomy — diverts urine, typically through an ileal conduit, after bladder removal.
  • Tracheostomy — an opening in the trachea (windpipe) to maintain an airway; the tube site is also called a stoma.
  • Gastrostomy — an opening into the stomach, commonly for feeding tubes (PEG).

Indications

Stomas may be temporary — allowing diseased or operated bowel to heal, for example after emergency surgery for perforation or obstruction — or permanent, as after abdominoperineal resection for rectal cancer. Common underlying conditions include colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), diverticular disease, trauma, and congenital disorders such as Hirschsprung's disease.

Appearance and care

A healthy stoma is pink or red, moist, and slightly protruding; it has no nerve endings, so it is not painful to touch, but its rich blood supply means it may bleed slightly when cleaned. Waste is collected in an adhesive ostomy pouch that is emptied or replaced as needed. Specialist stoma care nurses teach patients skin care, appliance fitting, and diet management. Common complications include peristomal skin irritation, prolapse, retraction, parastomal hernia, and stenosis.

History

Deliberate colostomy was first described in the eighteenth century — Alexis Littré proposed it in 1710 — and early operations were performed in the late 1700s. Modern stoma care was transformed in the 1950s by Bryan Brooke's everted stoma technique in England and by the work of nurse Norma Gill, herself an ostomate, who in 1961 became the first enterostomal therapist under Rupert Turnbull at the Cleveland Clinic.
