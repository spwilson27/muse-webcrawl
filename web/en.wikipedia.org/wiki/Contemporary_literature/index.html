Contemporary literature

Contemporary literature is literature produced roughly from the mid-to-late 20th century to the present, often dated from after World War II (1945) or from the emergence of postmodernism in the 1960s–1970s onward. The category is fluid: what counts as "contemporary" shifts as time passes, and the term is used by publishers, bookshops, and courses as much as by literary historians.

Characteristics

Contemporary writing spans postmodern experimentation (metafiction, fragmentation, intertextuality), a return to realism and narrative in various forms, autofiction, and strong currents of postcolonial, feminist, and diasporic writing that centre previously marginalised voices. Globalisation, migration, digital technology, and climate change are frequent themes.

Notable authors (illustrative)

Widely discussed contemporary authors include Toni Morrison, Salman Rushdie, Margaret Atwood, Don DeLillo, Haruki Murakami, Elena Ferrante, Olga Tokarczuk, Kazuo Ishiguro, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, Karl Ove Knausgård, Sally Rooney, and Ocean Vuong — spanning literary fiction, speculative fiction, and the modern epic.

Forms and publishing

Alongside the novel, contemporary literature includes the short-story revival, creative nonfiction and memoir, graphic narratives, spoken-word and slam poetry, and digital/hypertext fiction. Self-publishing, online magazines, and social-media reviewing have transformed how contemporary work is produced and read. Earlier movements such as confessional poetry feed directly into today's memoir and autofiction.

Debates

Critics debate whether "postmodernism" has been succeeded by metamodernism, new sincerity, or post-postmodernism, and whether global Anglophone publishing flattens diverse traditions into a single market style.
