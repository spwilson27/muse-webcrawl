Abd al-Rahman I (Arabic: عبد الرحمن الأول; 731 – 30 September 788), known as al-Dakhil ("the Immigrant") and Saqr Quraysh ("the Falcon of Quraysh"), was the founder of the Emirate of Córdoba and the Umayyad dynasty of al-Andalus. The lone Umayyad prince to escape the Abbasid massacre of his family, he crossed North Africa, seized Córdoba in 756, and ruled Muslim Spain for 32 years, laying the foundations of the state that became the Caliphate of Córdoba.

Escape from the Abbasids

Abd al-Rahman was a grandson of the caliph Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik. When the Abbasid Revolution overthrew the Umayyads in 750, the new regime hunted down and killed nearly the entire family. The young prince escaped the massacre at the river Abū Fuṭrus, swimming the Euphrates while his younger brother was lured back and killed. With his loyal freedman Badr he fled through Palestine, Egypt, and into the Maghreb, moving among Berber tribes — his mother was a Berber of the Nafza tribe — while Abbasid agents pursued him and local rulers alternately sheltered and plotted against him.

Conquest of al-Andalus

Muslim Spain was fractured among rival Arab factions and Berber garrisons. In 755 Abd al-Rahman sent Badr ahead to negotiate with Umayyad clients among the Syrian troops settled in Spain, then crossed the strait himself, landing at Almuñécar in September 755. Gathering support, he marched on Córdoba and on 14 May 756 defeated the governor Yusuf al-Fihri at the Battle of Musarah outside the city. He was proclaimed emir, establishing an independent Umayyad state that acknowledged no Abbasid authority — though for his first years he had the Friday prayer read, cautiously, without naming the Abbasid caliph.

Reign

Abd al-Rahman's 32-year reign was spent largely suppressing revolts: by Yusuf's partisans, by Yemeni and Qaysi factions, by Berber rebels in the centre and east, and by ambitious frontier governors. In 777 he faced a pro-Abbasid conspiracy coordinated with Baghdad, and in 778 he defeated a Frankish-allied expeditionary effort connected with Charlemagne's Spanish campaign (whose rearguard was destroyed at Roncevaux). He organized a standing army with a loyal guard of freedmen and Berber troops, built fortresses, and in 785 began construction of the Great Mosque of Córdoba on the site of a Visigothic church, the monument that would grow into one of the masterpieces of Islamic architecture. He also built the Munyat al-Rusafa palace outside the city, named for his grandfather's Syrian estate, where he is said to have planted the first date palm in Spain and composed a famous homesick poem to it.

Death and succession

Abd al-Rahman died in Córdoba on 30 September 788 and was succeeded by his son Hisham I. The dynasty he founded ruled al-Andalus for nearly 250 years; in 929 his descendant Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed the caliphate.
