Rangaku

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rangaku (蘭学, "Dutch learning") was the body of Western knowledge — especially medicine, astronomy, physics and military science — that Japan acquired during the Edo period through its contacts with the Dutch factory at Dejima in Nagasaki. While the Tokugawa shogunate's policy of seclusion (sakoku) closed Japan to nearly all other foreigners, the Dutch were permitted to trade, and Dutch books became Japan's principal window on Western science.

Development

Interest grew after the shogun Tokugawa Yoshimune relaxed restrictions on imported books (excepting Christian material) in 1720. Japanese interpreters and scholars learned Dutch and translated European works. A landmark was the Kaitai Shinsho (1774), a translation of a Dutch edition of Johann Adam Kulmus's anatomical tables by Sugita Genpaku, Maeno Ryōtaku and colleagues, undertaken after they observed a dissection and saw the accuracy of European anatomy. The physician Philipp Franz von Siebold, stationed at Dejima in the 1820s, taught Japanese students and spread knowledge in both directions.

Legacy

Rangaku scholars — including figures such as Hiraga Gennai, Shiba Kōkan and Ōtsuki Gentaku — introduced Western astronomy, cartography, chemistry, artillery and medicine, and produced Dutch–Japanese dictionaries. Although the flow of knowledge was narrow, it gave Japan a foundation of familiarity with Western science that eased the rapid modernisation of the Meiji Restoration after 1868.
