German Brazilians (Portuguese: teuto-brasileiros) are Brazilian citizens of full or partial German ancestry. German immigration to Brazil began in the 1820s and continued in several waves through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, making Germans one of the largest immigrant groups in the country. Most settlers concentrated in the southern states of Rio Grande do Sul, Santa Catarina and Paraná, where they founded towns that retain German-influenced architecture, festivals and dialects to this day.

History

The first German colony, São Leopoldo, was founded in Rio Grande do Sul in 1824, shortly after Brazilian independence. Recruitment was promoted by the imperial government, which sought European settlers both to populate the southern frontier and to supply soldiers. Further colonies followed, including Petrópolis in Rio de Janeiro state and, in Santa Catarina, Blumenau (1850) and Joinville. In Espírito Santo, German and especially Pomeranian settlers established communities in the mountainous interior.

Immigration peaked in the late nineteenth century and again in the 1920s, when economic hardship in Germany drove new arrivals. During the Estado Novo of Getúlio Vargas, however, the Brazilian government pursued a nationalisation campaign that closed German-language schools and newspapers and suppressed public use of German; restrictions intensified during the Second World War after Brazil joined the Allies in 1942.

Language and culture

Two German varieties remain widely spoken in southern Brazil: Hunsrik (Riograndenser Hunsrückisch), descended from the dialects of the Hunsrück region of the Rhineland, and East Pomeranian, preserved in parts of Espírito Santo and Santa Catarina. Both are recognised as cultural heritage in several municipalities, and some towns conduct bilingual education. Cultural institutions such as the Oktoberfest of Blumenau — one of the largest in the world — celebrate the German heritage of the region, alongside traditional music, dance groups, and half-timbered (enxaimel) architecture.

Notable communities

Blumenau, Pomerode, Joinville, Santa Cruz do Sul, Novo Hamburgo and Ivoti are among the municipalities most strongly associated with German-Brazilian identity. Pomerode, in Santa Catarina, is often called "the most German town in Brazil", with a large share of its population still speaking German daily.
