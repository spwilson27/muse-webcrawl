The Imamate in Nizari doctrine is the central theological institution of Nizari Ismaili Islam, the largest branch of Ismaili Shi'ism. It holds that after the Prophet Muhammad, religious and spiritual authority is vested in a line of Imams descended from Ali ibn Abi Talib and Fatima, and — unlike Twelver Shi'ism, whose twelfth Imam is believed to be in occultation — that the Imam of the time is present and living in the world, providing authoritative guidance (ta'lim) to his followers in every age.

Principles

Nizari doctrine teaches that the Imamate passes by nass, explicit designation by the previous Imam, within the hereditary line, and that the Imam is the bearer of a divine light (nur) inherited from Muhammad through Ali. The Imam possesses authoritative knowledge of the inner, esoteric meaning (batin) of revelation behind its outer form (zahir), and his interpretation (ta'wil) adapts the faith to changing times. Historically the doctrine was defended by theologians such as Nasir Khusraw and later systematised in works produced under the Imams of Alamut.

History

The Nizari line emerged from the succession dispute of 1094 following the death of the Fatimid caliph-Imam al-Mustansir, in which the Nizaris upheld the claim of his elder son Nizar against al-Musta'li. Under Hasan-i Sabbah the Nizaris established a state centred on Alamut Castle in Persia, which endured until the Mongol conquest of 1256. The Imamate then survived through periods of concealment (satr) in Persia before the Imams emerged publicly in the nineteenth century; the title Aga Khan was bestowed on the 46th Imam, and the line continued through Aga Khan III and Aga Khan IV, under whom the Imamate developed a global network of religious, development and cultural institutions.
