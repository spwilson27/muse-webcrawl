Chilean wine

Vineyards in the foothills of the Andes

Chilean wine has a long history, beginning in the 16th century when Spanish conquistadors brought Vitis vinifera vines to the region. Chile is today one of the world's largest wine exporters and a major producer in the Southern Hemisphere, known for Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Sauvignon Blanc and its signature red variety, Carménère.

Chile's vineyards benefit from a Mediterranean climate, the cooling Humboldt Current, and natural barriers — the Atacama Desert to the north, the Andes to the east, the Pacific to the west and Antarctica to the south — that kept the country free of the phylloxera louse that devastated European vineyards in the 19th century. Chilean vines are therefore typically planted on their own rootstock.

History

Vines arrived with Spanish settlers around 1554, and by the colonial period the País grape dominated. In the mid-19th century wealthy landowners such as Don Silvestre Ochagavía imported French varieties including Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Carménère. Twentieth-century political turmoil and high domestic taxes depressed quality, but trade liberalization in the 1980s and 1990s brought foreign investment, new technology and a rapid export boom.

In the 1990s Chilean ampelographers confirmed that much of what had been grown as Merlot was in fact Carménère, a Bordeaux variety long thought extinct in Europe; it became Chile's emblematic grape.

Regions and varieties

The principal wine regions run through the Central Valley: the Maipo, Rapel (including Colchagua and Cachapoal), Maule and Curicó valleys, with cooler coastal and northern areas such as Casablanca, San Antonio, Limarí and Elqui gaining renown for white wines and Syrah. Red varieties dominate plantings, led by Cabernet Sauvignon, followed by Merlot, Carménère and Syrah; Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay lead among whites.
