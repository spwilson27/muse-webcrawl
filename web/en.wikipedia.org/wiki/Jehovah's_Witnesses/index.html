Jehovah's Witnesses are a Christian denomination with beliefs distinct from mainstream Christianity, developed from the Bible Student movement founded by Charles Taze Russell in the 1870s in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. The name "Jehovah's Witnesses" was adopted in 1931 under Joseph Franklin Rutherford. The faith is administered by a Governing Body through the Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society and related corporations, and is known worldwide for door-to-door evangelism and its publications, including The Watchtower and the New World Translation of the Bible.

Beliefs

Witnesses worship Jehovah as the only true God; Jesus is God's first creation and archangel Michael in their theology, not God incarnate — the Trinity is rejected, as is the immortal soul (the dead are unconscious until resurrection) and hellfire (the wicked are annihilated). They believe Christ's invisible presence and kingdom rule began in 1914, that Armageddon is imminent, and that 144,000 anointed Christians will rule with Christ in heaven while other faithful will live forever on a restored earthly paradise. Members refuse blood transfusions, military service, saluting flags and celebrating birthdays and most holidays, on biblical grounds as they interpret them.

History and controversies

The movement survived failed expectations around several dates, schisms, and severe persecution — bans and imprisonment in many countries, and murder in Nazi concentration camps, where Witnesses wore the purple triangle. Legal battles by Witnesses produced landmark free-speech and religious-freedom rulings, including US Supreme Court cases protecting their evangelism and exempting their children from compulsory flag salutes (1943). Critics and former members raise concerns about disfellowshipping and shunning, the handling of child-abuse allegations, and the blood doctrine; the organisation defends its practices as scriptural and notes its record of peaceful conscientious objection.
