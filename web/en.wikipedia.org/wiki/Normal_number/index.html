Normal number

[Image: Digit frequency in a normal number]

In mathematics, a real number is said to be simply normal in an integer base b if its infinite sequence of digits is distributed uniformly in the sense that each of the b digit values has the same asymptotic frequency 1/b. A number is normal in base b if, for every positive integer n, all possible strings of length n of digits occur with the same asymptotic frequency b−n. A number is absolutely normal if it is normal in all integer bases.

History

The concept was introduced by Émile Borel in 1909, who proved that almost all real numbers are absolutely normal. Wacław Sierpiński gave an early construction of a normal number in 1916, and Champernowne's constant 0.123456789101112… was shown in 1933 to be normal in base 10. The Copeland–Erdős constant, formed from consecutive primes, is also normal in base 10.

Properties and open problems

Despite the fact that almost all numbers are normal, no naturally occurring constant — such as π, e, or √2 — has been proved normal in any base, although their computed digits pass statistical tests for randomness. Every rational number is disjunctive in no base and is in fact not normal in any base. Normality is related to equidistribution and to the concept of a disjunctive sequence.

Normality and randomness

Normal numbers form a classical example of numbers whose digits look random without being algorithmically random in all senses; normality can be characterised in terms of finite automata and incompressibility by finite-state compressors.
