Silicon carbide (SiC), also known as carborundum, is a semiconductor compound of silicon and carbon. It is extremely hard, chemically inert and thermally robust. Natural SiC — the mineral moissanite — is exceedingly rare, found in meteorites and a few terrestrial deposits; virtually all silicon carbide in use is synthetic, manufactured since the 1890s by the Acheson process. SiC exists in more than 250 crystalline forms (polytypes), the most important being 4H-SiC and 6H-SiC for electronics.

Production

In the Acheson process, silica sand and carbon (petroleum coke) are heated in an electric resistance furnace to around 2,500 °C, yielding masses of SiC crystals used for abrasives. High-purity single crystals for electronics are grown by physical vapour transport (the modified Lely method) onto seed crystals, then sliced into wafers.

Uses

Historically SiC served as an abrasive (grinding wheels, sandpaper), in refractory linings, heating elements, and as the detector in early crystal radios; its electroluminescence was observed in 1907, an early step toward the LED. Today its most important growth area is power electronics: SiC MOSFETs and diodes switch at higher voltages, temperatures and frequencies with lower losses than silicon, and are widely used in electric-vehicle inverters, solar inverters and fast chargers. Other uses include ceramic armour, brake discs, diesel particulate filters, and high-temperature structural ceramics. Its wide bandgap (about 3.2 eV for 4H-SiC) and high thermal conductivity underpin these applications.

Properties

SiC is nearly as hard as diamond, sublimes rather than melts at atmospheric pressure (decomposing near 2,700 °C), resists acids and oxidation (forming a protective silica layer), and has low thermal expansion. These traits also make it a candidate material for nuclear fuel cladding and telescope mirrors.
