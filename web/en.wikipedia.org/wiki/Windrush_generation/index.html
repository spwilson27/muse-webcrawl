The Windrush generation refers to the people who arrived in the United Kingdom from Caribbean countries between 1948 and the early 1970s. The name comes from the ship HMT Empire Windrush, which docked at Tilbury, Essex, on 22 June 1948 carrying 1,027 passengers, among them 802 people from Caribbean countries — veterans, students, and workers responding to Britain's post-war labour shortage. Under the British Nationality Act 1948, citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies had the right to settle and work in Britain.

Migration and settlement

Migration from the Caribbean grew through the 1950s, encouraged by demand for labour in the National Health Service, London Transport, and industry. It slowed after the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 introduced employment vouchers and largely ended with the Immigration Act 1971. The newcomers and their children formed the basis of Britain's modern Afro-Caribbean communities and profoundly shaped British culture, music, sport, and politics.

Windrush scandal

In 2018 it emerged that under the Home Office "hostile environment" policy, many members of the Windrush generation — legal residents who had never formally documented their status and whose landing cards had been destroyed by the Home Office — were wrongly denied healthcare, jobs, and benefits, detained, or even deported to countries they had left as children decades earlier. The scandal led to the resignation of Home Secretary Amber Rudd, a government apology, an independent lessons-learned review, and a compensation scheme.

Commemoration

22 June is observed annually as Windrush Day in the United Kingdom. A national Windrush monument was unveiled at London Waterloo station in 2022, and the generation's contribution is widely taught and celebrated in British public life.
