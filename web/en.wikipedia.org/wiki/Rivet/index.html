Rivet

Rivet
Type: Permanent mechanical fastener
Parts: Head, shank, tail (bucktail)
Materials: Steel, aluminium, copper, brass
Uses: Shipbuilding, bridges, aircraft, boilers

A rivet is a permanent mechanical fastener consisting of a smooth cylindrical shaft (the shank) with a head on one end. During installation, the rivet is inserted through aligned holes in the materials to be joined, and the protruding end (the tail) is deformed — by hammering, pressing, or with a rivet gun — to form a second head, clamping the parts together. Unlike bolts, riveted joints are intended to be permanent, and a rivet must usually be cut or drilled out to be removed.

History

Rivets have been used since the Bronze Age. They became of central importance during the Industrial Revolution, when riveted construction was used for boilers, bridges, ships, and early skyscrapers with steel frames. Structures such as the Eiffel Tower and the Sydney Harbour Bridge were assembled with millions of rivets, many installed hot: rivets were heated until glowing, inserted, and hammered by teams of riveters, contracting as they cooled to tighten the joint. From the mid-20th century, welding and high-strength bolts largely replaced hot riveting in structural work.

Types

Common types include solid rivets, the oldest and most reliable form, still used in aircraft structures; semi-tubular rivets; blind rivets (also called pop rivets), which can be installed from one side only using a rivet gun that pulls a mandrel through the rivet body; drive rivets; and flush rivets, used where a smooth aerodynamic surface is required. Rivets are made from materials including steel, aluminium alloys, copper, and Monel.

Applications

Riveting remains widely used in aircraft manufacture, where the fatigue resistance and reliability of riveted aluminium structures are valued, as well as in sheet-metal work, shipbuilding, boiler making, and in consumer products such as jeans, where small rivets reinforce pocket corners.
