Written vernacular Chinese (simplified Chinese: 白话文; traditional Chinese: 白話文; pinyin: báihuàwén) is the standard written form of Chinese based on the spoken language, chiefly Mandarin. It replaced Classical Chinese (wényán) as the dominant written standard in the early 20th century and is the form used today in books, newspapers, education and official documents throughout the Chinese-speaking world.

History

For over two thousand years, serious writing in China was done in Classical Chinese, a compact literary language modelled on the speech of antiquity and increasingly remote from everyday spoken language. Vernacular writing nevertheless had a long history: elements appear in Tang-dynasty transformation texts (biànwén), Song-dynasty records of Chan masters, and especially in the great vernacular novels of the Ming and Qing dynasties, such as Water Margin, Journey to the West and Dream of the Red Chamber.

The literary revolution came in 1917, when the scholar Hu Shih published an article in the journal New Youth advocating writing in the living vernacular; Chen Duxiu and Lu Xun championed the cause, and Lu Xun's 1918 story "Diary of a Madman" became a landmark of modern vernacular fiction. In 1920 the Ministry of Education ordered vernacular textbooks for primary schools, and by the 1920s báihuà had effectively displaced Classical Chinese in most publishing.

Features

Written vernacular differs from Classical Chinese in using disyllabic everyday vocabulary, explicit grammatical particles (such as 的, 了, 們), pronouns like 我, 你, 他, and word order closely following speech. European-style punctuation was adopted along with the vernacular. Modern written Chinese retains some classical elements in formal registers, idioms (chéngyǔ) and legal or official formulas. Written forms based on other varieties, notably written Cantonese, exist but are not the national standard.
