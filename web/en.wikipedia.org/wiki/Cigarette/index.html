Cigarette

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cigarette is a narrow cylinder containing a combustible material, typically finely cut tobacco leaves, rolled in thin paper for smoking. The cigarette is ignited at one end and the smoke drawn through the other, usually fitted with a cellulose acetate filter. The word derives from the French cigarette, "small cigar".

History

Tobacco was smoked in the Americas for centuries before Europeans arrived, but the modern cigarette emerged in the 19th century; paper-wrapped cigarettes became popular among soldiers during the Crimean War, and James Bonsack's cigarette-making machine (1881) enabled mass production. Consumption soared in the 20th century, peaking in many Western countries in the 1960s before health warnings, advertising bans and taxation drove long declines; China is now by far the largest producer and consumer.

Composition and manufacture

Modern cigarettes are engineered products: blended, reconstituted and expanded tobacco, treated with hundreds of additives that affect flavour, burn and nicotine delivery. Cigarette smoke contains thousands of chemical compounds, including nicotine, tar, carbon monoxide and dozens of known carcinogens.

Health effects

Smoking is a leading preventable cause of death worldwide, strongly linked to lung and other cancers, cardiovascular disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; the World Health Organization attributes roughly eight million deaths a year to tobacco, including over a million from second-hand smoke. Filter and "light" cigarettes did not remove these risks. Nearly all jurisdictions now mandate health warnings and restrict sale, advertising and public smoking, and the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (2003) coordinates global policy.
