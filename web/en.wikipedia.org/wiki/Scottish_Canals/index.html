Scottish Canals

Scottish Canals is the public corporation of the Scottish Government responsible for managing and operating Scotland's canals: the Caledonian Canal, the Crinan Canal, the Forth and Clyde Canal, the Union Canal and the Monkland Canal. It is an executive non-departmental public body, established under its current name on 2 July 2012, when it took over from British Waterways following devolution of the Scottish waterways.

The canals

  • The Caledonian Canal (1822) runs 97 km from Inverness to Fort William through the Great Glen, using Lochs Dochfour, Ness, Oich and Lochy, with 29 locks including the famous Neptune's Staircase flight at Banavie. Built to Thomas Telford's designs, it is navigable by seagoing vessels.
  • The Crinan Canal (1801), in Argyll, links Ardrishaig on Loch Fyne with Crinan on the Sound of Jura, saving small craft the long passage around the Mull of Kintyre.
  • The Forth and Clyde Canal (1790) crosses the Central Belt from the Firth of Forth at Grangemouth to the Clyde at Bowling. Closed in 1963, it was restored and reopened in 2001 as part of the Millennium Link project.
  • The Union Canal (1822) runs from Edinburgh to Falkirk, where it now joins the Forth and Clyde via the Falkirk Wheel, the world's only rotating boat lift, opened in 2002.
  • The Monkland Canal survives only in fragments near Coatbridge.

Role and activities

Scottish Canals is responsible for navigation, water management, heritage conservation and regeneration along some 220 km of waterways, 251 bridges, 212 locks and five reservoirs. Since restoration, the canals have become significant leisure and tourism assets: the Falkirk Wheel and the Kelpies — 30-metre-high horse-head sculptures at the eastern end of the Forth and Clyde — are among Scotland's most visited attractions. The organisation also promotes waterside housing, business and community development along the canal corridors.
