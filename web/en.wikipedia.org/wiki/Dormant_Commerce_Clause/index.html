Dormant Commerce Clause

In United States constitutional law, the dormant Commerce Clause, or negative Commerce Clause, is a legal doctrine that courts derive from the Commerce Clause of the United States Constitution (Article I, Section 8, Clause 3). Although the clause on its face grants Congress the power "to regulate Commerce ... among the several States", the dormant Commerce Clause is understood to also limit the power of the states to legislate in ways that burden or discriminate against interstate commerce, even where Congress has not acted.

Doctrine

The doctrine has two principal strands. First, state laws that discriminate against out-of-state commerce on their face, in purpose or in effect — for example by favouring local producers — are subject to strict scrutiny and are almost always invalid unless they advance a legitimate local purpose that cannot be served by nondiscriminatory means (Maine v. Taylor is the rare exception). Second, even nondiscriminatory laws are struck down under the balancing test of Pike v. Bruce Church (1970) if the burden they impose on interstate commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits.

Congress may authorise state action that would otherwise violate the doctrine, and the doctrine does not apply when a state acts as a market participant rather than a regulator.

History

Ideas underlying the doctrine appear in Chief Justice Marshall's opinion in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), and the doctrine took shape through nineteenth-century cases such as Cooley v. Board of Wardens (1852), which distinguished subjects requiring national uniformity from those admitting local diversity. Major modern applications include City of Philadelphia v. New Jersey (1978), blocking a state's exclusion of out-of-state waste; Granholm v. Heald (2005), on discriminatory wine-shipment laws; and National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023), where the Court upheld California's regulation of pork sold in the state against a dormant Commerce Clause challenge.

Criticism

Justices Antonin Scalia and Clarence Thomas criticised the doctrine as a "judicial fraud" lacking textual basis, arguing that the Commerce Clause grants power to Congress without itself restricting the states. Defenders respond that a free-trade principle was central to the Constitution's design as a remedy for the economic balkanisation experienced under the Articles of Confederation.
