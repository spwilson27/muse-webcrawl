The Battle of Puebla was fought on 5 May 1862 — Cinco de Mayo — near the city of Puebla, Mexico, during the Second French intervention in Mexico. A Mexican army under General Ignacio Zaragoza defeated a larger, better-equipped French expeditionary force commanded by General Charles Ferdinand Latrille, comte de Lorencez, in one of the most celebrated victories in Mexican history.

Background

In 1861 President Benito Juárez suspended payment on Mexico's foreign debts, prompting Britain, Spain and France to land forces at Veracruz to compel repayment. Britain and Spain withdrew when France's wider aim — installing a client empire under Maximilian of Austria — became clear, but France advanced inland toward Mexico City. Zaragoza, with roughly 4,500 men including militia and local volunteers, fortified Puebla, anchoring his defence on the hilltop forts of Loreto and Guadalupe.

The battle

Confident of an easy triumph, Lorencez launched frontal assaults on the forts on 5 May. The French attacks were beaten back three times; a sudden rainstorm turned the slopes to mud, hampering the final assault, and Mexican cavalry — including units led by the young Porfirio Díaz — drove off a flank attack. By evening the French withdrew toward Orizaba, having lost several hundred men. Zaragoza's laconic dispatch to Juárez reported that "the national arms have been covered with glory".

Aftermath and significance

The victory was strategically temporary: France sent some 30,000 reinforcements, took Puebla after a siege in 1863, and installed Maximilian as emperor. But the French were ultimately defeated and Maximilian executed in 1867. Puebla bought Mexico a year of preparation and became a lasting symbol of resistance to foreign intervention. The anniversary, Cinco de Mayo, is a regional holiday in the state of Puebla and is widely celebrated — often more festively than in Mexico itself — in the United States as a celebration of Mexican heritage.
