Scots is a West Germanic language variety traditionally spoken in Lowland Scotland and parts of Ulster in Ireland, where it is called Ulster Scots. It descends from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English, developing separately from the southern forms that became modern English, and forms a dialect continuum with Scottish English in which speakers move between the two depending on context.

History

Anglian speech spread into southeastern Scotland from the 7th century. By the late Middle Ages "Scottis" had become the language of the Scottish court, law and a major literature — the Makars such as Robert Henryson and William Dunbar wrote in Middle Scots. After the Union of the Crowns (1603) and Union of Parliaments (1707), English displaced Scots from formal use, though it remained the everyday tongue of most Lowlanders and was revived literarily by Allan Ramsay, Robert Fergusson and Robert Burns.

Status

Scots is recognised as a regional or minority language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and the Scottish Government promotes its use in education and media. Whether Scots is a language distinct from English or a group of dialects remains a matter of debate; linguists generally treat the Scots–English relationship as a continuum with historically distinct literary standards.

Features

Scots retains distinctive vocabulary (bairn "child", kirk "church", ken "know"), vowel patterns governed by the Scottish Vowel Length Rule, and consonants lost in standard English, such as /x/ in loch and dreich. Its dialects range from Doric in the north-east to the Scots of Glasgow and the Borders.
