The Qingming Festival or Ching Ming Festival (Chinese: 清明节, "pure brightness"), also known in English as Tomb-Sweeping Day, is a traditional Chinese festival observed on the 15th day after the Spring Equinox—typically 4 or 5 April—when families visit and clean the graves of their ancestors. It has been observed by the Han Chinese for over 2,500 years and is a public holiday in mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan.

Observances

Families travel to ancestral burial sites to sweep the tombs, clear weeds, add fresh soil, and make offerings of food, tea, wine, and joss paper ("spirit money"), sometimes burnt along with paper replicas of houses, cars, and electronics, expressing filial piety (xiao) toward the dead. Because the day falls in mid-spring, it is also traditionally a time for outings (taqing, "treading the green") to enjoy the season. Foods associated with the festival include qingtuan (green dumplings), zongzi, and sanzi.

History and heritage

Qingming's origins trace to the Zhou dynasty's Hanshi ("Cold Food") Festival, with which it eventually merged. It was recorded as one of the 24 solar terms of the traditional Chinese calendar, marking the point when spring's warmth returns in full. In 2008, the festival was reinstated as a national public holiday in mainland China, and it is inscribed in the national list of intangible cultural heritage. A classic Chinese painting, Along the River During the Qingming Festival by Zhang Zeduan, depicts capital life during the Northern Song dynasty on this day.
