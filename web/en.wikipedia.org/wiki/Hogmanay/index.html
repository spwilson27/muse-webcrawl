Hogmanay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hogmanay (Scots: [ˌhɔɡməˈneː]) is the Scots word for the last day of the old year and is synonymous with the celebration of the New Year in the Scottish manner. It is normally followed by further celebration on the morning of New Year's Day (1 January) and, in Scotland, also on 2 January — both of which are public holidays.

Origins

The origins of Hogmanay are unclear, but the festival likely draws on Norse and Gaelic winter solstice celebrations, blended with the French hoguinané. The Protestant Reformation suppressed Christmas in Scotland for roughly 400 years, so New Year festivities became the principal midwinter celebration, with "daft days" of feasting, visiting, and gift-giving.

Customs

Customs include "redding" the house (thorough cleaning and clearing debts before midnight), singing "Auld Lang Syne" as the bells ring, torchlight processions, and fire festivals such as the Stonehaven Fireballs, where locals swing blazing balls of tarred rags through the streets. The most famous custom is first-footing: the first person to cross a home's threshold after midnight sets the household's luck for the year; a tall, dark-haired man bearing gifts of coal, shortbread, salt, black bun, and whisky is considered most auspicious. Edinburgh's Hogmanay street party and fireworks are now internationally famous.
