History of Hawaii

The history of Hawaii begins with the settlement of the Hawaiian Islands by Polynesian voyagers, traditionally dated to the first millennium CE, and encompasses the rise of the unified Kingdom of Hawaii under Kamehameha I in 1795–1810, its overthrow in 1893, annexation by the United States in 1898, and statehood as the 50th state in 1959.

Polynesian settlement and chiefdoms

Polynesian navigators from the Marquesas and later Tahiti settled Hawaii, developing a sophisticated society of ranked chiefdoms (aliʻi), intensive taro irrigation and fishpond aquaculture. Warfare between rival island chiefdoms was frequent by the 18th century.

Contact and unification

Captain James Cook reached the islands in 1778 and named them the Sandwich Islands; he was killed at Kealakekua Bay in 1779. With the aid of Western weapons and advisers, Kamehameha of Hawaii island conquered most of the archipelago by 1795 and unified it fully in 1810, founding the Kingdom of Hawaii. His successors (the Kamehameha dynasty) abolished the kapu system in 1819, and American Protestant missionaries arrived from 1820, devising a written form of the Hawaiian language.

The Kingdom in the 19th century

The kingdom gained diplomatic recognition from Britain and the United States in the 1840s. The Great Māhele of 1848 introduced private land ownership, with lasting consequences for Native Hawaiians, whose population had already collapsed due to introduced diseases. Sugar plantations imported labourers from China, Japan, Portugal, the Philippines and elsewhere, reshaping the islands' demography. The Bayonet Constitution of 1887 curtailed royal power.

Overthrow, annexation and statehood

In 1893 a committee of American and European businessmen, backed by US forces, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani; a Republic of Hawaii was proclaimed in 1894. The United States annexed Hawaii by the Newlands Resolution in 1898. As a US territory, Hawaii's naval base at Pearl Harbor was attacked by Japan on 7 December 1941, bringing the United States into the Second World War. After decades of labour and civil-rights movements led largely by its Asian-American majority, Hawaii was admitted as the 50th state on 21 August 1959. In 1993 the US Congress passed the Apology Resolution acknowledging wrongdoing in the overthrow, and a Native Hawaiian sovereignty movement continues to the present.
