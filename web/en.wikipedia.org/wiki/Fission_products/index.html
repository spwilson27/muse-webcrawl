Fission products

Nuclear fission products are the atomic fragments left after a large atomic nucleus undergoes nuclear fission. Typically, a large nucleus such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239 splits into two smaller nuclei (fission fragments), along with a few neutrons and a large release of energy. The initial fission fragments are highly unstable and undergo further radioactive decay, producing the fission products of practical concern.

Formation and yields

Fission of heavy nuclei produces a broad distribution of fragment masses, with yields peaking near mass numbers 95 and 137. The resulting fission products include isotopes of elements such as caesium, strontium, iodine, xenon, and technetium. Many are radioactive with half-lives ranging from fractions of a second to millions of years.

Importance

Fission products dominate the radioactivity of spent nuclear fuel in the short term and produce decay heat that must be managed after a reactor shuts down. Notable isotopes include caesium-137 (half-life ~30 years) and strontium-90 (~29 years), which are major contributors to contamination from nuclear accidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima. Iodine-131, with an 8-day half-life, is a short-term health hazard. Some fission products, such as technetium-99 and iodine-129, are very long-lived and pose challenges for geological disposal. Other fission products have valuable uses: molybdenum-99 decays to technetium-99m, widely used in medical imaging.
