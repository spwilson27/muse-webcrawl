Non-invasive ventilation

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is the delivery of mechanical ventilation to the lungs without an invasive artificial airway such as an endotracheal or tracheostomy tube. Instead, air (often with supplemental oxygen) is delivered through a tightly fitting mask — nasal, oronasal or full-face — or occasionally a helmet interface. The two main modes are continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), which alternates between higher inspiratory and lower expiratory pressures.

Uses

NIV is a first-line treatment for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure due to exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), where trials show it reduces the need for intubation and lowers mortality. It is also used in acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, in immunocompromised patients with respiratory failure, in weaning from invasive ventilation, and for chronic nocturnal ventilatory support in neuromuscular disease, chest-wall disorders and obesity hypoventilation syndrome.

Advantages and limits

Compared with invasive ventilation, NIV avoids sedation, preserves the ability to speak and swallow, and reduces complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia. Its disadvantages include mask discomfort and air leaks, claustrophobia, gastric distension, and — most importantly — the danger of delaying a necessary intubation in a deteriorating patient; failure of NIV therefore requires close monitoring in a high-dependency setting.
