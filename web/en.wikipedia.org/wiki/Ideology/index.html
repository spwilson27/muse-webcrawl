Ideology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ideology is a set of beliefs and values held by an individual, group or culture, especially one that forms the basis of a political or economic theory and the policies derived from it. In political science the term covers coherent systems of ideas — such as Liberalism, Conservatism and Socialism — that explain how society is organised and how it ought to be organised.

History of the concept

The word was coined by Antoine Destutt de Tracy in the 1790s for a "science of ideas". Napoleon used it pejoratively for abstract theorising, and Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels gave it a critical sense: ideology as the ideas of the ruling class, naturalising its dominance — later analysed as "false consciousness". In the twentieth century, scholars such as Karl Mannheim (who distinguished ideology from utopia) and Clifford Geertz (ideology as a cultural system of symbols) broadened the concept beyond the Marxian critique.

Political ideologies

Political ideologies are commonly arranged along a left–right spectrum descending from the seating of the French revolutionary assemblies, though multi-dimensional models (economic vs. social/cultural axes) are widely used. Ideologies supply diagnoses of social problems, visions of the good society, and programmes for action; they are carried by political parties, movements, intellectuals and media. Debates since the 1950s have periodically proclaimed an "end of ideology", usually followed by the emergence of new ideological conflicts — over globalisation, identity, climate change and technology.

Analysis

Scholars analyse ideologies by their core, adjacent and peripheral concepts (Michael Freeden's morphological approach), by their function in legitimating or challenging power, and through discourse analysis of political language. In everyday usage "ideological" can be a criticism, implying dogmatism — a tension present since the term's invention.
