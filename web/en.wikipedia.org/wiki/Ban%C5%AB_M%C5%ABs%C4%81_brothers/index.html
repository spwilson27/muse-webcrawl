The Banū Mūsā ("Sons of Moses") were three 9th-century Persian scholars — Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir, Abū al-Qāsim Aḥmad ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir, and Al-Ḥasan ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir — who worked in Baghdad under the Abbasid caliphate and were patrons and practitioners of science at the House of Wisdom.

Life and patronage

Their father, Mūsā ibn Shākir, had been an astronomer (and, by some accounts, a reformed highwayman) at the court of al-Ma'mun. After his death the caliph raised and educated the brothers. They became wealthy scholars who funded expeditions to obtain Greek manuscripts and employed leading translators such as Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq, sometimes at considerable annual expense, in rivalry with other patrons.

Works

Their most famous work is the Book of Ingenious Devices (Kitāb al-Ḥiyal, c. 850), describing about one hundred automatic devices, including trick vessels, fountains, and automatic controls — landmarks in the history of mechanical engineering. They also wrote on geometry, including a refined treatment of the measurement of plane and spherical figures drawing on Archimedes, and made astronomical observations. The brothers' intense scientific rivalry with the philosopher al-Kindī is recorded in medieval biographical sources.
