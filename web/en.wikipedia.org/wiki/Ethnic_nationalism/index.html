Ethnic nationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ethnic nationalism, also known as ethnonationalism, is a form of nationalism in which the nation is defined in terms of ethnicity, descent and a shared ancestral culture. Membership is largely ascribed: one is born into the nation, and newcomers can be fully included only with difficulty. The ethnic conception corresponds to the jus sanguinis (law of blood) principle in nationality law, and to what Hans Kohn called the “Eastern” type of nationalism.

Ethnic nationalism defines belonging by language, religion, customs and supposed common ancestry, and tends to treat diasporas as members of the nation and minorities within the state as outsiders. Its institutional expressions have included ethnic citizenship laws (Germany's before the 2000 reform; Israel's Law of Return for Jews), kin-state policies toward co-ethnics abroad, and — in extreme cases — ethnic cleansing and exclusionary violence, as in the breakup of Yugoslavia.

Contrast and criticism

The standard contrast is with civic nationalism, where belonging rests on shared citizenship and commitment to political values. Scholars caution that the dichotomy is an idealisation: civic nations also have ethnic cores, and ethnic nations may assimilate outsiders. Critics argue ethnic nationalism is inherently exclusionary; defenders within multi-ethnic states sometimes present it as a minority's shield for cultural survival.
