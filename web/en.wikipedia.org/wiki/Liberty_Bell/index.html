Liberty Bell

The Liberty Bell, previously called the State House Bell or Old State House Bell, is an iconic symbol of American independence, located in Philadelphia. Commissioned in 1751 by the Pennsylvania Provincial Assembly for the Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall), it was cast in London by the Whitechapel Bell Foundry, cracked on its first test ring, and recast by Philadelphia founders John Pass and John Stow, whose names appear on the bell. It is inscribed with a verse from Leviticus: "Proclaim LIBERTY Throughout all the Land unto all the Inhabitants Thereof".

History

The bell summoned lawmakers and townspeople for public announcements; later tradition — popularized by an 1847 short story — holds that it rang on July 8, 1776, to mark the first public reading of the Declaration of Independence. In 1777 it was hidden under the floorboards of a church in Allentown to keep it from advancing British troops, who might have melted it down for cannon.

The crack and retirement

The bell's famous crack developed in the early 19th century. In 1846, while being rung for George Washington's birthday, the crack worsened irreparably, and the bell was retired from regular use. Repairs — including widening the crack to prevent the edges striking (the visibly drilled "crack" of today) — allowed ceremonial tapping on special occasions, but it has not been rung freely since.

Symbol

Abolitionists in the 1830s were the first to call it the "Liberty Bell", adopting it as an emblem of the antislavery cause; suffragists, civil rights campaigners, and Cold War governments later claimed it in turn. It toured American expositions between 1885 and 1915, drawing enormous crowds, before returning permanently to Philadelphia. Since 2003 it has been displayed in the Liberty Bell Center of Independence National Historical Park, opposite Independence Hall, where roughly two million visitors view it each year.
