American and British English pronunciation differences

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

American English and British English differ in pronunciation in a number of systematic and lexical ways. The standard reference accents usually compared are General American (GA) and Received Pronunciation (RP), although both countries have wide internal variation. The differences include the treatment of the letter R, characteristic vowel qualities, stress placement, and the pronunciation of particular words.

Rhoticity

The most conspicuous difference is rhoticity: General American is rhotic, pronouncing /r/ in all positions, as in hard /hɑːrd/ and butter /ˈbʌtər/. RP is non-rhotic, pronouncing /r/ only before a vowel, so hard is /hɑːd/, with "linking" and "intrusive" R appearing before following vowels in connected speech. Historically, non-rhoticity spread through southern England in the 18th and 19th centuries after American independence, which is one reason rhotic American accents can preserve older features.

Vowel differences

Several lexical sets differ systematically. In the BATH set (words such as bath, grass, dance), RP uses the long vowel /ɑː/ where GA retains /æ/. In the LOT set, GA has an unrounded /ɑː/ while RP has rounded /ɒ/. Most American accents have undergone the cot–caught merger or are in the process of doing so, unlike RP, which maintains the contrast. GA also features flapping of /t/ and /d/ between vowels, so butter and ladder may sound nearly identical.

Suffixes such as -ile (fertile, missile) are typically reduced to /əl/ in American English but pronounced with a full /aɪl/ in RP; -ary and -ory endings receive secondary stress in GA (e.g. dictionary) but are reduced in RP. French loanwords often take final-syllable stress in British English (ballet, garage varies) where American usage differs.
