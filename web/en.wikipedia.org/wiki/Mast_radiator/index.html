Mast radiator

A mast radiator (or radiating tower) is a radio mast or tower in which the entire metal structure itself serves as the antenna. Mast radiators are used extensively for medium-wave (AM) broadcasting and other low- and medium-frequency transmissions, where the required antenna height is a substantial fraction of the wavelength.

Construction

A mast radiator is typically a guyed lattice mast or a self-supporting tower of steel. Because the structure carries radio-frequency current, it must be insulated from the ground, usually by mounting it on a large ceramic base insulator capable of withstanding both the structural load and the high RF voltage at the feed point. Guy wires are broken into sections by strain insulators to prevent them from re-radiating or short-circuiting the antenna. A radial ground system of buried copper wires around the base provides the ground plane against which the mast works as a monopole.

Electrical properties

The classic mast radiator is a quarter-wavelength tall (about 75 metres at 1 MHz), exhibiting a feed-point resistance near 36 ohms. Taller masts of around 0.53–0.625 wavelengths ("anti-fading" heights) concentrate radiation at low angles and reduce high-angle skywave that causes fading near the station. Top loading, umbrella wires or sectionalised designs are sometimes used to adjust the current distribution. Where several stations share one mast, or where the mast supports other antennas, shunt feeding with a slanted "skirt" wire allows the base to remain grounded.

Use

Most medium-wave broadcast stations worldwide use mast radiators, often in directional arrays of two or more masts to protect co-channel stations at night. Tall examples, such as the former Warsaw radio mast (646 m, collapsed 1991) and the KVLY-TV mast (a non-radiating support), rank among the tallest human-made structures.
