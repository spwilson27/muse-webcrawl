Sovereignty of the Falkland Islands

The Falkland Islands (Islas Malvinas) in the South Atlantic

The sovereignty of the Falkland Islands (Spanish: Islas Malvinas) is disputed by Argentina and the United Kingdom. The British claim rests on uninterrupted administration of the islands since 1833 (and earlier settlement in the 18th century), together with the islanders' right of self-determination. Argentina maintains that it inherited the islands from Spain in 1816, that the British expulsion of its authorities in 1833 was an act of usurpation, and that the islands lie within its territorial sea and continental shelf.

Background

The islands were uninhabited when Europeans first sighted them. France established a settlement at Port Louis in 1764, and Britain founded Port Egmont on Saunders Island in 1765. Spain acquired the French settlement and later expelled the British garrison in 1770, provoking a diplomatic crisis; Britain withdrew in 1774 but did not renounce its claim. Spain maintained a settlement until 1811.

After independence, the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata asserted sovereignty and in 1829 appointed Louis Vernet as governor. In 1833 a British warship expelled the Argentine garrison, and Britain has administered the islands continuously since. Argentina has protested the occupation ever since.

Falklands War (1982)

On 2 April 1982 Argentina invaded the islands, which Argentina called Operación Rosario. The United Kingdom dispatched a naval task force some 8,000 miles (13,000 km) and retook the islands after a 74-day war, ending with the Argentine surrender on 14 June 1982. The war cost 649 Argentine and 255 British military lives, and three Falkland Islanders were killed. Diplomatic relations between the two countries were restored in 1990, but Argentina's claim — enshrined in its 1994 constitution — remains.

Positions and the 2013 referendum

The United Nations lists the Falklands as a non-self-governing territory and, since Resolution 2065 of 1965, has called for bilateral negotiations, a position Argentina supports. The United Kingdom maintains that the islanders' wishes are paramount. In a referendum held in March 2013, 99.8% of Falkland Islanders voted to remain a British Overseas Territory, on a turnout of over 90%. Argentina dismissed the referendum as having no legal validity.

The islands' economy, based on fishing licences, tourism, and agriculture, has been joined in recent years by hydrocarbon exploration in surrounding waters, which Argentina contests.
