Curse of dimensionality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Curse of dimensionality
Term coined byRichard E. Bellman (1957)
FieldMachine learning, statistics, numerical analysis
Core problemData sparsity grows exponentially with dimension

The curse of dimensionality refers to various phenomena that arise when analyzing and organizing data in high-dimensional spaces that do not occur in low-dimensional settings. The term was coined by Richard E. Bellman in 1957, in the context of dynamic programming.

The problem

As the number of dimensions (features) increases, the volume of the space grows exponentially, so fixed amounts of data become increasingly sparse. For many machine-learning methods, the amount of training data needed grows exponentially with dimension. Intuitively, in high dimensions nearly all of the volume of a cube lies near its surface, distances between random points concentrate (nearest and farthest neighbors become almost equidistant), and ordinary intuition from two or three dimensions fails.

Consequences

Neighborhood-based methods such as k-nearest neighbors degrade, clustering becomes harder, and models risk overfitting because the number of possible configurations explodes. Numerical integration, sampling, and optimization over high-dimensional spaces face similar exponential costs.

Remedies

Common responses include dimensionality reduction (principal component analysis, t-SNE, UMAP), feature selection, regularization, and exploiting structure such as sparsity or low intrinsic dimension — the "blessing of dimensionality" observation that real data often lie near a low-dimensional manifold.
