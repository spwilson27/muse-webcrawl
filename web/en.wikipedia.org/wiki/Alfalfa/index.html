Alfalfa

Scientific name: Medicago sativa
Family: Fabaceae
Other names: Lucerne

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa), also called lucerne, is a perennial flowering plant in the legume family Fabaceae. It is cultivated as an important forage crop in many countries around the world. It is used for grazing, hay, and silage, as well as a green manure and cover crop. The name alfalfa is used in North America; the name lucerne is more commonly used in the United Kingdom, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

Description

Alfalfa is a cool-season perennial legume living from three to twenty years, depending on variety and climate. The plant grows to a height of up to 1 metre (3 feet), and has a deep root system sometimes growing to more than 15 metres (49 feet), which makes it highly resilient, particularly to droughts. Its flowers are purple and its fruit are spiralled pods containing seeds. Like other legumes, its root nodules contain the bacteria Sinorhizobium meliloti, which fix atmospheric nitrogen, making alfalfa an effective improver of soil fertility.

Cultivation

Alfalfa probably originated in south-central Asia, in the region of modern Iran, and was first cultivated in ancient Persia; it was an important fodder for horses in the ancient Greek and Roman worlds. Today the United States is the largest producer. Alfalfa is widely grown throughout the world as forage for cattle, and is most often harvested as hay, but can also be made into silage, grazed, or fed as greenchop. It has the highest feeding value of all common hay crops and is also grown for human consumption as sprouted seeds.

Uses and ecology

Alfalfa is widely used as animal feed, especially for dairy cattle, but also for beef cattle, horses, sheep, and goats. Dehydrated alfalfa leaf meal is used as a pigmenting and nutritional agent in poultry feed. Because alfalfa fixes nitrogen and has very deep roots, it is valued in crop rotations and for erosion control. Alfalfa fields are also an important habitat for wildlife and a rich source of nectar for pollinators, including the alfalfa leafcutter bee, which is managed commercially for pollination.
