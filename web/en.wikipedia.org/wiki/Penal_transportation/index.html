Penal transportation

Penal transportation was the relocation of convicted criminals, or other persons regarded as undesirable, to a distant place, often a colony, for a specified term — usually for the duration of their sentence. Transported convicts were often put to hard labour as part of their punishment; transportation occupied the space between capital punishment and lesser penalties in the sentencing systems that used it.

Britain and its colonies

From the early seventeenth century, England transported convicts and vagrants to the American colonies, a practice formalised by the Transportation Act 1717; an estimated 50,000–120,000 convicts were sent to America before independence closed that destination. From 1787 Britain instead transported convicts to Australia: the First Fleet arrived at Sydney Cove in 1788, and transportation to New South Wales, Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania), and later Western Australia continued until 1868. In all, about 162,000 convicts were transported to Australia. Many earned tickets of leave and conditional pardons, and emancipated convicts became a significant part of the colonial population.

Other systems

France transported convicts to penal colonies in French Guiana — including the notorious Devil's Island — and New Caledonia from the mid-nineteenth century until 1953. Russia exiled convicts and political prisoners to Siberia under the katorga system, and the practice continued in different forms in the Soviet Gulag era. Spain, Portugal and the Netherlands also used transportation to overseas territories.

End of transportation

Opposition from free settlers in the receiving colonies, changing penal philosophy favouring imprisonment at home, and the rising cost of transport led to abolition in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Transportation remains a defining episode in Australian history, and convict ancestry is now widely researched and even celebrated.
