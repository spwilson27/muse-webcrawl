Neo-Druidism or Neo-Druidry is a form of modern spirituality or religion that generally promotes harmony and worship of nature. Many forms of modern Druidry are modern Pagan religions, although some take a more philosophical approach. The movement began in Britain during the 18th century as a romantic celebration of ancient Celtic peoples, and it takes its name from the druids, the priestly class of the Iron Age Celts.

Modern Druids generally acknowledge that little is reliably known about the actual beliefs of the ancient druids, and do not claim unbroken continuity with them. Instead they draw inspiration from archaeology, folklore, Celtic mythology and the landscape, combining these into contemporary practice.

History

The Druid revival took shape in the era of Romanticism. Antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley associated druids with monuments like Stonehenge. In 1717 the Ancient Druid Order is traditionally said to have been founded in London. Iolo Morganwg (Edward Williams) created Welsh Druidic ceremonies, including the Gorsedd of Bards, in the late 18th century. In 1963 American students founded the Reformed Druids of North America. Philip Carr-Gomm's leadership of the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (OBOD), and Isaac Bonewits's founding of Ár nDraíocht Féin in 1983, shaped the modern religious forms of the movement.

Beliefs and practices

Most Druid groups celebrate the eightfold "Wheel of the Year" of seasonal festivals shared with Wicca, including Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasadh, and hold rituals outdoors, often at stone circles or in groves. Trees — especially the oak — are sacred. Concepts such as Awen, divine inspiration, figure prominently in Welsh-influenced traditions. Many adherents identify as polytheists, animists or pantheists, honouring Celtic deities such as Brigid, Lugh and the Morrígan.
