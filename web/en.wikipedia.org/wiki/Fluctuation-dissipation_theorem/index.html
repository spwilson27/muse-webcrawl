Fluctuation-dissipation theorem

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) or fluctuation-dissipation relation (FDR) is a powerful tool in statistical physics for predicting the behaviour of systems that obey detailed balance. It states a general relationship between the response of a given system to a small external perturbation and the internal fluctuation of the system in the absence of the perturbation. By observing the spontaneous fluctuations of a system in thermal equilibrium, one can therefore infer how it will respond — and dissipate energy — when driven.

History

The theorem grew out of Albert Einstein's 1905 theory of Brownian motion, which connected the diffusion of suspended particles to the viscosity (dissipation) of the surrounding fluid. Harry Nyquist derived the corresponding relation for thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise in electrical conductors in 1928, and Herbert Callen and Theodore Welton gave the general quantum-mechanical formulation in 1951. Lars Onsager's reciprocal relations are a related consequence of microscopic reversibility.

Statement

In its classical form, for a variable x whose equilibrium power spectrum of fluctuations is Sx(ω) and whose dissipative response is described by the imaginary part of the susceptibility χ″(ω), the theorem reads:

Sx(ω) = (2 kB T / ω) χ″(ω)

where kB is Boltzmann's constant and T the absolute temperature. The same relation, applied to a Brownian particle, gives the Einstein relation D = μ kB T connecting the diffusion constant D to the mobility μ.

Applications

The theorem underlies the theory of Johnson noise in resistors, the fluctuation-dissipation analysis of optical tweezers and atomic force microscopy, microrheology, and the treatment of decoherence in quantum systems. Violations or generalisations of the theorem — through the notion of an "effective temperature" — are used to characterise glasses and other non-equilibrium systems.
