Gondor is a fictional kingdom in J. R. R. Tolkien's writings, the southern of the two kingdoms of the exiled Dúnedain in Middle-earth (its northern sister-realm being Arnor). It is a central setting of The Lord of the Rings, standing as the principal bulwark of the West against Sauron in Mordor, which lies on its eastern border.

History

Gondor was founded in S.A. 3320 by Isildur and Anárion, sons of Elendil, survivors of the downfall of Númenor. Its early kings expanded its power to a height in the early Third Age, when Gondor dominated much of north-western Middle-earth. From the later Third Age the kingdom declined through civil war (the Kin-strife), plague, and wars with the Wainriders and Corsairs of Umbar. When King Eärnur died without an heir in T.A. 2050, the Ruling Stewards governed Gondor "until the King returns", maintaining its defense against Mordor for nearly a thousand years.

The War of the Ring

Under Steward Denethor II, Gondor bore the brunt of Sauron's assault. Its capital, the tiered fortress-city of Minas Tirith, was besieged in the Battle of the Pelennor Fields (T.A. 3019), relieved by the Rohirrim and Aragorn's forces arriving by ship. After Sauron's fall, Aragorn was crowned King Elessar, reuniting Gondor and Arnor as the Reunited Kingdom, with the White Tree — long dead — replaced by a new sapling.

Geography and culture

Gondor's heartland lay between the White Mountains (Ered Nimrais) and the Bay of Belfalas, watered by the great river Anduin. Its fiefs included Dol Amroth, Lossarnach, Lebennin and Ithilien. Tolkien gave its people a culture recalling Byzantium and Egypt as much as medieval Europe: monumental stone architecture, embalmed kings, and a long memory of former glory.
