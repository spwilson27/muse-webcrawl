Lyra (Latin for "lyre") is a small constellation in the Northern Hemisphere. It is one of the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy and one of the modern 88 constellations recognised by the International Astronomical Union. Its brightest star, Vega, is the fifth-brightest star in the sky and forms one corner of the Summer Triangle asterism.

Stars

Vega (Alpha Lyrae) is a brilliant blue-white main-sequence star about 25 light-years from Earth; it was the northern pole star around 12,000 BCE and will be again around 13,727 CE. Below Vega lies a small parallelogram of stars containing two celebrated objects of amateur astronomy: Epsilon Lyrae, the "Double Double", a quadruple star system, and Beta Lyrae (Sheliak), the prototype of a class of eclipsing binary stars.

Deep-sky objects

The Ring Nebula (Messier 57), one of the most famous planetary nebulae, lies between Beta and Gamma Lyrae; it is the glowing shell of gas expelled by a dying sun-like star. Lyra also contains the globular cluster M56 and the open cluster NGC 6791, one of the oldest known open clusters. The Kepler space telescope's original field of view, centred largely on Lyra and Cygnus, discovered thousands of exoplanets in this region.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Lyra represents the lyre of Orpheus, whose music could charm all living things and even stones. After Orpheus was killed, Zeus placed his lyre in the sky at the request of Apollo and the Muses. In other traditions the constellation was depicted as an eagle or vulture — hence Vega's name, from an Arabic phrase meaning "the falling eagle".
