Iran–Contra affair

The Iran–Contra affair, often referred to as the Iran–Contra scandal, was a political scandal in the United States that occurred during the second term of the Reagan administration. Between 1981 and 1986, senior administration officials secretly facilitated the illegal sale of arms to Iran, which was subject to an arms embargo, in the hope of securing the release of American hostages held in Lebanon and allowing intelligence agencies to fund the Contras, Nicaraguan rebels, despite a congressional prohibition (the Boland Amendment).

Disclosure and investigation

The scheme unravelled in November 1986 when a Lebanese magazine reported the arms sales and a supply plane for the Contras was shot down over Nicaragua. The Tower Commission, congressional hearings in 1987, and Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh's investigation followed. Figures including National Security Council aide Oliver North and National Security Advisor John Poindexter became central; several officials were convicted, though some convictions were overturned on appeal and others were pardoned by President George H. W. Bush in 1992.

Legacy

The affair damaged President Ronald Reagan's standing before he recovered politically, and it remains a landmark case study in covert action, executive power, and congressional oversight.
