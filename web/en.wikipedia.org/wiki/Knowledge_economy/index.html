Knowledge economy

[Image: researchers working with data visualisations in a modern laboratory]

The knowledge economy is an economic system in which production, growth and employment depend primarily on the quantity, quality and accessibility of information and knowledge rather than on physical inputs or natural resources. The term was popularised by Peter Drucker (The Age of Discontinuity, 1969) and developed by OECD analysis in the 1990s.

Features

Its hallmarks are knowledge-intensive industries (software, pharmaceuticals, finance, education, media), a workforce dominated by 'knowledge workers', and investment in intangibles — research and development, data, brands, software and organisational capital — which in leading economies now rivals or exceeds investment in machinery and buildings. Because knowledge is partially non-rival (usable by many at once), knowledge economies exhibit increasing returns and spillovers at the heart of endogenous growth theory, and distinctive policy questions: intellectual property versus diffusion, universities as economic engines, digital platforms and their data, and now artificial intelligence as a knowledge technology. The World Bank's knowledge-economy index and similar scoreboards rank countries on education, innovation, ICT and institutions.
