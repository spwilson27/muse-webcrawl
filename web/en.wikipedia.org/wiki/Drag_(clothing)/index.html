Drag (clothing)

Drag is the wearing of clothing, makeup and accessories — often exaggerated and theatrical — traditionally associated with a gender different from, or a heightened version of, the performer's own, usually as performance art. Performers who present femininely are called drag queens and those who present masculinely drag kings. Drag is a central tradition of LGBTQ culture, though not all drag performers identify as LGBTQ, and doing drag is distinct from being transgender.

History

Cross-gender performance is as old as theatre itself: female roles in classical and Shakespearean theatre were played by men and boys, and 19th-century minstrel and vaudeville stages featured female impersonators such as Julian Eltinge. The word "drag" is often said to derive from the long skirts that dragged on the floor. In the 20th century drag flourished in underground gay bars and in the Harlem ballroom scene, where houses competed in balls judged on costume and voguing — a culture documented in the film Paris Is Burning (1990). Performers such as RuPaul carried drag into mainstream pop in the 1990s.

Modern drag culture

The television franchise RuPaul's Drag Race (from 2009) globalised drag, spawning international editions and turning queens into touring celebrities. Contemporary drag spans comedy, lip-sync, live singing, political satire and family-friendly events such as drag story hours, and it encompasses an expanding range of styles beyond female illusion, including gender-fluid and "bio queen" performance. Drag scenes have also been targets of protest and, in some jurisdictions, restrictive legislation, which performers and civil-liberties groups have challenged as infringements on free expression.

Terminology

Ballroom and drag culture contributed much slang now widespread — "shade", "reading", "werk", "slay" — and the concept of chosen "drag families" headed by a drag mother. Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity drew heavily on drag as an example exposing gender as imitative performance.
