Blacksmith

A blacksmith is a metalsmith who creates objects primarily from wrought iron or steel, but sometimes from other metals, by forging the metal — heating it in a forge until it glows and then hammering and bending it into shape. The "black" in the name refers to the black firescale that forms on the metal during heating, and to the metal itself, in contrast to the "white" metals worked by whitesmiths.

Craft and tools

The blacksmith's workshop, the smithy or forge, contains a hearth (forge) with bellows, an anvil, hammers, tongs, punches, chisels and a quench trough. Core techniques include drawing out, upsetting, bending, punching, welding and heat treatment. Historically blacksmiths made and repaired tools, agricultural implements, weapons, hardware, nails and horseshoes; farriers specialise in shoeing horses.

History and decline

Ironworking dates to the Iron Age, and blacksmiths held high status in mythology and village life alike — figures such as Hephaestus, Vulcan and Wayland the Smith reflect the craft's prestige. The village smithy remained a fixture of rural economies until industrial mass production in the 19th and 20th centuries displaced most of its functions. A revival since the late 20th century has sustained blacksmithing as an art and hobby craft, with organisations such as the Artist Blacksmiths' Association of North America promoting the trade.
