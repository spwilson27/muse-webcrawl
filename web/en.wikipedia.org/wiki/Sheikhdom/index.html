Sheikhdom

A sheikhdom (alternatively sheikdom or shaikhdom) is a geographical area or a society ruled by a sheikh (Arabic: شيخ, shaykh), a hereditary or acclaimed tribal and religious leader in Arab and Islamic societies. Sheikh is an honorific title denoting an elder or leader, and a sheikhdom is the territory or polity over which such a figure exercises authority.

Historical examples

The term is most often applied to the small Arab polities of the Persian Gulf and the Arabian Peninsula. In the 19th and 20th centuries Britain concluded treaties with a series of Gulf sheikhdoms — Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar and the seven Trucial States, among them Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Sharjah — giving them protected status while leaving internal rule with the sheikhs. Most of the Trucial sheikhdoms federated in 1971 as the United Arab Emirates, each still governed by its hereditary ruler, while Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar became independent states headed by ruling families whose heads retain the sheikhly style.

Elsewhere, sheikhdoms existed in Yemen, where dozens of small states and sultanates formed the Federation of South Arabia, and in the Arabian interior, where tribal sheikhs controlled oases and caravan routes before the Saudi unification. In a looser sense the word is also used for domains of Sufi sheikhs and for tribal confederations in parts of Africa and Central Asia.
