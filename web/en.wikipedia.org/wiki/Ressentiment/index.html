Ressentiment

Ressentiment (French pronunciation: [ʁə.sɑ̃.ti.mɑ̃]) is the French translation of the English word resentment. In philosophy, it has been understood as "the concept of a suppressed feeling of envy and resentment and the metaphysic of the 'slave's' opposition to whom it envies". Ressentiment is a sense of hostility directed toward an object that one identifies as the cause of one's frustration, that is, an assignment of blame for one's frustration.

Kierkegaard

The concept was introduced in the 19th century by the philosopher Søren Kierkegaard. According to Kierkegaard, ressentiment is a life-view (or mindset) that is found in a "reflective, passionless age", in which, as it were, people tear down what is great rather than raise themselves up to it.

Nietzsche

The term was taken from Kierkegaard's works and developed further by Friedrich Nietzsche, who used it extensively in his On the Genealogy of Morality (1887). For Nietzsche, ressentiment is the defining characteristic of slave morality: the weak and oppressed, unable to act against the noble and strong, take imaginary revenge by inverting values — calling the strong "evil" and their own weakness "good". In this way, ressentiment becomes "creative" and gives birth to a whole system of values.

Scheler and later use

Max Scheler developed the concept in his 1913 work Ressentiment, and it has since been applied by sociologists and political theorists, including to the analysis of mass movements and populist politics, where collective resentment of elites or out-groups is seen as a mobilizing force.
