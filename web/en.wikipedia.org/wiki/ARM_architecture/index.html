ARM is a family of reduced instruction set computer (RISC) instruction set architectures developed by Arm Ltd. Unlike most chip companies, Arm does not manufacture processors; it licenses its designs and the architecture itself to hundreds of partners. ARM processors dominate mobile devices — over 99% of smartphones use them — and are ubiquitous in embedded systems, and increasingly in laptops (Apple silicon) and servers (AWS Graviton, Ampere Altra).

History

The first ARM processor was designed at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, England, in 1983–85 by Roger Wilson, Sophie Wilson, and Steve Furber; the ARM1 first worked in April 1985. In 1990 Acorn spun out Advanced RISC Machines (later Arm) as a joint venture with Apple and VLSI Technology to develop the chip for Apple's Newton. The ARM7TDMI became a volume success in mobile phones including the Nokia 6110, and the Cortex family (2004 onward) standardized the A/R/M profiles.

Architecture

ARM is a load–store architecture with a large register file. The 64-bit AArch64 state (introduced with ARMv8-A, 2011) provides thirty-one 64-bit general registers and a clean fixed-length 32-bit instruction encoding, while AArch32 and the Thumb/Thumb-2 compressed encodings remain in embedded profiles. Extensions include NEON SIMD, Scalable Vector Extension (SVE/SVE2), virtualization, and TrustZone security. Modern versions are organized by profiles: Cortex-A for applications processors, Cortex-R for real-time systems, and Cortex-M for microcontrollers.

Licensing and market

Arm's business model combines upfront licence fees with per-chip royalties. Cumulative shipments of ARM-based chips passed 250 billion by the early 2020s, the highest volume of any processor architecture in history. SoftBank acquired Arm in 2016; a proposed sale to Nvidia was abandoned in 2022, and Arm listed on Nasdaq in 2023.
