French Consulate

Napoleon Bonaparte, First Consul of France (1799–1804).

The French Consulate (French: Le Consulat) was the top-level government of France from the fall of the Directory in the coup of 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799) until the commencement of the First French Empire on 18 May 1804. It was headed by three consuls, but real power lay with the First Consul, Napoleon Bonaparte, who progressively consolidated authority and was named Consul for Life in 1802.

Establishment

The Constitution of the Year VIII, drafted largely under the influence of Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès and revised by Bonaparte, created a system with a weak legislature and a strong executive. The three consuls were Bonaparte (First Consul), Jean Jacques Régis de Cambacérès (Second Consul) and Charles-François Lebrun (Third Consul). The constitution was approved by plebiscite, though the official results were manipulated to inflate support.

Reforms

The Consulate carried out lasting reforms of the French state: the creation of the Bank of France (1800) and the franc germinal, the Concordat of 1801 reconciling the state with the Catholic Church, the Lycée system of secondary education, the Legion of Honour (1802), a prefectoral system of centralised local government, and above all the Civil Code of 1804 — the Napoleonic Code — which consolidated revolutionary legal equality and property rights and influenced legal systems worldwide.

Foreign affairs

Bonaparte defeated Austria at Marengo (1800) and signed the Treaty of Lunéville (1801); the Treaty of Amiens (1802) briefly brought peace with Britain before war resumed in 1803. In 1804 the discovery of royalist plots and the execution of the Duc d'Enghien preceded the Senate's proclamation of the Empire, ending the Consulate.
