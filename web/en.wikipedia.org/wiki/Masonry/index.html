Masonry

Masonry
MaterialsBrick, stone, concrete block, mortar, grout
OccupationMason (bricklayer, stonemason)
ApplicationsWalls, buildings, bridges, chimneys, retaining structures

Masonry is the craft of building a structure with brick, stone, or similar material, including mortar laying. These are sometimes referred to as "masonry units", and are often laid in courses and joined together with mortar. A person who constructs masonry is called a mason or bricklayer. The materials used, the quality of the mortar and workmanship, and the pattern in which the units are assembled can substantially affect the durability of the overall masonry construction.

History

Masonry is one of the oldest building crafts: sun-dried and fired bricks were used in the Indus Valley and Mesopotamia, and cut-stone construction reached a peak in Egyptian, Greek and Roman building. Roman concrete (opus caementicium) faced with brick or stone enabled structures such as the Pantheon. Medieval masons raised the Gothic cathedrals of Europe, and masonry remained the principal structural material for large buildings until the advent of steel-frame and reinforced-concrete construction in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Materials

Common masonry units include brick and other clay products, concrete masonry units ("concrete blocks"), natural stone, glass blocks, and terra cotta. Mortar, typically a mixture of cement or lime, sand and water, bonds the units; reinforced masonry incorporates steel bars and grout to resist tensile and lateral loads.

Advantages and limitations

Masonry is fire-resistant, durable, requires little maintenance, and provides thermal mass and sound insulation. Its weaknesses are low tensile strength, vulnerability to lateral loads such as earthquakes when unreinforced, and labour-intensive construction. Modern veneer systems use a single wythe of masonry over a framed backup wall.
