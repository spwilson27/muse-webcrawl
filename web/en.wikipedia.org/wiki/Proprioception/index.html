Proprioception

Proprioceptors in muscle and tendon signal limb position and movement.

Proprioception is the sense by which the body perceives its own position, movement, and internal state in space, without relying on vision. Sometimes called the "sixth sense" or kinaesthesia (strictly, the sense of movement), proprioception allows a person to touch their nose with their eyes closed, to walk without watching their feet, and to judge the force needed to lift an object. The term was introduced by the neurophysiologist Charles Scott Sherrington in 1906, from the Latin proprius ("one's own") and capio ("to take or grasp").

Mechanism

Proprioceptive signals originate in specialised mechanoreceptors called proprioceptors, embedded in skeletal muscles, tendons, and joint capsules:

  • Muscle spindles lie parallel to the main (extrafusal) muscle fibres and detect changes in muscle length and the velocity of stretch.
  • Golgi tendon organs, located at the junction of muscle and tendon, respond to tension and the force of contraction.
  • Joint receptors in capsules and ligaments signal joint angle, particularly near the extremes of movement.
  • Mechanoreceptors in the skin also contribute information about joint position through skin stretch.

These signals travel to the central nervous system along large, fast, heavily myelinated sensory axons. Conscious proprioception ascends in the dorsal columns of the spinal cord to the medulla and thence to the somatosensory cortex; unconscious proprioception, used for fine coordination, travels in the spinocerebellar tracts to the cerebellum. The vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, which senses head acceleration and orientation, is closely related and works together with proprioception and vision to maintain balance.

Functions

Proprioception underlies motor control at every level. It provides the feedback for the stretch reflex, enables the precise grading of muscle force, and allows learned movements — typing, playing an instrument, athletic skills — to run without conscious attention to each joint. When proprioception is degraded, movements become clumsy and must be guided visually, a condition seen in the clinical sign known as sensory ataxia.

Impairment

Damage to peripheral nerves, the dorsal columns (as in tabes dorsalis or vitamin B12 deficiency), or the sensory pathways can abolish proprioception in a limb. Affected individuals may be unable to tell where a limb is unless they look at it. The neurologist Oliver Sacks described a celebrated case, "The Disembodied Lady", in which a woman lost proprioception throughout her body after an illness and had to relearn movement under constant visual control. Proprioceptive deficits also occur after stroke and in joint disease, and targeted proprioceptive training is a standard part of rehabilitation after ligament injuries such as anterior cruciate ligament tears.

Testing

Clinicians test proprioception by moving a patient's finger or toe up or down with the patient's eyes closed and asking which way it moved (joint position sense), and by the Romberg test, in which a patient stands with feet together and eyes closed: excessive sway or falling indicates loss of proprioceptive (or vestibular) input.

In animals and robots

Proprioception is found across the animal kingdom; insects use chordotonal organs and campaniform sensilla, and many animals display remarkable feats of limb coordination without vision. In robotics and prosthetics, engineers replicate the sense with encoders, strain gauges, and inertial sensors, giving machines and artificial limbs feedback about joint angles and loads.
