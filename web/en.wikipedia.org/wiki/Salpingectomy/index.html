Salpingectomy

SpecialtyGynecology
ICD-9-CM66.4–66.6
MeSHD058532

Salpingectomy refers to the surgical removal of a fallopian tube. This may be done to treat a tubal pregnancy, to remove a tube damaged by infection or endometriosis, for permanent contraception, or as a risk-reducing measure against ovarian and fallopian tube cancer. A salpingectomy differs from a salpingostomy (also called salpingotomy), in which an opening is made in the tube and its contents removed without removing the tube itself.

The fallopian tubes extend from the uterus to the ovaries; salpingectomy removes one or both tubes in their entirety.

Types

A unilateral salpingectomy removes one tube, while a bilateral salpingectomy removes both. A partial salpingectomy removes only part of a tube. When a tube is removed together with the adjacent ovary, the combined procedure is called a salpingo-oophorectomy. If performed together with removal of the uterus, it forms part of a total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy.

Indications

  • Ectopic pregnancy — particularly when the tube has ruptured or when future fertility is not a concern.
  • Hydrosalpinx — a fluid-filled, blocked tube, often removed before in vitro fertilization to improve implantation rates.
  • Sterilization — bilateral salpingectomy is increasingly used in place of tubal ligation, as it appears to lower the subsequent risk of ovarian cancer.
  • Cancer risk reduction — women with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations may elect risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy; "opportunistic salpingectomy" at the time of other pelvic surgery has been adopted in some regions.
  • Infection or torsion — severe pelvic inflammatory disease or tubal torsion may necessitate removal.

Procedure

The procedure is most often performed laparoscopically under general anesthesia; an open abdominal approach may be used in emergencies or when combined with other surgery. Operative time is short and most patients recover within one to two weeks. Removal of both tubes results in immediate, permanent sterility, though the ovaries are preserved and hormonal function is unaffected when an oophorectomy is not performed.

History

Salpingectomy was first systematically described in the 1880s, when Lawson Tait and others in Britain pioneered abdominal surgery for ectopic pregnancy, dramatically reducing its mortality. Through the twentieth century the operation became a standard gynecological procedure, and from around 2010 professional societies began endorsing opportunistic salpingectomy for average-risk women undergoing hysterectomy or sterilization, following evidence that many high-grade serous "ovarian" cancers originate in the fimbriated end of the tube.
