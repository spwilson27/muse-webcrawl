Software-defined radio

Software-defined radio (SDR) is a radio communication system where components that have been traditionally implemented in hardware (e.g. mixers, filters, amplifiers, modulators/demodulators, detectors, etc.) are instead implemented by means of software on a personal computer or embedded system. While the concept of SDR is not new, the rapidly evolving capabilities of digital electronics render practical many processes which were once only theoretically possible.

History

The term "software radio" was coined in 1984 by a team at the E-Systems Inc. (now Raytheon) Garland, Texas division. A software radio was described in a paper presented that year, and the concept gained wider attention after Joseph Mitola independently re-coined and popularised the term "software defined radio" in 1992, motivated by military requirements for radios that could interoperate across services and change their behaviour through software.

A major public milestone was the SPEAKeasy programme of the United States military, begun in 1991, which aimed to demonstrate a multiband, multimode radio. By the 2010s, inexpensive USB devices based on television tuner chips — notably the RTL2832U-based "RTL-SDR" dongles — made wideband SDR reception available to hobbyists for a few tens of pounds.

Architecture

Receiver

A basic SDR system may consist of a personal computer equipped with a sound card, or other analog-to-digital converter, preceded by some form of RF front end. Significant amounts of signal processing are handed over to the general-purpose processor, rather than being done in special-purpose hardware (electronic circuits). Such a design produces a radio which can receive and transmit widely different radio protocols (sometimes referred to as waveforms) based solely on the software used.

Transmitter

Software-defined transmitters perform the inverse operations: software generates a digital baseband signal which a digital-to-analog converter and RF upconverter translate to the desired frequency band. Power amplification remains an analog hardware function.

Advantages and limitations

  • Flexibility: new waveforms and protocols can be added by software updates alone.
  • Reconfigurability: a single device can act as receiver, transmitter, spectrum analyser or signal generator.
  • Cost: commodity hardware can serve many purposes.
  • Limitations: instantaneous bandwidth is bounded by converter sample rates; dynamic range and spurious performance often trail dedicated hardware; processing latency can be significant.

Applications

SDR underpins cognitive radio research, dynamic spectrum access, amateur radio experimentation, passive radar, radio astronomy, and signals intelligence. Open-source toolkits such as GNU Radio provide libraries of signal-processing blocks that can be composed graphically or in Python.
