Frank Norris

Benjamin Franklin Norris Jr. (March 5, 1870 – October 25, 1902) was an American journalist and novelist during the Progressive Era, whose fiction was predominantly in the naturalist genre. His notable works include McTeague: A Story of San Francisco (1899), The Octopus: A Story of California (1901), and The Pit (1903).

Life

Norris was born in Chicago and moved with his family to San Francisco in 1884. He studied art in Paris before attending the University of California, Berkeley (1890–1894) and Harvard University. He worked as a journalist in South Africa and Cuba — covering the Spanish–American War — and as an editor in San Francisco and New York.

Works

McTeague tells the story of a San Francisco dentist whose life unravels through greed and violence; it was later adapted by Erich von Stroheim into the famously epic film Greed (1924). The Octopus, the first volume of Norris's planned "Epic of the Wheat" trilogy, depicts the struggle between California wheat farmers and the railroad monopoly, symbolized by the octopus-like grip of the Southern Pacific. The Pit, published posthumously, concerns speculation on the Chicago wheat exchange. The third volume, The Wolf, was never written.

Influenced by Émile Zola, Norris argued in essays such as "A Plea for Romantic Fiction" that the novel should confront the harsh forces of heredity, environment, and economic power that shape human lives.

Death

Norris died in San Francisco in 1902 at age 32, of peritonitis following appendicitis, cutting short one of the most promising careers in American letters.
