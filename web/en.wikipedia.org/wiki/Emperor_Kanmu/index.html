Emperor Kanmu (桓武天皇, 736 – 9 April 806) was the 50th emperor of Japan, according to the traditional order of succession. He reigned from 781 to 806, and it was during his reign that the imperial capital was moved first to Nagaoka-kyō and then, in 794, to Heian-kyō (modern Kyoto), beginning the Heian period of Japanese history.

Early life

Kanmu was born Yamabe, the eldest son of Prince Shirakabe (later Emperor Kōnin) and Takano no Niigasa, a descendant of Baekje royalty. He was not originally expected to succeed, but after his father's accession in 770 he was made crown prince in 773, displacing his half-brother Prince Osabe.

Reign

Moving the capital

Seeking to escape the powerful Buddhist clergy of Nara and renew the imperial government, Kanmu moved the capital to Nagaoka-kyō in 784. After a series of calamities — including the assassination of the official in charge of construction, Fujiwara no Tanetsugu, flooding, and illness blamed on vengeful spirits — he moved again in 794 to Heian-kyō, "the capital of peace and tranquility", which remained Japan's imperial capital for more than a thousand years.

Reforms and campaigns

Kanmu strengthened imperial authority by reforming the bureaucracy, curtailing the power of the Nara Buddhist temples while patronising the new Tendai and Shingon schools of Saichō and Kūkai. He pressed the conquest of the Emishi peoples of northern Honshū, appointing Sakanoue no Tamuramaro as sei-i taishōgun ("barbarian-subduing generalissimo") — the first holder of the title later famous as shōgun. He also reduced the burden of military conscription and sent embassies and students to Tang China.

Legacy

Kanmu is regarded as one of Japan's most energetic early emperors: his transfer of the capital to Kyoto shaped Japanese civilisation for centuries, and his descendants (the Kanmu Heishi and Kanmu Genji lines) supplied many of the great warrior houses of later ages. He died in 806 and was succeeded by his son Emperor Heizei.
