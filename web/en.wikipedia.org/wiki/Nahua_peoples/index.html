The Nahua peoples are a group of Indigenous peoples of Mexico and El Salvador who speak Nahuatl, a language of the Nahuan branch of the Uto-Aztecan family. They are the largest Indigenous linguistic group in Mexico; the 2020 census counted roughly 2.5 million speakers of Nahuatl aged three and over. The Mexica, founders of Tenochtitlan and rulers of the Aztec Empire, were a Nahua people, as were the Tlaxcalteca, the Acolhua of Texcoco and the Pipil of El Salvador.

History

The Nahua are thought to have migrated into central Mexico from the north (the legendary Aztlán) in waves beginning around the 6th–7th centuries CE, spreading through the Valley of Mexico and beyond during the Postclassic period (c. 900–1521). Their city-states (altepetl) — Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan, Tlaxcala, Cholula — dominated highland Mesoamerica until the Spanish conquest of 1519–1521, in which Nahua allies of Tlaxcala were indispensable. In the colonial period Nahuatl served as a lingua franca of New Spain and was written extensively in Latin script, leaving a vast literature of annals, testaments and land documents studied by the "New Philology" school of ethnohistory.

Contemporary life

Today Nahua communities are concentrated in the Sierra Norte de Puebla, the Huasteca, the Balsas river region of Guerrero, Morelos, Tlaxcala, the State of Mexico, and parts of Veracruz, Durango and Michoacán. Many communities maintain cargo systems of rotating civil-religious office, milpa agriculture, and syncretic festivals centred on patron saints, alongside high rates of labour migration. Nahuatl remains Mexico's most widely spoken Indigenous language, though most of its some 30 recognised variants are endangered by Spanish dominance in schooling and media; revitalisation efforts include bilingual education and Nahuatl-language media.
