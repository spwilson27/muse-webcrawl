The United States Military Academy (USMA), commonly known as West Point, is a four-year federal service academy in West Point, New York. Established in 1802 under President Thomas Jefferson, it is the oldest of the five American service academies and educates cadets for commissioning as officers in the United States Army.

History

The site, a fortified position on the Hudson River during the Revolutionary War (and the objective of Benedict Arnold's attempted betrayal), became a military post in 1778. Under Superintendent Sylvanus Thayer (1817–1833), the "Father of the Military Academy", West Point developed a rigorous engineering curriculum and the honour system that still define it. Its graduates — including Ulysses S. Grant, Robert E. Lee, Douglas MacArthur and Dwight D. Eisenhower — have shaped American military history. Women were first admitted in 1976.

Academics and cadet life

Candidates require a nomination, usually from a member of Congress. Cadets pay no tuition but incur an active-duty service obligation after graduation, receiving a Bachelor of Science degree and a commission as second lieutenants. The curriculum combines liberal arts and sciences with professional military training and athletics; every cadet participates in competitive sport. Cadet life is governed by the Cadet Honor Code: "A cadet will not lie, cheat, steal, or tolerate those who do."

Campus

The academy occupies about 16,000 acres on the west bank of the Hudson, some 50 miles north of New York City. Its Gothic Revival buildings, monuments and cemetery make it a National Historic Landmark and a popular visitor destination. Its sports teams, the Black Knights, are famous above all for the annual Army–Navy football game.
