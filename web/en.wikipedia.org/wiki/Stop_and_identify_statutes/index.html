Stop and identify statutes

"Stop and identify" statutes are laws in a number of U.S. states that authorise police officers to detain a person briefly and require the person to identify themselves where the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime. Failure to identify oneself can itself be an arrestable offence in these jurisdictions.

Constitutional background

The framework derives from Terry v. Ohio (1968), in which the U.S. Supreme Court upheld brief investigative stops ("Terry stops") on reasonable suspicion short of probable cause. In Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court of Nevada (2004), the Court upheld Nevada's stop-and-identify statute against Fourth and Fifth Amendment challenges, holding that requiring a suspect to give his name during a lawful Terry stop did not violate the Constitution; the defendant, Larry Hiibel, had been arrested after refusing eleven times to identify himself. By contrast, in Brown v. Texas (1979) the Court struck down a statute that criminalised refusing to identify oneself to an officer who had no reasonable suspicion.

Operation

Roughly half of U.S. states have some form of stop-and-identify law, though they differ in what identification requires — most demand only the person's name (and sometimes date of birth or address), not the production of identity documents. Independent of these statutes, nearly all states require drivers stopped while operating a vehicle to present a licence. "Stop and frisk" practices, as exercised in New York City and elsewhere, have been litigated separately and remain controversial in debates over policing and civil rights.
