Aortic arches

The aortic arches, or pharyngeal arch arteries (formerly referred to as branchial arches in human embryos), are a series of six paired embryological vascular structures which give rise to several major arteries of the head, neck, and thorax. They develop within the pharyngeal arches of the embryo and connect the aortic sac of the primitive heart tube with the paired dorsal aortae. Although six arches are numbered I–VI, they do not all exist simultaneously, and the fifth is rudimentary or absent in humans.

Development

The arches form sequentially from cranial to caudal during the fourth and fifth weeks of human development. Each pharyngeal arch receives its own artery from the aortic sac. As new arches form caudally, earlier ones remodel or regress, so the final pattern of the great arteries is produced by selective persistence and disappearance of segments, accompanied by the caudal descent of the heart into the thorax, which lengthens the neck vessels and drags the recurrent laryngeal nerves into their characteristic loops.

Derivatives

  • First arch — largely disappears; a remnant contributes to the maxillary artery.
  • Second arch — mostly regresses; remnants form the hyoid and stapedial arteries.
  • Third arch — forms the common carotid artery and the proximal part of the internal carotid artery.
  • Fourth arch — asymmetric: on the left it forms the part of the aortic arch between the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries; on the right it forms the proximal right subclavian artery.
  • Fifth arch — rudimentary and transient; no significant adult derivatives.
  • Sixth arch — forms the proximal pulmonary arteries; on the left, its distal part persists as the ductus arteriosus, which after birth becomes the ligamentum arteriosum. The recurrent laryngeal nerves hook around the sixth-arch derivatives, explaining the left nerve's loop under the aortic arch.

Clinical significance

Abnormal persistence or regression of arch segments produces congenital anomalies of the great vessels, including a right-sided aortic arch, double aortic arch (forming a vascular ring that can compress the trachea and oesophagus), aberrant right subclavian artery, coarctation of the aorta near the ductus, and patent ductus arteriosus. These conditions are often diagnosed by echocardiography or cross-sectional imaging in infancy.

In other animals

In fish, the arches supply the gills and persist in modified form; comparative anatomy of the aortic arches is a classic example used in evolutionary biology, as the same basic pattern is remodelled differently in amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.
