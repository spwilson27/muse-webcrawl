Unequal treaty

Unequal treaties
Term originChinese: 不平等條約 (bùpíngděng tiáoyuē); popularised in the 1920s
PeriodMainly 1842–1943 (China); also Japan, Korea, Siam, Persia, Ottoman Empire
First (China)Treaty of Nanking, 1842
Typical featuresTreaty ports, extraterritoriality, fixed tariffs, territorial cessions/leases, indemnities

The term unequal treaty refers to a class of treaties imposed, largely in the 19th and early 20th centuries, by Western powers (and later Japan) on weaker states — above all Qing-dynasty China — under military pressure and on heavily one-sided terms. Such treaties typically granted the foreign power privileges without reciprocal concessions: the opening of treaty ports, extraterritoriality for its nationals, control over tariffs, and cessions or leases of territory.

China

The system began with the Treaty of Nanking (1842), ending the First Opium War: China ceded Hong Kong Island, opened five ports, paid a large indemnity and lost tariff autonomy. The Treaties of Tientsin (1858) and the Convention of Peking (1860) after the Second Opium War, the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895) with Japan, and the Boxer Protocol (1901) extended the system — more than a hundred ports were eventually opened, foreign concessions and settlements were established in cities such as Shanghai, and Russia, Germany, France and Britain took leased territories including Jiaozhou, Weihaiwei and the New Territories of Hong Kong. Chinese nationalists later framed 1839–1949 as the "century of humiliation", and the treaties were progressively abolished: Britain and the United States renounced extraterritoriality in 1943, and Hong Kong was returned in 1997.

Elsewhere

Japan itself was subjected to similar "Ansei Treaties" from 1858 (following Commodore Perry's opening of Japan), which it renegotiated by 1911 after modernising; Japan then imposed its own unequal treaties on Korea, including the Treaty of Ganghwa (1876). Siam, Persia and the Ottoman Empire (with its capitulations) also experienced comparable regimes. In modern international law the concept informs, but is distinct from, rules on treaties procured by coercion of a state, codified in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.
