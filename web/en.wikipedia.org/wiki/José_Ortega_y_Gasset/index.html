José Ortega y Gasset

José Ortega y Gasset (9 May 1883 – 18 October 1955) was a Spanish philosopher and essayist, the leading figure of the Generation of 1914 and the most influential Spanish thinker of the twentieth century. His best-known work, The Revolt of the Masses (1929), analysed the crisis of European liberal democracy in the age of mass society.

Life

Ortega was born in Madrid into a family prominent in journalism and politics; his grandfather founded the newspaper El Imparcial. He studied philosophy at the University of Madrid and in Germany — at Leipzig, Berlin, and Marburg — where he absorbed the neo-Kantianism of Hermann Cohen and Paul Natorp before turning toward phenomenology. In 1910 he became professor of metaphysics at the University of Madrid, a chair he held until the Spanish Civil War.

Ortega founded the journal Revista de Occidente (1923), which introduced Spanish readers to contemporary European thought, and was active in politics, serving briefly as a deputy in the Constituent Cortes of the Second Republic before becoming disillusioned. He spent the Civil War and much of the following decade in exile in Argentina and Europe, returning to Madrid in 1945, where he founded the Instituto de Humanidades with Julián Marías. He died in Madrid in 1955.

Philosophy

Ortega's thought centres on the maxim "I am I and my circumstance" (yo soy yo y mi circunstancia), first stated in Meditations on Quixote (1914): human life is not a thing but a project, enacted within a circumstance it did not choose. He developed this into "ratiovitalism", a philosophy holding life itself to be the radical reality, of which reason is one instrument. His doctrine of perspectivism holds that each life is an irreplaceable point of view on the universe, and that truth is the integration of perspectives.

In The Revolt of the Masses he described the "mass man" — satisfied, self-complacent, and ungrateful toward the civilisation that made his comfort possible — as the dominant type of the modern age. Other major works include The Dehumanization of Art (1925), Man and Crisis (1933), and the posthumous lectures on Leibniz and on the idea of principle.
