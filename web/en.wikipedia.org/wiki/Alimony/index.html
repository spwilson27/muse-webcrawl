Alimony

[Image: A family court hearing in which post-divorce maintenance is being determined]

Alimony, also called maintenance or spousal support, is a legal obligation on a person to provide financial support to their spouse before or after marital separation or divorce. It originates in the common law duty of a husband to support his wife, and in England was traditionally awarded by the ecclesiastical courts in separation proceedings. Modern alimony is gender-neutral in most jurisdictions and is distinct from child support and from the division of marital property.

Types

Courts distinguish several forms: temporary support during proceedings; rehabilitative support for a fixed period while the recipient gains education or employment; reimbursement support compensating a spouse who financed the other's training or career; and permanent (indefinite) support, historically awarded after long marriages. Payments may be periodic or a lump sum, and typically end on the recipient's remarriage or the death of either party; many orders can be varied if circumstances change substantially.

Factors and calculation

Statutes direct courts to consider the length of the marriage, each spouse's income, earning capacity, age and health, contributions to the marriage (including homemaking and childcare), the marital standard of living, and sometimes marital fault. Some US states use guidelines or formulas; others leave wide judicial discretion, which reformers criticise as unpredictable. England and Wales approach the question through the principles of needs, compensation and sharing developed in cases such as White v White and Miller v Miller, with a judicial trend towards independence and time-limited orders; Scots law provides for a shorter, adjustment-focused period of support.

Reform and debate

Twentieth-century no-fault divorce and women's increased labour-force participation transformed alimony from a near-automatic lifetime entitlement into a more transitional remedy. Several US states have abolished or limited permanent alimony by statute in recent decades, changes supported by payer-advocacy groups and criticised by those who argue that homemakers and carers remain economically vulnerable after long marriages. Tax treatment has also shifted: in the United States, for divorces finalised after 2018, alimony is no longer deductible by the payer nor taxable to the recipient.
