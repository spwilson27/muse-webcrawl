Peptide hormone


Class: Hormone
Examples: Insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, oxytocin

Peptide hormones are a class of hormones whose molecules are peptides—short chains of amino acids—synthesised by endocrine cells and secreted into the bloodstream to act on distant target tissues. They form one of the major chemical classes of hormones, alongside steroid hormones and amino-acid derivatives such as the catecholamines.

Synthesis and secretion

Peptide hormones are made as larger precursor proteins (preprohormones) on ribosomes, processed through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and cleaved into their active form before being stored in secretory vesicles. Release occurs by exocytosis in response to a stimulus. Because they are hydrophilic, peptide hormones circulate freely in plasma but cannot cross cell membranes.

Mechanism of action

Peptide hormones bind to receptors on the surface of target cells, typically G protein–coupled receptors or receptor tyrosine kinases, activating intracellular second-messenger systems such as cyclic AMP or the phosphoinositide pathway. Their effects are generally rapid in onset but short-lived, as circulating peptidases degrade them within minutes.

Examples

Important peptide hormones include insulin and glucagon from the pancreas; growth hormone, prolactin, and the pituitary releasing hormones; oxytocin and vasopressin; gastrointestinal hormones such as secretin and GLP-1—whose receptor agonists have become major diabetes and obesity drugs; and adipose and appetite hormones such as leptin and ghrelin.
