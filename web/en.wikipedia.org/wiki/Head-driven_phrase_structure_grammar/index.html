Head-driven phrase structure grammar

Head-driven phrase structure grammar (HPSG) is a highly lexicalized, constraint-based grammar theory developed by Carl Pollard and Ivan Sag in the 1980s and 1990s. It is a type of phrase structure grammar, as opposed to a dependency grammar, and it is the immediate successor of generalized phrase structure grammar (GPSG). HPSG draws on ideas from several fields, including computer science (data types and unification), philosophy (situations) and other grammatical frameworks.

Formalism

HPSG encodes linguistic information in typed feature structures (attribute–value matrices) called signs, which bundle phonological, syntactic and semantic information. Signs are organised in a multiple-inheritance type hierarchy, and grammatical knowledge is expressed as constraints — principles and grammar rules — that these structures must satisfy. Phrase structure schemata license combinations of a head daughter with its complements, adjuncts and specifiers, governed by principles such as the Head Feature Principle, the Valence Principle and the Semantics Principle.

Core components include:

  • a richly structured lexicon, where words carry most grammatical information;
  • lexical rules and inheritance hierarchies capturing related forms;
  • a model-theoretic semantics, originally situation semantics, later commonly implemented with Minimal Recursion Semantics (MRS);
  • binding and long-distance dependency mechanisms based on feature passing (e.g. SLASH features) rather than movement.

Implementations

HPSG has been implemented in several computational systems, including the Linguistic Knowledge Building (LKB) system and the PET parser, and large precision grammars exist for English (the English Resource Grammar), German, Japanese and other languages, many developed in the DELPH-IN consortium. These grammars are used in machine translation, question answering and grammar engineering.
