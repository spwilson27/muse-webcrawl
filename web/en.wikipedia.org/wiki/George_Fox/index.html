George Fox

George Fox (July 1624 – 13 January 1691) was an English Dissenter, founder of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers).

Born: Drayton-in-the-Clay, Leicestershire
Died: London
Known for: Founding the Quaker movement

George Fox was an English religious leader and the principal founder of the Religious Society of Friends, commonly known as the Quakers. A radical preacher during the English Civil War era, he taught that every person could experience the "Inner Light" of Christ directly, without clergy or consecrated buildings.

Early life

Fox was born in Drayton-in-the-Clay (now Fenny Drayton), Leicestershire, the son of a weaver. Apprenticed to a shoemaker, he became deeply religious and, dissatisfied with the answers of clergy, left home in 1643 to wander in search of spiritual truth. In 1647 he began preaching that God does not dwell in temples made with hands but in the hearts of obedient people.

Ministry

Fox travelled throughout England preaching, gathering followers who called themselves Friends. He refused to take oaths, pay tithes, doff his hat to social superiors, or serve in the militia, and he was repeatedly imprisoned for blasphemy and disturbing the peace. In 1650, Justice Gervase Bennet coined the name "Quaker" after Fox bade him "tremble at the word of the Lord". A pivotal moment came in 1652 on Pendle Hill and at Firbank Fell in the northwest, where Fox preached to a thousand people and the movement gained its stronghold.

Later years

In 1669 Fox married Margaret Fell, the widowed matriarch of Swarthmoor Hall, a centre of the movement. He travelled to Barbados, Jamaica, and the American colonies in 1671–73, and helped organise the Friends' system of monthly and yearly meetings. Despite persecution under the Restoration, the movement survived; toleration came with the Toleration Act of 1689, two years before Fox's death in London in 1691.

Teachings

Fox's Journal, published posthumously in 1694, records his spiritual journey. His teachings — the Inner Light, the rejection of outward sacraments and a paid ministry, and plain speech and dress — shaped Quaker practice, including later testimonies of peace, equality, integrity, and simplicity.
