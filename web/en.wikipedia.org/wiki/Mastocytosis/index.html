Mastocytosis is a rare disorder characterized by an abnormal accumulation of mast cells in one or more organs. It occurs when mast cells — immune cells involved in allergic responses — proliferate clonally, most often because of an activating mutation in the KIT gene (usually D816V). Mastocytosis is broadly divided into cutaneous mastocytosis, confined to the skin and most common in children, and systemic mastocytosis, in which mast cells infiltrate extracutaneous organs, especially the bone marrow, and which occurs mainly in adults.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms arise both from tissue infiltration and from the release of mast-cell mediators such as histamine and tryptase. They include itching, flushing, hives, abdominal cramping and diarrhoea, bone pain, fatigue, and episodes of anaphylaxis triggered by heat, friction, alcohol, certain drugs, or insect stings. Skin lesions of cutaneous disease are reddish-brown spots or plaques; stroking them produces a wheal, known as Darier's sign.

Classification

The World Health Organization classification includes cutaneous forms (maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis/urticaria pigmentosa, diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis, mastocytoma) and systemic forms ranging from indolent systemic mastocytosis, the most common adult type with a near-normal life expectancy, through smouldering and aggressive systemic mastocytosis, to mast cell leukaemia, the rarest and most severe variant.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on biopsy demonstrating dense mast-cell infiltrates, aberrant CD25 expression on mast cells, an elevated serum tryptase, and detection of the KIT D816V mutation. There is no cure; management avoids triggers and uses antihistamines, mast-cell stabilizers such as cromolyn sodium, and epinephrine auto-injectors for anaphylaxis. Advanced disease may be treated with KIT inhibitors such as midostaurin and avapritinib.
