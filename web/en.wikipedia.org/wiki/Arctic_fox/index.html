The Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus), also known as the white fox, polar fox, or snow fox, is a small fox native to the Arctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere and distributed throughout the Arctic tundra biome. It is well adapted to living in cold environments and is best known for its thick, warm fur, which also serves as camouflage.

Description

The Arctic fox averages 3–3.5 kg in weight and 46–68 cm in body length, with a bushy tail of about 30 cm. It exists in two colour morphs: the white morph, which turns pure white in winter and brown-grey in summer, and the "blue" morph, which remains dark brown or bluish-grey year-round. Its short muzzle, small rounded ears, and furred soles (reflected in the species name lagopus, "hare-foot") reduce heat loss; it can withstand temperature differences of up to 100 °C without shivering.

Diet and behaviour

The fox preys mainly on lemmings and other rodents, and also takes birds, eggs, hares, carrion, and seal pups; coastal animals feed on shellfish, sea urchins, and beached marine animals. It caches surplus food in summer. Arctic foxes are monogamous and breed in spring, producing litters of up to 11 kits — among the largest litter sizes of any mammal — in complex underground dens that may be used for centuries.

Distribution and status

The species is circumpolar, occurring in Alaska, Canada, Greenland, Iceland, Svalbard, Scandinavia, and Russia. Populations fluctuate dramatically with the lemming cycle. The global population is large, but the Scandinavian mainland population was driven near extinction by fur hunting in the early 20th century and remains critically endangered despite protection and reintroduction programmes.
