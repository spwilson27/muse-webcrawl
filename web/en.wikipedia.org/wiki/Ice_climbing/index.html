Ice climbing

Ice climbing is the activity of ascending inclined ice formations, such as frozen waterfalls, icefalls, and cliffs or rock slabs covered with ice refrozen from flows of water. Climbers use specialized equipment — principally two ice tools (technical ice axes) and crampons fitted with front points — to kick and swing their way up the ice. The sport is generally divided into alpine ice climbing, undertaken as part of mountaineering, and water ice climbing on frozen waterfalls.

Techniques and equipment

Modern technique involves kicking the front points of crampons into the ice and swinging curved ice tools overhead to gain purchase, moving in a methodical rhythm while placing ice screws for protection when leading. Ice quality varies greatly with temperature and structure: hard, brittle "dinner-plating" ice, soft plastic ice, chandelier formations, and thin verglas each demand different treatment. Helmets protect against falling ice dislodged by the climber or by parties above.

Grading

Water ice climbs are graded on the WI scale (WI1 to about WI7), reflecting steepness, technical difficulty, and length, while mixed climbs combining rock and ice are graded M1 to M13 and beyond. Commitment grades may reflect the seriousness and remoteness of the route.

Competition

Competitive ice climbing, governed by the UIAA, is held on artificial structures combining ice and wooden features, with lead (difficulty) and speed disciplines. The sport grew out of alpine mountaineering in the 1970s, when climbers began seeking out frozen waterfalls in places such as the Canadian Rockies, Colorado, and New Hampshire purely for their own sake.
