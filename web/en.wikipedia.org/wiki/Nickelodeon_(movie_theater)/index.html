Nickelodeon (movie theater)

A typical storefront nickelodeon, c. 1910.

The nickelodeon was the first type of indoor exhibition space dedicated to showing motion pictures in the United States. Flourishing between roughly 1905 and 1915, nickelodeons were small storefront theatres that charged five cents — a nickel — for admission; the name combines "nickel" with the Greek odeon, a roofed theatre. The first true nickelodeon is generally credited to the theatre opened by Harry Davis and John P. Harris in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, in June 1905.

History

Before the nickelodeon, films were shown as novelty acts in vaudeville houses, churches and travelling shows. The cheap storefront format proved explosively popular with working-class and immigrant audiences: by 1908 there were an estimated 8,000 to 10,000 nickelodeons across the United States, and weekly attendance reached tens of millions by 1910. Programmes ran continuously, typically consisting of several one-reel films of about ten to fifteen minutes each, interspersed with illustrated songs.

Decline

As feature-length films such as The Birth of a Nation (1915) demanded longer programmes and higher prices, audiences were drawn to larger, more comfortable picture palaces, and the storefront nickelodeon declined. The name survived in later uses, including the children's television channel Nickelodeon, launched in 1979, which took its name from these early theatres.
