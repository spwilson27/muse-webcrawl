Aquae Sulis (Latin for "the waters of Sulis") was a Roman town in the province of Britannia, on the site of the modern city of Bath, Somerset. It grew around Britain's only natural hot springs, which the Romans developed from about the AD 60s into a major bathing and temple complex dedicated to the goddess Sulis, whom they identified with Minerva.

The sanctuary and baths

The complex included a classical temple — one of only two known in Roman Britain — whose pediment bore a striking Gorgon's head, and a sequence of baths fed by the Sacred Spring, which still yields over a million litres of water a day at about 46 °C. Worshippers threw offerings into the spring, including over 12,000 coins and more than 130 lead "curse tablets" (defixiones) inscribed with prayers for justice, many written in a cursive Latin that preserves everyday grievances such as theft of clothing at the baths.

Later history

The town was walled and remained a regional centre, but the baths fell into ruin after Roman rule ended. The King's Bath was rebuilt in the medieval period, and the Roman remains were progressively rediscovered from the 18th century as Georgian Bath became a fashionable spa. The site is now the Roman Baths museum, and Bath is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
