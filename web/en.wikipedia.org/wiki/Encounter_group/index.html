Encounter group

An encounter group is a form of group psychotherapy and personal-growth practice in which participants are encouraged to express feelings openly and honestly in the here and now, giving and receiving direct interpersonal feedback. The method grew out of the T-groups (training groups) developed at the National Training Laboratories in the 1940s and was popularised in the 1960s by humanistic psychologists such as Carl Rogers.

Practice

A typical encounter group has eight to twelve members and a facilitator, and may run as a single intensive "marathon" session lasting many hours or as a series of meetings. Ground rules encourage spontaneity, self-disclosure, and expressing reactions to other members as they occur. Rogers described basic encounter groups as among the most significant social inventions of the twentieth century and documented their use in education, business, and conflict resolution.

Criticism

Research in the early 1970s, notably by Irvin Yalom, found that while many participants benefited, a minority — particularly in groups led by aggressive, confrontational leaders — suffered psychological harm, prompting greater emphasis on screening and facilitator training.
