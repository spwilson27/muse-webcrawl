Prosper Mérimée

Born28 September 1803, Paris, France
Died23 September 1870 (aged 66), Cannes, France
OccupationWriter, historian, archaeologist
NationalityFrench
Notable worksCarmen, La Vénus d'Ille, Colomba, Mateo Falcone

Prosper Mérimée (28 September 1803 – 23 September 1870) was a French writer in the movement of Romanticism, one of the pioneers of the novella, a short-story form of literature. His novella Carmen became famous as the basis of Georges Bizet's opera of the same name. He was also an archaeologist and historian, serving as an inspector-general of French historical monuments.

Life

Mérimée was born in Paris in 1803, the son of Léonor Mérimée, a painter and professor of design, and Anne Moreau, also a painter. He studied law briefly before turning to literature, moving in the Parisian salons of the 1820s and befriending writers such as Stendhal and George Sand, with whom he corresponded extensively.

Under the July Monarchy he entered the civil service, becoming chief of the ministerial cabinet and, in 1834, inspector-general of historical monuments. In that role he travelled throughout France cataloguing and protecting medieval architecture, helping to save monuments such as the abbey church of Vézelay. He was elected to the Académie française in 1844 and became a senator of the Second Empire in 1853, close to Napoleon III and the Empress Eugénie.

Literary works

Mérimée's early writings were playful literary hoaxes: the Théâtre de Clara Gazul (1825), plays presented as the work of a Spanish actress, and La Guzla (1827), a collection of supposed Illyrian folk ballads that fooled several readers, including Alexander Pushkin. He then mastered the novella: Mateo Falcone (1829), La Vénus d'Ille (1837), Colomba (1840) and Carmen (1845) are noted for their economy, irony and detached, almost clinical narration of violent passions.

Death and legacy

Mérimée died at Cannes on 23 September 1870, during the Franco-Prussian War. His base Mérimée, a database of French architectural heritage maintained by the Ministry of Culture, is named after him.
