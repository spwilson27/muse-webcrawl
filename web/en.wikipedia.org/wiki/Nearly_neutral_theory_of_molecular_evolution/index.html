Nearly neutral theory of molecular evolution

Fixation probability depends on the product of effective population size and selection coefficient.

The nearly neutral theory of molecular evolution is a modification of the neutral theory of molecular evolution that holds that the majority of evolutionary changes at the molecular level are caused by random drift of mutant alleles that are not completely neutral, but only nearly neutral — mutations whose selective effects are small enough that their fate is governed chiefly by drift rather than by natural selection.

History

The theory was proposed by Japanese geneticist Tomoko Ohta in 1973, in a short paper titled "Slightly deleterious mutant substitutions in evolution". Ohta developed the theory further with Motoo Kimura, and it was summarized in their joint work on the neutral and nearly neutral theories. The theory arose partly from the observation that protein evolution appeared slower in species with large populations than the strictly neutral theory predicted.

Principles

Under the neutral theory, the rate of molecular evolution equals the neutral mutation rate and is independent of population size. Under the nearly neutral theory, mutations with small selection coefficients behave effectively neutrally when the product of effective population size (Ne) and the selection coefficient (s) is small (roughly |Nes| < 1), but are efficiently removed by selection in large populations. Consequently, species with smaller effective population sizes should accumulate slightly deleterious substitutions faster and evolve more rapidly at the molecular level.

The theory predicts relationships between population size, generation time, and rates of evolution, and helps explain why the molecular clock ticks at different rates in different lineages. It has been invoked to explain, among other phenomena, the generation-time effect, the faster evolution of endosymbiont and parasite genomes (subject to strong drift), and variation in the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) between lineages.

Debates

Distinguishing nearly neutral evolution from strictly neutral evolution or from adaptive evolution is empirically difficult. Tests such as the McDonald–Kreitman test and analyses of polymorphism-versus-divergence data have been used to estimate the fraction of mutations that are slightly deleterious. The nearly neutral theory remains influential in population genetics and comparative genomics, where the efficacy of selection is routinely modeled as a function of effective population size.
