Sunset provision

In public policy, a sunset provision or sunset clause is a measure within a statute, regulation or other law that provides for the law to cease to have effect after a specific date, unless further legislative action is taken to extend it. Unlike most laws, which remain in force indefinitely, a law with a sunset provision has a predetermined expiration date. The device is intended to force legislatures to review policies periodically, to accommodate temporary emergencies, or to secure political compromise.

Rationale

Sunset provisions serve several purposes. They allow legislatures to experiment with policies on a trial basis, to respond to emergencies without permanently enlarging government powers, and to compel periodic re-examination of programmes that might otherwise continue through inertia. They are also used as bargaining tools: supporters of a controversial measure may accept a sunset to secure passage, while opponents take comfort that the measure will lapse unless re-approved. In budget legislation, sunset dates are sometimes used to limit the projected long-term cost of tax cuts under scoring rules.

Examples

United States

  • The Federal Assault Weapons Ban of 1994 contained a ten-year sunset and expired in 2004.
  • Several surveillance provisions of the USA PATRIOT Act (2001) were enacted with sunset dates and were repeatedly reauthorised, modified or allowed to lapse in subsequent years.
  • Many individual income-tax provisions of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 were scheduled to expire after 2025, largely to comply with Senate budget reconciliation rules.
  • The Texas Sunset Act subjects most state agencies to periodic review by the Sunset Advisory Commission and abolishes them unless the legislature affirmatively continues them.
  • The early Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 carried expiration dates, and the Sedition Act expired by its own terms in 1801.

Elsewhere

Sunset clauses are common in emergency and anti-terrorism legislation in countries such as the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, where extraordinary powers are frequently authorised for fixed periods. Germany's practice of time-limiting emergency statutes and the United Kingdom's Coronavirus Act 2020, which included periodic parliamentary review and expiry provisions, are recent examples.

Criticism

Critics argue that sunset clauses can create uncertainty for administrators and regulated parties, encourage brinkmanship as deadlines approach, and produce "temporary" policies that are repeatedly extended without serious review. In budget contexts, sunsets may also understate the true long-run cost of legislation.
