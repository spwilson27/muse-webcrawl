Horsepower


One horsepower raising 550 pounds by one foot in one second

Horsepower (hp) is a unit of measurement of power, or the rate at which work is done, most commonly in reference to the output of engines or motors. The term was adopted in the late 18th century by Scottish engineer James Watt to compare the output of steam engines with the power of draft horses. It was later extended to include the output of other types of piston engines, electric motors, turbines, and other machinery.

Definitions

Several definitions exist. The mechanical (or imperial) horsepower is defined as 550 foot-pounds per second, approximately 745.7 watts. The metric horsepower (PS, from German Pferdestärke) is defined as 75 kilogram-force metres per second, about 735.5 watts, and is common in European and Japanese automotive ratings. Electrical horsepower is defined as exactly 746 watts in the United States. "Brake horsepower" (bhp) is measured at the engine's output shaft with a dynamometer, whereas older SAE gross ratings and modern net ratings differ in the accessories fitted during the test.

History

Watt estimated that a brewery horse could raise a 330-pound load 100 feet in one minute, and increased this figure by 50 per cent to obtain a conservative rating for his engines of 33,000 ft·lbf/min, which became the standard horsepower. The unit was central to marketing steam engines and, later, automobiles; although the watt is the SI unit of power, horsepower remains widely used in the automotive industry and in North American equipment ratings.
