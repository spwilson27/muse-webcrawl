The deposit of faith (Latin: depositum fidei) is a theological term used in Roman Catholic theology for the body of revealed truth entrusted by Christ and the apostles to the Church. It comprises Sacred Scripture and Sacred Tradition, and is held to have been completed with the death of the last apostle: public revelation ended in the apostolic age, so the deposit does not grow, though the Church's understanding of it develops over time.

Scriptural basis and history

The image derives from passages such as 1 Timothy 6:20 ("guard the deposit entrusted to you") and Jude 3 ("the faith once for all delivered to the saints"). The Council of Trent (1545–1563) taught that the Gospel is preserved in written books and unwritten traditions, both to be received with equal reverence. The Second Vatican Council's constitution Dei Verbum (1965) describes Scripture and Tradition as forming "one sacred deposit of the word of God, committed to the Church", whose authentic interpretation is entrusted to the Magisterium.

Development of doctrine

While the deposit itself is complete, Catholic theology — notably in John Henry Newman's Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine (1845) — holds that doctrines can be made explicit over centuries, as with the definitions of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950), understood as unfoldings of what was always contained in the deposit.
