Drug liberalization

Drug liberalization is a drug policy process of decriminalizing or legalizing the use or sale of prohibited drugs. Variations of drug liberalization include drug legalization, drug re-legalization and drug decriminalization. Proponents argue that prohibition drives crime, corruption and unsafe consumption, while critics argue that liberalization increases use and harms public health.

Policies

Drug liberalization policies take several forms. Decriminalization removes criminal penalties for personal possession, often replacing them with fines or treatment referrals, as Portugal did in 2001. Legalization permits a regulated market in production and sale, as several US states and countries such as Canada (2018) and Uruguay (2013) have done for cannabis. Medical legalization permits prescribed use while keeping recreational use prohibited.

Arguments in support

Supporters argue that prohibition creates a lucrative black market controlled by organized crime, that enforcement is costly and racially unequal, that criminal records harm users more than the drugs themselves, and that regulation allows quality control, taxation and honest health information. Organizations such as the Global Commission on Drug Policy have called the war on drugs a failure.

Arguments against

Opponents argue that legal availability increases consumption and addiction, that commercial industries would market drugs aggressively as tobacco and alcohol companies have done, and that liberalization sends a harmful social signal, particularly to young people.

History

Calls for reform grew in the late 20th century. The Netherlands adopted a policy of tolerance (gedoogbeleid) toward cannabis sales in coffee shops from the 1970s. In the 2010s and 2020s, cannabis legalization spread across North America and parts of Europe, and cities and states experimented with decriminalizing small amounts of other substances, notably Oregon's Measure 110 (2020, partially reversed in 2024).
