The Conflict of the Orders, also referred to as the Struggle of the Orders, was a political struggle between the plebeians (commoners) and patricians (aristocrats) of the ancient Roman Republic lasting from 494 BC to 287 BC, in which the plebeians sought political equality with the patricians. It played a major role in the development of the Constitution of the Roman Republic.

Background

At the founding of the Republic in 509 BC, the patricians monopolised the magistracies, the Senate and the priesthoods, while the plebeians — the great majority of citizens — bore the burdens of military service and debt, including the harsh bondage of nexum. Plebeian grievances were sharpened by the economic distress that followed Rome's early wars.

Course of the struggle

Shortly after the founding of the Republic, the plebs engaged in a form of general strike known as the secessio plebis, withdrawing from the city to the Mons Sacer in 494 BC; the patricians conceded the creation of the office of tribune of the plebs, sacrosanct officials who could veto acts harmful to plebeians. Further milestones followed:

  • 449 BC — The Law of the Twelve Tables published Rome's laws for the first time, limiting patrician judicial discretion; the Valerio-Horatian laws restored the right of appeal.
  • 445 BC — The Lex Canuleia permitted intermarriage between patricians and plebeians.
  • 367 BC — The Licinian-Sextian laws opened the consulship to plebeians and limited occupation of public land.
  • 342 BC — The Lex Genucia forbade interest on loans and required one consul to be plebeian.
  • 300 BC — The Lex Ogulnia opened the major priestly colleges to plebeians.
  • 287 BC — After a final secession over debt, the Lex Hortensia made plebiscites (resolutions of the Plebeian Council) binding on all citizens, ending the conflict.

Outcome

By 287 BC the plebeians had achieved formal political equality: every magistracy and priesthood was open to them, and their assembly could legislate for the whole people. In practice political power passed to a new mixed patricio-plebeian aristocracy, the nobiles, and wealthy plebeian families merged with the old patriciate. Modern historians accept the broad outline in the literary sources (chiefly Livy) while doubting many details of the early episodes.
