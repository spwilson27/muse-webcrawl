Razor and blades model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The razor and blades model is a business model in which one item is sold at a low price (or given away) in order to increase sales of a complementary good. The classic example is the safety razor: the handle is sold cheaply — the "razor" — while profits are made on the recurring sale of disposable "blades" that fit only that handle. The model is a form of product tying and is also known as the "bait and hook" model.

History

The model is inseparably associated with King Camp Gillette, founder of the Gillette safety razor company, whose 1904 patents on disposable blades made repeat purchases inevitable once a consumer owned the handle. Contrary to a persistent legend, Gillette did not originally give razors away free; cheap handles became widespread later through promotions and military issue during the First World War, after which soldiers returned home as committed blade buyers. The expiry of the original patents in 1921 forced Gillette to innovate repeatedly to keep customers in its own consumable ecosystem.

Modern examples

  • Printers and ink — inexpensive inkjet printers with costly proprietary cartridges.
  • Video game consoles — hardware sold near or below cost, recouped through game licensing and sales.
  • Coffee pod machines — cheap brewers requiring branded pods (Keurig, Nespresso).
  • Mobile phones — handsets subsidised through service contracts.
  • E-readers and apps — devices and platforms monetised through content and in-app purchases.

Competition and criticism

The model's profitability depends on lock-in: patents, proprietary formats and digital rights management prevent rivals from undercutting the consumable. Competition regulators and courts have repeatedly examined the practice; cases such as Lexmark's litigation over third-party printer cartridges raised questions about whether patent exhaustion limits such lock-in. Consumers and competitors criticise the model for high consumable prices and electronic waste, and it has spawned markets for refills, compatible consumables and subscription alternatives such as Dollar Shave Club, which disrupted the original razor market itself.
