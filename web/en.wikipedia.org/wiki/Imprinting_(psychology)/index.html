Imprinting (psychology)

Imprinting is a phase of rapid learning that occurs in a specific, sensitive period shortly after birth or hatching in certain animals, during which the young form a strong attachment to and learn the characteristics of a parent or other object. The concept was brought to prominence by the Austrian ethologist Konrad Lorenz, whose studies of greylag geese showed that hatchlings would follow the first large moving object they encountered — including Lorenz himself — and treat it as their mother.

Goslings imprinted on a human follow him as they would their mother.

Filial imprinting

In precocial birds such as ducks, geese, and chickens, filial imprinting happens within hours of hatching. The young learn the appearance and calls of the imprinting object, and the resulting following response helps keep them close to a caregiver for protection and guidance. Lorenz proposed that imprinting was essentially irreversible and confined to a critical period; later research has shown it to be somewhat more flexible, occurring during a "sensitive period" whose timing can be influenced by experience.

Sexual imprinting

A second, slower form — sexual imprinting — shapes later mate choice: animals tend to prefer mates resembling those they were reared with. This can direct mating within a species and, in experiments, can even cause cross-fostered animals to court members of their foster species.

Significance

Imprinting became a cornerstone of ethology because it blended instinct and learning: an innate readiness to learn, restricted to a particular window, from stimuli the animal had never encountered. The idea influenced developmental psychology's notion of critical periods, including in human language acquisition, although direct imprinting does not occur in humans. Practical applications include the rearing of orphaned cranes, which are fed by handlers wearing crane costumes and using puppets so that the birds imprint on their own kind and can later be released.
