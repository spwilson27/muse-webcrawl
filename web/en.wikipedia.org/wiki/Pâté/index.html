Pâté

Pâté (French, roughly "paste") is a dish of ground meat, liver or other forcemeat — often mixed with fat, vegetables, herbs, spices and wine or brandy — cooked and served spreadable, usually on bread or toast. In classical French usage a pâté is baked in a pastry crust (pâté en croûte), while the same mixture baked in a mould without pastry is a terrine; in modern everyday usage "pâté" covers both, especially smooth liver preparations.

History

Forcemeats encased in pastry were staples of medieval European cookery: the crust served as cooking vessel, preservative and container, and ornate raised pies graced banquets from the Middle Ages onward. France became the dish's spiritual home, and the city of Chartres and the region of Pithiviers became famous for their game pâtés. The most prestigious of all, pâté de foie gras, is made from the fattened liver of geese or ducks and has been a luxury product since the 18th century — and a controversial one, because the birds are force-fed (gavage), a practice banned in several countries.

Varieties

  • Pâté de campagne — coarse country pâté of pork and liver, eaten across France.
  • Pâté en croûte — baked in a hot-water or puff pastry crust, often with a decorative design; an annual world championship is held in Lyon.
  • Pâté de foie gras — smooth pâté of fattened liver, sometimes studded with truffle.
  • Ardennes pâté and Belgian/Dutch leverpastei — spreadable liver pâtés in the Low Countries and Scandinavia, where liver pâté (leverpostej) is a sandwich staple.
  • Vietnamese pâté — a French colonial inheritance, spread in every bánh mì sandwich.
  • Vegetarian imitations use lentils, mushrooms, nuts or tofu in place of meat.

Preparation and serving

The meat mixture is seasoned, sometimes marinated in wine or Armagnac, then minced or puréed, packed into a terrine or crust — often lined with bacon or caul fat — and baked slowly in a water bath until just set. Pâté is matured for a day or more, and served cold or at room temperature with cornichons, mustard, chutney and crusty bread. Sealed under a layer of clarified fat or aspic, traditional pâtés keep for weeks under refrigeration.
