Skeletal dysplasia

Skeletal dysplasia is an umbrella term for a large, heterogeneous group of more than 450 distinct hereditary disorders (osteochondrodysplasias) affecting the growth and development of bone and cartilage. Collectively they occur in roughly 1 in 4,000–5,000 births. Many cause disproportionate short stature (dwarfism), while others primarily affect bone density, shape, or specific skeletal regions.

The International Skeletal Dysplasia Society's nosology groups these conditions by their molecular basis and radiographic features. Advances in genetic testing have identified causative genes for most of them, including FGFR3, the collagen genes (COL1A1, COL2A1, etc.), and genes of cartilage matrix and signalling pathways.

Examples

  • Achondroplasia — the most common non-lethal skeletal dysplasia, caused by gain-of-function variants in FGFR3; characterized by rhizomelic limb shortening, macrocephaly, and midface hypoplasia.
  • Osteogenesis imperfecta — "brittle bone disease," usually from defects in type I collagen, causing recurrent fractures and bone deformity.
  • Thanatophoric dysplasia — a severe, usually perinatal-lethal FGFR3 disorder with markedly shortened limbs and a narrow thorax.
  • Hypochondroplasia, multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia congenita, and mucopolysaccharidoses are among many others.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis combines clinical examination, skeletal survey radiographs, and molecular genetic testing; some dysplasias can be detected prenatally by ultrasound. Management is multidisciplinary and supportive: orthopedic surgery, physiotherapy, hearing and respiratory monitoring, and in some cases targeted drugs — for example vosoritide for achondroplasia or bisphosphonates for osteogenesis imperfecta.
