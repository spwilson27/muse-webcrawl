Coordinated vulnerability disclosure

Coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD), formerly commonly called responsible disclosure, is a model of vulnerability disclosure in computer security in which a vulnerability or issue is disclosed to the public only after the responsible parties have been allowed sufficient time to patch or remedy it. Security researchers who discover vulnerabilities report them privately to the vendor or maintainer, agree to keep the details confidential for an agreed period, and publish their findings once a fix is available or the deadline passes.

Practice

A typical coordinated disclosure involves the finder contacting the vendor, confirmation of the issue, development of a patch, and coordinated publication, often with a CVE identifier assigned. Embargo periods vary; Google's Project Zero applies a widely cited 90-day deadline (with a grace period in some cases), after which details are published whether or not a patch has shipped. Vendors increasingly publish vulnerability disclosure policies and operate bug bounty programmes that reward findings reported through proper channels.

Debate

Coordinated disclosure contrasts with full disclosure, in which details are published immediately to pressure vendors and inform users, and with non-disclosure, in which vulnerabilities are kept secret or sold. Supporters argue coordination reduces the window in which attackers can exploit details before a fix exists; critics argue deadlines that are too long leave users exposed, and that legal threats against researchers have historically discouraged reporting.
