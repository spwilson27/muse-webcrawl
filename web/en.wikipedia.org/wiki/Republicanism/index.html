Republicanism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Republicanism is a political ideology and tradition centred on the res publica — the commonwealth as a public thing, governed for the common good by citizens rather than held as private property by a monarch or faction. In its classical (civic humanist) form, descending from Aristotle, Polybius and Cicero through Machiavelli and the English commonwealthmen to the American founding, republicanism prizes civic virtue, mixed government, the rule of law, armed and propertied independence of citizens, and vigilance against corruption and domination — freedom as non-domination (not merely non-interference), in the modern neo-republican restatement by Pettit and Skinner.

Strands

The tradition forked: civic republicanism's demand for virtuous participation sat uneasily with the liberal rights-and-commerce republic the US actually built (a tension mapped in the "republican synthesis" historiography of Bailyn, Wood and Pocock); 19th-century republicanism became the ideology of anti-monarchical movements (Mazzini, French Third Republic laïcité); in Ireland, Spain and elsewhere "republican" names parties and national projects; and in party names worldwide (the US Republican Party included) the word survives detached from most of the classical doctrine. Contemporary neo-republicanism proposes non-domination as a third concept of liberty and a design programme (contestable institutions, social security as independence) distinct from both liberalism and communitarianism.
