Supercavitation

Supercavitation is the use of a cavitation bubble to reduce the skin friction drag of a body moving through a liquid. A fast-moving object fitted with a blunt "cavitator" at its nose generates a large, stable gas cavity that envelops most of the body, so that only the cavitator and small control surfaces touch the water. Because skin friction in gas is vastly lower than in water, supercavitating vehicles can achieve speeds far beyond those of conventional underwater craft.

Physics

Cavitation occurs when local pressure falls below the liquid's vapour pressure, forming vapour bubbles. The cavitation number σ describes the regime; at very low σ the cavity extends well behind the body, closing in re-entrant jet or twin-vortex patterns. Natural (vapour) supercavitation requires speeds of roughly 50 m/s or more in water; ventilated supercavitation injects gas (often rocket exhaust) behind the cavitator to sustain the cavity at lower speeds. Control is difficult: the vehicle rides within its own bubble, steered by the nose cavitator and fins piercing the cavity wall, and stability has been a central research problem since Soviet-era work in Kyiv and elsewhere.

Applications

The best-known application is Russia's VA-111 Shkval torpedo, first fielded in the 1970s and reported to reach around 200 knots (370 km/h) using a rocket motor and supercavitation, trading guidance sophistication and range for speed. Similar projects have been reported in Iran (Hoot torpedo), Germany (the Barracuda demonstrator), China, and the United States, where the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency explored high-speed underwater vehicles. Supercavitating propellers exploit partial cavitation for high-speed surface craft, and researchers have proposed small supercavitating projectiles for mine neutralization.
