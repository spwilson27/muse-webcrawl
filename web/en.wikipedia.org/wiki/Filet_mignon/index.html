Filet mignon

Filet mignon (French for "tender fillet", "delicate fillet", or "fine fillet") is a cut of meat taken from the smaller end of the tenderloin, or psoas major of a cow. In French, it mostly refers to cuts of pork tenderloin.

The tenderloin runs along both sides of the spine, and is usually butchered as two long, snake-shaped cuts of meat. The filet mignon is usually cut from the forward end of the tenderloin, close to the rib cage, where the diameter is smallest and the meat most tender.

Description

The tenderloin is the most tender cut of beef, and is also the most expensive. The average steer or heifer provides no more than 500 grams (about 18 ounces) of filet mignon. Because the muscle is not weight-bearing, it contains less connective tissue, which makes it tender. However, it lacks the flavour of other cuts, which is why it is often wrapped in bacon or served with a sauce, such as béarnaise or au poivre.

Cooking

Filet mignon is typically cooked by grilling, pan-frying, broiling, or roasting. Because the cut is so lean, it is prone to drying out if overcooked, and it is most often served rare to medium-rare. Chefs sometimes wrap the filet in bacon to add fat and moisture during cooking. It is the cut used for dishes such as steak au poivre and the centre cut of a beef Wellington.
