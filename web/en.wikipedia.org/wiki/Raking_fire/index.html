Raking fire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Raking fire is a naval warfare tactic in which a warship fires its guns along the length of an enemy vessel, typically through the stern or bow. Because the shot travels the full length of the target's decks, raking fire was vastly more destructive than a broadside striking the side of a ship.

Description

In the age of sail, warships carried most of their guns on their sides and were most heavily protected there. If an attacking ship could manoeuvre across the enemy's stern or bow — a position the enemy could not effectively answer — its broadside would rake the target from end to end. Cannonballs travelling down the length of crowded gun decks caused devastating casualties and splinter injuries, and could dismount guns, damage the rudder or tiller, and send shot through the relatively weak stern galleries.

Tactics

Achieving a raking position was a primary goal of fleet tactics, related to the manoeuvre known as crossing the T. The stern was generally the preferred target because it was weaker than the bow and because raking fire from astern could damage steering gear, potentially disabling the ship. Raking fire remained an important consideration into the era of steam and armoured warships, though changes in ship design and gun turret layout altered its significance.

The concept is related to enfilade fire in land warfare, in which fire is directed along the long axis of a target formation.
