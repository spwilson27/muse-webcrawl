British Afro-Caribbean people are British citizens or residents of Caribbean descent and African ancestry, largely descended from migrants who arrived from the British West Indies in the mid-20th century. They form one of the most established Black communities in the United Kingdom, with a profound influence on British music, sport, literature, politics and wider culture.

History

Small Afro-Caribbean communities existed in port cities from the era of slavery, and Caribbean servicemen served in both world wars. Mass migration began with the arrival of the Empire Windrush at Tilbury in June 1948, carrying 492 passengers from Jamaica; the migrants of 1948–1971 are known as the Windrush generation. Recruitment by London Transport and the new National Health Service drew further arrivals, mainly from Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados, and later Guyana and the smaller islands. Communities settled especially in London (Brixton, Notting Hill, Tottenham), Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds and Bristol.

The community faced discrimination — colour bars, the 1958 Notting Hill race riots, and later tensions with police that erupted in the Brixton uprisings of 1981. The Notting Hill Carnival, founded in 1964 by Rhaune Laslett and Claudia Jones's earlier indoor carnival, became Europe's largest street festival. The Windrush scandal of 2018, in which long-settled residents were wrongly detained, denied care or removed, led to a government apology and compensation scheme.

Culture

Afro-Caribbean Britons shaped British popular music through reggae, sound system culture, lovers rock, jungle, UK garage, grime and drill, and contributed carnival, cuisine (jerk, patties, rice and peas) and Caribbean creole influences to British English. Notable figures include writers Sam Selvon, Linton Kwesi Johnson and Zadie Smith; athletes such as Linford Christie; and parliamentarians including Diane Abbott, elected in 1987 among the first Black MPs of the modern era.

Demographics

In recent censuses, roughly 600,000 people in England and Wales identified as Black Caribbean, with a further large population of mixed White and Black Caribbean heritage. A majority of the third and fourth generations were born in the UK.
