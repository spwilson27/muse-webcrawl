Solar cooker

A solar cooker is a device which uses the energy of direct sunlight to heat, cook or pasteurize food and beverages. Many solar cookers currently in use are relatively inexpensive, low-tech devices, although some are as powerful or expensive as traditional stoves. Advanced solar cookers can cook for hundreds of people. Because they use no fuel and cost nothing to operate, many nonprofit organizations are promoting their use worldwide to help reduce fuel costs, air pollution, and deforestation associated with firewood collection.

A parabolic solar cooker concentrating sunlight on a pot.

History

The first academic description of the principles of a solar cooker was made by Swiss geologist and physicist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure in 1767, who built an insulated box that reached high temperatures under sunlight. In the 1860s and 1870s, French mathematician Augustin Mouchot developed solar-powered steam engines and cookers. Solar cooking was promoted during the 1970s energy crisis, and organisations such as Solar Cookers International, founded in 1987, have since spread simple designs to refugee camps and rural communities.

Principles

Solar cookers work by converting sunlight to heat energy, retaining that heat, and concentrating additional sunlight to raise temperatures. Most designs use reflective materials such as aluminium foil, polished metal, or mirrored surfaces to direct sunlight onto a dark cooking vessel inside an insulated container. Greenhouse-style covers of glass or plastic trap heat, while blackened pots absorb radiation efficiently. Box cookers typically reach 90–150 °C (200–300 °F), while parabolic concentrating cookers can exceed 250 °C (480 °F) at the focal point.

Types

The three main categories are box cookers, which are insulated boxes with transparent lids and reflectors; panel cookers, which use flat reflective panels to focus light on a pot inside a plastic bag or under a glass bowl; and parabolic cookers, which use a curved reflector to concentrate sunlight to a point. Evacuated tube cookers use glass tubes with vacuum insulation to achieve high efficiency even in cold or partly cloudy conditions.

Advantages and limitations

Solar cookers consume no fuel, produce no smoke, and can pasteurize water, making them valuable where firewood is scarce or expensive. Their main limitations are dependence on sunny weather, longer cooking times for box designs, and the need to cook outdoors during daylight hours. Foods that require frying or baking at high temperatures are better suited to concentrating designs.
