Johns Hopkins (19 May 1795 – 24 December 1873) was an American merchant, investor and philanthropist. His bequests founded Johns Hopkins University and Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, Maryland, and constituted the largest philanthropic gift in American history at the time.

Life

Hopkins was born into a Quaker family on a tobacco plantation in Anne Arundel County. Because his family freed their enslaved people in 1807 in accordance with Quaker teaching, the young Hopkins worked in the fields and received limited formal schooling. He went to Baltimore in 1812 to work in his uncle's wholesale grocery business, built his own successful merchant house, and later amassed a fortune through banking and as a major shareholder and director of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad.

Philanthropy

A lifelong bachelor, Hopkins devoted his wealth to charitable purposes: he supported housing for the poor, an asylum for Black orphans, and public works in Baltimore. His will left some $7 million — much of it in B&O Railroad stock — split principally between a university and a hospital. The will directed that the hospital care for the poor "without regard to sex, age, or color" and that the university and hospital be connected, creating the foundation for modern academic medicine in the United States.

Legacy

Johns Hopkins University opened in 1876 under president Daniel Coit Gilman as America's first research university on the German model; the hospital followed in 1889 and the School of Medicine in 1893, pioneering practices such as admitting women on equal terms (a condition of early fundraising), residency training, and full-time clinical faculty. The institutions bearing Hopkins's name remain among the world's leading centres of research and medicine.
