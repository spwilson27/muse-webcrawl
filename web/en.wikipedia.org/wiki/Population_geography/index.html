Population geography

Population geography is a branch of human geography that studies how the spatial variation in the distribution, composition, migration, and growth of populations relates to the geographical characteristics of places. Population geographers analyse why populations are distributed as they are, how demographic processes such as fertility, mortality, and migration vary across space, and how populations interact with environments, economies, and political boundaries.

Scope and themes

The subdiscipline covers the study of population density and distribution (including choropleth mapping of densities), population composition by age, sex, ethnicity, religion, language, and occupation, the causes and consequences of internal and international migration, urbanisation, and demographic transition in different regions. Classic measures include crude birth and death rates, total fertility rates, life expectancy, dependency ratios, and population pyramids used to display age-sex structure. Population geographers are also concerned with the geography of health and disease, residential segregation, and the politics of censuses and population data.

History

Population geography emerged as a recognised subfield after the Second World War. Glenn Trewartha, in his 1953 presidential address to the Association of American Geographers, argued that population was the pivotal element in geography — the point of reference from which all other elements derive significance. Wilbur Zelinsky, John Clarke (author of the influential textbook Population Geography, 1965), and others consolidated the field, initially with an emphasis on mapping and quantitative description. From the 1970s onward, population geography absorbed behavioural, welfare, feminist, and critical approaches, expanding into topics such as mobility and everyday movement, transnational migration, ageing populations, and population-environment relations.

Data and methods

The field depends heavily on censuses, vital registration systems, population registers, and surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys. Since the late 20th century, geographers have used geographic information systems (GIS), spatial statistics, and remote sensing to estimate and visualise population distributions, including gridded population datasets. Population geography informs policy and planning in areas including service provision, electoral boundaries, housing, transport, and disaster vulnerability.
