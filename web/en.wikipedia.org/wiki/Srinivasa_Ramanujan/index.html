Srinivasa Ramanujan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Died26 April 1920 (aged 32), Kumbakonam, Madras Presidency
Known forRamanujan prime, mock modular forms, partition function, highly composite numbers, taxicab numbers
AwardsFellow of the Royal Society (1918)

Srinivasa Ramanujan (22 December 1887 – 26 April 1920) was an Indian mathematician who, with almost no formal training in pure mathematics, made substantial contributions to number theory, mathematical analysis, infinite series and continued fractions. Working largely in isolation, he rediscovered many known results and produced several thousand original ones, including identities and equations that later opened entirely new areas of research. His work with the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy made him one of the most celebrated mathematicians of the twentieth century.

Early life

Ramanujan was born in Erode, Tamil Nadu, into a family of modest means, and grew up in Kumbakonam. He showed an extraordinary aptitude for mathematics from childhood, mastering advanced trigonometry from a borrowed book by his early teens and deriving his own results. He won a scholarship to college but lost it because he would study almost nothing except mathematics, and he dropped out of two colleges without a degree. He continued his research in poverty, taking a clerical post at the Madras Port Trust while seeking patrons for his work.

Contact with Hardy

In January 1913 Ramanujan wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, enclosing pages of theorems. Hardy, after initial scepticism, recognised the work of a genius: some results he knew, some he could prove with effort, and some he described as seeming almost impossible to believe. Hardy arranged for Ramanujan to come to England, and Ramanujan arrived in London in April 1914, shortly before the outbreak of the First World War.

Work in England

At Trinity College, Cambridge, Ramanujan published a series of papers with Hardy and alone, including major work on highly composite numbers and the asymptotic formula for the partition function. In 1917 Hardy and Ramanujan published their celebrated asymptotic series for the number of partitions of an integer, a method later refined into the circle method. In 1918 Ramanujan was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, one of the youngest fellows in its history, and a Fellow of Trinity College.

Illness and death

Ramanujan's health deteriorated in England, worsened by wartime food shortages and the difficulties his strict vegetarian diet faced in Cambridge. He suffered from a recurrent illness, variously diagnosed, and spent long periods in nursing homes. He returned to India in 1919 and died at Kumbakonam on 26 April 1920, aged 32. A famous anecdote recounts that when Hardy visited him and mentioned arriving in taxi number 1729, calling it a dull number, Ramanujan replied at once that it was very interesting: it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways — the origin of the term taxicab number.

Notebooks and legacy

Ramanujan left behind several notebooks filled with thousands of results, most without proofs. A "lost notebook", rediscovered in 1976 by George Andrews among papers at Trinity College, contained his final work on mock theta functions, which have since found applications in areas Ramanujan could not have foreseen, including the physics of black holes. His birth anniversary, 22 December, is celebrated in India as National Mathematics Day. The story of his life was popularised by Robert Kanigel's biography The Man Who Knew Infinity (1991), later adapted into a film.
