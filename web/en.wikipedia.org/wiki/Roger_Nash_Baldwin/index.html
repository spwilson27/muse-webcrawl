Roger Nash Baldwin

Roger Nash Baldwin (January 21, 1884 – August 26, 1981) was one of the founders of the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU). He served as executive director of the ACLU until 1950. He also founded the International League for the Rights of Man, which later became the International League for Human Rights.

Early life

Baldwin was born in Wellesley, Massachusetts, into a well-to-do family and educated at Harvard. He worked as a social worker and probation officer in St. Louis, taught sociology at Washington University, and wrote Juvenile Courts and Probation (1914). Influenced by Emma Goldman and the radical labour movement, he became a pacifist and civil libertarian.

ACLU

During the First World War Baldwin directed the National Civil Liberties Bureau, an offshoot of the American Union Against Militarism, and was imprisoned for nearly a year in 1918–19 for refusing induction under the draft. In January 1920 the bureau was reorganised as the ACLU, with Baldwin as its first director. Under his leadership the organisation took up free-speech cases including the Scopes Trial (1925), the Sacco and Vanzetti protests, and challenges to bans on books such as Ulysses.

Later years

Baldwin stepped down in 1950 but remained active in international human-rights work, advising the United Nations and post-war governments in Japan and Germany. Initially sympathetic to the Soviet Union — he wrote Liberty Under the Soviets (1928) — he became a firm anti-communist after the Moscow Trials, and in 1940 presided over the expulsion of Elizabeth Gurley Flynn from the ACLU board for her Communist Party membership. In 1981 President Jimmy Carter awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom.
