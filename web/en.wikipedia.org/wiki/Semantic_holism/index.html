Semantic holism

FieldPhilosophy of language
Key figuresW. V. O. Quine, Donald Davidson

Semantic holism is a theory in the philosophy of language that holds that the meaning of a linguistic expression cannot be understood in isolation, but only as part of a larger whole — a language, a theory, or a web of belief. On this view, the smallest unit of meaning is not the word or even the sentence, but an entire system of sentences.

Background

The doctrine is closely associated with W. V. O. Quine's critique of the analytic–synthetic distinction in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) and his thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, as well as with Donald Davidson's work on radical interpretation. Quine famously wrote that "the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science": statements face the tribunal of experience not individually but as a corporate body.

Arguments and problems

Supporters argue that holism explains how speakers can understand novel sentences (because they grasp the inferential roles expressions play within the whole language) and how meaning can survive changes in individual beliefs. Critics, notably Jerry Fodor and Ernest Lepore, object that strong holism makes communication and shared meaning impossible: if any difference in belief changes the meaning of every expression, then no two speakers ever mean the same thing by any sentence, and compositionality — how the meaning of a sentence is built from the meanings of its parts — becomes hard to explain.

Moderate or "molecularist" positions, defended by Michael Dummett among others, hold that meaning depends on grasping a limited neighbourhood of language rather than the whole of it.
