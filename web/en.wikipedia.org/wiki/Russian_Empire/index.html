The Russian Empire was a multinational empire that existed from 1721, when Peter the Great proclaimed Russia an empire after victory over Sweden in the Great Northern War, until the revolutions of 1917 ended the Romanov dynasty. At its greatest extent it stretched from Poland to the Pacific and from the Arctic to Central Asia and the Black Sea — nearly one-sixth of the Earth's land surface — ruling hundreds of peoples, languages and faiths under an autocratic tsar.

History

Peter westernised the state, built Saint Petersburg as his "window on Europe", and founded the navy and academy of sciences. Catherine the Great (reigned 1762–96) extended the empire to the Black Sea and partitioned Poland, while enlightenment rhetoric masked deepening serfdom — peasants were legally bound to the land until Alexander II's emancipation of 1861, the same tsar later assassinated by revolutionaries in 1881. Russia defeated Napoleon's invasion of 1812 — the Patriotic War, immortalised in Tolstoy's War and Peace and Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture — and garrisoned Paris in 1814, becoming the arbiter of Europe at the Congress of Vienna. The 19th century saw the "Great Game" rivalry with Britain in Central Asia, expansion to the Pacific (Vladivostok, 1860), the sale of Alaska to the United States (1867 — see Alaska), and the building of the Trans-Siberian Railway.

Collapse

Defeat by Japan (1905) sparked the first revolution and a limited constitution; defeat and collapse in the First World War finished the regime. In March 1917 (February by the Russian calendar), strikes and mutinies in Petrograd forced Nicholas II to abdicate; the Provisional Government fell to Lenin's Bolsheviks in October 1917, leading to civil war and the USSR. The imperial family's execution in 1918 closed three centuries of Romanov rule.
