Oxazole is the parent compound of a vast class of heterocyclic aromatic organic compounds. These are azoles with an oxygen and a nitrogen separated by one carbon. Oxazoles are aromatic compounds but less so than the thiazoles. Oxazole is a weak base; its conjugate acid has a pKa of 0.8, compared to 7 for imidazole.

Occurrence and production

Substituted oxazoles arise in nature as products of the cyclisation of serine and threonine residues in nonribosomal peptides; oxazoline and oxazole rings appear in many natural products and pharmaceuticals. Industrially, oxazoles are prepared by routes such as the Robinson–Gabriel synthesis (dehydration of 2-acylaminoketones) and the van Leusen reaction.

Reactions

With only one carbon between the heteroatoms, oxazole undergoes Diels–Alder reactions at C-2 with reactive dienophiles, followed by loss of water to give pyridines. Electrophilic aromatic substitution occurs chiefly at C-5, while deprotonation at C-2 is promoted by the adjacent heteroatoms.
