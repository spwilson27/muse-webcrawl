The Battle of the North Cape was a Second World War naval battle that occurred on 26 December 1943, as part of the Arctic convoy campaign. The German battleship Scharnhorst, pursuing a convoy to northern Russia, was brought to action and sunk by the Royal Navy battleship HMS Duke of York with cruisers and destroyers, off the North Cape of Norway. It was the last battle between big-gun capital ships of Britain and Germany.

Background

In December 1943 the Germans, acting on reconnaissance reports, sent Scharnhorst and a destroyer flotilla under Rear Admiral Erich Bey from Altafjord to intercept convoy JW 55B bound for Murmansk. British Admiral Sir Bruce Fraser, warned by Ultra intelligence, covered the convoy with Force 2, including Duke of York, while Vice Admiral Robert Burnett's cruisers screened the convoy directly.

The battle

In the polar twilight and heavy seas, Burnett's cruisers (Belfast, Norfolk and Sheffield) engaged Scharnhorst twice on the morning of 26 December, damaging her radar and shadowing her after she abandoned the attack on the convoy. In the afternoon Fraser intercepted her on her way back to base. Duke of York opened fire with radar-directed salvos, disabling Scharnhorst's forward turrets, and after a chase in which the German ship's speed fell, British destroyers closed in with torpedoes. Battered by gunfire and multiple torpedo hits, Scharnhorst capsized and sank that evening.

Aftermath

Of Scharnhorst's crew of about 1,968, only 36 survived. Admiral Bey went down with his ship. The sinking removed the last effective German surface threat to the Arctic convoys and allowed the Royal Navy to transfer heavy units to the Far East.
