Allopolyploidy

Allopolyploidy is a form of polyploidy in which an organism has multiple complete sets of chromosomes derived from two or more different ancestral species. It arises when hybridisation between species is followed by chromosome doubling, so that each chromosome gains a proper pairing partner and the hybrid becomes fertile. Allopolyploidy contrasts with autopolyploidy, in which the extra chromosome sets come from a single species.

Mechanism

When two species with chromosome numbers 2n1 and 2n2 cross, the hybrid's chromosomes often cannot pair properly in meiosis, making the hybrid sterile. If the chromosome complement doubles — spontaneously or through an unreduced gamete — each chromosome has an identical partner, meiosis proceeds normally, and a fertile allotetraploid (amphidiploid) results. Because the new form is reproductively isolated from both parents, allopolyploidy can create a new species in a single generation, a process called speciation by hybridisation.

Examples

Allopolyploidy is especially common and important in plants. Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) is an allohexaploid combining genomes from three grass species. Cultivated cotton, tobacco, canola/oilseed rape, and the coffee species Coffea arabica are allotetraploids. A classic experimental demonstration was Georgii Karpechenko's cross between radish and cabbage, and the salt-marsh grass Spartina anglica arose by allopolyploidy in the 19th century and spread worldwide. In animals, examples include the frog Xenopus laevis and many fish lineages.
