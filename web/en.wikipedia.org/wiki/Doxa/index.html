Doxa

Doxa (from ancient Greek δόξα, "glory", "praise", "belief", "opinion") is a common belief or popular opinion. In classical Greek rhetoric and philosophy, doxa was contrasted with episteme, true knowledge: Plato in particular treated doxa as mere opinion, belonging to the shifting world of appearances, while episteme concerned the eternal Forms. The term gained a technical sense in the sociology of Pierre Bourdieu, for whom doxa is the taken-for-granted, unquestioned set of beliefs and assumptions that structure a social field and make its order seem natural.

Usage

Plato's Republic uses the divided line to place doxa as the cognitive state corresponding to sensible objects, below knowledge of the intelligible. In Bourdieu's work, doxa is what goes without saying — the undisputed background against which orthodox and heterodox opinions compete. The word also survives in terms such as orthodoxy ("right opinion") and paradox ("against opinion").
