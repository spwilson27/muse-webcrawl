Waterjet

A waterjet is a propulsion system for boats and ships in which a pump draws water through an intake beneath the hull and expels it through a nozzle at high speed, driving the vessel forward by reaction. Steering is achieved by deflecting the jet, and reverse by a deflector bucket. The term also refers to a water jet cutter, the industrial cutting tool that uses a similar high-pressure stream.

Marine propulsion

Commercialised by Sir William Hamilton in New Zealand in the 1950s, waterjets are favoured for fast ferries, patrol boats, jet skis and shallow-draft vessels because they have no exposed propeller, can operate in very shallow water, and offer excellent manoeuvrability at speed. Their efficiency falls at low speeds, and intakes can be clogged by debris or damaged by grounding.

Water jet cutting

Industrial waterjet cutters pressurise water to 600 MPa or more and force it through a tiny orifice, often with abrasive garnet added, to cut metal, stone, glass and composites without heat damage — a property that makes the process valuable in aerospace and food processing alike.
