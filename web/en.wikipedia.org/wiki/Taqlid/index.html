Taqlid

Taqlid: following the rulings of a recognised legal school or scholar

Taqlid (Arabic: تقليد, taqlīd) is an Islamic legal term meaning the acceptance of, and adherence to, the rulings of a qualified scholar or school of law (madhhab) without independently examining the scriptural evidence for each ruling. A person who practises taqlid is called a muqallid. The term is generally contrasted with ijtihad, the independent reasoning by which a qualified jurist (mujtahid) derives rulings directly from the Qur'an and Sunnah.

In Sunni Islam

Within Sunni Islam, taqlid of one of the four surviving schools — Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali — has historically been the norm for laypeople and most scholars. Classical jurists generally held that those without the training to perform ijtihad were obliged to follow a qualified scholar, citing the Qur'anic injunction to "ask the people of remembrance if you do not know" (16:43). The widespread historical claim that the "gate of ijtihad" was closed after the formative period has been debated by historians, who note that independent reasoning continued in various forms.

In Shia Islam

In Twelver Shia Islam, taqlid is a central religious duty: every believer who is not himself a mujtahid must follow a living mujtahid, ideally the most learned (marja' al-taqlid, "source of emulation"), whose collected rulings (risalah) guide followers in ritual, personal and financial matters. Following a deceased marja' without permission from a living one is generally not permitted in the Usuli tradition.

Criticism and reform

Taqlid has been criticised by various reform movements — including Salafis, who advocate direct recourse to Qur'an and Sunnah, and some modernist thinkers — as blind imitation responsible for stagnation. Defenders respond that taqlid is simply the rational deference of non-specialists to experts, comparable to relying on physicians or engineers, and that it preserves legal continuity and consistency.
