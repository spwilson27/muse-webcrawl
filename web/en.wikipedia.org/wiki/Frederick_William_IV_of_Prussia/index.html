Frederick William IV (German: Friedrich Wilhelm IV.; 15 October 1795 – 2 January 1861) was King of Prussia from 1840 until his death in 1861. He was the eldest son of Frederick William III and Louise of Mecklenburg-Strelitz.

Known as the "romanticist on the throne", Frederick William was deeply interested in art, architecture and medieval German culture. His reign saw the completion of Cologne Cathedral's construction work resume, ambitious building projects in Berlin and Potsdam, and the upheavals of the Revolutions of 1848.

Early life

Frederick William grew up during the Napoleonic Wars; the Prussian royal family fled to East Prussia after the defeat at Jena in 1806, and his mother died in 1810. He received an education shaped by romantic and conservative tutors and served, largely as an observer, in the Wars of Liberation of 1813–1815. In 1823 he married Elisabeth Ludovika of Bavaria; the marriage was happy but childless.

Reign

Accession and early hopes

Succeeding his father on 7 June 1840, Frederick William raised hopes among liberals by easing censorship, releasing political prisoners and promising constitutional reform. He summoned the United Diet (Vereinigter Landtag) in 1847 but refused to grant it the powers of a true parliament, declaring he would never allow "a written sheet of paper" to come between God and his crown.

Revolution of 1848

When revolution broke out in Berlin in March 1848, the king at first conceded reforms and paid homage to citizens killed in street fighting, but after the army regained control he imposed a constitution on his own terms (December 1848, revised 1850), which preserved strong royal authority and introduced the three-class franchise. In April 1849 he refused the imperial crown offered by the Frankfurt Parliament, dismissing it as a crown "from the gutter" because it derived from popular sovereignty rather than divine right.

Later years

Prussia's attempt to lead a German union of princes collapsed with the Punctation of Olmütz (1850), under Austrian and Russian pressure. Frederick William devoted much energy to architecture — he sketched designs for churches and palaces and supported architects such as Karl Friedrich Schinkel's successors, including Friedrich August Stüler — and to the completion of Cologne Cathedral. After suffering a series of strokes from 1857, he became mentally incapacitated; his brother William acted as regent from 1858. He died at Sanssouci, Potsdam, on 2 January 1861, and William succeeded him as William I.
