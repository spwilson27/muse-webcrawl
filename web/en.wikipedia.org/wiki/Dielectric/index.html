A dielectric is an electrical insulator that can be polarised by an applied electric field. When a dielectric is placed in an electric field, electric charges do not flow through the material as they do in a conductor, but only slightly shift from their average equilibrium positions, causing dielectric polarisation: positive charges move slightly with the field and negative charges slightly against it, creating bound surface charge that partially cancels the field inside the material.

Dielectrics are essential to capacitors — inserting a dielectric between the plates increases the capacitance by the material's relative permittivity — and to the insulation of cables, transformers and radio-frequency devices. The term was coined by William Whewell in correspondence with Michael Faraday, whose experiments on "specific inductive capacity" founded the subject.

Polarisation mechanisms

Several mechanisms contribute, each acting on a different timescale: electronic polarisation (displacement of electron clouds, effective up to optical frequencies), ionic polarisation (relative displacement of ions in crystals), dipolar (orientation) polarisation (rotation of permanent molecular dipoles, as in water, effective up to microwave frequencies) and interfacial polarisation (charge accumulation at boundaries in heterogeneous materials). The polarisation P is related to the field by P = ε0χeE, where χe is the electric susceptibility.

Permittivity

The permittivity ε of a dielectric measures how much it resists the formation of an electric field within it; capacitance scales with ε. The relative permittivity (dielectric constant) εr = ε/ε0 is about 1 for gases, 2–4 for most plastics and oils, around 4–7 for glass and mica, roughly 80 for water at room temperature, and can reach thousands in ferroelectric ceramics such as barium titanate. A high permittivity concentrates electric flux, so dielectrics let capacitors store more charge at a given voltage.

Dielectric breakdown

Every dielectric has a dielectric strength: the maximum field it can withstand before suddenly becoming conductive. Breakdown in air at standard conditions occurs near 3 MV/m (the basis of lightning and of corona discharge), while good solid insulators sustain tens of MV/m. Breakdown may be intrinsic, thermal, or caused by partial discharges in voids, and it limits the voltage rating of insulation and capacitors.

Losses and dispersion

In alternating fields, polarisation lags the field, dissipating energy as heat — characterised by the loss tangent tan δ and by a complex, frequency-dependent permittivity. This dielectric heating is exploited in microwave ovens (dipole rotation of water at 2.45 GHz) and is a nuisance loss in RF capacitors and transmission lines. Debye relaxation describes the characteristic fall of permittivity as dipoles fail to follow faster fields.

Applications

Beyond capacitors and cable insulation, dielectrics serve as gate oxides in transistors (silicon dioxide, high-κ materials), as substrates and radomes in antennas, in piezoelectric and ferroelectric devices, in optical coatings, and as the working media of electrets and dielectric elastomer actuators. Mineral oil and SF₆ gas insulate high-voltage switchgear.
