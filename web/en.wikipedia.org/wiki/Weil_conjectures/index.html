Weil conjectures

The Weil conjectures are four conjectures about the zeta functions of algebraic varieties over finite fields, proposed by André Weil in 1949. They became among the most influential problems in 20th-century mathematics, driving the development of algebraic geometry — in particular the creation of étale cohomology by Alexander Grothendieck and his school — before their final resolution.

Statement

For a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over a finite field with q elements, its zeta function Z(X, t) is a generating function encoding the numbers of points of X over all finite extensions of the field. The conjectures assert:

  • Rationality – Z(X, t) is a rational function of t.
  • Functional equation – Z satisfies a symmetry relating t and 1/(qnt), reflecting Poincaré duality.
  • Riemann hypothesis – the zeros and poles of Z, expressed in terms of the variable q−s, lie on lines with real parts i/2 for i = 0, …, 2n, in analogy with the classical Riemann hypothesis.
  • Betti numbers – if X is the reduction of a variety in characteristic zero, the degrees of the polynomial factors of Z agree with the Betti numbers of the complex variety.

Proof

Rationality was proved by Bernard Dwork in 1960 using p-adic methods. The functional equation and Betti number statements followed from Grothendieck's development of étale cohomology in the 1960s. The final and hardest part, the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, was proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974 (published as "La conjecture de Weil II" extending it further in 1980), work for which Deligne received the Fields Medal in 1978.
