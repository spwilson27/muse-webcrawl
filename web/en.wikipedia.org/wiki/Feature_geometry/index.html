Feature geometry

Feature geometry is a theory in phonology that represents the distinctive features of speech sounds not as an unordered bundle but as a hierarchical tree of nodes. Developed in the 1980s by linguists including G. N. Clements and Morris Halle, the model groups features under class nodes — such as Laryngeal, Place and manner nodes — that can spread, delink or be targeted as units by phonological rules.

Motivation

Classical generative phonology treated a segment as a flat matrix of binary features. This struggled to explain why certain sets of features habitually behave together in assimilation: in many languages, for example, a nasal consonant takes on the full place of articulation of a following consonant, suggesting that place features form a single constituent that can spread as a whole. Feature geometry captures this by placing place features (labial, coronal, dorsal) under one Place node, manner features under another, and voicing and aspiration under a Laryngeal node, all dominated by a root node representing the segment.

Applications

The model is used to analyse assimilation, dissimilation (such as the Obligatory Contour Principle), feature spreading in tone and vowel harmony, and the structure of complex segments such as prenasalised stops and affricates, which may have two root nodes or ordered sub-structures. Feature geometry was an important precursor of later constraint-based and articulator-based phonologies.
