Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine belonging to the transforming growth factor superfamily, which also includes the bone morphogenetic proteins, activins and inhibins. In mammals there are three isoforms, TGF-β1, TGF-β2 and TGF-β3, encoded by separate genes. TGF-β is secreted in a latent complex and activated extracellularly; it signals through serine/threonine kinase receptors that phosphorylate SMAD transcription factors.

Functions

TGF-β controls proliferation, cellular differentiation, apoptosis, migration and extracellular matrix production in most cell types. It is a potent growth inhibitor for epithelial and immune cells, a master regulator of fibrosis and wound healing, and a key immunosuppressive cytokine: TGF-β1 knockout mice die of widespread inflammation. During development, TGF-β family gradients pattern the embryo and drive processes such as palate fusion and heart valve formation.

Role in disease

Dysregulated TGF-β signalling contributes to cancer (where it suppresses early tumours but promotes invasion and metastasis of advanced ones), fibrotic diseases of the lung, liver and kidney, and connective-tissue disorders. Mutations in TGF-β pathway components cause hereditary syndromes including Loeys–Dietz syndrome and hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia. TGF-β inhibitors have been explored as anti-cancer and anti-fibrotic drugs, with mixed clinical success.
