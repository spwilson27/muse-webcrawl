Old Prussian (Prussian: Prūsiskai) is an extinct West Baltic language once spoken by the Old Prussians, the Baltic peoples inhabiting the region of Prussia between the Vistula Lagoon and the Curonian Lagoon, in what is now northern Poland and the Kaliningrad Oblast of Russia. It belonged to the Western branch of the Baltic languages, of which the only surviving members of the family are the Eastern Baltic languages Lithuanian and Latvian.

Sources

The language is attested in a small corpus. The chief sources are the Basel Epigram (1369), the earliest known sentence in Old Prussian; the Elbing Vocabulary (c. 1400), a German–Prussian word list of 802 words; the vocabulary of Simon Grunau (c. 1517–1526); and three catechisms printed at Königsberg in 1545 and 1561, translated by order of Duke Albert of Prussia to serve the Lutheran Reformation among Prussian-speaking peasants. The texts show heavy German influence and inconsistencies reflecting the translators' limited command of the language.

Decline and extinction

Germanisation, the devastation of the Prussian lands by plague and war in the 17th century, and the resettlement of the region by German, Lithuanian, and Polish speakers drove the language into decline. It is generally considered to have died out in the early 18th century; the last speakers may have perished in the great plague of 1709–1711.

Revival

Since the late 20th century, scholars and enthusiasts in Lithuania, Poland, and Latvia have attempted a revival of Prussian, reconstructing a "Neo-Prussian" language from the surviving texts and place-names, with a small community of speakers and published literature.
