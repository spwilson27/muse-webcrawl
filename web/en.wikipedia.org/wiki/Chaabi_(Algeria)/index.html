Chaabi (Arabic: sha'bī, meaning "popular") is a traditional music genre of Algeria, originating in the Casbah of Algiers in the early 20th century. It developed from the classical Andalusi tradition and from melhun, sung poetry in Algerian dialectal Arabic, and became the defining popular music of working-class Algiers.

History

Chaabi emerged when musicians in Algiers began adapting the refined repertoire of Andalusi music — the classical tradition carried to North Africa by refugees from Muslim Spain — to a broader urban audience. The texts drew on melhun, dialectal poems by authors such as Lakhdar Ben Khlouf, whose verses treated religious, moral, and everyday themes in language ordinary people understood.

The genre was codified in the 1920s and 1930s by El Hadj M'Hamed El Anka (1907–1978), universally regarded as the founder and "Cardinal" of chaabi. El Anka modernised the orchestra, introduced the mandole (a large metal-bodied mandolin-family instrument built to his specifications), and recorded extensively, giving the genre its canonical form.

Characteristics

Chaabi songs are typically long, structured around a poetic text (qasida) sung over modal frameworks derived from Andalusi ṭubūʿ (modes). Performances often move from a slow, measured opening toward a livelier rhythmic conclusion. Core instruments include the mandole, oud, violin (played vertically on the knee), banjo, piano, and frame and goblet drums such as the tar and derbouka. Themes range from love and exile to moral counsel and praise of the Prophet.

Notable artists

After El Anka, the genre was carried forward by performers including Dahmane El Harrachi, whose "Ya Rayah" (about emigration and exile) became one of the most famous Algerian songs worldwide; El Hachemi Guerouabi; Reinette l'Oranaise, a leading Jewish-Algerian interpreter; and Kamel Messaoudi, who revived chaabi for a younger generation in the 1990s. The style also strongly influenced the development of raï and modern Algerian pop.
