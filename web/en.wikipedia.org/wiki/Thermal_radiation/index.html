Thermal radiation

Thermal radiation
TypeElectromagnetic radiation emitted due to temperature
Governing lawsPlanck's law; Stefan–Boltzmann law; Wien's displacement law; Kirchhoff's law
SpectrumMainly infrared at ordinary temperatures; extends into visible light when hot
ApplicationsThermography, pyrometry, radiative heating and cooling, astrophysics

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic radiation emitted by the thermal motion of particles in matter. All matter with a temperature above absolute zero emits thermal radiation, most of it in the infrared part of the spectrum at everyday temperatures. Unlike conduction and convection, radiative heat transfer requires no medium and can occur across a vacuum — it is the mechanism by which the Sun warms the Earth.

Properties

The spectrum of thermal radiation from an ideal emitter, a black body, is described by Planck's law and depends only on temperature. As temperature rises, the total emitted power grows with the fourth power of absolute temperature (the Stefan–Boltzmann law), while the wavelength of peak emission shifts shorter (Wien's displacement law) — which is why heated metal glows first dull red, then orange, then white. Real materials emit less than a black body; the ratio is their emissivity, and by Kirchhoff's law a good absorber is also a good emitter at the same wavelength.

Heat transfer and applications

Radiative exchange between surfaces is a major component of heat transfer in furnaces, engines and buildings, and dominates at high temperatures. Engineering applications include infrared thermography and non-contact pyrometers for measuring temperature, radiant heating panels, reflective insulation and multilayer spacecraft insulation, and radiative cooling surfaces. The thermal radiation of stars and planets is a fundamental observational tool of astronomy, and the cosmic microwave background — the cooled remnant radiation of the early universe — is a near-perfect black-body spectrum.

Historical notes

The study of thermal radiation in the nineteenth century, especially by Gustav Kirchhoff and Josef Stefan, culminated in Max Planck's quantum hypothesis of 1900, introduced to explain the black-body spectrum and conventionally regarded as the birth of quantum theory.
