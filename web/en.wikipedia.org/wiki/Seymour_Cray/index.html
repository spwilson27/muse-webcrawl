Seymour Cray

Seymour Roger Cray (September 28, 1925 – October 5, 1996) was an American electrical engineer and supercomputer architect who designed a series of the world's fastest computers over three decades. Often called "the father of supercomputing", he founded Cray Research in 1972 and later Cray Computer Corporation and SRC Computers.

Early career

Born in Chippewa Falls, Wisconsin, Cray served as a radio operator in the Second World War and earned degrees in electrical engineering and applied mathematics from the University of Minnesota. At Engineering Research Associates and its successor Remington Rand Univac he worked on the ERA 1103. In 1957 he co-founded Control Data Corporation (CDC) with William Norris, where he designed the CDC 1604, the CDC 6600 (1964) — generally considered the first successful supercomputer — and the CDC 7600.

Cray Research

Frustrated by corporate bureaucracy, Cray left CDC in 1972 to found Cray Research in his home town. The Cray-1 (1976), with its distinctive C-shaped chassis, vector registers and Freon cooling, became an icon of supercomputing and sold for millions of dollars to national laboratories. The multiprocessor Cray X-MP and the Cray-2 (1985), immersed in Fluorinert liquid coolant, followed. In 1989 he left to found Cray Computer Corporation to build the gallium-arsenide Cray-3; the company went bankrupt in 1995. His final venture, SRC Computers, was developing the Cray-4 concept when he died from injuries sustained in a car accident in Colorado Springs in 1996.

Design philosophy

Cray favoured small teams, radical simplicity and building the fastest possible machine without regard to conventional design rules, famously remarking when told that Apple had bought a Cray to design the next Macintosh that he had just bought a Mac to design the next Cray.
