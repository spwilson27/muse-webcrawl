Enriched uranium

Enriched uranium is uranium in which the proportion of the fissile isotope uranium-235 (U-235) has been increased above the 0.72% found in natural uranium. Enrichment makes the material capable of sustaining a nuclear chain reaction in light-water reactors and, at high levels of enrichment, usable in nuclear weapons. The leftover material, depleted in U-235, is called depleted uranium.

Enrichment methods

Because isotopes are chemically identical, separation exploits the tiny mass difference between U-235 and U-238. Historical methods include gaseous diffusion, which forced uranium hexafluoride gas through porous membranes (used on an enormous scale by the Manhattan Project at Oak Ridge), and electromagnetic separation in calutrons. Modern plants overwhelmingly use gas centrifuges, which spin uranium hexafluoride at very high speeds so the heavier isotope migrates outward; centrifuges use a small fraction of the electricity of diffusion. Laser isotope separation has been developed but is not in widespread commercial use. The effort required is measured in separative work units (SWU).

Proliferation concerns

The same centrifuge technology that produces 4% reactor fuel can, with further passes, produce weapons-grade material, which is why enrichment facilities are among the most sensitive points in the nuclear fuel cycle and are monitored under IAEA safeguards. International disputes over enrichment — most prominently concerning Iran's programme — have been central to nuclear non-proliferation diplomacy.

History

The first large-scale enrichment was carried out during the Second World War: the "Little Boy" bomb dropped on Hiroshima used about 64 kg of uranium enriched to an average of roughly 80% U-235. After the war, enrichment capacity spread to the Soviet Union/Russia, the United Kingdom, France, China and, through the Urenco consortium and others, to several further states.
