Iñupiaq language

Iñupiaq (Iñupiatun)
Native to: United States (Alaska)
Region: North Slope, Northwest Arctic, Seward Peninsula
Speakers: Approximately 2,000–3,000
Language family: Eskimo–Aleut → Eskimo → Inuit
Official status: Official language of Alaska (symbolic, since 2014)

Iñupiaq (also spelled Inupiaq, Inupiat, or Iñupiatun) is an Inuit language, or dialect continuum, spoken by the Iñupiat people of northern and northwestern Alaska. It is closely related to the Inuit languages of Canada and Greenland, forming a chain of mutually intelligible varieties across the Arctic coast. Major Alaskan dialect groups include North Slope Iñupiaq and Seward Peninsula Iñupiaq (including Qawiaraq).

Classification and dialects

Iñupiaq belongs to the Inuit branch of the Eskimo (Inuit-Yupik-Unangaax) family. Within Alaska, dialect differences in phonology and vocabulary separate the Malimiut, Seward Peninsula, and North Slope varieties; the variety of the Diomede Islands shares features with Siberian Yupik-speaking communities across the Bering Strait. Further east, Iñupiaq grades into Canadian Inuvialuktun and Inuktitut.

Phonology and grammar

Like other Inuit languages, Iñupiaq is polysynthetic: long words are built from a root plus many derivational and inflectional suffixes, so that a single word can express what English requires a whole sentence for. It has three vowels (/a/, /i/, /u/) with length contrasts, and a consonant inventory including voiceless uvular stops and retroflex sounds in some dialects. Nouns inflect for three numbers (singular, dual, plural) and for case; verbs agree with subject and object. The basic word order is subject–object–verb.

Writing and status

Iñupiaq is written in the Latin alphabet, standardized in the 20th century with missionary and linguist involvement; the orthography uses characters such as ġ, ł, ñ, and ŋ. After decades of suppression in boarding schools, the language is severely endangered: most fluent speakers are elders. Revitalization efforts include immersion preschools ("language nests"), the Iñupiaq program at Ilisagvik College, school instruction in the North Slope Borough, and digital resources. In 2014 Alaska made Iñupiaq one of its official languages.
