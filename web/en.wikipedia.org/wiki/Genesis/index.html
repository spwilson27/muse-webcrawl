The Book of Genesis (from Greek Γένεσις, "origin"; Biblical Hebrew: בְּרֵאשִׁית, romanized: Bərēʾšīṯ, "In [the] beginning") is the first book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. Its Hebrew name comes from its opening words, "In the beginning". Genesis narrates the primeval history of the world — creation, the fall of humanity, and the flood — and the ancestral history of the Israelites through the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph.

"In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." — Genesis 1:1

Structure and contents

The book divides naturally into two parts. The primeval history (chapters 1–11) tells of the creation of the world in six days, the Garden of Eden and the Fall, Cain and Abel, the generations from Adam to Noah, the Great Flood, and the Tower of Babel. The patriarchal history (chapters 12–50) follows God's covenant with Abraham, the stories of Isaac and Jacob (renamed Israel, father of the twelve tribes), and the saga of Joseph, whose descent into Egypt sets the scene for the Exodus.

A recurring structural device is the toledot formula — "these are the generations of..." — which marks the book's major divisions ten times.

Composition

Tradition ascribes Genesis to Moses, but modern scholars regard it as a composite work assembled over centuries. The documentary hypothesis identified sources including the Yahwist (J), Elohist (E), and Priestly (P) traditions; current scholarship generally sees Genesis reaching its final form in the Persian period, around the 5th century BCE, drawing on much older oral and written traditions. Its creation and flood narratives show parallels with older Mesopotamian literature such as the Enūma Eliš and the Epic of Gilgamesh.

Themes

Central themes include creation and God's sovereignty, covenant and promise (of land and descendants), sin and its consequences, blessing and election, and the tension between God's purposes and human failing. Genesis provides the foundation for Jewish and Christian theology: the doctrines of creation ex nihilo, the image of God in humanity, and original sin all draw primarily on its opening chapters.
