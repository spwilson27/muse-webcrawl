Sankin-kōtai


A daimyō procession (daimyō gyōretsu) travelling to Edo

Sankin-kōtai (Japanese: 参覲交代, "alternate attendance") was a policy of the Tokugawa shogunate during most of the Edo period of Japanese history. The system required every daimyō (feudal lord) to spend alternating years in Edo (modern Tokyo), the shogun's capital, and in his own domain (han), and to leave his wife and heir in Edo as, in effect, hostages.

Formalised by the third shogun Tokugawa Iemitsu in the 1630s — building on earlier practice and codified in the 1635 revision of the Buke shohatto (Laws for the Military Houses) — the system lasted until 1862, when it was drastically relaxed shortly before the shogunate's fall.

Purpose and operation

The policy served several ends. By keeping the daimyō's family permanently in Edo, it deterred rebellion: a lord could not readily attack the shogunate while his heir was within the shogun's reach. The costly journeys and the maintenance of multiple Edo residences consumed a large share of domain income — often estimated at half or more of a domain's budget — limiting the funds available for military purposes. It also gave the shogunate a regular occasion to monitor the lords and demand displays of loyalty.

Fudai daimyō typically attended Edo every six months or year, while the more distantly trusted tozama daimyō of great domains might alternate full years. Lords travelled in grand processions (daimyō gyōretsu) whose size was regulated according to rank and revenue, along the great highways such as the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō, lodging at post stations (shukuba).

Effects

Although designed as a control mechanism, sankin-kōtai had wide unintended consequences. It stimulated road building, post towns, and a national market, as lords transported goods and spent money in Edo — the largest city in the world at the time largely because of their retinues. It spread Edo culture and consumption habits into the provinces and provincial products into the capital, strengthening Japan's economic and cultural integration. The burden, however, weighed heavily on domain finances, contributing to the fiscal difficulties of many han in the later Edo period and to peasant unrest.

The system was relaxed in 1862 under pressure from the imperial court and reformers, reducing attendance to a nominal obligation; the release of hostages and the lords' return to their domains weakened shogunal control in the years before the Meiji Restoration of 1868.
