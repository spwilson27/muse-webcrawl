The Bruderhof (German for "place of brothers") is a Christian movement of intentional communities founded in Germany in 1920 by Eberhard and Emmy Arnold. Drawing on Anabaptist theology and the example of the early church in Acts, members practise full community of goods, holding all property in common, and commit to nonviolence, adult (believer's) baptism, and mutual service. Bruderhof communities — called Hofs — now exist in several countries and are known for their publishing house, Plough Publishing, and their businesses producing children's equipment and community playthings.

History

Eberhard Arnold, a German theologian influenced by the Youth Movement and by the Blumhardts, began communal living with his family and followers at Sannerz in 1920. In 1930–31 Arnold was commissioned by the North American Hutterites, and the Bruderhof affiliated with the Hutterian Brethren for a period. Fleeing Nazi persecution — the Rhön Bruderhof was forcibly dissolved by the Gestapo in 1937 — members emigrated to England (the Cotswold Bruderhof), then, facing wartime internment as German nationals, to Paraguay in 1941, where they founded settlements including Primavera and a hospital serving local people. From 1954 the movement established communities in the United States (Woodcrest, in Rifton, New York, was the first), and many members later migrated from Paraguay to North America. The Bruderhof's relationship with the Hutterites was severed in 1974 and formally re-established in 2019–2020 as the two movements recognized each other again as sister communities. After 1990, communities were re-founded in Germany, and new ones opened in Australia, Austria, and South Korea.

Beliefs and way of life

Members profess the creeds of the early church, practise believer's baptism (usually around age 21 or later), and follow the Sermon on the Mount as a practical rule, including nonresistance and refusal of military service. Income from community businesses — principally Community Playthings and Rifton Equipment, which manufactures equipment for children with disabilities — is pooled; members receive no wages and are provided housing, food, and needs from the common fund. Dress is plain and modest by historic custom, meals are eaten communally, and singing is a prominent part of communal life. Membership is by lifelong vow after a novitiate period; children are raised in the community but are free to leave as adults, and many spend time outside before deciding whether to join.

Outreach

The Bruderhof is active in peace and justice work, opposing the death penalty and supporting victims of violence, and publishes books and the Plough Quarterly magazine. Members maintain relations with other Anabaptist and communal groups, including the Hutterites and Amish.
