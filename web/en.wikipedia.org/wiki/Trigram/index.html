Trigram

The word trigram has two principal meanings. In Chinese cosmology, the bagua (eight trigrams) are figures of three lines, each line solid (yang) or broken (yin), used in the I Ching and feng shui. In linguistics and computing, a trigram is a sequence of three consecutive elements — characters or words — from a text, used in statistical language modelling.

The eight trigrams (bagua)

Combining solid and broken lines in triples gives 2³ = 8 figures: Qián (heaven), Duì (lake), Lí (fire), Zhèn (thunder), Xùn (wind), Kǎn (water), Gèn (mountain), and Kūn (earth). Stacking two trigrams produces the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching. The trigrams are arranged in two traditional orders — the Earlier Heaven arrangement attributed to Fu Xi, and the Later Heaven arrangement of King Wen — and appear on the flag of South Korea (four trigrams around the taegeuk) and in Taoist and martial-arts symbolism.

Trigrams in language technology

An n-gram model estimates the probability of a word or character from its predecessors; a trigram model uses the previous two items. Trigram character models are used in spell checking, language identification, text search indexing, and plagiarism detection. The concept generalizes the bigram (two elements) and is named from the Greek tri- (three) and gramma (letter).
