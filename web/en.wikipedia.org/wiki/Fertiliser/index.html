A fertiliser (British English; American English fertilizer) is any material of natural or synthetic origin that is applied to soil or to plant tissues to supply plant nutrients. Fertilisers enhance the growth of plants by providing essential chemical elements—most importantly nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium—that may be deficient in soils.

Nutrients

Plants require macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur) and micronutrients (including iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum). Commercial fertilisers are labelled with an NPK analysis giving the percentage by weight of nitrogen, phosphorus (as P2O5) and potassium (as K2O).

History

Farmers have used manure, ash, bone and compost for millennia. In the 19th century, Justus von Liebig's mineral theory of plant nutrition and the development of superphosphate (patented by John Bennet Lawes in 1842) founded the modern fertiliser industry. The Haber–Bosch process (1909–1913) made synthetic ammonia production possible, enabling nitrogen fertilisers on an industrial scale and underpinning the Green Revolution of the 20th century.

Production and use

Synthetic nitrogen fertilisers are produced by fixing atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, which is converted into urea, ammonium nitrate and other products. Phosphate fertilisers derive from mined phosphate rock, and potassium fertilisers from potash deposits. Roughly half of the world's population is fed by crops grown with synthetic nitrogen fertiliser.

Environmental effects

Excess fertiliser use causes nutrient runoff, eutrophication of waterways, soil acidification and emissions of nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. Organic fertilisers and precision-application techniques are promoted to reduce these impacts while maintaining yields.
