El Camino Real (Spanish for "The Royal Road", also known as the California Mission Trail) is the historic road connecting the 21 Spanish missions of Alta California, along with associated presidios, pueblos and asistencias, stretching roughly 600 miles (970 km) from Mission San Diego de Alcalá in the south to Mission San Francisco Solano in Sonoma in the north.

History

The route grew out of the Spanish colonisation of California beginning in 1769 under Junípero Serra and Gaspar de Portolá. The missions were deliberately sited about a day's journey apart by horseback, so that the road linked a chain of settlements up the coast. Travel along it was on foot, horseback or by ox cart; it was less a single engineered road than a recognised trail whose precise alignment shifted with use and season. After Mexican secularisation of the missions in the 1830s, the road remained the principal north–south route of California and was heavily travelled during the Gold Rush era.

Commemoration

In 1904, inspired by the preservation movement for eastern historic trails, the El Camino Real Association was formed to mark the route. Beginning in 1906, distinctive markers were installed: a cast-iron mission bell suspended from a shepherd's-crook standard, designed by Mrs. A.S.C. Forbes and cast at her California Bell Company. Hundreds of bells were placed along the highway corridor approximating the old road; after losses to theft and roadworks, a state programme has restored and replaced many of them since the 1990s.

Modern corridor

No single modern road follows the entire historic route, but U.S. Route 101 approximates much of it between Los Angeles and San Francisco, while Interstate 280, State Route 82 and Interstate 5 cover other portions. Many California cities retain streets named El Camino Real along the historic corridor.
