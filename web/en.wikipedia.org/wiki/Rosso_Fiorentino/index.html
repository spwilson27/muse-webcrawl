Giovanni Battista di Jacopo (8 March 1494/95 – 14 November 1540), known as Rosso Fiorentino ("the Red Florentine", after his red hair), was an Italian painter and draughtsman of the early Mannerist school. Together with his contemporary Pontormo, he transformed the balanced art of the Florentine High Renaissance into something more expressive, elongated, and unsettling.

Life and work in Italy

Rosso trained in Florence, in the milieu of Andrea del Sarto's workshop, though he was never formally Sarto's pupil, according to Vasari, who gives a vivid account of his unconventional temperament. His early works, such as the Assumption of the Virgin fresco in Santissima Annunziata (1517), already show a rebellious streak. His masterpiece of the Italian years is the Deposition from the Cross (1521) in the Pinacoteca of Volterra, a jagged, wind-blown composition of grief, painted in acidic colours quite unlike the serenity of Raphael. Other major Italian works include the Marriage of the Virgin (1523) in San Lorenzo, Florence, and the brooding Moses Defending the Daughters of Jethro (c. 1523–24, Uffizi).

Rosso moved to Rome in 1524, where the work of Michelangelo deeply influenced him, but the Sack of Rome in 1527 scattered his work and drove him into itinerant years in central Italy.

Fontainebleau

In 1530 Rosso accepted the invitation of King Francis I of France and travelled to the French court, where he remained for the rest of his life. With Francesco Primaticcio he directed the decoration of the Château de Fontainebleau, above all the Gallery of Francis I (c. 1534–1539), in which fresco painting was combined for the first time with elaborate high-relief stucco frames — the founding achievement of the so-called School of Fontainebleau, which spread Mannerism across northern Europe. Rosso received a house, a generous salary, and a canonry at the Sainte-Chapelle.

Vasari relates that Rosso died by suicide in 1540 after falsely accusing a friend, the miniaturist Francesco Pellegrino, of theft; the story is now generally doubted, but the date of his death in Paris is secure.
