Witch of Endor

The Witch of Endor (Hebrew: בַּעֲלַת־אֹוב בְּעֵין דּוֹר, baʿălat-ʾōḇ bə-ʿĒyn Dōr, "she who has a familiar spirit at Endor") is a woman who, according to the Hebrew Bible, was visited by Saul, the first king of Israel, to summon the spirit of the prophet Samuel. The episode is recounted in 1 Samuel 28. She is described in the King James Version as a "woman that hath a familiar spirit"; modern translations variously render her as a medium or necromancer. The village of Endor is generally identified with the site near modern Endor in the Jezreel Valley, south-east of Nazareth.

Biblical account

Before the battle of Mount Gilboa against the Philistines, Saul sought guidance from God but received no answer "by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets". Although Saul had previously expelled mediums and spiritists from Israel, he disguised himself and visited the woman at Endor by night, asking her to bring up Samuel. The woman, fearing punishment, was reassured by Saul's oath that no harm would come to her.

When she conjured the spirit, she cried out, realising she had been deceived about her visitor's identity. She described seeing "elohim ascending out of the earth" — an old man wrapped in a mantle. Samuel's spirit rebuked Saul for disturbing him and repeated the prophecy that the kingdom would be given to David, adding that Saul and his sons would die the following day. Saul collapsed in terror. The woman and his servants persuaded him to eat — she slaughtered a fatted calf and baked unleavened bread — before he departed. The next day Saul and three of his sons died at Gilboa, as foretold.

Interpretation

In Judaism

Classical rabbinic commentators debated whether the woman truly raised Samuel or whether the encounter was a fraud or a vision. The medieval commentator Radak and others accepted the event at face value, while some rationalists held that the medium deceived Saul. The Talmud (Shabbat 152b–153a) discusses necromancy in connection with the passage.

In Christianity

Church Fathers including Origen took the account literally, arguing that Samuel genuinely appeared; others, such as Eustathius of Antioch, argued that a demon impersonated the prophet, since a holy soul could not be subject to a witch. King James VI and I devoted part of his treatise Daemonologie (1597) to the episode, treating it as genuine necromancy and evidence of the reality of witchcraft.

In later culture

The story has inspired numerous works of art and literature, including paintings by Salvator Rosa and Benjamin West, and treatments in Handel's oratorio Saul (1739). The figure is sometimes called the "Witch of Endor" in occult literature as an archetype of the necromancer.
