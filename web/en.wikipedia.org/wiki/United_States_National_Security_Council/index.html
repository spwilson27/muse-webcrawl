United States National Security Council

Agency overview
Formed: September 18, 1947
Headquarters: Eisenhower Executive Office Building, Washington, D.C.
Parent agency: Executive Office of the President

The United States National Security Council (NSC) is the principal forum used by the President of the United States for deliberating national security and foreign policy matters with senior advisors and cabinet officials, and is part of the Executive Office of the President.

History

The NSC was created by the National Security Act of 1947, signed by President Harry S. Truman, which also established the Central Intelligence Agency and unified the armed services into the National Military Establishment (later the Department of Defense). The council began meeting in September 1947.

During the Korean War, Truman used the NSC extensively to coordinate war policy. President Dwight D. Eisenhower formalized its structure, creating the post of National Security Advisor and a regular weekly meeting schedule. Under later presidents the council's role waxed and waned: it was curtailed under John F. Kennedy, enlarged under Richard Nixon and his advisor Henry Kissinger, and became central to policy formulation during the Cold War.

Composition

Statutory members are the President (chair), the Vice President, the Secretary of State, the Secretary of Defense, and the Secretary of Energy. Regular attendees include the National Security Advisor, the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Director of National Intelligence, and the Director of the Central Intelligence Agency. A Principals Committee and Deputies Committee meet without the President to prepare options.

Role

The NSC staff, led by the National Security Advisor, coordinates interagency policy, prepares decision memoranda, and monitors implementation. The council's most famous venue is the White House Situation Room, created in 1961 after the Bay of Pigs Invasion highlighted communications failures.
