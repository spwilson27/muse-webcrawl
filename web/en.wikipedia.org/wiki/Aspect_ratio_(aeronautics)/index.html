Aspect ratio (aeronautics)

High aspect ratio wings, as on gliders, reduce induced drag

In aeronautics, the aspect ratio of a wing is the ratio of its span to its mean chord. It is equal to the square of the wingspan divided by the wing area: AR = b²/S, where b is the span and S the wing area. Aspect ratio is one of the principal parameters determining a wing's aerodynamic efficiency.

Effect on performance

A high aspect ratio wing — long and narrow — produces less induced drag for a given amount of lift, because the wingtip vortices are proportionally weaker. This is why gliders have very long, slender wings with aspect ratios of 20 to 40 or more. The trade-offs are structural: a long wing bends more and must be built stronger and heavier, it is less manoeuvrable in roll, and it takes up more space on the ground.

Typical values

Airliners have aspect ratios of roughly 7 to 10, increased in recent designs by the use of composite structures and winglets; the Boeing 787 is around 11. Fighter aircraft, by contrast, have low aspect ratios of about 2 to 4, favouring strength, roll rate and compactness over cruise efficiency. Birds show a similar spread: albatrosses, specialised for oceanic soaring, have high aspect ratio wings, while birds of prey that manoeuvre in woodland have short, broad wings.
