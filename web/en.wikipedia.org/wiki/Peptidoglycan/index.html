Peptidoglycan

Peptidoglycan
Also calledMurein
ComponentsN-acetylglucosamine (NAG), N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM), peptide cross-links
LocationBacterial cell wall
FunctionMechanical strength, shape, osmotic protection

Peptidoglycan (also called murein) is a polymer, specifically a polysaccharide, that consists of sugars and amino acids that forms a mesh-like, stress-bearing layer, thicker in Gram-positive bacteria, that protects the cell by providing structural strength and by counteracting the osmotic pressure of the cytoplasm. It is found in essentially all bacterial cell walls and is absent from the cell walls of archaea and eukaryotes, making it a classic target for antibiotics.

Structure

The peptidoglycan layer consists of long chains of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) residues linked by β-(1,4) glycosidic bonds. Attached to each NAM is a short peptide stem of four or five amino acids — characteristically including the unusual D-amino acids and meso-diaminopimelic acid or lysine — and adjacent stems are cross-linked, directly or through a short peptide bridge, weaving the glycan chains into a single enormous net-like macromolecule (the "sacculus") that surrounds the whole cell.

Distribution

In Gram-positive bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thick (roughly 20–80 nm, many layers) and interwoven with teichoic acids, lying outside the cytoplasmic membrane. In Gram-negative bacteria it is thin (a few nanometres) and sits in the periplasm between the inner membrane and an outer membrane. This difference underlies the Gram stain: the thick Gram-positive wall retains the crystal violet dye.

Synthesis and antibiotic targets

Peptidoglycan is assembled from the lipid-linked precursor Lipid II, which is flipped across the membrane and polymerised by transglycosylases, with cross-linking performed by transpeptidases known as penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillin inhibit the PBPs, weakening the wall so that growing bacteria lyse; the glycopeptide vancomycin instead binds the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the peptide stem. The enzyme lysozyme, found in tears and saliva, hydrolyses the NAG–NAM bond and is part of innate immunity. Fragments of peptidoglycan are also recognised by the innate immune receptors NOD1 and NOD2.
