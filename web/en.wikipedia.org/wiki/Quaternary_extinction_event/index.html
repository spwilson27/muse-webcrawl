Quaternary extinction event

The Quaternary extinction event (also called the late Quaternary extinctions) is the wave of extinctions of mainly large animals — megafauna — that occurred during the Quaternary period, especially in the last 50,000 years or so of the Late Pleistocene and into the Holocene. Losses include the woolly mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, giant ground sloths, the short-faced bear, the saber-toothed cat Smilodon, giant kangaroos and the moa.

Pattern

The extinctions were strongly size-selective: most terrestrial mammal genera heavier than about 44 kg disappeared from the Americas, Australia and northern Eurasia, while Africa and southern Asia retained far more of their megafauna. Timing differed by region: Australian megafauna vanished around 50,000–40,000 years ago, American losses concentrated near 13,000 years ago, and island faunas — mammoths on Wrangel Island, moa in New Zealand, elephant birds in Madagascar — survived until human arrival, in some cases within the last few thousand years.

Causes

Two main hypotheses compete and may have acted in combination. The overkill hypothesis, championed by Paul S. Martin, attributes the extinctions to hunting by newly arrived human populations on fauna that had evolved without human predators. The climate hypothesis points to the rapid warming and habitat change at the end of the last glacial period, including the Younger Dryas. Supporting evidence includes the close correlation worldwide between human arrival and extinction, kill sites and cut-marked bones, and simulation studies; counterarguments note secure evidence of hunting for only some species and the survival of some large animals alongside humans for millennia. Many researchers now favour synergy between hunting pressure and climate stress.

Consequences

The loss of megafauna reshaped ecosystems: vegetation structure changed without giant browsers and grazers, seed dispersal of large-fruited plants declined, and nutrient transport by animal herds collapsed in some regions. The event is often treated as the opening phase of the ongoing Holocene extinction driven by human activity.
