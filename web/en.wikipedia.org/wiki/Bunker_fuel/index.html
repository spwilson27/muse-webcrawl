Bunker fuel

Bunker fuel is the generic term for the fuel oils used to power ships, derived from the coal bunkers of steamships where later fuel tanks inherited the name. The principal grade is heavy fuel oil (HFO) — residual fuel oil left after the lighter fractions of crude oil have been distilled or cracked — graded under ISO 8217, with the common IFO 380 and IFO 180 (named for their kinematic viscosity in centistokes at 50 °C) long dominating ocean shipping. Lighter marine distillates include marine diesel oil (MDO) and marine gas oil (MGO).

Properties and handling

Residual bunker fuel is a thick, tar-like liquid that must be heated to around 120–150 °C to flow and be atomised in the engine. It is cheap but dirty, containing ash, vanadium, asphaltènes and historically up to 3.5% sulphur. Aboard ship it is stored in bunker tanks, settled, centrifuged to remove water and solids, and supplied to slow-speed two-stroke diesel engines or boilers.

Regulation

Under MARPOL Annex VI, the International Maritime Organization progressively tightened sulphur limits in emission control areas and, from 1 January 2020 ("IMO 2020"), capped the sulphur content of fuel used worldwide at 0.50% m/m, down from 3.50%. Owners complied by switching to very-low-sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO) blends, installing exhaust gas scrubbers to keep using HSFO, or adopting alternatives such as LNG and methanol.

Markets

Bunkers are bought in major bunkering ports — Singapore is the world's largest, followed by hubs such as Rotterdam and Fujairah — and fuel is commonly the single largest operating cost of a vessel. Contaminated-fuel incidents, notably in Houston in 2018 and Singapore in 2022, have repeatedly drawn attention to the opaque quality of blends.
