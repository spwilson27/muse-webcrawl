Frankokratia

The Frankokratia (Greek: Φραγκοκρατία, "rule of the Franks"), also known as the Latinokratia, is the period in Greek history after the Fourth Crusade (1204) when a number of Western European — mainly French and Italian — states were established on the territory of the dissolved Byzantine Empire. The term derives from the fact that Orthodox Greeks called Western Europeans "Franks" or "Latins".

Begins: 1204 (Fourth Crusade)
Ends: variously 15th–18th centuries
Major states: Latin Empire, Kingdom of Thessalonica, Principality of Achaea, Duchy of Athens, Venetian Crete

Establishment

The sack of Constantinople in April 1204 and the Partitio Romaniae treaty divided the Byzantine lands among the crusaders and Venice. The resulting states included the Latin Empire of Constantinople, the Kingdom of Thessalonica, the Principality of Achaea (the Morea), the Duchy of Athens, and numerous Venetian colonies such as Crete, Euboea (Negroponte) and the Ionian Islands.

Rule and society

The Latin rulers imposed Western feudal structures — codified for the Morea in the Assizes of Romania — and a Latin church hierarchy over the largely Greek Orthodox population, which remained in a subordinate position. The Chronicle of the Morea is a principal source for the period. Nevertheless the Frankish principalities flourished culturally, and the Principality of Achaea survived until 1432.

Decline

The Byzantine recovery of Constantinople in 1261 ended the Latin Empire. Over the following centuries the remaining Latin possessions fell piecemeal to the restored Byzantines, the Catalan Company, the Ottoman Empire and Venice. Some island possessions lasted far longer: Venetian Crete until 1669, the Ionian Islands until 1797, and tiny Tinos until 1715. In Greek historical memory the Frankokratia parallels the later Tourkokratia, the period of Ottoman rule.
