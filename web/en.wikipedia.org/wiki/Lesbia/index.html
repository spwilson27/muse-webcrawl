Lesbia is the literary pseudonym of the beloved addressed in the love poems of the Roman poet Gaius Valerius Catullus (c. 84 – c. 54 BC). She dominates the surviving corpus: of Catullus's 116 poems, some two dozen are addressed to or about her, tracing an affair that moves from ecstatic devotion through jealousy and reconciliation to bitter disillusion.

Lesbia with her pet sparrow, subject of Catullus 2 and 3.

Identity

Following a hint in Apuleius, scholars have traditionally identified Lesbia with Clodia, wife of the consul Quintus Caecilius Metellus Celer and sister of Cicero's enemy Clodius Pulcher — the Clodia whom Cicero savaged in his speech Pro Caelio (56 BC). The identification fits the chronology and Clodia's reputation, though it cannot be proved and modern scholars often stress that Lesbia is above all a literary construction. The pseudonym itself honours Sappho of Lesbos, "the Lesbian", whose lyric Catullus translated in poem 51 ("Ille mi par esse deo videtur").

The Lesbia cycle

The poems include the joyful counting of kisses in poem 5 ("Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus" — "Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love") and poem 7; the mourning of Lesbia's sparrow (poem 3); the tortured brevity of poem 85, "Odi et amo" ("I hate and I love"); and finally the obscene contempt of poem 58, addressed to "Caelius" — Marcus Caelius Rufus, another of Clodia's lovers, defended by Cicero.

Legacy

Lesbia became the archetype of the poet's unattainable mistress in European lyric, echoed from the Roman elegists through Petrarch's Laura to the present. The Lesbos connection of her name also contributed to the later association of the island — home of Sappho and of Alcaeus — with female love poetry.
