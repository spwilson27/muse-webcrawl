Frederick Barbarossa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Cappenberg head, a gilded reliquary bust of Frederick Barbarossa]

Frederick I (1122 – 10 June 1190), nicknamed Barbarossa ('red beard' in Italian), was Holy Roman Emperor from 1155 until his death — one of the most celebrated medieval German rulers. Elected king in 1152, he sought to restore imperial authority in Italy and Germany alike: six expeditions across the Alps, war with the Lombard League (defeated at Legnano, 1176) and the papacy, the humbling of his over-mighty cousin Henry the Lion, and a legal revival of Roman law all marked his reign. Answering Saladin's capture of Jerusalem, he led the largest contingent of the Third Crusade east in 1189 — and drowned crossing the Saleph (Göksu) River in Cilicia, at about sixty-eight, before reaching the Holy Land.

Emperor and Italy

Frederick's programme — the honor imperii, the recovery of imperial rights (regalia) codified by Bolognese jurists at Roncaglia (1158) — collided with the Italian communes' de facto independence. Milan was destroyed (1162) and rebuilt; the Lombard League, allied with Pope Alexander III, beat the imperial army at Legnano (1176, the first great victory of communal infantry over knights). The Peace of Constance (1183) conceded the cities their autonomy in all but name. In Germany Frederick ruled by balancing princes: he broke Henry the Lion of Saxony and Bavaria (1180) and partitioned his duchies, while (by the Privilegium Minus, 1156) Austria had been raised to a duchy for his Babenberg uncle.

Crusade and legend

At the Diet of Mainz (1188) Frederick took the cross; his army marched overland through Hungary and Byzantium — fighting Isaac II Angelos' obstruction — and defeated the Seljuks at Iconium (1190). On 10 June 1190 he drowned in the Saleph (whether bathing, fording, or by heart failure in the cold water, the sources differ); his army dissolved, only a remnant reaching Acre with his son Frederick of Swabia, who died there too. In German legend Barbarossa did not die: he sleeps in the Kyffhäuser mountain with his beard growing through the table, to wake when the empire needs him — a myth transferred to him from his grandson Frederick II and revived by 19th-century nationalism (the Kyffhäuser monument, 1896).
