Recidivism

Recidivism (from Latin recidivus, "recurring") is the act of a person repeating an undesirable behaviour after they have experienced negative consequences of that behaviour, or have been trained to extinguish it. In criminology, it usually refers to reoffending by a person who has previously been convicted, and is measured by rearrest, reconviction, or return to prison within a set period after release.

Measurement

Recidivism rates vary widely between countries and according to definitions used. Studies of released prisoners in the United States have found that a substantial proportion — often around two-thirds within three years in Bureau of Justice Statistics cohorts — are rearrested. Norway is frequently cited for much lower rates, around 20–25 percent within two to five years, attributed to its rehabilitative penal model and shorter sentences.

Factors

Reoffending is associated with factors such as age at first conviction, substance misuse, unemployment, unstable housing, and the criminogenic effect of imprisonment itself. Interventions shown to reduce recidivism include cognitive behavioural programmes, drug treatment, education and vocational training in prison, stable post-release supervision under probation or parole, and restorative justice schemes.

Policy debates

Recidivism statistics are central to debates between deterrence-based and rehabilitation-based penal policy. High rates are used to argue both for longer incapacitating sentences and for greater investment in reintegration; the measure chosen (rearrest versus reconviction) can substantially change the apparent outcome.
