Stark effect

The Stark effect is the shifting and splitting of spectral lines of atoms and molecules, due to the presence of an external electric field. It is the electric-field analogue of the Zeeman effect, where a spectral line is split into several components due to the presence of a magnetic field. It was discovered in 1913 by the German physicist Johannes Stark, who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1919 for the discovery.

Description

The effect can be explained with both classical and quantum mechanical theories. In perturbation theory, the interaction of the atom's dipole moment with the external field shifts its energy levels; in hydrogen, degenerate states mix, giving a linear Stark effect in which splitting is proportional to the field strength, whereas most other atoms show a quadratic effect proportional to the square of the field. The Schrödinger equation for hydrogen in an electric field is separable in parabolic coordinates, allowing exact calculation of the linear effect.

Applications

The Stark effect is used to measure electric fields in plasmas, in Stark spectroscopy and electroabsorption studies of molecules, and in the characterization of semiconductors and quantum dots; Stark broadening of spectral lines is also an important diagnostic in astrophysics for stellar atmospheres.
