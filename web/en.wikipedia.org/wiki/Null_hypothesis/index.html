Null hypothesis

In statistical hypothesis testing, the null hypothesis (denoted H0) is the claim that there is no relationship between two measured phenomena or no association among groups — that any observed difference or effect is due to chance alone. It is the default position that a researcher attempts to disprove in favour of an alternative hypothesis (H1), which asserts that a real effect exists.

Testing

A test statistic is computed from sample data, and the p-value — the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true — is compared with a pre-chosen significance level α, conventionally 0.05. If the p-value falls below α, the null hypothesis is rejected as implausible. Rejecting a true null hypothesis is a Type I error; failing to reject a false null hypothesis is a Type II error, whose probability is related to the statistical power of the test.

History and criticism

The framework arose from the work of Ronald Fisher, who introduced significance testing and the p-value in the 1920s, and of Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, who formalized the alternative hypothesis, error rates, and decision rules. The hybrid procedure used in practice blends the two approaches. The null hypothesis is never "proved" by a test — data can only fail to reject it — and modern critics have attacked the ritualized use of the 0.05 threshold, misinterpretation of p-values, and the neglect of effect sizes and confidence intervals, contributing to the replication crisis in some sciences.

Examples

Typical null hypotheses include: a new drug has the same effect as a placebo; two population means are equal; a coin is fair (probability of heads = 0.5); observed data follow a specified distribution.
