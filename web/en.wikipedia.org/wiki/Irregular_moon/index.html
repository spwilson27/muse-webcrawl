Irregular moon

An irregular moon, or irregular satellite, is a natural satellite following a distant, inclined and frequently retrograde orbit around its planet. Unlike regular moons, which are thought to have formed in orbit from a circumplanetary disk, irregular moons are believed to be captured asteroids, comets or fragments of such bodies.

Characteristics

Irregular moons orbit far from their planets — often tens of millions of kilometres — with high orbital eccentricities and inclinations. Roughly half orbit retrograde (opposite to the planet's rotation), which makes those orbits stable at greater distances. Their radii are generally only a few kilometres to a few tens of kilometres; the largest irregular moon is Neptune's Triton, a captured Kuiper belt object, followed by Saturn's Phoebe at about 213 km across.

Families and origins

Many irregular moons cluster into families with similar orbits and colours, interpreted as the fragments of a single captured body shattered by collisions. Well-known groups include Jupiter's Himalia (prograde) and Pasiphae and Carme (retrograde) groups, and Saturn's Inuit, Gallic and Norse groups. Jupiter and Saturn each possess well over a hundred known irregular moons, most discovered since 2000 by deep surveys; the total counts of known moons of the giant planets are dominated by these small captured bodies.
