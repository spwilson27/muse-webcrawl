The Pike Committee was the common name of the United States House Select Committee on Intelligence, chaired by Representative Otis G. Pike of New York, which investigated the activities of America's intelligence agencies during 1975–76. It was the House of Representatives' counterpart to the Senate's Church Committee, created in the wake of the Watergate scandal and revelations by journalist Seymour Hersh of CIA domestic spying.

The House Select Committee on Intelligence under Chairman Otis Pike, 1975

Investigation

The committee examined the budgets, performance and abuses of the Central Intelligence Agency, the Federal Bureau of Investigation and the National Security Agency. Its inquiries covered covert operations, the accuracy of intelligence estimates, and the cost-effectiveness of the intelligence community. Relations with the Ford administration were strained; Secretary of State Henry Kissinger and the CIA resisted document requests, and the committee's demand for classified material led to a confrontation over congressional access to executive-branch secrets.

The suppressed report

In January 1976 the full House voted 246–124 not to release the committee's final report unless material objected to by the President was removed. A copy was leaked to journalist Daniel Schorr, who passed it to The Village Voice, which published extensive extracts in February 1976. The leak provoked an ethics investigation of Schorr and remains a landmark episode in the history of press freedom and congressional oversight. The Pike Committee's work, together with the Church Committee's, led to the creation of permanent intelligence oversight committees in both houses of Congress.
