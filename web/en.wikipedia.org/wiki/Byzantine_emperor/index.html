The Byzantine emperor was the ruler of the Byzantine Empire (the medieval continuation of the Roman Empire in its eastern provinces) from the empire's conventional beginnings under Constantine I until its fall to the Ottoman Turks in 1453. The emperor was simultaneously head of state, commander-in-chief of the army, and the supreme authority in the Eastern Orthodox Church's affairs in practice, embodying an ideology in which imperial power was understood as delegated by God.

Titles and ideology

Early rulers used the Latin titles imperator, augustus, and caesar, and their Greek equivalents. From the 7th century the characteristic title was basileus (βασιλεύς, "king/emperor"), formally adopted by Heraclius in 629, often expanded to "basileus and autokrator of the Romans" (basileus kai autokratōr tōn Rhōmaiōn) — the emperors always insisted they were Roman emperors, not a separate "Byzantine" monarchy. Imperial ideology held that there was one God, one faith, and one emperor on earth; the emperor was God's vicegerent, "equal of the apostles" (isapostolos), crowned by the Patriarch of Constantinople in Hagia Sophia yet in principle deriving authority from God, the senate, the army, and the people.

Succession

The empire never developed a strict law of primogeniture. Succession was hereditary in tendency — dynasties such as the Macedonian, Komnenian, and Palaiologan houses supplied long runs of emperors — but was punctuated by usurpations, military coups, and palace intrigues. Emperors secured their heirs by crowning them co-emperor in childhood; rivals were neutralised by blinding or tonsuring, mutilation being held to disqualify a man from the throne. Legitimacy could also be acquired by marrying an imperial widow or a porphyrogennete princess — one "born in the purple" chamber of the Great Palace.

Powers and court

The emperor legislated by issuing novels (new laws), commanded the military, directed diplomacy, controlled the bureaucracy and taxation, and summoned and presided over church councils. Court life was elaborately ceremonial — codified in Constantine VII's De Ceremoniis — with the emperor robed in purple silk and the double-headed eagle displayed as an imperial emblem in later centuries. Administration ran through great offices including the logothetes of the drome and the general logothete, eunuch chamberlains, and, in the provinces, the generals (strategoi) of the themes.

From Constantine to Constantine

The line runs from Constantine I, who refounded Byzantium as Constantinople in 330, through Justinian I, whose reconquests and law code marked an early apex; Heraclius, who faced the Persian and Arab onslaughts; Leo III and the iconoclast emperors; Basil II, the "Bulgar-slayer," under whom the empire reached its medieval zenith around 1025; Alexios I Komnenos, who managed recovery after Manzikert and received the First Crusade; and finally the Palaiologan emperors, who ruled a shrinking state after the catastrophe of the Fourth Crusade (1204) until Constantine XI died defending Constantinople on 29 May 1453.
