Third Age

SettingJ. R. R. Tolkien's Middle-earth
Duration3,021 years
BeginsDefeat of Sauron by the Last Alliance and Isildur's taking of the One Ring
EndsDestruction of the Ring and departure of the Ring-bearers

The Third Age is a period in J. R. R. Tolkien’s fictional history of Middle-earth. It begins after the War of the Last Alliance, when Sauron is overthrown but the One Ring is not destroyed, and ends 3,021 years later with the Ring’s destruction, Sauron’s final fall and the departure of Frodo, Bilbo, Gandalf, Elrond and Galadriel from the Grey Havens. The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings are set near its close.

Chronology and character

Unlike the mythic First Age of the Silmarils and the Elvish wars against Morgoth, the Third Age is an age of decline, waiting and hidden endurance. The great Elven realms diminish; many Elves sail west. Númenórean kingdoms in exile—Arnor in the north and Gondor in the south—preserve law, lineage and memory but are gradually worn down by plague, civil war, invasion and the long strategy of Sauron. The age’s drama is therefore often defensive: keeping roads, archives, bloodlines and hope alive until an unlooked-for resolution.

Major events

The early centuries see the Disaster of the Gladden Fields, where Isildur is killed and the Ring is lost in the Anduin. Arnor fragments into Arthedain, Cardolan and Rhudaur and eventually falls to the Witch-king of Angmar, while Gondor survives attacks by Wainriders, Corsairs and plague and loses its last king, Eärnur, to Minas Morgul in 2050. The Wizards arrive around the year 1000 as emissaries of the Valar. The Watchful Peace ends when Sauron returns to Dol Guldur; the White Council drives him out in 2941, the year of Bilbo Baggins’s journey to Erebor, but Sauron merely withdraws to Mordor prepared.

The final years compress the age’s themes. Gandalf identifies Bilbo’s ring as the One Ring; the War of the Ring brings battles at Helm’s Deep, Minas Tirith and the Black Gate; Aragorn is crowned King Elessar; and the Ring is destroyed when Gollum falls into the Fire of Mount Doom. The Fourth Age, the dominion of Men, begins.

Peoples and themes

Hobbits enter recorded history during the Third Age, migrating into Eriador and settling the Shire in 1601. Their obscurity becomes providential: Sauron’s attention is fixed on kings, wizards and warriors, not on a small rural people whose virtues are domestic and stubborn. Tolkien contrasts fading Elvish beauty and Gondorian stone with Hobbit gardens and meals, suggesting that the future belongs not only to heirs of ancient kingdoms but to ordinary creatures capable of pity and endurance.

Sources

Within the fiction, much Third Age history is preserved in Gondorian annals, the Red Book of Westmarch and Elvish records. In Tolkien’s published work, the narrative core appears in The Lord of the Rings and its Appendix A and B annals, with background in The Hobbit, Unfinished Tales and the later volumes of The History of Middle-earth.
