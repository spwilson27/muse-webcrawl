Richard Trevithick

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A replica of Trevithick's 1804 Pen-y-darren locomotive on a tramroad]
Replica of the Pen-y-darren locomotive.

Richard Trevithick (1771–1833) was a Cornish inventor and mining engineer who built the first full-scale working railway steam locomotive. A pioneer of high-pressure steam — \'strong steam\' — he demonstrated a road locomotive in 1801 (the Puffing Devil) and in 1804 hauled iron 16 km along the Pen-y-darren tramroad in Wales, the first recorded journey by a self-propelled railway locomotive.

High-pressure steam

Where Boulton & Watt used low-pressure steam with a separate condenser, Trevithick used compact high-pressure engines without condensers, exhausting steam up the chimney — smaller, lighter and suitable for vehicles. Watt\'s patent and safety fears (a boiler explosion at Greenwich killed four in 1803) shadowed his work.

Railway firsts

Commissioned by ironmaster Samuel Homfray, Trevithick\'s 1804 Pen-y-darren locomotive hauled 10 tons of iron and some 70 men, winning a bet, though it broke the cast-iron rails. In 1808 his Catch Me Who Can ran passenger rides on a circular track in London — the first steam locomotive to carry paying passengers.

Later life

Trevithick took his engines to Peru and Costa Rica for mining, lost his fortune in revolutionary wars, and returned penniless in 1827 (helped home, legend says, by Robert Stephenson). He died in Dartford in 1833 and was buried in an unmarked grave; his importance was recognised only later.
