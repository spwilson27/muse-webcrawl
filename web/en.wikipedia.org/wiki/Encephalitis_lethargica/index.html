Encephalitis lethargica

Encephalitis lethargica is an atypical form of encephalitis which caused an epidemic between 1916 and 1927. The disease was first described by the Austrian neurologist Constantin von Economo, and is sometimes known as von Economo's encephalitis or "sleepy sickness". It attacks the brain, leaving some victims in a statue-like condition, speechless and motionless. Between 1916 and 1927, a pandemic of encephalitis lethargica spread around the world, affecting an estimated one million people; roughly a third of those affected died in the acute phase.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include high fever, sore throat, headache, lethargy, double vision, delayed physical and mental response, sleep inversion and catatonia. In severe cases, patients may enter a coma-like state. Many survivors developed post-encephalitic parkinsonism months or years later, sometimes becoming trapped in rigid, motionless states for decades. The neurologist Oliver Sacks described such patients, and their temporary awakening with the drug L-DOPA, in his book Awakenings (1973).

Cause

The cause of encephalitis lethargica is unknown. The epidemic coincided with the 1918 influenza pandemic, leading to speculation that the two were linked, but modern molecular studies have not confirmed a direct connection. Some researchers have suggested an autoimmune mechanism, in which the immune system attacks the brain's basal ganglia.

History

The disease appeared in Europe in the winter of 1916–17 and spread worldwide over the following decade. No epidemic on the same scale has recurred, although isolated cases continue to be reported. Since 1940, only sporadic cases have been described.
