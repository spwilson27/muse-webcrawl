Dryland


Semi-arid dryland in a rain-shadow region.

Drylands are regions where precipitation is low relative to potential evaporation, conventionally defined by an aridity index (the ratio of annual precipitation to potential evapotranspiration) below 0.65. They include hyper-arid deserts, arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones, and cover roughly 40 per cent of the Earth's land surface, supporting more than two billion people.

Characteristics

Dryland climates are marked by low and highly variable rainfall, high temperatures and evaporation, and sparse vegetation adapted to water scarcity, from desert shrubs to grasslands and savannas. Soils are often thin, saline or low in organic matter. Despite their aridity, drylands host significant biodiversity, including many endemic species, and provide grazing land, dryland farming (relying on rainfall rather than irrigation), minerals and growing solar-energy potential.

Degradation

Drylands are vulnerable to land degradation and desertification driven by overgrazing, unsustainable cultivation, deforestation and climate change. The United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification, adopted in 1994, is the principal international framework addressing dryland degradation, and land degradation neutrality has become a target under the Sustainable Development Goals. Restoration techniques include farmer-managed natural regeneration, zaï pits and stone bunds that harvest scarce rainfall, as practised in parts of the Sahel.
