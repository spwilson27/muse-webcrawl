Reporter gene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cells expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP), one of the most widely used reporter genes.

In molecular biology, a reporter gene (often simply reporter) is a gene that researchers attach to a regulatory sequence of another gene of interest in bacteria, cell culture, animals or plants. Reporter genes are used as indicators of whether a certain gene has been taken up by or expressed in the cell or organism population. To be useful, the reporter must produce a signal that is easily and selectively measurable and that the host does not normally produce — typically an enzyme whose activity can be assayed, or a fluorescent protein that can be seen directly.

Uses

Promoter and regulatory analysis

The most common application is to fuse a promoter or other regulatory element to the reporter coding sequence: the amount of reporter signal then quantifies the element's activity under different conditions, deletions, or mutations. Dual-reporter assays (for example firefly plus Renilla luciferase) add an internal control for transfection efficiency.

Transfection and transformation markers

Reporters allow researchers to identify which cells received a DNA construct and to measure how efficiently it is expressed, complementing selectable markers that only record survival. In whole organisms, knock-in reporters (such as lacZ inserted into an endogenous locus in mice) reveal where and when a gene is normally active during development.

Protein localisation and interactions

Fluorescent reporters fused to a protein of interest let its location and movement be followed in living cells; variants such as split-GFP and FRET pairs report protein–protein interactions. Reporter strains of pathogens and environmental microbes are used to track colonisation and gene expression in vivo.

Considerations

Reporter expression can differ from that of the endogenous gene, because the construct omits distant enhancers, chromatin context, and post-transcriptional controls; reporters with long-lived products (such as GFP) also report past rather than present activity, which destabilised reporter variants address. Controls for autofluorescence, substrate availability and reporter copy number are standard in quantitative work.
