The Imperial Harem (Ottoman Turkish: Harem-i Hümâyûn) was the household of women — wives, concubines, female relatives and servants — of the Ottoman sultan, housed in the secluded harem section of Topkapı Palace in Constantinople. Far from a mere residence, it was a hierarchical institution that educated its members, managed dynastic reproduction, and, especially in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, exerted substantial influence over imperial politics.

Organisation

The harem was headed by the valide sultan, the sultan's mother, the most powerful woman of the empire. Below her ranked the haseki sultan (chief consort), other favoured consorts (kadıns and ikbals), the sultan's daughters and sisters, and hundreds of female slaves and servants (cariyes) who received training in music, letters, needlework and court etiquette, and could rise in rank or be married to officials. The harem was guarded and administered by black eunuchs under the Chief Black Eunuch (Kızlar Ağası), one of the highest officials of the palace.

History and political role

The harem took shape as the court settled in Topkapı in the later fifteenth century, and its influence peaked in the era historians call the "Sultanate of Women" (c. 1533–1656), when valide sultans such as Hürrem Sultan (Roxelana), Nurbanu, Safiye and Kösem Sultan acted as regents and power brokers. The institution declined with the empire's transformation in the nineteenth century, and it was finally dispersed in 1909 after the deposition of Abdülhamid II.

Daily life and culture

Life in the harem followed strict hierarchy and ceremonial. Its apartments — some 400 rooms around courtyards in Topkapı, decorated with İznik tiles and mother-of-pearl inlay — included baths, mosques, a hospital and the apartments of the valide sultan and the princes, who in earlier centuries were confined in the kafes ("cage").
