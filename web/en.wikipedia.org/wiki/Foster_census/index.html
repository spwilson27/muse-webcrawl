Foster census

In ancient Rome, a foster census (Latin: lustrum in related contexts, though the term foster census is used by some historians for censorial registration) refers to the registration of citizens and their property conducted by the censors for the purposes of taxation, military levy, and the assignment of citizens to voting centuries and tribes. More generally, historians use the term for the periodic Roman census in which adult male citizens declared themselves, their families, and their property before the censors.

The Roman census

The census was traditionally instituted by King Servius Tullius in the 6th century BC and was conducted by the Roman censors, a pair of senior magistrates elected roughly every five years from the reign of the Republic onward. Citizens who failed to register could lose their property and civic rights. The ceremony concluded with the lustrum, a purification sacrifice that gave its name to the five-year interval between censuses.

Figures

Livy and other ancient sources preserve census totals for the Republic, rising from roughly 130,000 registered citizens in the early Republic to about 900,000 in the census of 70/69 BC; Augustus recorded totals of over four million citizens in the Res Gestae, reflecting the extension of citizenship across Italy and changed counting methods. Modern historians debate how far these figures can be used for demographic reconstruction — the well-known "low count" versus "high count" controversy in Roman population studies.
