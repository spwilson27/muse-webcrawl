French invasion of Russia

The catastrophic retreat from Moscow destroyed the Grande Armée.

The French invasion of Russia, also known as the Russian campaign (French: Campagne de Russie) and, in Russia, the Patriotic War of 1812, began on 24 June 1812 when Napoleon Bonaparte's Grande Armée crossed the Niemen River into the Russian Empire. Napoleon sought to force Tsar Alexander I back into the Continental System and end Russian defiance of French policy; instead the campaign ended in disaster and marked the turning point of the Napoleonic Wars.

The advance

The Grande Armée, numbering roughly 450,000–600,000 men drawn from across French-dominated Europe, was the largest army yet assembled in European history. The Russian armies under Barclay de Tolly and Bagration retreated deep into Russia, avoiding decisive battle and scorching the earth as they went. After the costly and indecisive Battle of Borodino on 7 September 1812, Napoleon entered a deserted Moscow on 14 September; fires broke out that night and destroyed most of the city.

The retreat

Alexander refused to negotiate, and on 19 October Napoleon began the retreat westward along the already-devastated Smolensk road. Harried by Cossacks, partisan attacks, starvation, disease and eventually the Russian winter, the army disintegrated. The crossing of the Berezina River in late November was a final catastrophe narrowly avoiding total annihilation. Of the forces that had entered Russia, only a small fraction — perhaps 100,000 or fewer — recrossed the frontier in December.

Consequences

The destruction of the Grande Armée emboldened Prussia, Austria and other states to join Russia in the Sixth Coalition. Defeated at Leipzig in 1813 and invaded in 1814, Napoleon abdicated in April 1814. The campaign has remained a cultural touchstone, commemorated in Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture and Tolstoy's War and Peace, and Charles Joseph Minard's famous flow map of the army's losses.
