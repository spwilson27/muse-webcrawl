Richard Georg Strauss (11 June 1864 – 8 September 1949) was a German composer and conductor best known for his tone poems and operas. Considered a leading composer of the late Romantic and early modern eras, he was a successor to Richard Wagner and Franz Liszt, and his music — from the modernist shock of Salome and Elektra to the nostalgic lyricism of Der Rosenkavalier — dominated German musical life for more than half a century.

Early life

Strauss was born in Munich, the son of Franz Strauss, principal horn player of the Munich Court Opera. He began composing at six and had works performed while still a schoolboy. In the mid-1880s he came under the influence of Alexander Ritter, who converted him to the music of Wagner and Liszt and to the idea of the symphonic poem.

Tone poems

Between 1888 and 1903 Strauss produced the series of tone poems that established his fame: Don Juan, Death and Transfiguration, Till Eulenspiegel's Merry Pranks, Also sprach Zarathustra (whose opening "Sunrise" became famous through the film 2001: A Space Odyssey), Don Quixote, Ein Heldenleben, and the Symphonia Domestica, culminating later in An Alpine Symphony (1915).

Operas

After the success of Salome (1905) and Elektra (1909), Strauss began his celebrated collaboration with the librettist Hugo von Hofmannsthal, producing Der Rosenkavalier (1911), Ariadne auf Naxos, Die Frau ohne Schatten, and Arabella. Later operas, written with Stefan Zweig and then Joseph Gregor, include Die schweigsame Frau and his final opera, Capriccio (1942).

Nazi era and final years

In 1933 Strauss accepted the presidency of the Reichsmusikkammer under the Nazi regime, a decision that has remained controversial; he was later dismissed after a letter critical of the regime was intercepted, and he maintained that his motive was to protect his Jewish daughter-in-law and grandchildren. After the war he was cleared by a denazification tribunal. His last works — among them the Oboe Concerto, Metamorphosen for 23 strings, and the Four Last Songs (1948) — are among his most beloved. He died at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in 1949.
