Adam (short for adaptive moment estimation) is an algorithm for first-order gradient-based optimisation of stochastic objective functions, widely used to train neural networks. It was introduced by Diederik Kingma and Jimmy Ba in their 2014 paper "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization" and has become one of the default optimisers in deep learning.

Algorithm

Adam maintains, for each parameter, exponentially decaying moving averages of the gradient (the first moment, m) and of the squared gradient (the second moment, v), controlled by decay rates β1 and β2 (typically 0.9 and 0.999). Because the averages are initialised at zero, they are corrected for bias toward zero, and the parameter update divides the corrected first moment by the square root of the corrected second moment, scaled by a learning rate α (often 0.001). The result is an individual adaptive learning rate for each parameter, combining ideas from two earlier methods, AdaGrad and RMSProp.

Properties

Adam is computationally efficient, needs little memory beyond the two moment vectors, is invariant to diagonal rescaling of gradients, and works well with sparse gradients and noisy problems. It requires little hyperparameter tuning in practice, which accounts for much of its popularity.

Variants and criticism

Known variants include AdamW, which decouples weight decay from the gradient update and is now standard in training large language models; Nadam, which adds Nesterov momentum; and AMSGrad, proposed after researchers exhibited cases in which Adam fails to converge. Some practitioners still prefer stochastic gradient descent with momentum for best final accuracy in image classification.
