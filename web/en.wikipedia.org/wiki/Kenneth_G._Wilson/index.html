Wilson in the 1970s.

Kenneth Geddes Wilson (8 June 1936 – 15 June 2013) was an American theoretical physicist and pioneer of computer simulation in physics. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1982 'for his theory for critical phenomena in connection with phase transitions' — the renormalisation group — an achievement that also transformed Quantum field theory and gave physics the concept of effective theories.

Wilson's 1971 renormalisation group explained universality (why fluids and magnets share critical exponents): at a continuous phase transition, physics at large scales forgets microscopic detail. He then applied the same thinking to field theory, taming renormalisation into a scale-by-scale procedure, and in 1974 invented Lattice QCD to compute the strong interaction non-perturbatively. Late in his career he crusaded for supercomputing in science and for reforming physics education (the 'physics on everything' syllabus). He is often ranked with the most influential theorists of the late twentieth century for giving physics its modern understanding of scale.

The renormalisation group

Wilson's insight recast a technical trick as a worldview: theories flow under changes of scale, most details wash out ('irrelevant'), and the few that remain define universal behaviour — the deep reason the same few theories (Quantum electrodynamics, the Standard Model) work from atoms to accelerators. His student generation carried the method into every branch of physics.
