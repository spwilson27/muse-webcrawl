Hemophilia in European royalty

Hemophilia in European royalty
OriginQueen Victoria (spontaneous mutation, or inherited from her mother or father)
TypeHemophilia B (confirmed by DNA analysis of Romanov remains)
InheritanceX-linked recessive
Affected housesBritish, German (Hesse), Russian (Romanov), Spanish (Bourbon)

Hemophilia figured prominently in the history of European royalty in the 19th and 20th centuries. Britain's Queen Victoria, through two of her five daughters — Princess Alice and Princess Beatrice — passed the mutation to various royal houses across the continent, including the royal families of Spain, Germany, and Russia. For this reason hemophilia was once popularly called "the royal disease".

Hemophilia is an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder caused by deficiency of a clotting factor. It affects males who inherit the mutated gene on their single X chromosome, while females with one mutated copy are usually unaffected carriers. DNA analysis published in 2009 on the remains of the Romanov family identified the royal mutation as a defect in the gene for factor IX, meaning the royal disease was hemophilia B rather than the more common hemophilia A.

Queen Victoria and her descendants

There is no record of hemophilia in Queen Victoria's ancestors, so the mutation is thought to have arisen spontaneously, possibly in Victoria herself or in the germline of her father, Prince Edward, Duke of Kent, who was in his fifties at her conception. Victoria's eighth child, Prince Leopold, Duke of Albany, was a hemophiliac who died at 30 after a fall. Her daughters Alice and Beatrice were carriers.

The Hessian and Russian line

Princess Alice married Louis IV, Grand Duke of Hesse. Of her children, her son Friedrich ("Frittie") was a hemophiliac who died of internal bleeding after a fall from a window at age two. Her daughter Irene, who married Prince Heinrich of Prussia, had two hemophiliac sons. Another daughter, Alix — the future Tsarina Alexandra of Russia — carried the gene to Russia: her son, Tsarevich Alexei Nikolaevich, heir to the Romanov throne, suffered severely from hemophilia. The Tsarina's desperate search for a cure brought the mystic Grigori Rasputin into the imperial court, and his influence over the family contributed to the discrediting of the monarchy before the Russian Revolution. Alexei was murdered with his family in 1918, at the age of 13.

The Spanish line

Through Victoria's youngest daughter Beatrice, the gene reached Spain. Beatrice's daughter Victoria Eugenie ("Ena") married Alfonso XIII of Spain; two of their sons, Alfonso, Prince of Asturias, and Infante Gonzalo, were hemophiliacs, and both died young after minor car accidents caused fatal internal bleeding. The affliction damaged the Spanish monarchy's prestige and the royal marriage itself. The present Spanish royal family descends through a non-carrier son, Don Juan.

The British line today

No member of the current British royal family is known to have hemophilia: the gene descended through Leopold's daughter Alice, Countess of Athlone, whose son Rupert was a hemophiliac, but the line of descent into the present royal family — through Victoria's eldest son, Edward VII, who did not inherit the gene — is unaffected. Because no living descendant of Victoria in the male line is affected, and no carrier has been identified among her descendants in recent generations, the "royal disease" is generally considered extinct in the reigning houses, although carrier status cannot be ruled out in every collateral branch.
