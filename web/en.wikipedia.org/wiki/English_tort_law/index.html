English tort law

English tort law concerns civil wrongs in England and Wales arising primarily from the common law. A tort is a wrongful act or omission, other than a breach of contract, that causes harm to another and gives rise to a claim for damages or another remedy. Its central tort is negligence, alongside nuisance, trespass, defamation and others.

Negligence

The modern law stems from Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], where Lord Atkin articulated the neighbour principle and a general duty of care. Claimants must establish duty, breach, causation and loss. The Caparo test refines duty for novel situations. Professional and public-authority liability, pure economic loss, and psychiatric injury are specially restricted.

Nuisance and land torts

Private nuisance protects use and enjoyment of land; the rule in Rylands v Fletcher imposed strict liability for escapes of dangerous things. Trespass to land, goods and the person (battery, assault, false imprisonment) is actionable without proof of damage.

Defamation

Libel and slander protect reputation, reformed by the Defamation Act 2013, which introduced a serious-harm threshold. Privacy claims have developed through misuse of private information under the Human Rights Act 1998.

Remedies and reform

Damages are generally compensatory. The Law Commission and the Pearson Commission have prompted debate on personal-injury compensation and a possible no-fault scheme.
