Dinshaway incident

The Dinshaway incident was a violent confrontation involving British officers and Egyptian villagers that took place in June 1906, near the village of Dinshaway (Denshawai) in the Nile Delta of Egypt, which was then under British occupation. The harsh punishment meted out to villagers after the incident provoked outrage in Egypt and abroad and is widely regarded as a turning point in the development of Egyptian nationalism and anti-colonial sentiment.

Background

British troops had occupied Egypt since 1882. In June 1906 a party of British officers went pigeon shooting near Dinshaway, a pastime that brought them into conflict with villagers for whom the pigeons were a source of food and income. A shot fired by one of the officers wounded the wife of the village prayer leader, and a barn or threshing floor caught fire. Villagers confronted the officers; in the ensuing scuffle several officers were injured, and one officer who fled on foot in the heat later died, apparently of heatstroke or sunstroke, after being found by a soldier.

Trial and punishments

The British authorities, under the Consul-General Lord Cromer, responded with a special tribunal. Fifty-two villagers were tried; sentences included executions by hanging, floggings, and terms of imprisonment with hard labour. Four men were hanged and others publicly flogged in the village, punishments carried out in view of the local population. The severity of the sentences caused shock in Egypt and criticism in Britain, where the Liberal government demanded an inquiry and Cromer eventually resigned in 1907.

Legacy

The incident became a symbol of the brutality of colonial rule. Egyptian nationalist leader Mustafa Kamil publicised the affair internationally, and it contributed to the growth of the nationalist movement that culminated in the Egyptian revolution of 1919. The writer George Bernard Shaw commented on the affair in the preface to his play John Bull's Other Island. A museum in Dinshaway commemorates the incident.
