Quantum vacuum state

The quantum vacuum state (also called the quantum vacuum or vacuum state) is the quantum state with the lowest possible energy. Generally, it contains no physical particles. The word vacuum derives from Latin, meaning "an empty space"; yet according to quantum field theory, even the lowest-energy state is not truly empty: it exhibits fluctuating electromagnetic and other fields with an average energy of zero — the "zero-point energy" left after subtracting an arbitrary constant.

Vacuum fluctuations: virtual particle–antiparticle pairs continually appear and annihilate.

Properties

In quantum field theory the vacuum is the state annihilated by all annihilation operators; particles are excitations of the fields above this vacuum. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle forbids the fields from sitting perfectly still, so the vacuum has measurable physical effects: it produces the Casimir force between conducting plates, contributes to the Lamb shift in atomic spectra, and underlies spontaneous emission by atoms. Virtual particles — internal lines in Feynman diagrams — are said to be "borrowed" from the vacuum.

Vacuums in different theories

Theories can possess more than one candidate vacuum; field theories with spontaneous symmetry breaking, such as the Higgs mechanism in the Standard Model, have a vacuum characterised by a non-zero vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field. Whether our universe's vacuum is the true ground state or a long-lived "false vacuum" is an open question with cosmological implications. In quantum chromodynamics, the vacuum is thought to contain condensates of quarks and gluons.

Zero-point energy and cosmology

The naive estimate of the vacuum energy density from quantum field theory exceeds the observed dark-energy density by many orders of magnitude — the cosmological constant problem, often called the worst prediction in physics. Attempts to exploit zero-point energy for propulsion or free energy have no accepted scientific basis.
