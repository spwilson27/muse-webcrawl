Central planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Central planning is the allocation of resources in an economy by a central authority — typically a state planning agency — rather than by markets. In a centrally planned or command economy, the state owns or controls the means of production, sets production targets and quotas, fixes prices and wages, and directs investment according to a plan, commonly a five-year plan. Central planning was a defining feature of the economic systems of the Soviet Union and its allies, and of Maoist China, and it remains a subject of the socialist calculation debate.

Mechanism

Planners such as the Soviet Gosplan drew up material balances intended to match inputs with outputs across the whole economy. Enterprises received output targets and allocated inputs, prices were set administratively (often remaining unchanged for years), and success was measured by plan fulfilment, which encouraged phenomena such as "storming" — frantic production at the end of a plan period — and the hoarding of inputs and labour. Plans were aggregated into annual plans and five-year plans, beginning with the Soviet First Five-Year Plan of 1928.

Criticism

Ludwig von Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for capital goods, rational economic calculation is impossible; Friedrich Hayek later framed the problem as one of dispersed knowledge that no central authority can collect. Critics pointed to chronic shortages, queues, low-quality goods, and misallocation in planned economies, as well as the suppression of consumer sovereignty. Defenders have argued that planning mobilised resources for rapid industrialisation and wartime production, provided full employment and basic goods, and avoided the instability of market cycles; some modern writers revisit planning with reference to large-scale corporate logistics and computational methods.

Decline

Most centrally planned economies were reformed or abandoned from the late 1970s onward: China introduced market mechanisms under Deng Xiaoping while retaining state plans in indicative form, and the Eastern European systems collapsed with the revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. Comprehensive central planning survives only in attenuated forms, notably in North Korea and, partially, Cuba.
