Bayesian epistemology

Bayesian epistemology is a formal approach in epistemology that studies the norms governing belief using the mathematical framework of probability theory. It understands an agent's doxastic state not in terms of outright belief or disbelief, but in terms of degrees of belief, called credences, which range from 0 (complete disbelief) to 1 (complete certainty).

Bayesian conditionalization: a prior credence is revised in light of new evidence.

Core principles

Bayesian epistemology rests on several norms. Probabilism holds that rational credences must obey the axioms of probability. Conditionalization holds that when an agent acquires evidence E, her new credence in a hypothesis H should equal her previous conditional credence in H given E, as calculated by Bayes' theorem. Arguments for these norms include Dutch book arguments — showing that violating them exposes an agent to a set of bets guaranteeing a loss — and accuracy-based arguments, which show that non-probabilistic credences are dominated by probabilistic ones in terms of closeness to the truth.

Applications

The framework has been applied to central problems in epistemology and the philosophy of science: the confirmation of hypotheses by evidence, the problem of old evidence, the ravens paradox, the analysis of testimony, and the epistemology of disagreement. Confirmation theorists measure how much a piece of evidence raises or lowers the probability of a hypothesis; incremental confirmation is often analysed with Bayes' theorem.

Criticisms

Critics raise the problem of the priors (where initial credences come from and whether rationality constrains them), the demands of logical omniscience implicit in the probability axioms, and the question of how graded credences relate to outright beliefs. Objective Bayesians seek rational constraints on priors, while subjective Bayesians treat them as largely a matter of the agent's perspective, constrained mainly by coherence and updating.
