Burin (lithic flake)

In archaeology, a burin (from the French burin, meaning "cold chisel" or "engraving tool") is a type of stone tool, a lithic flake with a chisel-like edge, produced by the removal of a narrow sliver of stone — a burin spall or "burin blow" — from a blade or flake. Burins were used primarily for engraving and working bone, antler, ivory, and wood.

Characteristics

The defining feature of a burin is the burin facet: the scar left when one or more longitudinal spalls are struck from the edge of a flake. The resulting intersection of two surfaces produces a strong, narrow, right-angled working edge that could be resharpened repeatedly by further burin blows. Burins occur in many forms, including dihedral burins, angle burins, and burins on truncation.

Distribution

Burins are characteristic of Upper Paleolithic industries in Europe, such as the Gravettian and Magdalenian, where they are associated with the production of engraved art, eyed needles, and worked osseous tools; they also occur in later Mesolithic and Neolithic assemblages and in the Americas. In Epipaleolithic and Neolithic contexts of the Near East, burins have been linked to drilling and bead-making.
