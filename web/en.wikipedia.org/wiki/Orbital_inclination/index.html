Orbital inclination

Orbital inclination measures the tilt of an object's orbit around a celestial body. It is expressed as the angle between a reference plane and the orbital plane (or axis) of the object. An inclination of 0° means the orbit lies in the reference plane in the same direction as the central body's rotation; 90° is a polar orbit; and an inclination above 90° describes a retrograde orbit.

Definition

For planets in the Solar System the reference plane is usually the ecliptic (the plane of Earth's orbit). For artificial satellites of Earth it is the equatorial plane. Inclination is one of the six Keplerian orbital elements. It is measured at the ascending node — the point where the orbiting body crosses the reference plane moving northward.

Examples

  • Most planets have small inclinations to the ecliptic; Mercury's is the largest at about 7°.
  • The Moon's orbit is inclined about 5.14° to the ecliptic.
  • The International Space Station orbits at 51.6° inclination, reachable from both US and Russian launch sites.
  • Sun-synchronous satellites use near-polar retrograde inclinations around 97–99°.
  • Pluto's orbit is inclined about 17.16°, unusually high for a major Solar System body.

Inclination change

Changing a satellite's inclination is one of the most fuel-expensive orbital maneuvers, since it requires rotating the velocity vector; this is why rockets are typically launched into inclinations close to their launch latitude, and why plane changes are best done at low orbital velocity (near apoapsis).
