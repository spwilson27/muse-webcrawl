Albrecht Durer (21 May 1471 – 6 April 1528) was a German painter, printmaker and theorist of the Northern Renaissance. Born in Nuremberg, he established his reputation across Europe in his twenties through his superb woodcuts and engravings, and is generally regarded as the greatest artist of the German Renaissance and one of the most influential printmakers of all time.

Early life

Durer was the third child of a Hungarian-born goldsmith whose surname derived from the village of Ajtós ("door" in Hungarian, Tür in German — hence Dürer, in unaccented English often written Durer). He was apprenticed first to his father and then, in 1486, to the Nuremberg painter and woodcut illustrator Michael Wolgemut. After his Wanderjahre travels he returned to Nuremberg and married Agnes Frey in 1494.

Career

Journeys to Italy in 1494–95 and 1505–07 exposed Durer to Renaissance theories of perspective and proportion, which he fused with northern attention to observed detail. His woodcut series such as the Apocalypse (1498), and master engravings including Knight, Death and the Devil (1513), Saint Jerome in His Study (1514) and Melencolia I (1514), spread his fame throughout Europe. His watercolours, such as the Young Hare (1502) and Great Piece of Turf (1503), are landmarks of nature study.

Durer also wrote treatises on geometry, fortification and human proportion, and enjoyed the patronage of Emperor Maximilian I, for whom he produced the monumental Triumphal Arch woodcut. In 1520–21 he travelled through the Netherlands, recording the journey in a remarkable diary, and witnessed the early stirrings of the Reformation, whose cause he sympathised with.

Legacy

Durer's monogram "AD" became one of the first artist's brands, and he famously pursued imitators in court. His work bridges the Gothic north and the Renaissance south, and his prints — widely distributed and copied — shaped the visual imagination of 16th-century Europe more than any artist before him.
