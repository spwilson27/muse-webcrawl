Gỏi cuốn (Vietnamese: [ɣɔj˧˩ kuən˧˦]), also known as Vietnamese spring rolls, fresh spring rolls, summer rolls, or rice paper rolls, is a Vietnamese dish traditionally consisting of pork, prawn (shrimp), vegetables, bún (rice vermicelli), and other ingredients wrapped in bánh tráng (commonly known as rice paper). Unlike the fried spring rolls of several other Asian cuisines, gỏi cuốn are served fresh at room temperature and are not fried.

Preparation

A sheet of bánh tráng is briefly dipped in water to soften, then laid flat. Rows of ingredients — typically sliced boiled pork, halved prawns (so their orange-pink colour shows through the translucent wrapper), rice vermicelli, lettuce, mint, perilla, garlic chives and bean sprouts — are placed on the lower third of the sheet, which is then folded and rolled tightly into a cylinder roughly 8 centimetres (3 in) long.

Gỏi cuốn are served with a dipping sauce. The two most common are tương xào, a hoisin-based peanut sauce topped with crushed peanuts and sometimes chilli, and nước chấm, a fish-sauce dip with lime, garlic and sugar. In southern Vietnam, where the dish is strongly associated with everyday home cooking and street food, the rolls are often made at the table by diners themselves.

History and popularity

The dish originates in southern Vietnam and is one of the country's most internationally recognised foods. It is frequently listed among the world's best-known appetizers and is a staple of Vietnamese restaurants worldwide. A vegetarian version, gỏi cuốn chay, replaces the meat and shrimp with tofu and additional vegetables, and is common during Buddhist observance days.
