Syncope

Syncope
Other namesFainting, passing out, swooning
SpecialtyCardiology, neurology
SymptomsBrief loss of consciousness and muscle tone, rapid recovery; often preceded by light-headedness, sweating, nausea, pallor
CausesGlobal cerebral hypoperfusion: reflex (vasovagal), orthostatic hypotension, cardiac arrhythmia or structural disease
Risk factorsDehydration, prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotional stress, heart disease

Syncope, commonly known as fainting or passing out, is a transient loss of consciousness and postural tone caused by a temporary reduction of blood flow to the brain, characterised by rapid onset, short duration and spontaneous complete recovery. It is a common medical problem, accounting for a significant share of emergency department visits, and although often benign — the typical faint is the reflex (vasovagal) syncope triggered by pain, fear or prolonged standing — it can be the presenting symptom of dangerous cardiac disease. (For the linguistic term, see syncope (phonology).)

Fainting is a brief, self-limited loss of consciousness.

Classification and causes

Guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology divide syncope into three main groups. Reflex (neurally mediated) syncope includes vasovagal syncope, situational syncope (e.g. during coughing, swallowing or micturition) and carotid sinus hypersensitivity. Syncope due to orthostatic hypotension results from failure of autonomic regulation or volume depletion, and from drugs. Cardiac syncope arises from arrhythmias (bradycardia, tachycardia, heart block) or structural disease such as aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and carries the worst prognosis. Warning features include syncope during exertion or while supine, palpitations, chest pain, and a family history of sudden death.

Evaluation and management

Diagnosis rests chiefly on a careful history, physical examination including orthostatic blood pressure, and an electrocardiogram; further testing (echocardiography, rhythm monitoring, tilt-table testing, blood tests) is guided by risk stratification. Treatment addresses the underlying cause: education and physical counter-pressure manoeuvres for vasovagal syncope, medication review and volume repletion for orthostatic causes, and pacemakers, implantable defibrillators or ablation for cardiac causes. Immediate first aid is to lay the person flat and elevate the legs to restore cerebral perfusion.

Epidemiology

Syncope is very common: roughly a third or more of people experience at least one episode in their lifetime, with peaks in adolescence/young adulthood and in old age. Most cases are reflex and benign, but syncope in older patients is more often cardiac or orthostatic and more likely to cause injury from falls.
