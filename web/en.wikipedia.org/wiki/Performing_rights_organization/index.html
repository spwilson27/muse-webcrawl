Performing rights organization

A performing rights organization (PRO), also called a collecting society or copyright collective, is an organisation that licenses the public performance rights in copyrighted musical works on behalf of songwriters, composers, and music publishers, collects the resulting royalties, and distributes them to its members.

How it works

Rights holders assign or license their performing rights to the society. The society then offers users of music — broadcasters, streaming services, concert venues, shops, restaurants, and other businesses — a blanket licence permitting them to perform any work in its repertoire. Fees are set by tariffs, negotiation, or rate courts and tribunals. The society monitors usage through broadcaster logs, sampling, and increasingly digital fingerprinting, then distributes royalties after deducting administrative costs. Reciprocal agreements between societies in different countries let each society license the world repertoire within its territory.

The United States is unusual in having several competing PROs; most countries have a single society for musical performing rights, often operating as a de facto or legal monopoly and subject to government oversight or consent decrees.

History

The first performing rights society, SACEM, was founded in France in 1851 after composers successfully asserted a right to payment when their music was performed in cafés. ASCAP followed in the United States in 1914, and the British Performing Right Society in the same year.
