Keratoacanthoma

Placeholder image: a keratoacanthoma on sun-exposed skin.

Keratoacanthoma (KA) is a common, rapidly growing skin tumour that arises from hair follicles, typically on sun-exposed skin of older adults. It is characterised by a dome-shaped nodule with a central crater filled with keratin, and classically grows quickly over weeks, stabilises, and may regress spontaneously over months.

Classification debate

Dermatopathologists debate whether keratoacanthoma is a benign tumour or a well-differentiated variant of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Because it can be difficult to distinguish from SCC and a minority of lesions behave aggressively, many clinicians manage KA as if it were SCC.

Causes and risk factors

Ultraviolet radiation is the principal risk factor; others include immunosuppression, trauma, chemical exposures such as tar and pitch, and human papillomavirus. Rare hereditary syndromes, including Ferguson–Smith syndrome and Muir–Torre syndrome, feature multiple keratoacanthomas.

Treatment

Surgical excision is the most common treatment, allowing full histological examination. Alternatives in selected cases include intralesional methotrexate or 5-fluorouracil, cryotherapy, curettage, and radiotherapy.
