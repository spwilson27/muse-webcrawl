Abdullah Yusuf Azzam (Arabic: عبد الله يوسف عزام; 1941 – 24 November 1989) was a Palestinian Sunni Islamic scholar and theologian who became the leading ideologue and organiser of the Arab volunteers in the Soviet–Afghan War. A mentor of Osama bin Laden, he co-founded the Maktab al-Khidamat (Services Bureau), a precursor network to al-Qaeda, and popularised the doctrine that defensive jihad was an individual obligation for all Muslims.

Early life and education

Azzam was born in the village of Silat al-Harithiya near Jenin. After the 1967 Six-Day War he moved to Jordan, joined the Muslim Brotherhood, and studied in Damascus and at al-Azhar in Cairo, where he completed a doctorate in the principles of Islamic jurisprudence in 1973. He taught at the University of Jordan until his dismissal in 1980, then lectured at King Abdulaziz University in Jeddah, where bin Laden was among those influenced by him.

The Afghan jihad

Moving to Pakistan in 1981, Azzam settled in Peshawar and in 1984 founded, with bin Laden, the Maktab al-Khidamat to raise funds, publish propaganda (notably the magazine Al Jihad) and channel Arab volunteers to the Afghan mujahideen. His 1984 fatwa Defence of the Muslim Lands, endorsed by the Saudi grand mufti Abd al-Aziz ibn Baz, declared the Afghan jihad fard ayn — an individual duty. His slogan-laden writings made him the best-known recruiter of the "Afghan Arabs".

Death

As the war wound down, Azzam clashed with more radical figures, including Ayman al-Zawahiri, over strategy and the turn toward global jihad. On 24 November 1989 Azzam and his two sons were killed by a powerful bomb detonated as they drove to Friday prayers in Peshawar. The assassination was never solved; suspects named in accounts include rival jihadis, and various intelligence services.
