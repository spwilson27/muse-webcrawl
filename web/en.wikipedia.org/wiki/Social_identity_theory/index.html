Social identity theory

Social identity theory is a theory in social psychology developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s and 1980s. It proposes that part of an individual's self-concept derives from their perceived membership in relevant social groups, and that people strive to maintain or enhance a positive social identity, favouring their own group (the "in-group") over other groups (the "out-group").

Background

Tajfel, a Polish-born social psychologist who had survived the Second World War, was motivated by questions of prejudice and intergroup discrimination. His minimal group paradigm experiments of the early 1970s showed that merely assigning people to arbitrary groups — for example, by a coin toss or by their supposed preference for paintings by Klee or Kandinsky — was enough to produce in-group favouritism: participants allocated more rewards to members of their own group even when doing so cost the in-group in absolute terms.

Key processes

The theory identifies three cognitive processes:

  • Social categorisation — people organise the social world into categories, placing themselves and others into groups.
  • Social identification — individuals adopt the identity of the group they belong to, taking on its norms and values.
  • Social comparison — people compare their group with other groups, seeking positive distinctiveness, the perception that their group is better than relevant out-groups on valued dimensions.

Strategies for identity management

When a group's status is unsatisfactory, members may pursue several strategies. Individual mobility involves leaving the group for a higher-status one. Social creativity involves redefining the comparison — choosing new dimensions on which the in-group excels, or comparing with different out-groups. Social competition involves directly challenging the out-group's superior position, as in social movements and collective action.

Development and influence

Turner later extended the theory into self-categorisation theory, which examines how group membership becomes salient in a given context. The social identity approach has been applied widely in organisational psychology, political psychology, health, and the study of leadership, where "identity leadership" research examines how leaders create and represent a shared group identity.

Criticism

Critics have noted that the theory can be difficult to falsify, because almost any intergroup behaviour can be explained post hoc by identity motives. Others argue that it underemphasises material interests and the content of group identities, and that the minimal group effect may partly reflect expectations of reciprocity or generic fairness norms rather than identity enhancement alone.
