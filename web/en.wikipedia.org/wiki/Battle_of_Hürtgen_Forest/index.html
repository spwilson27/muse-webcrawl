The Battle of Hürtgen Forest (German: Schlacht im Hürtgenwald) was a series of battles fought from 19 September 1944 to 10 February 1945 between American and German forces in the densely wooded Hürtgen Forest on the Belgian–German border. At some 88 square miles fought over for nearly five months, it was the longest battle on German ground during the Second World War and one of the longest the U.S. Army has ever fought.

Background

U.S. First Army commanders pursued retreating German forces into the forest, which covered the approaches to the Roer River dams and the Siegfried Line. The terrain — thick conifers, deep ravines, mud and later snow — negated American advantages in armour and air power, while German defenders occupied prepared pillboxes and minefields.

Battle

Successive American divisions — among them the 9th, 28th and 4th Infantry Divisions — were fed into the forest and ground down in fighting for villages such as Schmidt, Vossenack and Hürtgen. The struggle for Schmidt in November 1944 became notorious for its losses. Only in February 1945, after the Roer dams were finally taken by the 78th Infantry Division, did the battle end, too late to prevent the Germans from flooding the Roer valley and delaying the Allied advance by two weeks.

Aftermath

The battle cost the Americans some 33,000 casualties including trench foot and disease, against roughly 28,000 German losses. Historians have often criticised the campaign as an avoidable battle of attrition fought for ground of secondary importance, overshadowing its place between the failure of Market Garden and the Battle of the Bulge.
