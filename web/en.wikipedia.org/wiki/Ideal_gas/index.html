In the ideal gas model, particles are point masses that collide elastically with the container walls.

An ideal gas is a theoretical gas composed of a set of randomly moving point particles that do not interact except through perfectly elastic collisions. The model is the foundation of the kinetic theory of gases and obeys the ideal gas law,

PV = nRT

where P is the pressure, V the volume, n the amount of substance, R the molar gas constant (8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹), and T the absolute temperature. Although no real gas behaves ideally at all temperatures and pressures, many gases approximate ideal behaviour at moderate pressures and temperatures well above their boiling points, and the model remains a workhorse of physics, chemistry, and engineering.

Kinetic theory

The kinetic theory derives macroscopic gas behaviour from the motion of particles with five key assumptions: the gas consists of many tiny particles in constant random motion; the particles are point masses (negligible volume); collisions are perfectly elastic; there are no attractive or repulsive forces between particles; and the average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature. From these assumptions one derives the ideal gas law and results such as the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of particle speeds.

Ideal gas law and its history

The ideal gas law unifies four empirical laws: Boyle's law (1662, P ∝ 1/V at constant T and n), Charles's law (T ∝ V at constant P), Gay-Lussac's law (P ∝ T at constant V), and Avogadro's law (V ∝ n). Émile Clapeyron combined the first three into a single equation of state in 1834, and the modern form with the molar gas constant was introduced by Dmitri Mendeleev in the 1870s.

Deviations and refinements

Real gases deviate from the model at high pressures, where particle volume matters, and at low temperatures, where intermolecular forces and phase change matter. Corrections are expressed via the compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT (Z = 1 for an ideal gas) or through equations of state such as van der Waals', which adds terms for particle volume and attraction, or the virial expansion. At very high densities, the Fermi gas and Bose gas quantum models take over.

Thermodynamics

The ideal gas has further simplifying properties: its internal energy depends only on temperature (for a monatomic gas, U = 3⁄2 nRT), its specific heats are constant (Cv = 3⁄2 R per mole for a monatomic gas, with Cp = Cv + R, Meyer's relation), and its entropy changes in simple ways under adiabatic and isothermal processes, making it the standard model gas of introductory thermodynamics.
