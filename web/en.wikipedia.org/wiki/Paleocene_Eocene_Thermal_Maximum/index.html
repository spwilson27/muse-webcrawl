Paleocene Eocene Thermal Maximum

The Paleocene Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM) is the abrupt global warming event that defines the boundary between the Paleocene and Eocene epochs, about 56 million years ago. This article presents the event under the underscore form of the name, complementing the main article at Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum.

Overview

During the PETM the Earth warmed by at least 5 °C globally, and by as much as 8 °C at high latitudes, over a geologically brief interval. The event is recorded as a pronounced negative excursion in carbon isotope ratios in marine carbonates, soils and organic matter, signifying the rapid addition of several thousand gigatonnes of carbon to the exogenic carbon cycle.

Timing and duration

High-resolution records from deep-sea cores (such as ODP Site 690 in the Weddell Sea and cores from the New Jersey margin) and from terrestrial sections such as the Bighorn Basin of Wyoming show a rapid onset, possibly with several stepped pulses of carbon release, followed by a plateau and a gradual recovery over a total of roughly 170,000–220,000 years, paced by silicate weathering and organic carbon burial.

Biotic response

The event coincides with the extinction of many deep-sea benthic foraminifera, turnovers in plankton groups, the temporary dwarfing of mammalian body sizes of up to 30 per cent in some lineages, and the intercontinental dispersal of early primates, artiodactyls and perissodactyls. Ocean acidification is inferred from a sharp shoaling of the carbonate compensation depth.

Trigger hypotheses

Candidate triggers include massive volcanism of the North Atlantic Igneous Province, thermal metamorphism of sedimentary carbon by igneous sills, methane clathrate destabilisation amplified by warming, and an orbital trigger acting on carbon reservoirs. The North Atlantic sill-intrusion hypothesis gained support from seismic and geochronological work published from the 2000s onward.
