Body odour

Body odour
Primary sourceApocrine sweat gland secretions broken down by skin bacteria
Key compounds3-methyl-2-hexenoic acid; sulfanylalkanols; androstenone; androstenol
Related conditionsBromhidrosis; hyperhidrosis; trimethylaminuria

Body odour (also spelled body odor in American English) is the smell produced by the human body, arising chiefly when bacteria on the skin metabolize secretions from sweat glands — particularly the apocrine glands concentrated in the armpits and groin — into volatile, odorous molecules. Body odour varies greatly between individuals owing to genetics, diet, health, and hygiene, and it plays a role in social perception and possibly mate choice.

Causes

Fresh apocrine sweat is nearly odourless. Skin bacteria such as Corynebacterium and Staphylococcus hominis cleave precursor molecules in the secretion, releasing compounds including 3-methyl-2-hexenoic acid (a "goaty" note), sulfur-containing sulfanylalkanols (onion-like), and steroids such as androstenone, which different people perceive as urinous, sweet, or not at all. Eccrine sweat, mostly water and salts, contributes little odour but provides moisture that encourages bacterial growth.

Genetics

A well-studied genetic factor is the ABCC11 gene: a common variant widespread in East Asian populations markedly reduces apocrine secretion and earwax wetness, and people homozygous for it produce little underarm odour. Diet (garlic, spices, alcohol), medications, and diseases also alter body smell; the fishy odour of trimethylaminuria is a metabolic example, while excessive odour is termed bromhidrosis.

Control and social aspects

Washing removes bacteria and secretions; deodorants inhibit bacterial growth or mask scent, while antiperspirants use aluminium salts to reduce sweating itself. Surgical and medical options for severe cases include botulinum toxin injections and gland removal. Culturally, attitudes to body odour differ widely, and the modern deodorant industry grew from late nineteenth-century products such as Mum and the first antiperspirant, Everdry (1903).
