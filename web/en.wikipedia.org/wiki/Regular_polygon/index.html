Regular polygon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Regular polygons with 3 to 12 sides

In Euclidean geometry, a regular polygon is a polygon that is equiangular (all angles are equal in measure) and equilateral (all sides have the same length). Regular polygons may be either convex or self-intersecting (star polygons). In the limit, a sequence of regular polygons with increasing numbers of sides approximates a circle if the perimeter or area is fixed, or a regular apeirogon if the edge length is fixed.

Properties

All vertices of a regular polygon lie on a common circle (the circumcircle), and its symmetry group is the dihedral group Dn of order 2n. For a regular n-gon with side length s, the interior angle is (1 − 2/n) × 180°, the area is (n s² / 4) cot(π/n), and the apothem, circumradius and perimeter follow simple trigonometric formulas. Regular polygons are the polygon cases of regular polytopes in two dimensions.

Constructibility

A regular n-gon can be constructed with compass and straightedge if and only if the odd prime factors of n are distinct Fermat primes (the Gauss–Wantzel theorem). The constructible cases begin 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 16, 17, ... — Carl Friedrich Gauss famously constructed the heptadecagon (17-gon) in 1796. Regular polygons tile the plane alone only for n = 3, 4 and 6, but appear in Archimedean tilings and as faces of uniform polyhedra.
