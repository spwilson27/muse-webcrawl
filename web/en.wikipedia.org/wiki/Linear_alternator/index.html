Linear alternator

A linear alternator is an electromechanical device that converts linear mechanical motion directly into alternating electric current, without the rotary motion of a conventional alternator. A magnet moves back and forth through (or past) a coil, inducing an alternating voltage according to Faraday's law of induction.

Applications

Linear alternators are used in free-piston engines and Stirling engines, where the piston oscillates without a crankshaft; the alternator both generates power and can act as a motor to start or control the piston. They also appear in wave-energy converters, vibration energy harvesters, and "shake" flashlights, where a magnet sliding through a coil charges a capacitor or battery when the device is shaken.

Advantages and disadvantages

Eliminating the crankshaft and rotary bearings reduces friction and wear, and the hermetically sealed designs of Stirling generators achieve long maintenance-free lifetimes. Drawbacks include the need for resonant spring systems to sustain oscillation and power electronics to condition the variable-frequency output.
