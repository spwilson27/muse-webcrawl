Ethics of artificial intelligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ethics of artificial intelligence is the branch of the ethics of technology specific to artificial intelligence systems. It covers the moral behaviour of humans in designing, building, using and treating AI systems, and the possible moral status and behaviour of the machines themselves — the latter often treated under machine ethics.

Near-term issues

Practical debates concern bias and fairness (systems trained on historical data can reproduce discrimination in hiring, lending and criminal justice), transparency (the difficulty of explaining decisions of complex models such as deep neural networks), privacy (mass data collection, facial recognition and surveillance), and accountability when autonomous systems such as self-driving cars cause harm. Other widely discussed topics include unemployment from automation, deepfakes and disinformation, lethal autonomous weapons, and the environmental cost of training large models.

Long-term issues

A distinct literature addresses risks from hypothetical future systems more capable than humans: the alignment problem of ensuring advanced AI pursues goals compatible with human values, the possibility of an intelligence explosion, and existential risk from artificial general intelligence, argued by writers such as Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky. Critics regard some of these concerns as speculative or distracting from present harms, and debate continues over priorities.

Governance

Governments and bodies have issued principle frameworks — including the OECD AI Principles (2019) — and binding law, most prominently the European Union's AI Act (2024), which regulates applications by risk tier. Professional standards, algorithmic auditing and proposed ideas such as a "trolley problem" framework for vehicles are part of the field, as is the study of robot ethics going back to Asimov's fictional Three Laws of Robotics.
