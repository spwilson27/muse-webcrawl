Maximum sustainable yield

In population ecology and resource economics, maximum sustainable yield (MSY) is theoretically the largest yield (catch) that can be taken from a renewable resource stock — classically a fishery — over an indefinite period without depleting the population.

Concept

Under logistic growth, a population grows fastest at intermediate size. MSY occurs when the harvest exactly balances the population's surplus growth, which in the simple logistic model happens at half the carrying capacity (K/2), with a maximum yield of rK/4, where r is the intrinsic growth rate. The concept was developed in the 1930s–1950s (notably by Hjort, Schaefer, and Beverton and Holt) and became a cornerstone of fisheries management, enshrined in agreements such as the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea and the U.S. Magnuson–Stevens Act.

Criticism

MSY has been widely criticized as a management target: it ignores age structure, multispecies interactions, environmental variability, and uncertainty in stock assessments, and fishing exactly at MSY leaves no safety margin. Many scientists and managers now treat MSY as an upper limit (a threshold to avoid) rather than a target, preferring precautionary reference points and "optimum yield" concepts that incorporate ecological and economic factors.
