Supersecondary structure

Supersecondary structure in proteins refers to a specific geometric arrangement of several adjacent elements of secondary structure — such as α helices and β strands — that recurs in many unrelated proteins. These motifs are intermediate in the hierarchy of protein organisation between secondary structure and full domains or tertiary folds.

Common motifs

  • Helix-turn-helix — two α helices joined by a short loop; a classic DNA-binding motif.
  • β-hairpin — two antiparallel β strands connected by a tight turn.
  • β-α-β motif — a β strand, an α helix and a second β strand, typically with right-handed connectivity, common in nucleotide-binding proteins.
  • Greek key — a pattern of four β strands folded in a characteristic arrangement found in immunoglobulin-like and jelly-roll folds.
  • Coiled coil — two or more α helices wound around each other, stabilised by a heptad repeat of hydrophobic residues.
  • Zinc finger — a small motif in which a zinc ion stabilises a loop and helix involved in nucleic-acid binding.

Significance

Because supersecondary structures are stable and recur across protein families, recognising them helps in classifying folds, predicting structure from sequence, and understanding how larger domains are assembled from modular building blocks. Databases of structural classification, such as SCOP and CATH, use motif and topology descriptions to organise protein structures.
