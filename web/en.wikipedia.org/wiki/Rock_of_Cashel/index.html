The Rock of Cashel (Irish: Carraig Phádraig), also known as Cashel of the Kings and St. Patrick's Rock, is a historic site located at Cashel, County Tipperary, Ireland. A dramatic limestone outcrop crowned by medieval ecclesiastical buildings, it was the traditional seat of the kings of Munster for centuries before becoming a major church centre.

History

According to tradition, the Eóganachta kings of Munster ruled from Cashel from the early centuries AD; legend holds that St Patrick converted King Aengus here in the fifth century, and the site is associated with Brian Boru, crowned king of Munster at Cashel in 978 and later High King of Ireland. In 1101 the king-bishop Muirchertach Ua Briain granted the Rock to the Church. The Synod of Cashel of 1172, held after the Norman invasion, reorganised the Irish Church under Henry II.

In 1647, during the Irish Confederate Wars, Cashel was sacked by English Parliamentarian troops under Lord Inchiquin; Irish soldiers and clergy who had taken refuge in the cathedral were massacred, and the building's roof was burned. The cathedral was finally abandoned in the eighteenth century after the archbishop moved to St John's Cathedral in the town below.

Buildings

The oldest structure is the perfectly preserved round tower, about 28 metres high, dating to around 1100. Cormac's Chapel, consecrated in 1134, is one of the finest Romanesque churches in Ireland, with carved doorways, blind arcading and fragments of early frescoes. The cruciform Gothic cathedral, built mainly between 1235 and 1270, is roofless but retains its lancet windows, tombs and a central tower. The fifteenth-century Hall of the Vicars Choral, built for the cathedral's lay singers, has been restored and houses a museum; the original St Patrick's Cross, a twelfth-century high cross, is kept inside for protection, with a replica on its base outside.

Tourism

The Rock of Cashel is among Ireland's most visited heritage sites; Queen Elizabeth II toured it during her state visit to Ireland in 2011.
