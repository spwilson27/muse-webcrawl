Cloud physics

Cloud physics is the study of the physical processes that lead to the formation, growth, and precipitation of atmospheric clouds. These aerological processes are studied in the field of meteorology and include the microphysics of individual cloud particles, the dynamics of cloud systems, and the interactions between clouds and their environment.

Cloud formation

Clouds form when air becomes saturated with water vapour, usually through cooling by ascent. Water vapour condenses onto microscopic aerosol particles called cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) to form droplets, or deposits onto ice nuclei to form ice crystals at low temperatures. Köhler theory describes the equilibrium growth of solution droplets on hygroscopic nuclei.

Droplet growth and precipitation

Condensation alone grows droplets only slowly, so precipitation-sized drops require additional processes: collision and coalescence of droplets in warm clouds, and the Bergeron process in mixed-phase clouds, in which ice crystals grow rapidly at the expense of supercooled droplets because saturation vapour pressure is lower over ice than over water. Aggregation of ice crystals forms snowflakes, and riming produces graupel and hail.

Cloud classification and observation

Cloud physicists study warm clouds, mixed-phase clouds, cirrus, and convective clouds such as cumulonimbus, using ground-based radar, lidar, instrumented aircraft, satellites, and cloud chambers in the laboratory. The discipline underpins weather modification research, including cloud seeding, and improves the representation of clouds in weather and climate models — historically one of the largest sources of uncertainty in climate sensitivity estimates.
