Habilitation is a qualification examination and the highest academic qualification in many European countries, earned after a doctorate by writing a professorial thesis (the Habilitationsschrift), passing examinations and delivering a public lecture. It confers the venia legendi — the formal permission to teach a particular subject independently at a university — and has traditionally been a prerequisite for appointment to a full professorship in Germany, Austria and Switzerland. The word derives from the Latin habilitare, "to make fit".

By country

Germany

The candidate, usually several years after the doctorate, submits a substantial second thesis or a cumulative body of publications, defends it in a colloquium and gives a trial lecture. Success brings the academic title Privatdozent and the designation "Dr. habil." Although reforms since 2002 introduced the Juniorprofessur as an alternative route, the habilitation remains common, especially in medicine, law and the humanities.

France

The habilitation à diriger des recherches (HDR), created in 1984 to replace the older doctorat d'État, qualifies scholars to supervise doctoral research and is generally required for appointment as professeur des universités.

Elsewhere

Comparable qualifications exist in Austria, Switzerland (often combined with the title of Privatdozent), Poland (doktor habilitowany), the Czech Republic and Slovakia (docent), Hungary, Russia and Ukraine (Doctor of Sciences), and other systems descended from the German model.

Criticism

The system has been criticised for lengthening the road to a chair into a scholar's forties and for the dependence of candidates on a sponsoring professor, prompting reforms and tenure-track alternatives in several countries.
