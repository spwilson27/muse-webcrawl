Sudarium of Oviedo

The Sudarium of Oviedo, also known as the Oviedo Cloth, is a bloodstained linen cloth measuring approximately 84 × 53 cm (33 × 21 in), kept in the Cámara Santa (Holy Chamber) of the Cathedral of San Salvador in Oviedo, Spain. According to tradition it is the cloth that was wrapped around the head of Jesus Christ after his death, corresponding to the "napkin" (Greek soudarion) mentioned in the Gospel of John (20:6–7) as found in the empty tomb, separate from the linen burial sheets.

History

Tradition holds that the cloth was preserved in Jerusalem, moved to Alexandria, and carried through North Africa to Spain in the early 7th century to escape the Persian invasion, eventually reaching Toledo and then Oviedo, where it was kept in the Arca Santa (Holy Ark). The ark's documented opening in 1075, in the presence of King Alfonso VI, produced an inventory listing the sudarium among the relics. The cloth is displayed to the public three times a year: on Good Friday, on 14 September (the Feast of the Triumph of the Cross) and on 21 September.

Scientific study

The cloth bears stains of blood and pulmonary fluid but, unlike the Shroud of Turin, carries no image of a face. Studies by the Spanish Sindonology Research Centre (EDICES) argued that the stain pattern is consistent with covering the face of a crucified man and that pollen and blood-group findings link it to the Shroud of Turin. A radiocarbon test carried out in the 1990s dated a sample to around the 7th century AD, a result proponents attribute to contamination of the sampled area by later repair and handling.
