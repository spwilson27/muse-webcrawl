Unification (computer science)

In logic and computer science, unification is an algorithmic process of solving equations between symbolic expressions. Given two terms containing variables, unification attempts to find a substitution that assigns terms to the variables so that the two expressions become identical; such a substitution is called a unifier, and when a most general unifier (MGU) exists it is unique up to renaming of variables.

Applications

Unification is a central operation in logic programming languages such as Prolog, where clause resolution unifies goals with rule heads, and in automated theorem proving, where J. Alan Robinson's 1965 resolution principle relies on unification in first-order logic. It also underlies type inference in languages such as ML and Haskell (the Hindley–Milner algorithm), pattern matching, and term rewriting.

Algorithm

First-order unification of terms was first described by Jacques Herbrand and given an efficient algorithm by Robinson; Martelli and Montanari (1982) presented the classic rule-based algorithm, which repeatedly decomposes equations, eliminates variables, and detects failures such as the occurs check — a variable cannot be unified with a term containing itself, as in X = f(X). Omitting the occurs check (as Prolog traditionally does) can produce cyclic, infinite terms. Higher-order unification, used in systems such as λProlog and Isabelle, is undecidable in general, though decidable fragments such as pattern unification are widely used.
