Land reform in India refers to the series of measures taken after Indian independence in 1947 to change the ownership and control of agricultural land, abolish intermediaries between the state and the cultivator, protect tenant farmers, and redistribute land more equitably. Land reform was a central promise of the Indian national movement and the first Five-Year Plans, though implementation — a state government responsibility under the Indian Constitution — varied widely between states.

Background

Under British rule, land revenue systems such as the zamindari (Permanent Settlement) in the east, ryotwari in the south and west, and mahalwari in the north created layers of intermediaries, rack-renting, and tenant insecurity. Absentee landlordism and high rents were blamed for agricultural stagnation and rural poverty.

Main measures

Abolition of intermediaries. In the early 1950s, state laws abolished zamindari and similar tenures across nearly all of India, bringing an estimated 20 million tenants into a direct relationship with the state; this is generally judged the most successful component of reform.

Tenancy reform. Laws sought to regulate rents (typically to one-fourth or one-fifth of produce), give tenants security of tenure, and in some cases confer ownership. West Bengal's Operation Barga (from 1978) recorded sharecroppers (bargadars), protecting them from eviction and is widely studied as a relatively effective reform.

Ceilings on land holdings. Ceiling laws set maximum holdings and declared surplus land for redistribution, but were widely evaded through benami transfers and loopholes; only a small fraction of cultivated land was actually redistributed.

Consolidation and records. Several states consolidated fragmented holdings, and later programmes have focused on updating land records, including the Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme.

Assessment

Kerala and West Bengal, under left governments, are usually cited as the states where tenancy reform and redistribution went furthest; elsewhere implementation was hampered by litigation, weak records, and the political influence of landowners. Land reform coincided with, and partly yielded priority to, the Green Revolution's technology-led strategy from the late 1960s. Scholars continue to debate its effects on productivity and poverty, while land acquisition and the rights of tribal and forest-dwelling communities remain live issues in Indian politics.
