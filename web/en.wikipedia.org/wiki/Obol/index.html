Obol

The obol (Greek: ὀβολός, obolos) was a form of ancient Greek currency and weight. In the classical period an obol was a small silver coin worth one-sixth of a drachma. The name derives from the Greek word for a spit or spike, since early forms of the currency were metal spits; six of them (a handful, or drax) made a drachma ("handful").

Charon's obol

In Greek funerary practice, a coin — often an obol — was placed in or on the mouth of the dead as payment to Charon, the ferryman who carried souls across the river Styx into the underworld. This custom, known as "Charon's obol", is attested in burials across the Greek and Roman worlds and is frequently referenced in classical literature.

Weight

As a unit of weight, the Attic obol was about 0.72 grams. Obols were minted by many city-states, often with distinctive local types; tiny fractional denominations such as the hemiobol (half-obol) also circulated.
