A periplus (Greek: περίπλους, "a sailing around") is an ancient manuscript document that lists, in order, the ports and coastal landmarks a ship passes on a voyage, with approximate distances between them and notes on anchorage, water, trade goods and hazards. Periploi were the practical navigational literature of the ancient Mediterranean and Indian Ocean, serving the purposes that charts and coastal pilots serve today. Unlike a map, a periplus is essentially a linear itinerary, reflecting the way ancient seafarers actually navigated — by following coasts.

Famous examples

The oldest surviving periplus is that attributed to Scylax of Caryanda (Pseudo-Scylax, 4th century BC), describing the coasts of the Mediterranean and Black Sea. The periplus of Hanno the Navigator (c. 5th century BC) recounts a Carthaginian voyage down the Atlantic coast of Africa. The most famous of all is the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century AD), an anonymous Greek merchant's guide to the ports of the Red Sea, East Africa, Arabia and India, which is a primary source for Roman-era Indian Ocean trade. Later examples include the periplus of Arrian (2nd century AD) around the Black Sea and the Stadiasmus Maris Magni.

Content and value

Periploi typically give sailing directions in sequence: the name of each harbour, whether it has a market or anchorage, the distance in stades or days' sail from the previous port, the availability of drinking water, goods imported and exported, and notes on local peoples. For historians and archaeologists they are indispensable evidence for ancient geography, toponymy and trade networks, and they have been used to identify the sites of ancient ports.
