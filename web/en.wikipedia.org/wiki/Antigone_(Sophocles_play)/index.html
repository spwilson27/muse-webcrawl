Antigone (Sophocles play)

Antigone (Ἀντιγόνη)
AuthorSophocles

Antigone (Ancient Greek: Ἀντιγόνη) is an Athenian tragedy written by Sophocles in around 441 BC. Although it was written (or at least first performed) before Oedipus Rex and Oedipus at Colonus, in the sequence of the myth it comes last: the story of Antigone, daughter of Oedipus, who defies the king's decree to bury her brother and is condemned to death.

Plot

After the attack of the Seven against Thebes, the brothers Eteocles and Polynices have killed each other. Creon, the new king, orders Eteocles buried with honours but leaves Polynices — the attacker of his own city — unburied on pain of death. Antigone performs the burial rites anyway and, caught, defends herself before Creon by appealing to the unwritten, eternal laws of the gods (agrapta nomima) against the edicts of men. Creon condemns her to be walled up alive in a tomb. His son Haemon, her betrothed, pleads in vain; the prophet Tiresias warns that the gods reject Thebes' altars. Creon relents too late: Antigone has hanged herself, Haemon kills himself over her body, and Creon's wife Eurydice, hearing of her son's death, also dies. Creon is left broken, praying for death.

Themes and influence

The play's central conflict — divine or natural law against the law of the state, family obligation against civic obedience, conscience against authority — has made Antigone an enduring figure of civil disobedience. Hegel read the tragedy as a collision between two equally justified ethical powers, the family and the state. Adaptations and reworkings include Jean Anouilh's Antigone (1944), a veiled protest under the German occupation of France, and Seamus Heaney's The Burial at Thebes (2004).
