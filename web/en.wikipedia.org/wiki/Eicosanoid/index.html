Eicosanoid

In biochemistry, eicosanoids are signaling molecules made by the enzymatic or non-enzymatic oxidation of arachidonic acid or other polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that are, similar in structure, 20 carbon units in length. Eicosanoids are a sub-category of oxylipins – i.e. oxidised fatty acids of diverse carbon units in length – and are distinguished from other oxylipins by their overwhelming importance as cell signaling molecules.

Classes

The principal families of eicosanoids include:

  • Prostaglandins — mediators of inflammation, pain, fever, and smooth muscle contraction, produced via the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway.
  • Thromboxanes — produced by platelets, involved in blood clotting and vasoconstriction.
  • Leukotrienes — produced in leukocytes via the 5-lipoxygenase pathway; important in asthma and allergic responses.
  • Lipoxins — anti-inflammatory, pro-resolving mediators.
  • Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) — produced by cytochrome P450 epoxygenases.

Biosynthesis and function

Most eicosanoids derive from arachidonic acid (20:4 ω-6), released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2. They act locally as autocrine and paracrine mediators, binding to G protein-coupled receptors. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen exert their effects largely by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes and thereby reducing prostaglandin and thromboxane synthesis.

Nomenclature

The term derives from the Greek eicosa-, "twenty", referring to the twenty carbon atoms of the precursor fatty acids. An eicosanoid is denoted by a two- or three-letter abbreviation, a subscript numeral indicating the number of double bonds in the side chains, and further letters distinguishing isomers.
