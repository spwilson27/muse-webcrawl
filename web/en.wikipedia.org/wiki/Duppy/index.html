Duppy

Duppy is a word of African origin used in Caribbean folklore, particularly in Jamaica and Barbados, to mean a ghost or spirit. Most duppies are said to be malevolent spirits of the dead that return to trouble the living, though some are considered benign. The belief derives from West African traditions carried to the Caribbean by enslaved people.

Beliefs and practices

In Jamaican tradition a person's spirit may linger after death; funeral rituals such as the "nine nights" gathering help send the spirit on its way. Duppies are said to be repelled by salt, tar, wearing clothes inside out, and the smoke of certain plants. The word appears in the famous Bajan calypso and in the Jamaican proverb "duppy know who fi frighten".

In culture

The duppy features widely in Caribbean literature and music, from the Anansi stories' ghost tales to dancehall songs. The reggae classic "Duppy Conqueror" by Bob Marley and the Wailers celebrates overcoming evil spirits. In British Caribbean communities the word remains a lively part of everyday speech for any ghost.
