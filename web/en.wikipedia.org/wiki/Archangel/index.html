Archangel

Archangels are high-ranking angels in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

An archangel (from Ancient Greek ἀρχάγγελος, archángelos, "chief angel") is an angel of high rank in several religious traditions, including Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Zoroastrianism. Archangels are typically depicted as leaders of the heavenly host, messengers of major announcements, and protectors of nations and individuals.

In the Bible

The term "archangel" itself appears only twice in the canonical Bible: in Jude 1:9 (referring to Michael) and in 1 Thessalonians 4:16. Michael is the only angel explicitly called an archangel in scripture. Gabriel, who announces the births of John the Baptist and Jesus, is called an angel but not an archangel in the New Testament; his archangel rank comes from tradition.

In the deuterocanonical Book of Tobit, Raphael appears as one of seven angels who stand before God. The non-canonical Book of Enoch names seven archangels: Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel, Raguel, Remiel, and Saraqael.

Traditions by religion

  • Judaism — Michael and Gabriel are the most prominent angels; later Jewish tradition (e.g. the Talmud and Kabbalah) develops lists of archangels including Raphael and Uriel.
  • Roman Catholicism — venerates Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael as archangels, with a feast day on 29 September. Michael is patron of soldiers and the protector against evil, especially in the Prayer to Saint Michael.
  • Eastern Orthodoxy — recognises seven or eight archangels, adding Uriel, Selaphiel, Jegudiel, Barachiel, and Jeremiel.
  • Protestantism — generally recognises Michael as the only archangel strictly named as such, though Gabriel and Raphael are often also honoured in Lutheran and Anglican traditions.
  • Islam — the chief angels include Jibril (Gabriel), who revealed the Qur'an; Mikail (Michael); Israfil, who will blow the trumpet at the resurrection; and Azrael, the angel of death.

Hierarchy

In the influential 5th–6th-century scheme of Pseudo-Dionysius, angels are arranged in nine choirs in three triads; archangels form the second order of the lowest triad, above ordinary angels and below principalities. In art, archangels are shown winged, often in armour, with swords, trumpets, scales, or lilies depending on their office.
