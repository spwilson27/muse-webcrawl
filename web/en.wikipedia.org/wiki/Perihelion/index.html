Perihelion is the point in the orbit of a planet, asteroid or comet where it is nearest to the Sun. The word comes from the Greek peri (near) and helios (Sun). The generic term for the closest point to a central body is periapsis; the opposite point in the orbit, farthest from the Sun, is the aphelion. For objects orbiting Earth the corresponding terms are perigee and apogee.

Earth's perihelion

Earth reaches perihelion in early January, currently around January 3–4, at a distance of about 147.1 million kilometres (0.9833 AU) from the Sun, about 3.3% closer than at aphelion in early July. The difference in solar energy received is roughly 7%, but because the Northern Hemisphere has more land and the Southern Hemisphere more ocean, and because perihelion currently falls during the southern summer, the effect on global climate is modest. The seasons are driven primarily by axial tilt, not by distance from the Sun.

Apsidal precession

The date of perihelion shifts slowly because the line of apsides precesses. Earth's orbit undergoes apsidal precession with a period of about 112,000 years relative to inertial space; combined with the precession of the equinoxes, the timing of perihelion relative to the seasons cycles with a period of about 21,000 years — one of the Milankovitch cycles influencing ice-age climates. Roughly 11,000 years ago perihelion occurred near the June solstice rather than in January.

Mercury's perihelion advance

The gradual advance of Mercury's perihelion — 574 arcseconds per century, of which 43 arcseconds could not be explained by Newtonian perturbations from other planets — was a famous anomaly of 19th-century astronomy. Urbain Le Verrier proposed an unseen planet "Vulcan"; instead, Einstein's general theory of relativity (1915) accounted exactly for the discrepancy, providing one of the theory's first great confirmations.
