Appetite

Appetite is the desire to eat food, sometimes due to hunger. Appealing foods can stimulate appetite even when hunger is absent, although appetite can be reduced by satiety. Appetite exists in all higher life-forms and serves to regulate the intake of energy needed to maintain metabolic processes. It is a psychological desire, distinguished from hunger, which is the physiological need for food arising from energy deficiency and bodily signals such as stomach contractions and falling blood glucose.

Attractive, palatable food can stimulate appetite even in the absence of hunger.

Regulation

Appetite is regulated by a complex interplay between the digestive tract, adipose tissue, and the brain, especially the hypothalamus. The hormone ghrelin, secreted by the empty stomach, rises before meals and stimulates hunger and appetite, while leptin, secreted by fat cells, suppresses appetite in proportion to energy stores. Other signals include the satiety hormones cholecystokinin (CCK), peptide YY, and insulin, the neurotransmitters serotonin and dopamine, and sensory cues — sight, smell, taste, and learned associations — that can override homeostatic signals. This reward-based "hedonic" pathway explains why highly palatable foods high in fat, sugar, and salt are eaten beyond energetic need.

Disorders and changes

Loss of appetite (anorexia, in the symptom sense) accompanies many illnesses, infections, cancers, and treatments such as chemotherapy, and can cause dangerous weight loss; in older adults it contributes to frailty. Increased appetite (hyperphagia) occurs in conditions such as hyperthyroidism, some genetic syndromes (e.g., Prader–Willi syndrome), and certain psychiatric and medication contexts. Eating disorders, including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating disorder, involve profound disturbances in appetite regulation and food-related behaviour. Many drugs alter appetite: stimulants and many antidepressants reduce it, while some antipsychotics, corticosteroids, and cannabis increase it — a phenomenon long exploited in appetite-stimulant therapy for cachexia.

Measurement and research

Researchers measure appetite with visual analogue scales of hunger, fullness, and desire to eat, along with blood markers of appetite hormones. Contemporary obesity medicine increasingly targets appetite pathways: GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide reduce appetite and food intake and have produced substantial weight loss in clinical trials, reshaping treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes since the early 2020s.
