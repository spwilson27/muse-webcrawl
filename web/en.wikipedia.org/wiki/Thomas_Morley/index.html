Thomas Morley

Bornc. 1557, Norwich, England
DiedOctober 1602, London, England
OccupationComposer, organist, music theorist, publisher

Thomas Morley (c. 1557 – October 1602) was an English composer, theorist, singer and organist of the Renaissance. He was one of the foremost members of the English Madrigal School — indeed, the most famous composer of secular music in Elizabethan England — and was also a publisher and entrepreneur who held, with William Byrd before him, a monopoly on music printing in England. His treatise A Plaine and Easie Introduction to Practicall Musicke (1597) remained one of the most influential works of music theory of the period.

Life

Morley was born in Norwich, the son of a brewer; he may have been a chorister at Norwich Cathedral. He is thought to have studied with William Byrd, to whom he dedicated his treatise. In 1583 he became organist and master of the choristers at Norwich Cathedral, and in 1588 he took the degree of Bachelor of Music at Oxford. By the early 1590s he was organist of St Paul's Cathedral in London, and in 1592 he became a Gentleman of the Chapel Royal, in which post he remained until his death. Morley was a near neighbour of William Shakespeare in Bishopsgate in the late 1590s, and his setting of "It was a lover and his lass" was long associated with Shakespeare's As You Like It.

Music

Morley's output spans sacred music — services, anthems and psalm settings — keyboard and consort music, and a large body of secular vocal music. His publications included canzonets for three and four voices, madrigals for four and five voices, and his celebrated balletts (1595), modelled on those of Gastoldi, including the evergreen "Now is the month of maying" and "Sing we and chant it". His First Booke of Ayres (1600) contains lute songs, and he edited and contributed to The Triumphs of Oriana (1601), the great collection of madrigals in honour of Queen Elizabeth I.

In 1598 Morley was granted the music-printing monopoly previously held by Byrd, and he used it to issue both his own works and music by contemporaries, helping to create the remarkable flowering of English secular song around 1600.

Legacy

Morley's light, tuneful balletts and madrigals remain among the best-loved of English Renaissance music, and his treatise — written as a dialogue between a master and two pupils — is a major primary source for Elizabethan musical practice, notation and taste.
