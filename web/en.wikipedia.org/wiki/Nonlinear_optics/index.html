Nonlinear optics

Nonlinear optics (NLO) is the branch of optics that describes the behaviour of light in nonlinear media, that is, media in which the polarization density P responds non-linearly to the electric field E of the light. The non-linearity is typically observed only at very high light intensities (when the electric field of the light is strong, comparable to interatomic electric fields, approximately 108 V/m) such as those provided by lasers. Above the Schwinger limit, the vacuum itself is expected to become nonlinear. In nonlinear optics, the superposition principle no longer holds.

History

The first nonlinear optical effect to be predicted was two-photon absorption, by Maria Goeppert Mayer for her PhD thesis in 1931, but it remained an untested theoretical concept until 1961 and the almost simultaneous observation of two-photon absorption at Bell Laboratories and the discovery of second-harmonic generation by Peter Franken et al. at the University of Michigan, both shortly after Theodore Maiman's construction of the first laser. Some nonlinear effects were discovered before the advent of the laser: for example, the Kerr effect (1875), the Pockels effect (1893), and the Raman effect (1928), although these were originally studied with ordinary light sources or explained phenomenologically.

Nonlinear optical processes

In a nonlinear medium, the polarization can be expanded as a power series in the electric field:

P = ε0(χ(1)E + χ(2)E2 + χ(3)E3 + ⋯)

where χ(2) and χ(3) are the second- and third-order nonlinear susceptibilities. The second-order terms give rise to phenomena such as:

  • Second-harmonic generation (SHG), or frequency doubling
  • Sum-frequency generation and difference-frequency generation
  • Optical parametric amplification and oscillation
  • Pockels effect (linear electro-optic effect)
  • Spontaneous parametric down-conversion, used to produce entangled photon pairs

Third-order processes include:

  • Third-harmonic generation
  • Four-wave mixing
  • Kerr effect and self-focusing
  • Cross-phase modulation and stimulated Raman and Brillouin scattering
  • Two-photon absorption

Phase matching

For efficient conversion, the interacting waves must maintain a fixed phase relationship as they propagate through the medium, a condition called phase matching. In practice this is achieved by exploiting birefringence in nonlinear crystals such as beta barium borate (BBO), lithium niobate, and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KDP), or by quasi-phase-matching in periodically poled materials.

Applications

Nonlinear optics underlies many technologies, including frequency-doubled green laser pointers, optical parametric oscillators used as tunable light sources, supercontinuum generation, optical frequency combs, quantum optics experiments producing entangled photons, and all-optical signal processing in telecommunications.
