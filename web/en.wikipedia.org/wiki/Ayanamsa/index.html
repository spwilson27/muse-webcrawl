Ayanamsa

[ Image placeholder: Diagram of tropical and sidereal zodiacs ]

Ayanamsa (Sanskrit: ayanāṃśa, from ayana "movement" and aṃśa "component") is the difference in longitude between the tropical (sayana) and sidereal (nirayana) zodiacs used in Hindu astrology (Jyotiṣa). Because of the precession of the equinoxes — the slow westward drift of the vernal equinox point against the fixed stars, at about 50.3 arcseconds per year — the two zodiacs, which coincided in late antiquity, have steadily separated. The ayanamsa in the early 21st century is approximately 24 degrees.

Systems

Several ayanamsa values are in use, differing in the date at which the zodiacs are taken to have coincided and in the chosen fiducial star:

  • Lahiri (Chitrapaksha) — based on the star Spica (Chitra) placed at 180°; adopted by the Indian Calendar Reform Committee (1955) and the most widely used in India.
  • Raman — B. V. Raman's value, slightly smaller than Lahiri.
  • Krishnamurti — used in the KP system, close to Lahiri.
  • Fagan–Bradley — the standard sidereal ayanamsa in Western sidereal astrology.
  • Yukteswar — based on Sri Yukteswar's yuga calculations in The Holy Science.

History

Indian astronomical texts such as the Sūrya Siddhānta treated precession (in some models as an oscillation or trepidation) and gave correction procedures. The exact epoch of coincidence is debated; dates proposed range from about 285 AD (Lahiri) to the 4th century BC. Western tropical astrology ignores the ayanamsa, while Vedic astrologers subtract it from tropical longitudes to obtain sidereal positions of planets and the ascendant.
