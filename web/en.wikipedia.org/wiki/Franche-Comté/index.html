Franche-Comté (French pronunciation: [fʁɑ̃ʃ kɔ̃te]; literally "Free County") is a cultural and historical region of eastern France, bordering Switzerland. It now forms part of the administrative region of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté. Its capital and largest city is Besançon.


The Citadel of Besançon, designed by Vauban

Geography

The region is dominated by the Jura Mountains in the east, rising to over 1,400 metres, and by the rolling plateaux and valleys of the Doubs and Saône rivers in the west. It is one of the most heavily wooded parts of France, with forests of fir, spruce and oak covering much of its area. The landscape is known for its lakes, waterfalls (such as the Hérisson waterfalls) and deep river gorges.

History

Franche-Comté corresponds roughly to the former Free County of Burgundy, a state of the Holy Roman Empire. It passed to the Habsburgs in the late 15th century and was long ruled from Spain. France conquered it during the Franco-Dutch War, and it was formally ceded by Spain in the Treaty of Nijmegen in 1678. The Citadel of Besançon, built by the military engineer Vauban after the conquest, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The region was a province of France until the Revolution and was reconstituted as an administrative region in the 20th century. On 1 January 2016 it merged with Burgundy to form Bourgogne-Franche-Comté.

Economy and culture

Historically agricultural and industrial, Franche-Comté is famous for Comté and Morbier cheeses, Jura wines (including the distinctive vin jaune), watchmaking around Besançon and Morteau, and the automotive industry — Peugeot's historic home at Sochaux is in the region. The novelist Victor Hugo and the painter Gustave Courbet were born here.
