Clustering illusion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Randomly scattered points naturally form apparent clusters and streaks.

The clustering illusion is the intuition that random events will appear non-randomly clustered, and that apparent streaks or groupings in data must have a cause. It is a cognitive bias in which a person underestimates the amount of variability that naturally occurs in small samples of random data, and so sees patterns that are not really there.

Explanation

Truly random distributions are far "clumpier" than most people expect. In a fair coin toss, runs of five or six heads in a row occur frequently; in random spatial data, points bunch together in some areas and leave gaps in others. When observers assume randomness should look evenly spread, the natural clumps strike them as meaningful.

The psychologists Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone, and Amos Tversky discussed the illusion in their famous 1985 study of the "hot hand" in basketball, arguing that fans and players perceive shooting streaks where the statistics show largely random sequences. Related analyses concern cancer clusters: many reported "clusters" of disease around a location turn out, on investigation, to be consistent with chance—an issue sharpened by the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, in which a boundary is drawn around a cluster after the fact.

Examples

  • Perceiving faces or shapes in random patterns (pareidolia is a related phenomenon).
  • Believing a roulette wheel is "due" for a result, or that a slot machine is on a streak.
  • Seeing meaningful patterns in stock price movements or in strings of lottery numbers.
  • Attributing significance to clusters of accidents, illnesses, or UFO sightings in one locality.
