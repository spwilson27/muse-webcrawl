Cosmic distance ladder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A staircase diagram rising from radar measurements of the solar system to Cepheids, supernovae and the cosmic horizon]
A staircase diagram rising from radar measurements of the solar system to Cepheids, supernovae and the cosmic horizon

The cosmic distance ladder (also the extragalactic distance scale) is the succession of methods by which astronomers determine distances to celestial objects. Because no single technique spans the range, each 'rung' calibrates the next: geometry within the solar system and to nearby stars, Standard candles such as Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae to galaxies, and redshift-based measures to the observable universe's edge.

The rungs

Radar ranging and spacecraft tracking fix the astronomical unit. Stellar Parallax — now measured to micro-arcsecond precision by Gaia — reaches thousands of light-years directly. Within galaxies, Cepheid variables (Henrietta Swan Leavitt's period–luminosity law) and the tip of the red-giant branch give distances to tens of millions of light-years. Type Ia supernovae, standardisable candles visible across billions of light-years, revealed the universe's accelerating expansion (1998; Nobel 2011). Beyond, distance follows redshift through the Hubble–Lemaître law, itself calibrated by the lower rungs.

The Hubble tension

Precisely because errors compound up the ladder, the local measurement of the Hubble constant (~73 km/s/Mpc from the Cepheid–supernova ladder) persistently exceeds the value inferred from the early universe (~67.4), a discrepancy — the 'Hubble tension' — now significant enough to suggest either unrecognised systematics or new physics. James Webb Space Telescope observations since 2023 have broadly confirmed the ladder's Cepheid photometry while sharpening the puzzle.
