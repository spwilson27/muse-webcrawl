Double beta decay
TypeRadioactive decay
ModesTwo-neutrino (2νββ); neutrinoless (0νββ, hypothetical)

Double beta decay (ββ) is a rare type of radioactive decay in which two neutrons in an atomic nucleus are simultaneously transformed into two protons — or two protons into two neutrons — emitting two electrons (or positrons) and two electron antineutrinos (or neutrinos). It occurs in even–even nuclei for which ordinary single beta decay is energetically forbidden or strongly suppressed, and it is the rarest known type of decay, with half-lives typically exceeding 1018 years.

Two-neutrino double beta decay

In the standard two-neutrino mode (2νββ), the two antineutrinos carry away energy, so the summed electron spectrum is continuous. The process was theorised by Maria Goeppert Mayer in 1935 and first observed in the laboratory in 1987 in 82Se. It has since been measured in about a dozen isotopes and is fully consistent with the Standard Model, conserving lepton number.

Neutrinoless double beta decay

If the neutrino is its own antiparticle (a Majorana particle), the two antineutrinos can annihilate internally and the nucleus can decay with emission of only the two electrons — neutrinoless double beta decay (0νββ). This process would violate lepton number by two units and would prove that neutrinos are Majorana fermions, with implications for the origin of neutrino mass and the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe. The electrons would share a fixed total energy, appearing as a sharp peak at the decay Q-value.

Experiments

Large underground experiments — including GERDA and its successor LEGEND (76Ge), CUORE (130Te), KamLAND-Zen, EXO-200 and nEXO (136Xe) — search for the neutrinoless mode with tonne-scale detectors shielded from cosmic rays. As of the mid-2020s no confirmed signal has been seen, and lower limits on the 0νββ half-life exceed 1026 years for some isotopes, constraining the effective Majorana neutrino mass to below roughly a tenth of an electronvolt, depending on nuclear matrix elements.
