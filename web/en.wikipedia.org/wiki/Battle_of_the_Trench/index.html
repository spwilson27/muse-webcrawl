Battle of the Trench

The Battle of the Trench (Arabic: غزوة الخندق, Ghazwat al-Khandaq), also known as the Battle of the Confederates (غزوة الأحزاب), was a 27-day-long siege of Yathrib (now Medina) by Arab and Jewish tribes in 627 CE. The largely outnumbered defenders, led by the Islamic prophet Muhammad, dug a trench around the north-western perimeter of the city — a defensive tactic then unfamiliar in Arabia — and withstood the siege until the confederate army withdrew.

Background

After the Muslim setback at the Battle of Uhud (625), the Meccan Quraysh assembled the largest army yet seen in the region — some 10,000 men with allies including the Ghatafan — intending to destroy the Muslim community in Medina once and for all.

The siege

On the advice of Salman the Persian, the Muslims dug a deep trench across the open northern approach to the city, the only side not protected by lava fields, houses and palm groves. Muslim families withdrew into fortified quarters and the ~3,000 defenders manned the line. Confederate cavalry could not cross the trench, and the siege settled into weeks of archery exchanges, dwindling supplies and cold winds. Diplomacy and intrigue — notably Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud's sowing of distrust between the Quraysh, Ghatafan and the Banu Qurayza — eroded the alliance, and after a stormy night ruined the confederate camp, Abu Sufyan ordered a withdrawal.

Aftermath

The failure of the siege was a turning point: Meccan prestige collapsed, Medina's security was established, and Muhammad's position in Arabia grew steadily stronger, leading within three years to the peaceful Muslim takeover of Mecca. The battle is described in the Qur'an, notably in Surah Al-Ahzab ("The Confederates").
