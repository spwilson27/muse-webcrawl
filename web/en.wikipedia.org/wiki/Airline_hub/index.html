Airline hub

An airline hub or hub airport is an airport used by one or more airlines to concentrate passenger traffic and flight operations at a given point. It serves as a transfer point where passengers and cargo change planes to continue to their destinations, in contrast to point-to-point routes. Operations are typically organised in "banks" — waves of arrivals followed by waves of departures — to minimise connection times.

History

The hub-and-spoke model expanded rapidly after the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 in the United States, when carriers such as Delta at Atlanta and American at Dallas/Fort Worth reorganised their networks around connecting complexes. The model had earlier been pioneered in overnight cargo networks, notably by FedEx at Memphis.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hubs let airlines serve far more city-pairs with a given fleet, fill larger aircraft, and dominate local markets, sometimes earning a "fortress hub" premium. Downsides include congestion, vulnerability to weather disruption at a single airport, and longer total journey times for connecting passengers. Low-cost carriers have generally favoured point-to-point networks, though many also operate de facto bases. A focus city is a smaller station with many point-to-point flights but limited connecting traffic.
