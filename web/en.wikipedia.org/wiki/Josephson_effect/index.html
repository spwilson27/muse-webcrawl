Josephson effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a Josephson junction: two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier]
A Josephson junction: supercurrent tunnels between two superconductors.

The Josephson effect is a phenomenon that occurs when two superconductors are placed in proximity, separated by a very thin insulating barrier (a Josephson junction): a supercurrent can tunnel through the barrier without any voltage, and an applied voltage produces an alternating current whose frequency depends only on fundamental constants (483.6 MHz per microvolt).

Predicted by the 22-year-old Brian Josephson in 1962 (Nobel Prize 1973, with Esaki and Giaever) — to scepticism from John Bardeen himself — the effect directly demonstrates the macroscopic phase coherence of the superconducting wave function. Its applications define modern precision and quantum technology: SQUIDs (superconducting quantum interference devices, loops with two junctions) are the most sensitive magnetometers known, used in geophysics, brain imaging and axion searches; Josephson junctions define the volt (the Josephson constant fixes voltage calibration worldwide); and Josephson circuits are the leading hardware of superconducting quantum computers (transmon qubits).

AC and DC effects

DC effect: current flows without voltage, up to a critical current, when the phase difference across the junction is static. AC effect: a constant voltage V drives oscillations at frequency 2eV/h — so precise that frequency measurement (easy and exact) converts to voltage, inverting naturally into the world's voltage standard. Magnetic flux through a loop containing junctions is quantised in units of h/2e, the basis of SQUID operation.
