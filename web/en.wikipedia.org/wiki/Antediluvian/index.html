Antediluvian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The word antediluvian (from Latin ante, "before", and diluvium, "flood") refers in biblical usage to the period before the Flood described in the Book of Genesis. In ordinary modern English it is used figuratively to mean absurdly old-fashioned or out of date.

The biblical period

In Genesis, the antediluvian era stretches from the creation of Adam to Noah and the Flood. It is populated by the patriarchs — Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech and Noah — ten generations noted for extreme longevity, Methuselah reaching 969 years. The period also includes the enigmatic Nephilim, the "sons of God" of Genesis 6, and the increasing human wickedness that prompts the Flood. Enoch, who "walked with God" and was taken without dying, inspired a large body of Second Temple literature, including the Book of Enoch.

Chronology

Traditional biblical chronologies place the Flood roughly 1,656 years after creation in the Masoretic text, a figure used by Archbishop James Ussher in dating the Flood to 2348 BC. Mesopotamian king lists similarly divide their rulers into pre-flood and post-flood dynasties, with the Sumerian antediluvian kings reigning for tens of thousands of years.

Figurative use

Because the Flood was once seen as the boundary of geological time, "antediluvian" entered geology for strata and fossils predating it, before coming to its modern humorous sense of anything hopelessly antiquated.
