The Benedictus (Latin for "blessed"), also known as the Song of Zechariah, is one of the three great canticles of the New Testament, alongside the Magnificat and the Nunc dimittis. It is recorded in the Gospel of Luke (Luke 1:68–79), where it is sung by Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, on the occasion of his son's circumcision and naming.

The canticle takes its name from its first word in the Latin Vulgate: Benedictus Dominus Deus Israel ("Blessed be the Lord God of Israel"). In Christian liturgy it has for centuries formed the climax of the morning office of Lauds, and it is also sung at Morning Prayer in the Anglican Book of Common Prayer.

Context

According to Luke's narrative, Zechariah, a priest of the division of Abijah, was struck mute by the angel Gabriel after doubting the announcement that his elderly wife Elizabeth would bear a son. When the child was born and named John — against the expectation of relatives who assumed he would be called after his father — Zechariah's speech was restored, and "filled with the Holy Spirit" he prophesied in the words of the Benedictus.

Text and structure

The canticle falls naturally into two parts. The first (verses 68–75) is a hymn of thanksgiving for God's fulfilment of the promises made to Israel: God has "raised up a horn of salvation" in the house of David, spoken by the prophets, and sworn in the oath to Abraham. The second part (verses 76–79) turns to the child John, who is addressed directly as "prophet of the Most High" who will "go before the Lord to prepare his ways", giving knowledge of salvation through the forgiveness of sins. The song concludes with the famous image of the dawn from on high — in Latin oriens ex alto, sometimes translated "the dayspring from on high" — visiting those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death.

Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has visited and redeemed his people, and has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David.

Liturgical use

In the Roman Catholic Liturgy of the Hours, the Benedictus is sung or recited daily at Lauds, framing the psalmody of the morning. Its position at the hour of sunrise suits its closing image of the dawn. In the Church of England's Book of Common Prayer (1662), the Benedictus follows the first lesson at Morning Prayer as an alternative to the Te Deum, in the translation of the Great Bible. In Lutheran worship it has traditionally been associated with the feast of the Nativity of John the Baptist (24 June).

Musical settings

As part of the daily office, the Benedictus has been set to music countless times, most often as plainchant tones and Anglican chant. Settings form part of morning services by composers such as Thomas Tallis, William Byrd, Orlando Gibbons, Henry Purcell and Charles Villiers Stanford. The text has also been incorporated into larger works of Advent and John the Baptist devotion.
