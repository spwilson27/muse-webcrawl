Benedictines

Full name: Order of Saint Benedict (OSB)

Founded: 6th century (Monte Cassino, c. 529)

Founder: Benedict of Nursia

Motto: Ora et labora ("pray and work")

The Benedictines, officially the Order of Saint Benedict (Latin: Ordo Sancti Benedicti, abbreviated OSB), are a monastic religious order of the Catholic Church following the Rule of Saint Benedict. The order is the oldest religious order in the Latin Church and has played a central role in the history of Western monasticism, scholarship, and education.

History

Saint Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547) founded the abbey of Monte Cassino in Italy around 529 and wrote his Rule — a moderate, balanced guide to communal life under an abbot — for the monks there. During the early Middle Ages, Benedictine monasteries spread across Europe and became centres of learning, agriculture, manuscript copying, and hospitality; the Carolingian reforms of Benedict of Aniane made the Rule the standard for Western monasticism. Reform movements repeatedly renewed the order, giving rise to branches such as the Cluniacs, Cistercians, and Camaldolese.

Life and influence

Benedictine monks and nuns take vows of stability (lifelong commitment to one community), obedience, and conversion of life, and divide their day between the communal prayer of the Liturgy of the Hours, sacred reading (lectio divina), and manual or intellectual work — summarised in the motto ora et labora. Benedictine houses are autonomous but linked in congregations and, since 1893, in the Benedictine Confederation, whose abbot primate resides at Sant'Anselmo in Rome. Benedictine schools, universities, and guesthouses remain influential worldwide.
