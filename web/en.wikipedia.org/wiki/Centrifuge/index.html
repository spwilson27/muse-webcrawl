Centrifuge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A centrifuge is a device that uses centrifugal force to subject a specimen to a specified constant force — for example, to separate various components of a fluid. This is achieved by spinning the fluid at high speed within a container, thereby separating fluids of different densities (e.g. cream from milk) or liquids from solids. It works by causing denser substances and particles to move outward in the radial direction, while less dense substances are displaced and move to the centre. In a laboratory centrifuge that uses sample tubes, the radial acceleration causes denser particles to settle to the bottom of the tube, while low-density substances rise to the top.

History

Early centrifuges included hand-powered cream separators; the Swedish engineer Gustaf de Laval developed a practical centrifugal cream separator in 1878. Antonin Prandtl is credited with inventing the first dairy centrifuge in 1864. In the 1920s Theodor Svedberg developed the ultracentrifuge, which made possible the study and sizing of macromolecules and won him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1926.

Types and applications

  • Laboratory centrifuges: microcentrifuges, clinical centrifuges and ultracentrifuges spin samples at relative centrifugal forces from a few hundred to over 1,000,000 × g, separating blood components, cells, organelles, proteins and nucleic acids.
  • Industrial centrifuges: decanter and basket centrifuges separate solids from liquids in food processing, wastewater treatment and chemical manufacture; cream separators and milk clarifiers are ubiquitous in the dairy industry.
  • Gas centrifuges: cascades of gas centrifuges enrich uranium by separating uranium-235 from uranium-238 in uranium hexafluoride — a technology central to nuclear fuel production and to proliferation concerns.
  • Human centrifuges: large-radius centrifuges train pilots and astronauts to withstand high g-forces.

Theory

The relative centrifugal force (RCF) depends on the rotor radius and the square of the rotational speed. Particles sediment at a rate determined by their size, density and shape relative to the medium, described for small particles by Stokes' law adapted to the centrifugal field; the sedimentation coefficient, measured in svedbergs (S), characterises a particle's behaviour.
