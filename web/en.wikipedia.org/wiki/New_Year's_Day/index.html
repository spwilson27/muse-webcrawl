New Year's Day

New Year's Day is the first day of the year in the Gregorian calendar, observed on 1 January. It is a public holiday in most countries of the world and is traditionally marked by gatherings, fireworks, resolutions and ceremonies welcoming the year ahead.

History

The Roman calendar originally began the year in March. In 153 BC the Roman consuls began taking office on 1 January, and Julius Caesar's calendar reform of 45 BC fixed 1 January as the start of the year, honouring Janus, the two-faced god of beginnings who looked backward and forward. During the Middle Ages many European countries moved the start of the year to religious feasts such as 25 March or 25 December, but the Gregorian calendar, promulgated by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582, restored 1 January, which was gradually adopted worldwide.

Observances

Celebrations often begin the night before, on New Year's Eve, with parties, countdowns and fireworks at midnight. On the day itself people make New Year's resolutions — promises of self-improvement for the coming year — exchange greetings, watch parades such as London's New Year's Day Parade and the Rose Parade in Pasadena, and attend sporting events. In the United States, college football bowl games are a traditional feature; polar bear plunges into cold water are held in several countries.

In other calendars

Many cultures celebrate new year at other times: Chinese New Year falls on the second new moon after the winter solstice, the Islamic New Year begins the month of Muharram, the Jewish New Year (Rosh Hashanah) falls in Tishrei, and Hindu festivals such as Ugadi, Gudi Padwa and Vaisakhi mark regional new years in spring.
