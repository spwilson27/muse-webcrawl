Jensen's inequality

In mathematics, Jensen's inequality, named after the Danish mathematician Johan Jensen, relates the value of a convex function of an integral to the integral of the convex function. Jensen proved the inequality in 1906, building on an earlier result by Otto Hölder.

Statements

For a convex function f and numbers x1, …, xn with nonnegative weights summing to one:

f(Σ aixi) ≤ Σ ai f(xi).

In probabilistic form, for a random variable X and convex f: f(E[X]) ≤ E[f(X)]. For a concave function the inequality is reversed. Intuitively, a convex function of an average is at most the average of the function values, because the graph of a convex function lies below its chords.

Proofs and consequences

The finite form follows from the definition of convexity by induction; the general integral form follows by approximation or from the existence of a supporting line at the mean. Jensen's inequality implies the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality (via the concavity of the logarithm) and many inequalities of information theory, including Gibbs' inequality and bounds on Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Applications

Jensen's inequality is fundamental in probability theory, statistics, information theory and economics — for example, it shows that a risk-averse agent with concave utility prefers a certain outcome to a fair gamble with the same mean.
