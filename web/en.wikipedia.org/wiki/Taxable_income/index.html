Taxable income


Taxable income is what remains after deductions from gross or adjusted gross income

Taxable income is the portion of an individual's or company's income that is used to calculate how much tax is owed to the government in a given tax year. It can be broadly described as adjusted gross income (AGI) minus allowable deductions — either itemised deductions or the standard deduction — and, in some systems, personal exemptions. Tax rates are then applied to taxable income, frequently through a schedule of progressive brackets.

United States

Under the US Internal Revenue Code, the computation runs: gross income (all income from whatever source, minus statutory exclusions) less deductions allowed to arrive at adjusted gross income, less either the standard deduction or itemised deductions, gives taxable income for individuals. For corporations, taxable income is gross income less deductions generally, with special rules for items such as dividends received and net operating losses.

Items commonly excluded from gross income include interest on municipal bonds, certain employer-provided benefits, and portions of Social Security benefits. Before 2018, personal exemptions also reduced taxable income; the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act suspended them through 2025 while roughly doubling the standard deduction.

Other jurisdictions

Most income-tax systems follow the same broad architecture — a comprehensive measure of income reduced by allowances and expenses — but differ in detail: the United Kingdom taxes total income less the personal allowance; Canada subtracts deductions to reach "taxable income" after computing "net income"; many countries exempt a basic subsistence amount entirely.

Taxable income vs. economic income

Taxable income rarely equals economic (Haig–Simons) income. Unrealised capital gains, imputed rent of owner-occupied housing, and various tax expenditures cause the two to diverge, a gap that is central to debates about the fairness and efficiency of the income tax.
