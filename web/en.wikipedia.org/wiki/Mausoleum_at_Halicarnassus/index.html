Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A scale reconstruction model of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus with its stepped pyramid roof and quadriga]
A scale reconstruction model of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus with its stepped pyramid roof and quadriga

The Mausoleum at Halicarnassus was a tomb built between about 351 and 350 BC at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum, Turkey) for Mausolus, a satrap of the Achaemenid Empire in Caria, and his wife-sister Artemisia II of Caria. Designed by the Greek architects Satyros and Pythius of Priene and adorned by the age's greatest sculptors, it stood about 45 metres high and was counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World; its fame made 'mausoleum' the generic word for a grand tomb.

Design

The tomb fused Lycian, Egyptian and Greek elements: a high rectangular podium carrying an Ionic colonnade of 36 columns, topped by a stepped pyramid crowned with a marble quadriga bearing statues of Mausolus and Artemisia. Friezes by Scopas, Bryaxis, Leochares and Timotheus — each responsible for one side — depicted battles with Amazons and Greeks; freestanding statues filled the intercolumniations.

History and destruction

Mausolus died in 353 BC; Artemisia, who legend says drank his ashes in her wine, oversaw completion before her own death two years later. The Mausoleum dominated Halicarnassus for over 1,600 years until earthquakes between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries ruined it; the Knights Hospitaller then quarried it for Bodrum Castle, burning marble statues for lime — a loss lamented since the first excavations.

Rediscovery

Charles Newton's excavations in 1856–57 recovered the quadriga fragments and the colossal statues of the royal pair, now in the British Museum — whose Mausoleum Room remains a touchstone in restitution debates. Reconstructions of the building's exact form, guided by Pliny's dimensions, still vary in detail.
