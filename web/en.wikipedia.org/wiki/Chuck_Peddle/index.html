Charles Ingerham "Chuck" Peddle (1937 – December 15, 2019) was an American electrical engineer best known as the principal designer of the MOS Technology 6502 microprocessor, the chip that powered a generation of personal computers including the Apple II, the Commodore 64's predecessors the PET and VIC-20, and the Atari 8-bit family. His insistence on selling the 6502 for $25 — when rivals cost hundreds — is widely credited with igniting the personal computer revolution.

Early life and career

Peddle was born in Bangor, Maine, served in the US Marine Corps, and studied engineering at the University of Maine. He worked at General Electric on time-sharing systems and point-of-sale terminals before joining Motorola in 1973 to work on the 6800 microprocessor.

The 6502

At Motorola, Peddle concluded that the market needed a far cheaper processor than the $175–$300 devices then on offer. When Motorola declined to pursue the low-cost design, Peddle and several colleagues left for MOS Technology in 1975. There his team designed the 6501/6502 family, radically simplifying the design — removing features the market did not need — and exploiting improvements in manufacturing yield. At the Wescon trade show in September 1975 the 6502 was offered for $25, dramatically undercutting the Intel 8080 and Motorola 6800.

Peddle personally promoted the chip to hobbyists and young engineers, among them Steve Wozniak, who used it in the Apple I and Apple II. He also designed the KIM-1 single-board computer and later led the design of Commodore's PET personal computer after Commodore acquired MOS Technology; he subsequently designed the VIC-20 at Commodore before leaving to found Sirius Systems Technology, whose Victor 9000 was an early 16-bit business machine.

Legacy

Peddle is regarded as one of the founding figures of the personal computer industry. The 6502 architecture remained in production for decades in embedded systems, and its descendants powered the Nintendo Entertainment System and other consoles.
