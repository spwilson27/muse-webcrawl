Oil cooling

Type: Cooling method

Coolant: Engine or dielectric oil

Applications: Internal combustion engines, transformers, electric motors, computers

Oil cooling is the use of oil as a coolant to remove heat from machinery. Because oil has a higher boiling point than water, can lubricate the surfaces it cools, and is electrically insulating, it serves a dual purpose in many machines: the same fluid both lubricates moving parts and carries away their heat. The heated oil is typically passed through a radiator or heat exchanger (an oil cooler) and recirculated.

Applications

In internal combustion engines, oil cooling supplements or replaces water cooling. Some motorcycle engines — famously Suzuki's SACS (Suzuki Advanced Cooling System) used on early GSX-R models — spray large volumes of oil onto the undersides of pistons and cylinder heads and are described as oil-cooled. Oil-cooled aircraft piston engines rely on oil flow to cool components that airflow cannot reach. In electrical engineering, large power transformers are immersed in insulating oil, which transfers heat from the windings to the tank walls and radiators; dielectric oils are also used for immersion cooling of electric motors and, occasionally, of enthusiast computers, in which the electronics are submerged in mineral oil.

Comparison with other methods

Compared with water, oil transfers heat less effectively per unit volume and is more viscous, especially when cold, but it can operate at temperatures above water's boiling point without pressurization, does not cause corrosion or electrolysis, and in engines avoids the weight and complexity of a separate water jacket. Air cooling is simpler still but less capable in high-output designs; many engines combine all three: air over finned cylinders, oil for internal hotspots, and liquid coolant for the cylinder head.
