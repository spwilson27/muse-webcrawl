No-slip condition

In fluid dynamics, the no-slip condition is the assumption that a viscous fluid at a solid boundary has zero velocity relative to the boundary — the fluid "sticks" to the wall rather than sliding along it. It is applied as a boundary condition when solving the Navier–Stokes equations and underlies the concept of the boundary layer, within which the flow velocity rises from zero at the wall to the free-stream value.

Justification

The condition is empirical rather than derived from first principles, but it is supported by extensive experiment at ordinary (macroscopic) scales: fluid particles adjacent to a solid surface adhere to it through intermolecular forces, so the tangential fluid velocity matches the velocity of the wall. It was debated historically — Navier himself proposed a partial-slip condition proportional to the shear rate — but by the late nineteenth century measurements of pipe flow and rotating-cylinder viscometry confirmed no-slip for common liquids and gases under normal conditions.

Exceptions

No-slip can break down in special regimes. In rarefied gas flows, characterised by a Knudsen number above about 0.01, measurable slip occurs at the wall. Slip is also observed for liquids on hydrophobic or superhydrophobic surfaces, in flows at micro- and nano-scales, and in some polymer melts and granular flows; these cases are often modelled with a Navier slip length. Nonetheless, for most engineering calculations the no-slip condition is the standard assumption.
