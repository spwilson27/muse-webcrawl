EOKA

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

EOKA fighters in Cyprus, 1950s.

EOKA (Greek: ΕΟΚΑ, /eɪˈoʊkə/) was a Greek Cypriot nationalist guerrilla organisation that fought against British colonial rule in Cyprus from 1955 to 1959, with the aim of enosis — the union of Cyprus with Greece. The name is an acronym of Ethniki Organosis Kyprion Agoniston ("National Organisation of Cypriot Fighters"). Its military leader was Georgios Grivas, a Cypriot-born former colonel in the Greek army who used the nom de guerre "Digenis"; its political leader was Archbishop Makarios III.

The campaign

EOKA began its campaign on 1 April 1955 with coordinated bomb attacks on government and military installations in Nicosia, Famagusta, Larnaca and Limassol. The fighting developed into a guerrilla war concentrated in the Troodos and Kyrenia mountains and in the towns, involving ambushes, assassinations of British personnel and Greek Cypriot "traitors", and bombings. Britain responded with a state of emergency, large troop deployments, curfews, collective punishments and detention camps. Young fighters such as Michalakis Karaolis and Andreas Demetriou, hanged in 1956, became martyrs for the Greek Cypriot cause; the teenage schoolboy distributor Evagoras Pallikarides, hanged in 1957, remains a national symbol. In total, several hundred people were killed, including over 100 British servicemen.

Outcome

Violence between Greek and Turkish Cypriots from 1958, and the threat of partition pressed by Turkey, pushed the parties toward compromise. The Zurich and London Agreements of February 1959 established Cyprus as an independent republic — not united with Greece, as EOKA had fought for, nor partitioned. EOKA declared a ceasefire and was dissolved in March 1959; the Republic of Cyprus was proclaimed on 16 August 1960, with Makarios as president.

EOKA B

In 1971 Grivas returned to Cyprus and founded EOKA B, which fought the government of Makarios, whom he accused of betraying enosis. EOKA B's campaign culminated in its support for the coup of 15 July 1974, which triggered the Turkish invasion of Cyprus five days later and the island's de facto division, which persists today.
