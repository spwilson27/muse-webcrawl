The Archimedean spiral (also known as the arithmetic spiral) is a spiral named after the 3rd-century BC Greek mathematician Archimedes. It is the locus of points corresponding to the locations over time of a point moving away from a fixed point with a constant speed along a line that rotates with constant angular velocity.

Equation

In polar coordinates (r, θ), the Archimedean spiral is described by the equation

r = a + bθ

where a and b are real numbers. Changing a rotates the spiral, while b controls the distance between successive turnings, which is constant and equal to 2πb when θ is measured in radians. In Cartesian coordinates it may be written parametrically as x = (a + bθ) cos θ, y = (a + bθ) sin θ.

Properties

The most distinctive property of the Archimedean spiral is that the separation between successive turns measured along a ray from the origin is constant — unlike the logarithmic spiral, whose turns grow geometrically. Archimedes studied the curve in his treatise On Spirals (c. 225 BC) and used it to solve problems such as trisecting an angle and finding the area enclosed by the first turn, which he showed equals one third of the area of the circle through its endpoint.

Applications

Because the spacing between turns is uniform, the Archimedean spiral appears in the grooves of vinyl records and CDs (as the track path), in spiral compressors and pumps, in the coils of watch balance springs, and in rolled materials such as paper and adhesive tape, where it describes the cross-section of a uniform roll.

Generalizations

The family r = aθ1/n and related curves include the Fermat spiral (n = 2) and the hyperbolic spiral (n = −1). The Archimedean spiral is the case n = 1.
