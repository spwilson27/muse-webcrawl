Dominant caste

A dominant caste is one which preponderates numerically over other castes and also wields preponderant economic and political power. The concept was introduced by the Indian sociologist M. N. Srinivas in his studies of village life in India, notably in his work on the village of Rampura in Karnataka. A dominant caste need not occupy the highest position in the ritual hierarchy of the caste system; its dominance rests on a combination of numerical strength, ownership of land, and political influence.

Village institutions have often been the arena in which dominant castes exercise their influence.

Criteria of dominance

Srinivas identified several attributes that make a caste dominant in a locality:

  • Numerical strength relative to other castes in the village or region
  • Ownership of a large proportion of the arable land
  • A reasonably high position in the local caste hierarchy
  • Political power, including representation in village councils and links to parties and officials
  • Increasingly, Western education and jobs in administration and professions

Examples

Examples of regionally dominant castes include the Jats in parts of Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, the Yadavs in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, the Marathas in Maharashtra, the Reddys and Kammas in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, the Patidars (Patels) in Gujarat, and the Vokkaligas and Lingayats in Karnataka.

Significance

The concept shifted attention in Indian sociology from the purely ritual ranking of castes toward the actual distribution of power in rural society. Dominant castes have played a central role in state politics since independence, and competition among them, as well as the political mobilisation of non-dominant and Dalit groups against them, is a recurring theme in Indian electoral politics. Srinivas also linked the idea to Sanskritisation, the process by which lower castes adopt the practices of higher castes to raise their status.
