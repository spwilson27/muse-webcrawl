Membrane technology

Membrane technology encompasses the use of semi-permeable membranes to separate substances, usually by transporting some components of a mixture while rejecting others. Membrane processes are central to water purification and desalination, wastewater treatment, food processing, medicine (haemodialysis) and gas separation.

Processes

Pressure-driven membrane processes are classified by pore size and the particles they retain:

  • Microfiltration — removes bacteria and suspended solids (~0.1–10 µm pores).
  • Ultrafiltration — removes viruses, proteins and colloids.
  • Nanofiltration — removes multivalent ions and small organic molecules; used for water softening.
  • Reverse osmosis — uses dense membranes that pass water but reject dissolved salts; the principal technology for seawater desalination.

Other membrane operations include electrodialysis (driven by an electric field), pervaporation, membrane distillation and gas permeation, used for example to separate oxygen and nitrogen from air or to recover hydrogen in refineries.

Materials and modules

Membranes are made from polymers (such as polyethersulfone, PVDF and thin-film composite polyamides), ceramics, or metals. They are packaged into modules — spiral-wound, hollow-fibre, plate-and-frame and tubular — designed to pack large surface areas into compact volumes. The main operational challenge is fouling: the accumulation of rejected material on the membrane surface, which must be managed by pretreatment and cleaning.
