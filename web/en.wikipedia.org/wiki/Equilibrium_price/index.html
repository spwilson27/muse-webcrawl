Equilibrium price

The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity of a good supplied equals the quantity demanded. It is the point at which the supply and demand curves intersect in the standard economic model, and it is sometimes called the market-clearing price, because at this price every consumer who wishes to buy at that price can do so and every producer who wishes to sell can do so.

Determination

If the market price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a surplus that pressures sellers to lower the price. If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a shortage that bids the price up. Through this adjustment the market tends toward the equilibrium price, at which the equilibrium quantity is traded.

Shifts

Changes in the determinants of supply or demand shift the curves and produce a new equilibrium. An increase in demand, supply held constant, raises both the equilibrium price and quantity; an increase in supply lowers the equilibrium price while raising quantity. The equilibrium price also underlies analysis of the effects of price controls: a binding price ceiling set below equilibrium generates a shortage, and a binding price floor above it generates a surplus.
