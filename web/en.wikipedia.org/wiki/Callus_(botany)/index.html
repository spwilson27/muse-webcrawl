Callus (botany)

Callus
Type: undifferentiated plant tissue
Composed of: parenchyma cells
Forms at: wound sites, cuttings, tissue culture

Callus is a mass of largely undifferentiated, loosely arranged parenchyma cells that a plant produces in response to wounding, during grafting, and in laboratory tissue culture. In nature, callus forms over cut surfaces and injuries, sealing the wound with a protective layer of corky or soft tissue from which new vascular tissue and roots may later develop. In horticulture, the callusing of cuttings is a familiar precursor to rooting.

Formation

When plant tissue is injured, cells near the wound dedifferentiate and begin rapid division, stimulated by the wound hormones and by plant growth regulators such as auxins and cytokinins. The resulting tissue is typically soft, pale, and disorganized. Given the right balance of hormones, a callus can be induced to regenerate shoots and roots, a phenomenon known as totipotency when a whole plant is regenerated from such cells.

Tissue culture

Callus culture is a cornerstone of plant biotechnology. Explants placed on a nutrient medium containing auxin and cytokinin produce callus that can be maintained indefinitely, used for micropropagation, genetic transformation, somaclonal variation studies, and the production of secondary metabolites. The ratio of auxin to cytokinin in the medium determines whether the callus remains undifferentiated or regenerates roots (high auxin) or shoots (high cytokinin), a principle demonstrated by Skoog and Miller in the 1950s.
