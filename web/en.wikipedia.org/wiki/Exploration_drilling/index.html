Exploration drilling

Image placeholder: Exploration drill rig in the field

Exploration drilling is the process of drilling boreholes to investigate the subsurface geology of an area, typically in search of mineral deposits, oil and gas, groundwater, or for geotechnical purposes. It is a key stage in mineral exploration and petroleum exploration, providing direct samples of rocks and fluids that cannot be obtained from surface mapping or geophysical surveys alone.

Methods

In mineral exploration, the principal techniques are diamond core drilling, which recovers an intact cylinder of rock (drill core) for detailed geological logging and assay; reverse circulation (RC) drilling, which returns crushed rock chips to the surface through dual-wall drill pipe and is faster and cheaper; and rotary air blast (RAB) and aircore drilling, used mainly for shallow, first-pass testing. Core is measured, photographed, logged by geologists, and sampled at intervals for laboratory analysis of metal grades.

In the petroleum industry, exploration wells (wildcat wells) are drilled on prospects identified by seismic surveys; results determine whether a discovery is commercially viable and feed into reserve estimates.

Purpose

Drilling results are used to build three-dimensional geological models, classify mineral resources and reserves, design mines, and decide whether a project should proceed to feasibility study and development. Because drilling is expensive, holes are usually targeted using geological mapping, geochemistry, and geophysics.
