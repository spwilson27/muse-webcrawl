The Legion of Decency, formally the National Legion of Decency, was an organisation dedicated to identifying objectionable content in films, founded in the United States in 1933–34 by the Catholic Church and supported by other religious groups. It was established amid public concern over the content of Hollywood films and worked in tandem with the industry's strengthened Production Code Administration under Joseph Breen.

Work and ratings

The Legion reviewed films and classified them: A (morally unobjectionable, with sub-classes for general audiences, adults and adolescents), B (morally objectionable in part for all), and C (condemned). Catholics were encouraged to take a pledge to avoid condemned films, and bishops organised boycotts; a C rating could seriously damage a film's commercial prospects. Studios submitted scripts and cuts for approval, and the Legion's influence helped enforce the moral strictures of the Hays Code era.

Decline

The Legion's power waned in the 1950s and 1960s as court decisions weakened censorship, foreign films and changing social mores challenged its standards, and the Production Code was replaced by the MPAA ratings system in 1968. The organisation liberalised its classifications, adding categories for adult and mature audiences, and in 1980 its reviewing functions were folded into the U.S. bishops' Office for Film and Broadcasting. Its ratings archive remains a record of mid-twentieth-century American cultural standards.
