Pentium FDIV bug

Pentium FDIV bug

Discovered: 1994

Discovered by: Thomas Nicely

Affected: Early Intel Pentium processors (60–100 MHz)

Cost to Intel: US$475 million

The Pentium FDIV bug is a hardware bug affecting the floating-point unit (FPU) of early Intel Pentium processors, in which certain division operations using the FDIV instruction returned imprecise results. It became one of the most publicised product defects in computing history.

Discovery

In 1994, Thomas Nicely, a professor of mathematics at Lynchburg College, noticed discrepancies while computing reciprocals of twin primes. After ruling out his own code, he traced the error to the processor and contacted Intel. The flaw lay in the radix-4 SRT division algorithm implemented in the chip: five entries in a lookup table in the programmable logic array had been incorrectly left as zero instead of +2, so certain operand patterns produced quotients with errors in the fourth or later significant digit.

Response and fallout

Intel initially offered replacements only to users who could demonstrate they were affected, prompting public outrage and press coverage. In December 1994, IBM halted shipments of Pentium-based PCs, and Intel reversed course, offering a no-questions-asked replacement to all owners of affected chips. The company took a US$475 million charge against earnings. The incident is a classic case study in crisis management and in how a technically small error can cause enormous reputational damage.

Scope

The bug affected only the earliest Pentium models at 60, 66, 75, 90 and 100 MHz. Later chips corrected the lookup table. The probability of an ordinary user encountering the error was estimated by Intel at roughly once in 27,000 years of typical use, a figure critics disputed.
