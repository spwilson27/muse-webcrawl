Minsky moment

A Minsky moment is a sudden, major collapse of asset values that marks the end of the growth phase of a credit cycle or business cycle. It is named after economist Hyman Minsky, whose financial instability hypothesis argued that long periods of stability encourage progressively riskier borrowing, until the financial system becomes fragile enough to collapse under its own weight.

Minsky's theory

Minsky classified borrowers into three types: hedge units, which can repay principal and interest from cash flows; speculative units, which can pay interest but must roll over principal; and Ponzi units, which can pay neither and rely on rising asset prices. Prosperity shifts the system from hedge toward Ponzi finance. When lenders refuse to refinance, borrowers must sell assets, prices fall, and the moment of collapse arrives.

Usage

The term gained wide currency during the 2007–2008 financial crisis, when analysts applied it to the collapse of the US subprime mortgage market and the ensuing credit crunch. It has since been invoked for later debt build-ups in corporate credit, sovereign debt, and emerging markets.
