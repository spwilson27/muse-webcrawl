Intention

An intention is a mental state that represents a commitment to carrying out an action or actions in the future. Intentions involve mental activities such as planning and forethought, and they play a central role in explaining human action: people typically act because they intend to bring about some outcome. The concept is studied in the philosophy of action and mind, psychology, cognitive science and law, where the intent behind an act (for example, in distinguishing murder from manslaughter) is often legally decisive.

Philosophy

G. E. M. Anscombe's Intention (1957) is the foundational modern work, distinguishing three uses: expressions of intention for the future ("I am going to…"), intentional action, and the intention with which an action is done. Donald Davidson treated intentions as part of the reasons (beliefs and desires) that cause and rationalise action. Michael Bratman developed an influential planning theory: intentions are elements of partial, hierarchical plans that constrain further deliberation and coordination with others, and are governed by norms of consistency and means-end coherence. Philosophers debate whether intention can be reduced to belief and desire, whether one can intend what one believes impossible, and the relation between intention and foresight — notably in the doctrine of double effect, where an intended harm is judged differently from a merely foreseen one.

Psychology and neuroscience

Psychological research distinguishes prospective intentions (formed in advance) from the execution of action, and studies implementation intentions — "if–then" plans studied by Peter Gollwitzer — which reliably increase follow-through on goals. Developmental psychologists investigate when children come to understand others' intentions, a milestone in theory of mind. Neuroscientific work associates intention formation with prefrontal and parietal networks, and experiments on the timing of conscious intention in simple movements, such as Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential studies, have provoked debate about free will.

In law

Legal systems grade offences by mental state: in criminal law, mens rea categories such as intention, recklessness and negligence determine culpability, and "specific intent" crimes require proof of a particular purpose. In contract and other areas of civil law, the objective manifestations of intention are typically decisive.
