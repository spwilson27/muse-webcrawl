Strict implication

In logic, a strict implication is a conditional relation between statements where the antecedent necessarily implies the consequent. The notion was introduced by C. I. Lewis in the early 20th century as part of his development of modal logic, in response to the paradoxes of material implication in classical logic.

Where material implication "p → q" is true whenever p is false or q is true — producing such counterintuitive truths as "a false statement implies anything" — strict implication strengthens the connection: p strictly implies q just in case it is necessary that if p is true then q is true. In modern modal notation using the necessity operator □, strict implication is defined as □(p → q): it is impossible for the antecedent to be true while the consequent is false.

History

Lewis presented strict implication in his works of 1912 and 1918, and with Cooper Harold Langford in Symbolic Logic (1932). His systems S1 through S5 of modal propositional logic were designed to capture strict implication and related notions such as strict equivalence. S5 became the most widely studied of these systems.

Paradoxes

Strict implication gives rise to its own paradoxes: a necessary truth is strictly implied by any statement, and an impossible statement strictly implies any statement. These results motivated the development of relevance logic, in which implication requires a genuine relevance between antecedent and consequent.
