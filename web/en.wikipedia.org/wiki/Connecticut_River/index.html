The Connecticut River, the longest river in New England.

The Connecticut River is the longest river in the New England region of the United States, flowing roughly southward for 410 miles (660 km) through four states. It rises at the Connecticut Lakes in northern New Hampshire near the Canadian border, flows along the border between New Hampshire and Vermont, then through western Massachusetts and central Connecticut, emptying into Long Island Sound at Old Saybrook. Its name derives from an Algonquian word meaning "beside the long tidal river".

Course

The river begins at Fourth Connecticut Lake, a small pond a few hundred yards from Quebec, and descends through the chain of Connecticut Lakes. For most of its upper course it forms the Vermont–New Hampshire boundary — notably, it was settled that the border lies at the low-water mark on the Vermont shore, so nearly the entire river lies in New Hampshire. Major cities on its banks include Springfield, Massachusetts, and Hartford, Connecticut, the state capital. Large tributaries include the White, Black, Ammonoosuc, Passumpsic, Ashuelot, Millers, Chicopee, Farmington and Westfield rivers.

History

Indigenous peoples, including the Abenaki and Pequot, lived along the river for thousands of years. Dutch explorer Adriaen Block sailed up it in 1614, and the Dutch established a trading post at present-day Hartford; English settlements followed in the 1630s, among the earliest in New England. The river powered the mills of the Industrial Revolution, drove log drives from northern forests into the mid-20th century, and was a major corridor for the American shad fishery — shad remains Connecticut's state fish. Calvin Coolidge's flood control concerns after the devastating 1927 and 1936 floods led to a system of dams and reservoirs.

Ecology and conservation

The watershed covers 11,260 square miles and is home to many migratory fish, including Atlantic salmon (subject of a long-running restoration effort), American shad, alewife and shortnose sturgeon. In 1997 the river was designated an American Heritage River, and in 2012 its estuary-area wetlands gained recognition under the Ramsar Convention through the Connecticut River Estuary complex. The Connecticut River Conservancy (founded 1952) coordinates cleanup and advocacy, and the 410-mile Connecticut River Paddlers' Trail is a popular recreation route.
