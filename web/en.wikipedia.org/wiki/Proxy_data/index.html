Proxy data

Proxy data are preserved physical characteristics of the environment that can stand in for direct measurements. In paleoclimatology, scientists use proxies to reconstruct climate conditions before the instrumental record (which extends back only to about the mid-19th century for global temperature measurements). By calibrating natural recorders of climate against modern observations, researchers can extend reconstructions of temperature, precipitation and other variables hundreds of thousands — in some cases millions — of years into the past.

Types of proxies

  • Ice cores — annual layers and trapped air bubbles record temperature (via oxygen isotopes), atmospheric composition and volcanic eruptions for up to ~800,000 years in Antarctica.
  • Tree rings — ring width and density reflect growing-season temperature and moisture, datable to the exact year (dendroclimatology).
  • Marine and lake sediments — microfossils (foraminifera, diatoms, pollen) and their shell chemistry record ocean and lake conditions.
  • Corals — growth bands and strontium/calcium ratios record sea-surface temperature.
  • Speleothems — cave deposits whose isotope composition records rainfall history.
  • Boreholes — temperature profiles down wells preserve a filtered record of past surface temperatures.
  • Historical documents — harvest dates, diaries and ships' logs provide qualitative climate information.

Methods and uncertainty

Reconstructions combine many proxy records using statistical methods, and are validated against the instrumental record during a calibration period. Each proxy has seasonal sensitivity, dating error and non-climatic influences (such as tree growth being affected by CO2 fertilisation), so confidence decreases further back in time. Multi-proxy reconstructions — such as those assessed by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change — consistently show recent warming to be unusual in at least the last two millennia.
