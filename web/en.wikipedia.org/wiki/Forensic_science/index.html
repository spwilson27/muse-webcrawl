Forensic science

Forensic science, also known as criminalistics, is the application of science to criminal and civil laws, mainly — on the criminal side — during criminal investigation, as governed by the legal standards of admissible evidence and criminal procedure. Forensic scientists collect, preserve, and analyse physical evidence found at crime scenes and elsewhere, and frequently present their findings as expert witnesses in court.

[Image: A forensic scientist examining evidence in a laboratory]

Subfields

Forensic science encompasses many disciplines, including forensic pathology, toxicology, DNA analysis, fingerprint examination, forensic anthropology and odontology, questioned documents, ballistics, trace evidence analysis, and digital forensics. A guiding idea is Locard's exchange principle — that contact between a perpetrator and a scene leaves traces in both directions — often summarised as "every contact leaves a trace".

History

Systematic forensic methods developed in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries with advances such as fingerprint classification (associated with Francis Galton and Edward Henry), the comparison microscope for ballistics, and early toxicological tests. The introduction of DNA profiling in the 1980s, developed from the work of Alec Jeffreys, transformed identification evidence.

Reliability and reform

Several forensic techniques have come under scientific scrutiny. A 2009 report of the United States National Academy of Sciences criticised the lack of rigorous validation in many pattern-matching disciplines, and wrongful convictions exposed by DNA exonerations have prompted reforms in accreditation, blind testing, and the handling of evidence, including strict attention to the chain of custody.
