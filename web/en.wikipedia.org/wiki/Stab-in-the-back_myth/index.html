Stab-in-the-back myth

The stab-in-the-back myth (German: Dolchstoßlegende) was a conspiracy theory widely believed and promulgated in Germany after the First World War, holding that the German Army did not lose the war on the battlefield but was instead "stabbed in the back" by civilians on the home front — especially Jews, socialists, communists and the politicians who founded the Weimar Republic, who became known to the far right as the "November criminals" for signing the armistice of 11 November 1918.

Origins

In reality, by autumn 1918 the German military position was hopeless: the spring offensive had failed, the Allies' Hundred Days Offensive was breaking the front, Germany's allies were collapsing, and the High Command under Erich Ludendorff and Paul von Hindenburg demanded that the government seek an armistice. Yet military leaders soon shifted blame to the home front. Hindenburg's testimony to a Reichstag investigative committee in November 1919, quoting an unnamed English general, popularised the phrase; Ludendorff had earlier used similar language at a dinner with the British General Neill Malcolm.

Spread and consequences

The myth was propagated by right-wing newspapers, veterans' groups and the German National People's Party, and was later a central element of Nazi propaganda. It undermined the legitimacy of the Weimar Republic from its birth, feeding the belief that democracy itself was a product of treason. Jewish Germans, who had served in the army in large numbers — some 12,000 died in action — were scapegoated, intensifying antisemitism.

Historians regard the myth as a deliberate distortion: the army leadership itself had insisted on the armistice and on a civilian government to request it, before disavowing responsibility for the defeat and the Treaty of Versailles.
