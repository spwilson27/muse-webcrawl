Nondelegation doctrine

The nondelegation doctrine is a principle in United States constitutional law that the Congress of the United States, being vested with "all legislative powers" by Article One of the United States Constitution, cannot delegate that legislative authority to executive branch agencies. The doctrine rests on the maxim delegata potestas non potest delegari ("delegated power cannot be further delegated"). In practice, the Supreme Court has permitted broad delegations so long as Congress provides an "intelligible principle" to guide the agency's discretion.

The doctrine polices the boundary between Congress and the executive agencies.

History

The Supreme Court articulated the permissive "intelligible principle" standard in J.W. Hampton Jr. & Co. v. United States (1928). Only twice — both in 1935, in Panama Refining and Schechter Poultry, striking down New Deal measures — has the Court invalidated statutes on nondelegation grounds. Since then it has upheld very broad grants of rulemaking power, as in Mistretta v. United States (1989) and Whitman v. American Trucking Associations (2001).

Modern debate

In the 21st century several justices have expressed interest in reinvigorating the doctrine. In Gundy v. United States (2019), a plurality upheld a delegation under the intelligible-principle test, while Justice Gorsuch's dissent, joined by the Chief Justice and Justice Thomas, proposed a stricter framework; in a separate case Justice Alito indicated openness to reconsidering the doctrine. Critics of revival warn it would destabilise the modern administrative state; supporters argue broad delegations undermine democratic accountability.
