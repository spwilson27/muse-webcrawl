Proleptic Julian calendar

The proleptic Julian calendar is produced by extending the Julian calendar backwards to dates preceding its introduction in 45 BC — "proleptic" meaning that the calendar is applied to a period before it existed. In the historical Julian calendar as actually used in Rome, leap years were applied inconsistently for several decades after its introduction (the leap year error, corrected by Augustus by omitting leap years until AD 8). The proleptic version ignores this irregularity and applies the four-year leap rule uniformly.

Usage

The proleptic Julian calendar is used by historians to give dates for events of classical antiquity and the early Middle Ages in a consistent Julian framework, and it is the calendar underlying the system of Julian day numbers used in astronomy for reckoning intervals. For dates after the introduction of the Gregorian calendar (1582), the proleptic Gregorian calendar is more often used, for instance in ISO 8601.

Year numbering

Because the Julian calendar predates the Anno Domini era devised by Dionysius Exiguus in AD 525, applying it proleptically requires a convention for years before AD 1. Historians generally use 1 BC, 2 BC, etc., with no year zero, whereas astronomers use a year 0 (equal to 1 BC) and negative years. The proleptic Julian calendar differs from the proleptic Gregorian calendar only in its leap-year rule: every year divisible by 4 is a leap year, whereas the Gregorian rule omits most century years.

Relation to the historical calendar

Between 45 BC and AD 8, Roman practice inserted leap days every three years at first and then not at all for twelve years, so dates given in contemporary sources can differ from proleptic Julian dates by up to three days in this interval. From AD 8 onward, the historical and proleptic calendars agree.
