Thymidine (symbol dT or dThd), also known as deoxythymidine, is a pyrimidine deoxynucleoside. It consists of the nucleobase thymine joined to the sugar deoxyribose. Deoxythymidine is the nucleoside T, which pairs with deoxyadenosine (A) in double-stranded DNA. In cell biology it is used to synchronize the cells in S phase of the cell cycle.

Structure and properties

Thymidine is composed of deoxyribose (a pentose sugar) joined to the pyrimidine base thymine. Thymine can be converted to uracil — thymine is essentially a methylated uracil — and the corresponding ribonucleoside, ribothymidine, is rare, occurring in transfer RNA. Thymidine can be phosphorylated to thymidine monophosphate (TMP), diphosphate (TDP) and triphosphate (TTP); TTP is one of the four deoxynucleotide building blocks of DNA.

Biological role and uses

Cells salvage thymidine from the environment via thymidine kinase, an enzyme widely used as a selectable marker in molecular biology (for example in HAT medium, where thymidine kinase-deficient cells are selected). Radiolabelled thymidine (tritiated thymidine) was historically used to measure DNA synthesis and cell proliferation. The "thymidine block" technique — excess thymidine inhibiting ribonucleotide reductase and halting cells at the G1/S boundary — remains a standard synchronization method.

Derivatives

The antiviral and anticancer drug zidovudine (AZT) is a thymidine analogue (3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine), as is stavudine. Idoxuridine and trifluridine are halogenated thymidine analogues used as antivirals, and bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) is widely used as a label for newly synthesized DNA.
