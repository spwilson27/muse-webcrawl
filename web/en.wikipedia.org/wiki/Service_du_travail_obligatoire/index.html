The Service du travail obligatoire (STO; "Compulsory Work Service") was the forced enlistment and deportation of hundreds of thousands of French workers to Nazi Germany to work as forced labour for the German war effort during the Second World War. Established by a law of the Vichy regime of 16 February 1943, it conscripted young men of the classes of 1920–1922 for two years of labour in Germany.

Background

After the occupation of France, Germany demanded ever more labour as its own workforce was mobilised. Gauleiter Fritz Sauckel, the Reich's plenipotentiary for labour deployment, pressed Vichy for workers. Prime Minister Pierre Laval first promoted the relève (1942), an exchange supposedly trading French workers for prisoners of war, but volunteer numbers fell short of German quotas, prompting the compulsory STO law.

Effects and resistance

Under STO, roughly 600,000 to 650,000 French workers were sent to Germany; altogether, including the relève and other schemes, France supplied some 1.5 million workers over the occupation. The measure was deeply unpopular and proved a turning point in French opinion against Vichy: tens of thousands of young men evaded the draft, many joining the maquis of the French Resistance, which swelled dramatically in 1943 partly as a result. STO workers laboured in German factories, agriculture, and construction under harsh conditions, often exposed to Allied bombing; many thousands died.

Aftermath

After the Liberation, returning STO workers often felt their experience was unrecognised beside that of deportees and resisters; formal recognition as réfractaires (draft evaders) and victims came only gradually over subsequent decades.
