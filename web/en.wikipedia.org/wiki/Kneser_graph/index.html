Kneser graph

The Petersen graph is the Kneser graph KG(5,2)

In graph theory, the Kneser graph KG(n, k) is the graph whose vertices correspond to the k-element subsets of a set of n elements, and where two vertices are adjacent if and only if the corresponding subsets are disjoint. Kneser graphs are named after Martin Kneser, who first studied their chromatic number in 1955.

Examples

The Kneser graph KG(5, 2) is the famous Petersen graph: its vertices are the ten 2-element subsets of a 5-element set, joined when disjoint. The odd graphs are the special case KG(2k + 1, k). For k = 1, KG(n, 1) is simply the complete graph on n vertices.

Chromatic number

Kneser conjectured that the chromatic number of KG(n, k) is n − 2k + 2 (for n ≥ 2k). Upper bounds of this size are easy to construct, but the lower bound resisted proof for over two decades. In 1978 László Lovász proved the conjecture using the Borsuk–Ulam theorem, in a landmark early application of topological methods to combinatorics. Simpler proofs followed, including one by Imre Bárány and a famously short argument by Joshua Greene based on work of Jiří Matoušek.

Properties

Kneser graphs are vertex-transitive and contain large cliques only in trivial ranges; their independence number is given by the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, which counts the largest family of pairwise intersecting k-subsets. The generalised Kneser graph KG(n, k, s) joins vertices when their intersections have size at most s.
