P-value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A null distribution with the tail area beyond an observed value shaded, representing a p-value]

In hypothesis testing, the p-value is the probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those actually observed.

Small p-values indicate the data would be unusual under the null hypothesis. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability the result occurred by chance in the everyday sense — persistent misreadings the American Statistical Association warned against in 2016.

History

Informal significance calculations date to the 1700s; Karl Pearson formalised the p-value around 1900 and Ronald Fisher popularised it in the 1920s, suggesting 0.05 as a convenient threshold.

Because results just below 0.05 are prized, researchers may consciously or unconsciously 'p-hack' — trying analyses until one is significant — inflating false positives across the literature.
