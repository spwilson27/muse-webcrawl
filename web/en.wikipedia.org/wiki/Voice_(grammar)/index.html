Voice (grammar)

Voice expresses how the subject relates to the action of the verb

In grammar, voice (also called diathesis) is a property of verbs that expresses the relationship between the action (or state) the verb describes and the participants identified by its arguments — in particular, whether the subject performs the action (active voice) or undergoes it (passive voice).

Active and passive

In the active sentence "The cat chased the mouse", the subject is the agent. The passive counterpart, "The mouse was chased (by the cat)", promotes the patient to subject position, demotes the agent to an optional by-phrase, and marks the verb with passive morphology — in English, the auxiliary be (or get) plus the past participle. Passives serve discourse functions such as keeping a given topic in subject position, backgrounding an unknown or obvious agent, and achieving formality in scientific writing.

Other voices

Many languages have further voices. The middle voice, found in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit, marks actions the subject performs on or for itself, or experiences. Antipassive voice, common in ergative languages, demotes the patient instead. Applicative and causative constructions increase valency by promoting an oblique participant or adding a causer. Philippine-type languages such as Tagalog are famous for rich "focus" systems in which the verb marks which participant — actor, patient, location, instrument or beneficiary — occupies the privileged syntactic position, an analysis debated as voice versus case alignment. English also has a middle construction ("This book reads easily") and an impersonal passive in some registers.

Voice and valency

Voice alternations are a principal means by which languages change a verb's valency and argument structure without changing its core lexical meaning, and they interact with case marking, word order and information structure in every language that has them.
