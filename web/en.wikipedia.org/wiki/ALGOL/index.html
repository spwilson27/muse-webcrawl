ALGOL (short for "Algorithmic Language") is a family of imperative computer programming languages originally developed in 1958. It was the standard method for algorithm description used by the Association for Computing Machinery for many years, and it profoundly influenced later language design.

History

ALGOL arose from a joint committee of American and European computer scientists seeking a universal language for describing algorithms, in reaction to the machine-specific codes and early FORTRAN. ALGOL 58 was followed by ALGOL 60, defined by the Report on the Algorithmic Language ALGOL 60 edited by Peter Naur, which introduced the Backus–Naur form (BNF) for syntax description and lexical block structure with nested scopes. Contributors included John Backus, Edsger W. Dijkstra (who built the first ALGOL 60 compiler in 1960), and Alan Perlis. ALGOL 68, designed under Adriaan van Wijngaarden, was more powerful but controversial and less widely implemented.

Influence

ALGOL introduced block structure, begin/end delimiters, explicit variable declarations, recursion as a general feature, and call-by-name parameter passing. Its descendants — Pascal, Modula, Ada, C and, through them, most modern imperative languages — dominate programming to this day; as Tony Hoare remarked, ALGOL was "an improvement on nearly all its successors". Burroughs mainframes were architected around ALGOL and its dialects persisted there for decades.
