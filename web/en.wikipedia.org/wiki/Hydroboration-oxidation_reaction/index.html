Hydroboration–oxidation reaction

The hydroboration–oxidation reaction is a two-step organic reaction that converts an alkene into an alcohol with anti-Markovnikov regiochemistry. In the first step, a borane reagent such as diborane (B2H6), borane–THF, or a substituted borane adds across the carbon–carbon double bond to give an organoborane. In the second step, oxidation with hydrogen peroxide in aqueous sodium hydroxide replaces the carbon–boron bond with a carbon–oxygen bond, with retention of configuration. The net result is the hydration of the double bond with the hydroxyl group ending up on the less substituted carbon — the opposite regiochemistry to acid-catalysed hydration — and with syn stereochemistry.

History

The reaction grew out of Herbert C. Brown's systematic studies of borane chemistry in the 1950s, including his discovery that boranes add readily to alkenes. Brown shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of boron compounds as reagents in organic synthesis. Hydroboration–oxidation became a standard textbook method for preparing primary alcohols from terminal alkenes and remains a cornerstone of organoborane chemistry.

Mechanism

Hydroboration proceeds by a concerted syn addition through a four-membered cyclic transition state in which the boron and hydrogen add to the same face of the double bond simultaneously; because no carbocation intermediate forms, rearrangements do not occur. Boron attaches to the less hindered, less substituted carbon, both for steric reasons and because partial positive charge in the transition state is better borne by the more substituted carbon. With borane itself, three alkene molecules can add successively to give a trialkylborane. In the oxidation step, hydroperoxide anion attacks boron, and the alkyl group migrates from boron to oxygen (a 1,2-shift with retention at carbon); repetition and hydrolysis of the resulting borate ester release the alcohol.

Scope and variants

Bulky boranes such as disiamylborane and 9-BBN improve regioselectivity with internal alkenes. Chiral boranes derived from α-pinene (such as diisopinocampheylborane) allow enantioselective hydroboration. Alkynes can be hydroborated and oxidized to give aldehydes (from terminal alkynes) or ketones, providing an alternative to mercury-catalysed hydration. Organoboranes from hydroboration also serve as intermediates in other reactions, notably the Suzuki coupling.
