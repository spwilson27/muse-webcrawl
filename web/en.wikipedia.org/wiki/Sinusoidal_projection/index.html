Sinusoidal projection

The sinusoidal projection is a pseudocylindrical equal-area map projection, sometimes called the Sanson–Flamsteed or Mercator equal-area projection. It preserves areas exactly and represents the equator and central meridian true to scale.

Formulas

With longitude λ measured from the central meridian and latitude φ, the coordinates are:

x = λ cos φ,   y = φ

Parallels are horizontal lines spaced evenly, with lengths proportional to the cosine of latitude, while meridians are sinusoidal curves (except the straight central meridian). The poles project as points, and the outer meridians bulge so that the map's outline resembles a heart-like or apple-like shape when drawn for the whole globe.

History

The projection was known to Jean Cossin of Dieppe in the 1570s and was used by Nicolas Sanson from about 1650 and by John Flamsteed for star charts, hence the name Sanson–Flamsteed. It was widely used for atlases of continents such as Africa and South America, where its low distortion near the equator and central meridian is advantageous.

Interrupted form

Because distortion grows with distance from the central meridian, the projection is frequently used in interrupted (lobed) form, with separate central meridians for each continent or ocean — an arrangement that is also the basis of the MODIS global grid used for satellite imagery.
