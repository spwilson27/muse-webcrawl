Fahrenheit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A dual-scale thermometer with Fahrenheit markings prominent]
A dual-scale thermometer with Fahrenheit markings prominent

The Fahrenheit scale (°F) is a temperature scale in which water freezes at 32° and boils at 212° at standard atmospheric pressure, placing 180 degrees between the two points. Proposed by the Polish-Dutch physicist Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit in 1724, it was the standard scale of the English-speaking world and remains the official everyday scale of the United States and a few other countries. Conversion: °C = (°F − 32) × 5⁄9.

Origins

Fahrenheit, a Gdańsk-born instrument maker in Amsterdam who pioneered the mercury-in-glass thermometer, fixed his scale on three points: a brine mixture's temperature (0°), freezing water (32°) and — approximately, on his instruments — human body temperature (originally 96°). His 1724 paper to the Royal Society made the scale the precision standard of its century.

Modern status

Metrication moved most countries to Celsius during the twentieth century; the United States retained Fahrenheit in the failed metric transition of the 1970s, and it persists in US weather, cooking and industry, with dual reporting common in science and aviation. Defenders argue its finer everyday gradations and 0–100 'human range' suit weather description; critics cite global consistency.
