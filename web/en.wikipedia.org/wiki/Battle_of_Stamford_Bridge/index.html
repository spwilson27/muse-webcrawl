The Battle of Stamford Bridge took place at the village of Stamford Bridge, East Riding of Yorkshire, in England, on 25 September 1066, between an English army under King Harold Godwinson and a Norwegian invading force led by King Harald Hardrada and the English king's brother Tostig Godwinson. After a bloody battle, both Hardrada and Tostig were killed, and the Norwegian army was comprehensively defeated. The battle is traditionally regarded as marking the end of the Viking Age.

Background

The death of Edward the Confessor in January 1066 left the English throne contested. Harold Godwinson was crowned king, but Harald Hardrada of Norway pressed his own claim and invaded northern England in September 1066, sailing up the Humber with a fleet of some 300 ships. Allied with Tostig, Harold's exiled brother, the Norwegians defeated the northern earls Edwin and Morcar at the Battle of Fulford on 20 September and occupied York.

Battle

Harold marched his army north from London with remarkable speed — covering roughly 185 miles in four days — and caught the Norwegians by surprise at Stamford Bridge, where they had gathered to receive hostages. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and later Norse sagas describe a fierce fight at the bridge over the River Derwent; saga tradition holds that a single giant Norwegian axeman held the bridge against the English until he was killed by a soldier who floated beneath the bridge and thrust a spear upward through its timbers. Once across, the English engaged the Norwegian army, much of which had left its armour aboard the ships because of the warm day. Hardrada was killed by an arrow to the throat, and Tostig fell soon after. The Norwegians were routed; of the 300 ships that had brought the invasion force, only 24 were needed to carry the survivors home.

Aftermath

Harold's triumph was short-lived. Three days after the battle, William of Normandy landed on the south coast, forcing Harold to march his exhausted army south again. On 14 October 1066 Harold was killed at the Battle of Hastings, and the Norman Conquest of England began. Stamford Bridge nevertheless stands as one of the most decisive English victories of the medieval period and is often seen as the final end of large-scale Viking invasions of England.
