Fractional-reserve banking

Fractional-reserve banking is the system under which banks hold only a fraction of their deposit liabilities in reserve (as cash and central-bank balances) and lend or invest the remainder. It is the dominant banking model worldwide and allows banks to create money in the form of deposit balances when they lend.

Fractional-reserve banking
Type: Banking system
Key feature: Reserves < deposits
Regulation: Reserve requirements, capital rules, deposit insurance

How it works

Because depositors rarely withdraw all funds at once, banks keep a prudent fraction liquid and earn interest on loans from the rest. Textbooks describe a money multiplier: an initial deposit supports a larger total of deposits through successive lending. Modern central banks stress that in practice lending is constrained by capital, credit demand and settlement needs rather than mechanically by reserves.

Risks and regulation

The maturity mismatch between instant-access deposits and long-term loans makes banks vulnerable to runs, mitigated by central-bank lending of last resort, deposit insurance, reserve and liquidity requirements and capital adequacy rules (Basel accords). Critics, including full-reserve advocates, argue the system is inherently unstable or unfair; defenders credit it with efficient credit creation.
