Diophantine approximation

In number theory, Diophantine approximation deals with the approximation of real numbers by rational numbers. It takes its name from the 3rd-century Alexandrian mathematician Diophantus. The central question is how well a given real number α can be approximated by a fraction p/q, as a function of the size of the denominator q.

Dirichlet's approximation theorem guarantees that for any real α there are infinitely many fractions p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/q², and continued fractions provide the very best such approximations. Hurwitz's theorem sharpens the constant for the most difficult case, showing that √5 is optimal and that the golden ratio is the hardest number to approximate — a fact expressed through the Markov and Lagrange spectra.

Deeper results include Roth's theorem (1955), for which Klaus Roth won a Fields Medal: algebraic irrationals cannot be approximated to any order better than 2 + ε. The theory connects with transcendental number theory — Liouville's construction of the first explicit transcendental numbers came from extremely good rational approximations — as well as with the geometry of numbers, simultaneous approximation of several numbers, and badly approximable numbers.
