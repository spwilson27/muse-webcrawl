Military chaplain

A military chaplain is a chaplain who ministers to military personnel and, in most cases, their families and civilians who work for the military. In some cases they also work with local civilians within a military area of operations. Although the term chaplain originally had Christian roots, it is generally used today in military organisations to describe all professionals specially trained to serve any spiritual need, regardless of religious affiliation.

Role and duties

Military chaplains provide pastoral care, religious services, counselling and moral support to service members of all faiths and none. They conduct worship, administer sacraments or rites for their own faith tradition, facilitate the religious practice of others, and advise commanders on religion, ethics and morale. Chaplains typically hold commissioned officer status but are non-combatants; under the Geneva Conventions retained chaplains are protected personnel and are not treated as prisoners of war.

History

Priests accompanied armies in many ancient and medieval societies. In England, chaplains served with expeditionary forces from the medieval period, and the modern chaplaincy developed during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Chaplains of many faiths — Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh among them — now serve in numerous armed forces. Many chaplains have been decorated for bravery; in the United States several have received the Medal of Honor, often posthumously, for ministering under fire.

Chaplaincies by country

In the United Kingdom, chaplains serve in the Royal Army Chaplains' Department, the Royal Navy Chaplaincy Service and the Royal Air Force Chaplains Branch. In the United States, each service has its own chaplain corps, headed by a Chief of Chaplains. Other countries maintain similar corps, sometimes organised as a separate joint service.
