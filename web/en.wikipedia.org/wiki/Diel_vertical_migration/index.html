Diel vertical migration

Daily migration between deep daytime refuge and surface feeding grounds

Diel vertical migration (DVM), also known as diurnal vertical migration, is a pattern of movement used by many organisms living in the world's oceans and lakes: they migrate upward into the surface waters at night to feed, and return to the darker depths during the day. It is thought to be the largest migration on Earth by biomass, undertaken daily by zooplankton, krill, lanternfish, squid and many other animals.

Mechanism and causes

The migration is synchronised by changes in light intensity at dawn and dusk. The prevailing explanation is predator avoidance: organisms feed on phytoplankton-rich surface waters under cover of darkness and retreat to deep, dark water by day, where visual predators cannot easily hunt them. Other contributing factors may include energy conservation in cold deep water and exposure to damaging ultraviolet radiation at the surface.

Discovery

The phenomenon was first widely recognised during the Second World War, when naval sonar operators observed a "deep scattering layer" that appeared to rise toward the surface at night and sink during the day — at first mistaken for the seabed. The layer consists of huge aggregations of migrating organisms reflecting sonar signals.

Ecological significance

DVM is a major component of the ocean's biological carbon pump: animals feed at the surface at night and respire, excrete and die at depth, actively transporting carbon and nutrients from the surface into the deep ocean. It also links surface and deep food webs, sustaining predators such as tuna, sharks and whales.
