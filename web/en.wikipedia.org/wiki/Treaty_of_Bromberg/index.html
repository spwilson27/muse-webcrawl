Treaty of Bromberg

Signed: 6 November 1657

Location: Bromberg (Bydgoszcz), Poland

Parties: Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth; Brandenburg-Prussia

Context: Second Northern War (the Deluge)

The Treaty of Bromberg (German: Vertrag von Bromberg, Polish: Traktat bydgoski), signed on 6 November 1657, was a treaty between the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and Brandenburg-Prussia during the Second Northern War. Together with the preceding Treaty of Wehlau of 19 September 1657, it is often referred to as the Treaty of Wehlau and Bromberg.

Terms

In return for Frederick William, Elector of Brandenburg and Duke of Prussia, breaking his alliance with Sweden and joining the war against Charles X Gustav, King John II Casimir of Poland recognised the full sovereignty of the Hohenzollern dynasty in the Duchy of Prussia, ending the feudal vassalage that had existed since 1525. Brandenburg also received the Lauenburg and Bütow Land and the town of Elbing (Elbląg) as a pledge (Elbing was never in fact handed over), while the Elector undertook to provide troops for the war and pay towards Poland's costs. Drahim (Draheim) was additionally pledged to Brandenburg in 1668 for a loan.

Significance

The treaty was a landmark in the rise of Prussia: the sovereignty gained in 1657 was recognised internationally at the Peace of Oliva in 1660, and it was on this sovereign Prussian soil — outside the Holy Roman Empire — that Frederick William's son could crown himself "King in Prussia" at Königsberg in 1701, a foundational step toward the Kingdom of Prussia and, ultimately, the unification of Germany.
