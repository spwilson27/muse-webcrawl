The Pyramid of Khufu, also known as the Great Pyramid of Giza, is the largest Egyptian pyramid and the tomb of the Fourth-Dynasty pharaoh Khufu (Greek: Cheops). Built on the Giza plateau over roughly two to three decades around 2560 BCE, it was the tallest human-made structure in the world for more than 3,800 years — until the completion of Lincoln Cathedral's spire around 1311 CE — and is the oldest of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World and the only one to remain largely intact.

Construction

The pyramid was built of an estimated 2.3 million blocks of local limestone, with fine white Tura limestone casing (mostly stripped in the Middle Ages for building in Cairo) and granite from Aswan for the internal chambers. Its sides are aligned to the cardinal points to within a few arc-minutes and its base is level to within centimetres. How the blocks were raised remains debated — ramps of various designs are the leading candidates — but the discovery of the Wadi al-Jarf papyri (the journal of the inspector Merer) confirmed that casing stones were shipped by boat from Tura during Khufu's reign. Modern estimates put the workforce at some tens of thousands of rotating labourers, housed in a purpose-built town, rather than the vast slave armies imagined by Herodotus.

Interior

Unlike most pyramids, Khufu's has an ascending internal system: an entrance leading to a Descending Passage and an unfinished subterranean chamber; an Ascending Passage to the Grand Gallery and the "Queen's Chamber"; and the King's Chamber, lined in red granite, containing a granite sarcophagus, with five relieving chambers above it to spread the load. Narrow "air shafts" run from both chambers; robot explorations since the 1990s have revealed blocking stones with copper fittings, and muon tomography announced in 2017 (the ScanPyramids project) detected a large void above the Grand Gallery, whose nature remains unexplained.

The complex

The pyramid formed the centre of a funerary complex: a mortuary temple, a causeway to a valley temple, subsidiary pyramids for queens, boat pits — including the intact Khufu ship, a 43-metre cedar vessel now housed at the Grand Egyptian Museum — and cemeteries of mastaba tombs for officials.

Exploration and robbery

The pyramid was robbed in antiquity; the present tourist entrance follows a tunnel forced by Caliph al-Ma'mun's men around 820 CE. Systematic measurement began with Flinders Petrie's survey of 1880–82, which set new standards in archaeological precision. The Great Pyramid has attracted centuries of fringe theories ("pyramidology"), which Egyptologists reject; its documented achievement needs no such embellishment.
