Algae fuel

Algae fuel, also called algal biofuel or algaeoleum, is an alternative to liquid fossil fuels that uses algae as its source of energy-rich oils. Algae can be grown in open ponds or closed photobioreactors, harvested, and processed into biodiesel, bioethanol, biogas, or jet fuel, in principle offering much higher yields per acre than conventional oilseed crops without competing directly for arable land or fresh water.

History

Research began in earnest during the 1970s energy crisis, notably through the U.S. Department of Energy's Aquatic Species Program (1978–1996), which concluded that algae fuel was technically feasible but not then cost-competitive. Interest revived in the 2000s with high oil prices and large investments from major energy companies, though many early ventures pivoted to higher-value products such as nutraceuticals and cosmetics when fuel economics proved stubborn.

Production

Selected strains of microalgae accumulate lipids — sometimes over half their dry weight — under nutrient stress. Cultivation systems range from cheap raceway ponds to capital-intensive tubular photobioreactors. Harvesting and dewatering the dilute culture is a major energy cost; extracted oils are converted to biodiesel by transesterification, or to drop-in fuels by hydroprocessing. Residual biomass can be fermented, digested to biogas, or sold as feed.

Advantages and challenges

Advocates cite rapid growth, the ability to use saline or wastewater, and CO2 capture from flue gas. Critics point to the energy balance of harvesting, the cost of nutrients, contamination in open ponds, and the difficulty of scaling; as of the 2020s no algae fuel had reached sustained price parity with petroleum fuels.
