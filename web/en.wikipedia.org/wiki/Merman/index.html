Merman

Mermen appear in the folklore of cultures around the world.

A merman (plural mermen; the corresponding female is the mermaid) is a legendary aquatic creature with the head and torso of a human man and the tail of a fish. Mermen figure in the mythology and folklore of many cultures worldwide, including those of the Near East, Europe, Africa, Asia and the Americas. Collectively, mermen and mermaids are sometimes called merfolk.

In mythology

The earliest fish-tailed male deities appear in Mesopotamia: Ea (Enki), god of waters and wisdom, and the apkallu sages were depicted with fish cloaks or fish tails. In Greek tradition the sea god Triton, son of Poseidon and Amphitrite, was imagined as a merman blowing a conch shell, and the ichthyocentaurs combined human torsos with serpentine fish tails and horse's forelegs. Sea-bishops and other marvellous fish-men populate medieval bestiaries.

Character

In folklore, mermen are often portrayed as wilder and less beautiful than mermaids, and more indifferent or hostile to humans: where mermaids may lure sailors with song, mermen were credited with raising storms and sinking ships. In some traditions, such as the Irish merrow (in which the male is notably uglier than the female), they keep the souls of drowned sailors in cages under the sea. In other stories mermen are wise healers or guardians of sunken treasure. The figure survives in heraldry, in Hans Christian Andersen's tale (where the sea king and his court are mermen), in Matthew Arnold's poem "The Forsaken Merman" (1849), and in modern fantasy and film.

Reported sightings

Like mermaids, mermen appear in historical reports of sea monsters — from medieval chronicles to the logbooks of early modern sailors — usually explained today as misidentified seals, dugongs, manatees or rays, or as deliberate hoaxes such as the "Fiji mermaid" exhibited by P. T. Barnum.
