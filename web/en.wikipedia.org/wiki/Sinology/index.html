Sinology

Sinology, also referred to as China studies, is the academic study of China primarily through Chinese language, history, culture, literature, philosophy, and society. Its origin, like that of other classical studies such as Egyptology, is traditionally associated with philology — the close study of a civilisation's classical texts and language. A scholar of Sinology is a Sinologist.

History

European knowledge of China grew through the reports of merchants and travellers such as Marco Polo, and especially through Jesuit missionaries of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including Matteo Ricci, who learned Chinese and translated Chinese classics into Latin. Sinology became a university discipline in the nineteenth century: the first chair of Chinese in Europe was established at the Collège de France in 1814 for Jean-Pierre Abel-Rémusat. Major nineteenth- and early twentieth-century figures include James Legge, whose translations of the Chinese classics into English remain standard; Herbert Giles; Édouard Chavannes; and Paul Pelliot.

In the twentieth century, figures such as Marcel Granet, Joseph Needham — whose monumental Science and Civilisation in China documented Chinese scientific achievement — John King Fairbank, and Derk Bodde shaped the field. The political upheavals of 1949 redirected much Western scholarship toward contemporary China ("China studies" in the social-science sense), while classical Sinology continued in philology, history, and archaeology. Chinese scholars such as Hu Shih and Ch'ien Mu developed parallel traditions within China, and Sinology is also a long-standing discipline in Japan.

Subfields and debates

Modern Sinology spans archaeology, linguistics, art history, religious studies, and the study of modern politics and economics. Debates within the field include the proper balance between philological and social-scientific methods, the concept of "Orientalism" applied to Western writing on China, and, more recently, the influence of Chinese state funding (such as Confucius Institutes) on academic freedom.
