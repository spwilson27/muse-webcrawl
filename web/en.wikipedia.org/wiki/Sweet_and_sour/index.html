Sweet and sour

Sweet and sour is a generic term encompassing many styles of sauce, cuisine and cooking methods. It is commonly used in East Asia and Southeast Asia, and has been used in England since the Middle Ages. Sweet and sour sauces remain popular in Asian and Western cuisines alike.

Sweet and sour in Chinese cuisine

Many places in China claim to be the birthplace of sweet and sour dishes. Sweet and sour pork (gu lǎo ròu, 咕咾肉) is one of the best-known examples: battered, deep-fried pork pieces are stir-fried in a sauce of sugar, vinegar, ketchup or hawthorn, and often served with bell peppers and pineapple. Cantonese cuisine is particularly associated with a refined balance of sweetness and acidity, and the style spread worldwide with the Cantonese diaspora.

Other Chinese sweet and sour dishes include sweet and sour fish, ribs, and the Shandong-style carp in sweet and sour sauce, which uses black vinegar. In northern China, sauces may be thinner and based on vinegar and sugar alone, while Cantonese versions are thicker and more vividly coloured.

In other cuisines

In the Philippines, escabeche is a sweet and sour fish dish. Korean cuisine has tangsuyuk, sweet and sour pork or beef. German and Ashkenazi Jewish cooking also feature sweet-and-sour preparations, such as cabbage and brisket dishes flavoured with vinegar and sugar.

Western adaptations

In Western countries, bright red sweet and sour sauce is a common dipping sauce for egg rolls, crab rangoon and chicken nuggets, and sweet and sour chicken balls are a staple of Chinese takeaway menus in Britain, Canada and the United States.
