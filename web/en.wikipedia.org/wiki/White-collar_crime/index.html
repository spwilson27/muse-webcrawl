White-collar crime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

White-collar crime is a term for financially motivated, nonviolent crime committed by businesses, government and business professionals — fraud, embezzlement, insider trading, bribery, money laundering, tax evasion and kindred offences. Coined by criminologist Edwin Sutherland in 1939, the concept was an accusation: criminology studied the poor's street crimes while ignoring the "crime in the upper or white-collar class", committed by respectable persons in the course of their occupation, whose aggregate social cost (financial loss, erosion of trust) he argued exceeded that of conventional crime.

Enforcement debates

The category resists the standard criminal-justice machinery: offences are technically complex, concealed within legitimate transactions, dispersed among many victims (often unaware), and organisationally authored (the corporation as defendant — deferred prosecution agreements, compliance monitors and fines versus the rare individual prosecution). Enforcement oscillates politically: savings-and-loan prosecutions (1980s), the post-Enron Sarbanes–Oxley era (2002), relative leniency after 2008 (few senior individual convictions, enormous corporate settlements — "too big to jail" becoming the critique), and crypto-era fraud cases re-centring individual culpability. Sutherland's equality challenge — whether the system punishes the powerful as it punishes the poor — remains the concept's permanent question.
