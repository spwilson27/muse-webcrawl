Six Sigma (6σ) is a set of techniques and tools for process improvement. It was introduced by American engineer Bill Smith while working at Motorola in 1986. Six Sigma strategies seek to improve manufacturing quality by identifying and removing the causes of defects and minimizing variability in manufacturing and business processes.

Overview

The term derives from the Greek letter sigma (σ), denoting standard deviation. A process that operates at "six sigma" quality is one in which, allowing for a conventional 1.5-sigma long-term drift, only 3.4 defects occur per million opportunities. Practitioners use statistical methods and a defined sequence of steps, and create a special infrastructure of people within the organization — "Champions", "Black Belts", "Green Belts" — who lead and execute projects.

Methodologies

DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) is used to improve existing processes. DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify) is used to create new product or process designs. Six Sigma is frequently combined with lean manufacturing principles as Lean Six Sigma.

History and adoption

Motorola reported billions of dollars in savings from the program and won the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award in 1988. General Electric under Jack Welch adopted Six Sigma in 1995 and credited it with large cost savings; it subsequently spread across manufacturing, healthcare, finance, and services.

Criticism

Critics argue that Six Sigma can stifle innovation, that its statistical foundations are sometimes applied loosely, and that reported savings are difficult to verify independently.
