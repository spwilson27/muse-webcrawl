Livestock guardian dog

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A Great Pyrenees with its flock

A livestock guardian dog (LGD) is a type of dog bred and trained to live full-time with sheep, goats, cattle or poultry in order to protect them from predators such as wolves, coyotes, bears, leopards and stray dogs. Unlike herding dogs, which move stock under a handler's direction, guardian dogs work largely independently: they are raised from puppyhood with the animals they will guard, bond with the flock rather than with people, and defend it by patrolling, scent-marking, barking warnings and, if necessary, confronting the predator directly.

Breeds and history

Livestock guardians are among the oldest types of domestic dog, depicted guarding flocks in ancient Mesopotamian and Roman writings; the Roman agricultural writer Columella already recommended large white flock guardians so shepherds could distinguish them from wolves at dawn. Traditional breeds developed across the mountain zones of Europe and Asia where transhumant shepherding met large predators: the Great Pyrenees and Pyrenean Mastiff of the Pyrenees, the Maremma Sheepdog of Italy, the Kuvasz and Komondor of Hungary, the Šarplaninac of the Balkans, the Kangal and Akbash of Anatolia, the Tibetan Mastiff and the Central Asian Shepherd. Most are large (45–70 kg), heavily coated, often white or light-coloured, and selected for a calm, observant, low-prey-drive temperament. The type fell into decline in Western Europe as wolves were exterminated, but revived from the 1970s in the United States, Australia and South Africa as a non-lethal predator-control tool, and again in Europe as wolves and bears returned; conservation programmes also use LGDs to protect cheetahs' prey in Namibia, reducing retaliatory killing of predators.
