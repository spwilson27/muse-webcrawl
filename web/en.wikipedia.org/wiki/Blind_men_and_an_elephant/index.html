The blind men and an elephant is a parable from ancient India about a group of blind men who have never encountered an elephant and try to learn what it is like by touching it. Each man feels only one part — the side, tusk, trunk, leg, ear, or tail — and confidently generalises from that part, so they disagree completely and come to blows. The story illustrates how limited experience and failure to account for others' perspectives produce confident error, and how the truth may exceed any single viewpoint.

Versions

The earliest surviving versions are Buddhist: in the Udana (c. 1st century BC), the Buddha tells the story to monks quarrelling over doctrine, comparing sectarian teachers to the blind men. Hindu versions appear in commentaries on the Upanishads, and Jain versions use the tale to teach anekantavada, the doctrine of many-sidedness. The story travelled westward — the Sufi teacher Rumi retold it in the Masnavi — and eastward into Japanese and Thai folklore. In the nineteenth century the American poet John Godfrey Saxe popularised it in English verse.

Interpretations

Different traditions draw different morals. Buddhist tellings stress the folly of dogmatic dispute; Jain tellings commend epistemic humility and synthesis; modern users apply the parable to scientific specialisation, interdisciplinary research, and political polarisation. Its "elephant in the room" imagery is unrelated, but the parable remains one of the most widely used metaphors for partial truth.
