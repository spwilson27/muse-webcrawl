The saxtuba is a large valved brass instrument invented by the Belgian instrument maker Adolphe Sax in the mid-19th century. It belongs to the family of valved bugles that also includes the saxhorns, and was designed for use in French military bands and for ceremonial and operatic spectacle.

Design

The saxtuba was built in several sizes, from tenor to contrabass, with a wide conical bore, a large upward-facing bell, and a vertical form modelled on the Roman buccina — the circular trumpet carried in ancient triumphal processions. Sax arranged for the instrument's tubing to wrap in a great circle so that the bell projected above the player's head, an arrangement intended for outdoor and theatrical effect.

History

Sax developed the saxtuba around 1845 and refined it in the early 1850s. Its most famous appearance is in Fromental Halévy's opera Le Juif errant (1852), whose triumphal scenes called for a band of saxtubas on stage. Berlioz also took an interest in Sax's large brass instruments. The saxtuba never became a standard orchestral or band instrument: it was expensive, cumbersome, and largely superseded by the modern tuba and euphonium family, and surviving examples are now mostly museum pieces.
