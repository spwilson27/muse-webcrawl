A stone tool is, in the most general sense, any tool made wholly or partly of stone that has been deliberately shaped. Stone tools were manufactured and used by early humans and their ancestors for millions of years, and their study is central to archaeology and the understanding of human evolution. Although stone tool–dependent cultures existed into recent times, stone tools are most strongly associated with prehistoric cultures that preceded the invention of metalworking.

History

The oldest known stone tools come from Lomekwi 3 in Kenya, dated to about 3.3 million years ago — older than the genus Homo itself. The Oldowan industry, beginning around 2.6 million years ago, produced simple choppers and flakes. The later Acheulean industry is famous for its symmetrical hand axes (bifaces), which remained in use for well over a million years. Later technologies, such as the Mousterian prepared-core techniques of Neanderthals and the blade and microlith industries of the Upper Palaeolithic, show increasing sophistication.

Manufacture

Stone tools are typically made by knapping: striking a core of suitable stone — such as flint, chert, or obsidian — with a hammerstone or antler billet to detach sharp flakes. Because these stones fracture conchoidally, a skilled knapper can control the shape of the resulting flakes and edges. Techniques include hard-hammer and soft-hammer percussion, pressure flaking, and indirect percussion.

Uses

Stone tools served for cutting meat, scraping hides, shaping wood and bone, processing plants, and as projectile points and other weapons. Microwear analysis of ancient edges allows archaeologists to infer the materials a tool was used on.
