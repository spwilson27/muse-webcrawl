Organist

An organist performing at a pipe organ console.

An organist is a musician who plays any type of organ, including church organs, theatre organs, the chapel organ or the home organ. An organist may play organ works solo, play with an ensemble or orchestra, or accompany one or more singers or instrumental soloists. In addition, an organist may accompany congregational hymn singing and play liturgical music.

History

Organists were already important figures in the Middle Ages and Renaissance, when organs became standard fixtures of cathedrals and large churches. The Baroque era produced celebrated organist-composers such as Dieterich Buxtehude and Johann Sebastian Bach, whose organ works remain central to the repertoire. The nineteenth century saw virtuoso concert organists such as César Franck, Charles-Marie Widor and Louis Vierne in France, while the twentieth century expanded the role into concert halls and recording studios.

Roles

The term "cathedral organist" is used for the organist of a cathedral, many of whom combine the role with that of choirmaster. Town hall and concert hall organists are often civic appointments. Because the organist frequently directs the choir from the console, church organists commonly receive training in choral conducting, improvisation and liturgical accompaniment.

Training

Organ playing requires the coordination of both hands on multiple manuals and the feet on the pedalboard, and professional organists typically study at conservatoires or university music departments. Professional bodies such as the Royal College of Organists in the United Kingdom and the American Guild of Organists in the United States award diplomas and fellowships (such as ARCO and FRCO) recognising proficiency.
