Commander-in-Chief of the Forces

Commander-in-Chief of the Forces
CountryGreat Britain / United Kingdom
Established1672 (first appointment)
Abolished1904

Commander-in-Chief of the Forces, often abbreviated to C-in-C, was the professional head of the English and later British Army from the late seventeenth century until 1904. The postholder exercised command of the land forces under the authority of the monarch, working alongside the Secretary at War and later the Secretary of State for War.

History

The first appointment is generally dated to 1672, when the Duke of Monmouth was made Captain-General. The office was held by a succession of senior generals, including the Duke of Cambridge, who served for nearly four decades (1856–1895) and resisted many reforms. Its last holder was Field Marshal Lord Roberts.

Abolition

Following criticism of the Army's performance in the Second Boer War, the Esher Committee's recommendations led in 1904 to the abolition of the post and its replacement by the Army Council, with the professional headship passing to the Chief of the General Staff.
