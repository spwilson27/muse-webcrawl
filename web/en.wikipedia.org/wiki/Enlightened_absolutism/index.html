Enlightened absolutism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Enlightened absolutism, also called enlightened despotism, is the form of absolute monarchy practised by several eighteenth-century European rulers influenced by the Enlightenment: Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia, Joseph II of Austria, Charles III of Spain, Gustav III of Sweden and Leopold of Tuscany among them. Without surrendering supreme power, they pursued reform from above — religious toleration (limited), legal codification, abolition of torture and sometimes of serfdom, promotion of education, science and efficient, uniform administration.

Frederick styled himself the “first servant of the state”, corresponded with Voltaire and codified Prussian law; Joseph II went furthest and fastest — the Toleration Patent (1781), abolition of personal serfdom, dissolution of contemplative monasteries, equality before the law in criminal matters — provoking revolts that forced his successors to rescind much. Catherine's Nakaz (1767) borrowed Montesquieu and Beccaria while her reign deepened Russian serfdom in practice. Historians debate whether enlightened absolutism was genuine reform or a modernising technique of power — “everything for the people, nothing by the people” in the contemporary formula — but agree it reshaped European states and prepared, paradoxically, both administrative modernity and the revolutionary reaction against unaccountable power.
