Autosomal recessive

Autosomal recessive is a pattern of inheritance characteristic of some genetic disorders. "Autosomal" means that the gene in question is located on one of the numbered, or non-sex, chromosomes. "Recessive" means that two copies of the mutated gene (one from each parent) are required to cause the disorder. A person with only one copy is a carrier and usually does not exhibit symptoms.

Inheritance pattern

When two carriers have a child, each child has, on average, a 25% chance of inheriting two mutated copies and being affected, a 50% chance of being a carrier, and a 25% chance of inheriting two normal copies. Affected individuals are often born to unaffected parents, and the condition may appear to "skip" generations. Males and females are affected with equal frequency.

Examples

Well-known autosomal recessive conditions include cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Tay–Sachs disease, phenylketonuria, spinal muscular atrophy, and thalassaemias. Many metabolic disorders follow this pattern. The frequency of some recessive conditions is elevated in populations where consanguineous marriage is common, and some recessive alleles persist because carriers gain an advantage—for example, sickle cell trait confers partial resistance to malaria.
